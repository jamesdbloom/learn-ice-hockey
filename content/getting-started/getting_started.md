## Hockey is administered locally, but it sits on a national floor

**A national governing body sets the playing rules, insurance and safeguarding — and two things beginners assume are local: your age category and the protective equipment you must wear.** Your club, association or rink sets the rest — cost, level names, session times, and the gear a session asks for — and that changes every season. So most of your questions have a person attached rather than a document, and this page is mostly about who to ask.

One question does not work that way — whether your league allows body checking — and it leads the Key focus below.

## Key focus

**Find out whether your league allows body checking before your first game, not after your first hit.** ⚠️ **Ask your coach or your league secretary, because the rink desk cannot answer it.** It is set by your age group, your league and your country, and it is a different question from what a casual session's house rules allow. [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md#1-which-contact-rules-are-you-actually-playing-under) covers it.

**Before any session, ask what gear is required, what standard it is, and whether there is contact.** Turning up to a full-contact session expecting a skills session is how people get hurt.

**Learn to skate first, or alongside, and work hardest on stopping on both sides and skating backwards.** Skating, not shooting, is what sorts players into levels.

**Start a level below where your pride puts you.** Moving up mid-season is easy and welcomed; moving down is not. This is judgement, not sourced guidance.

**Register through your club before you play, and check that the league is sanctioned before you pay.** Registration is your insurance, safeguarding and eligibility, and in England you are insured only in sanctioned hockey.

**In Britain, wear a neck laceration protector every time you go on the ice.** It is mandatory at every level in all four home nations, adults included, and under England Ice Hockey's last published rules and the Elite League's it covers training too. ⚠️ **Do not count on a warning: in IHUK, England, Scottish and university competitions you are penalised on the spot** — only the Elite League warns first.

**Arrive dressed 20 to 30 minutes before a practice, 45 to 60 before a game, and a full hour before your first session.** Dressing takes twice as long the first three times.

**Keep your shifts short and go hard while you are out there.** Good shifts last 30 to 45 seconds. New players stay out too long, and that is the main reason first sessions feel so exhausting. At a drop-in, a long shift is someone else's turn.

**Keep your eyes up and look around between touches.** You will barely touch the puck, so most of what you do happens without it. Look while a pass is travelling, not after it lands.

## How to tell

Three cues you can read for yourself:

- **A session's name tells you almost nothing.** "Drop-in", "shinny" and "stick-and-puck" are loose terms. The rink's posted description is the authority; if it says nothing, ask at the desk.
- **Skating sorts players into levels**, not shooting or hockey sense — **experience-based judgement rather than sourced guidance.** If you cannot stop on both sides and skate backwards, start in a beginner group.
- **A goaltender is ready when they are facing you, set, with the stick down.** One who is drinking, adjusting a strap or talking is not. Shooting at a goalie who is not ready can hurt them.

## Overview

**The way in is graded, and it starts before hockey.** Public skating or a learn-to-skate class, then a one-off taster, then a beginners' block, then a team or league. The NHL/NHLPA Learn to Play programme strongly encourages learning to skate before signing up for hockey, and that applies to adults too.

**Skating decides how fast you get into real games.** Until you can stop on both sides and skate backwards, a beginner group is where you belong, however good your hands. A player placed a tier too high spends the season hanging on rather than improving.

**Most first-season safety comes from questions asked before you step on the ice.** Whether your league allows body checking matters most, and your coach or league secretary knows the answer. What one session allows is a house rule the rink can tell you that night. In Britain the neck guard is never optional, and in England you are insured only when you are registered and the game is sanctioned.

**On the ice, two habits matter more than any skill.** Short shifts: go hard for well under a minute and come off, because a tired player is a slow and sloppy one. Eyes up: most of a shift is away from the puck, so use that time to see where your teammates are, where the space is and who is coming.

## 1. How hockey is organised, and why that matters to you

Almost everywhere, there are four layers between you and the ice:

| Layer | What it does | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **International federation** | Rules for international play; recognises one national body per country | The IIHF, which has **84 member national associations** — 61 full members and 23 associate members (IIHF, *Member National Associations*) |
| **National governing body** | Registration, insurance, playing rules, coach and official certification, safeguarding | USA Hockey; Hockey Canada; **Ice Hockey UK** — see the Britain section immediately below, because this layer changed on 30 June 2026 |
| **Region / district / branch** | Governance and administration between the national body and your club | USA Hockey is divided into **12 geographical districts** containing **34 affiliates** (*USA Hockey Parent Handbook 2025-26*) |
| **Local association, club or rink** | The thing you actually join. Sets the fees, the schedule, the teams and the tiers | Your town's minor hockey association; your city's adult league; your local rink's drop-in schedule |

**In practice:** the national body's website tells you what registration *is* and what it buys. It will not tell you what your season costs or which night you skate. That comes from the bottom layer, and you get it by ringing them.

### If you are starting in Britain

Four things about British hockey differ enough from the North American picture that a beginner needs them first.

**1. Who governs what.** England Ice Hockey's *Rules & Regulations*, written before the June 2026 merger in point 2, set out the bodies:

| Body | What it governed before the merger |
|---|---|
| **England Ice Hockey (EIH)** | *"the National Governing Body (NGB) responsible for the governance, administration, development and promotion of the sport of ice hockey in **England and Wales**"* |
| **Scottish Ice Hockey Association (SIHA, also written SIH)** | Scotland. EIH's own document names it as one of the stakeholders it works with |
| **Ice Hockey UK (IHUK)** | The IIHF member body and the GB national teams. It also administers the paperwork that lets a player arrive from abroad |
| **British Universities Ice Hockey Association (BUIHA)** | University hockey. It adopts the IIHF Rule Book and the same In-House Rules alongside IHUK, and is the one body named beside IHUK in the current edition — it did not merge into it |

**Two details in EIH's own words.** The sport EIH governs is *"grassroots (junior) ice hockey, women's ice hockey, senior ice hockey, para (sledge) ice hockey, SEND (Special Educational Needs and Disability) ice hockey, and recreational (community) ice hockey"*. And a player arriving from abroad goes through Ice Hockey UK for both IIHF international transfers and work visas *"defined by Ice Hockey UK and HM Border Agency"*.

**"EIHA" and "England Ice Hockey" are the same organisation** — formerly the English Ice Hockey Association, a name you will still see on club sites and older paperwork. Since 30 June 2026 both names refer to a body that has merged into Ice Hockey UK (point 2). EIH's Rules & Regulations say "England and Wales" throughout, which is more than the playing-rules documents establish.

**2. There is now one governing body for all four home nations, and it is very new.** On **30 June 2026** **England Ice Hockey**, **Scottish Ice Hockey** and **Ice Hockey UK** merged into a single body covering England, Northern Ireland, Scotland and Wales. Ice Hockey UK is now the national governing body for all four, and the IIHF member for the United Kingdom.

From IHUK's member update of 26 June 2026: **Scottish Ice Hockey and England Ice Hockey memberships were terminated from 30 June**, because new IHUK memberships replace them, and *"all current insurance arrangements under SIH and EIH will continue unchanged."* So **do not assume last season's registration process still applies.** Ask your club which body you are registering with, for which membership year, and what your insurance covers — as of late July 2026 those answers were still being rebuilt.

The EIHA material quoted in this document, above all in section 6, is still the clearest account of how English club registration works, but the body that issued it has merged. Treat it as the shape of the process, not the current paperwork.

**Two things the merger has not changed.** The **game** is the same: the 2026-27 In-House Rules name **IHUK and the BUIHA** as adopting the IIHF Rule Book, where the 2025-26 edition named England Ice Hockey, the SIHA and the BUIHA, and the IIHF book underneath is the same. (Neither edition says who *issues* the In-House Rules.) And the **substance** of registration — what it covers, your obligations, when the year runs — is in EIH's Rules & Regulations, quoted in section 6. Read the merger as a change to who collects your fee.

**3. Your league plays IIHF rules, not NHL rules — and that is written down.** These documents default to the NHL book; yours is the **IIHF Official Rule Book**. England Ice Hockey: *"All EIH sanctioned games and tournaments must abide by the Rules of Ice Hockey as described by the International Ice Hockey Federation (IIHF)"*, and *"[w]here this section contradicts IIHF rules, EIH rules will take precedence."* The In-House Rules agree: **IHUK and the BUIHA** *"adopt the latest IIHF Official Rule Book (Version 1.1, published July 2025) as the foundation for the rules of the game to be applied in England, Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland (the Home Countries)"*, and are *"not intended to serve as a standalone rule book."*

So expect **three** layers: **the IIHF rulebook** for what is a penalty, **the In-House Rules** for the domestic amendments, and **your section's Rules of Competition** for how the competition runs — roster sizes, minimum bench strength, tiering, suspensions. EIH says the third layer is elsewhere and that it wins: *"League rules can be found in the Rules of Competition for each section"*, and *"[a]ny section specific requirements detailed in their rules of competition override this document"*.

**[UK and England Rules](../foundation/uk_rules.md) carries the amendment list** — read it before your first game. On day one, what matters is that the **IIHF** flags in these documents are yours:

- **Under the In-House Rules your centre is replaced for any faceoff violation except after an icing** (In-House Rule 76). The Elite League writes no Rule 76, so its draws run the 2026/27 IIHF book, where the centre is also *"ejected from the face-off circle"* (76.6). Either way, a second violation by the same team on the same draw is a bench minor, and ⚠️ **winning a draw with your skates is itself a violation** (IIHF Rule 76.3(VI), both editions), where the NHL allows it. See [Faceoffs](../systems/faceoffs.md).
- **After your own icing you cannot change lines or take a time-out** (an injured player can still be replaced), as in the NHL (IIHF Rules 81.4 and 87.1). USA Hockey allows both, so North American advice here does not transfer.
- **Never hit anyone from behind.** Under the IIHF book — yours — there is no minor for it: Rule 43.2 says *"there is no option to award a minor penalty"*, and Rule 43.3 makes a check from behind that *"recklessly endangers"* the player a major and an automatic game misconduct. USA Hockey, Hockey Canada and CARHA do write a minor, so their coaching material may describe an option your game lacks — ⚠️ **but in none of them is it a bare two minutes.** Under CARHA and Hockey Canada the minor always carries a game misconduct, so it ends your game, and driving a player from behind into the boards or goal frame so they cannot protect themselves is a match penalty. Under USA Hockey the minor carries at least a misconduct, a forceful hit on a player facing the boards, or one sending them head first into the boards or goal frame, is at least a major and a game misconduct, and a reckless hit with excessive force on a defenceless player is a match penalty. [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md) sets the books side by side.
- **Wherever you play in Britain, a goalie should play as though the trapezoid binds, and attackers should expect the other goalie to come out for a dumped puck.** IIHF Rule 27.7 restricts the goaltender to a marked area behind the net, on pain of a delay-of-game minor. England Ice Hockey's Rules & Regulations say the *"Goalkeepers' Restricted Area is not currently enforced to allow ice rinks the necessary time to make the changes to implement this rule"* — but EIH governs England and Wales only (*Rules & Regulations* 1.1), the In-House Rules that reach Scotland, Northern Ireland and BUIHA hockey are silent on it, and the Elite League's Casebook falls back on the 2026/27 IIHF book. So nobody can tell you for certain, and both habits cost nothing either way. The EIH edition quoted is **2024-2025**, and neither the 2025-26 nor the 2026-27 In-House Rules mentions the area (both were searched), so this may have moved. Your rink may also have no trapezoid painted on it. See [Goaltender](../positions/goaltender.md).

**4. Your rink is probably not 200 by 85 feet.** Most diagrams, distances and angles in hockey coaching come from the NHL sheet. The IIHF book allows a range: **Rule 1.2 permits any sheet 60 m long and between 26 m and 30 m wide** — over thirteen feet of difference in width between two legal rinks. So "we play IIHF rules" tells you nothing about the size of the ice.

On a wide sheet the corners are further from the net, the half-wall (the boards level with the faceoff dot) is further from the slot (the dangerous ice in front of the net), and angling — steering an attacker with your skating line rather than hitting them — into the boards stops being a kill shot. **[Rink Map](../foundation/rink_map.md) covers rink geometry**, including what to check on your first skate at an unfamiliar rink. Read it before you take the systems documents' spacing literally.

**Here is one end zone of the NHL sheet, showing the corners, half-wall and slot. Take the names from it, not the distances.** The names are convention rather than rulebook: many of these areas are not painted on the ice, coaches draw their edges differently, and the trapezoid behind the net is painted on some rinks and not others. [Rink Map](../foundation/rink_map.md) has the whole sheet.

![](diagram:rink-map-end-zone)

Where a diagram draws players rather than places, [Reading Ice Hockey Diagrams](../reading-diagrams/reading_ice_hockey_diagrams.md) is the key: a circle is a forward, a triangle a defenceman, a bare **G** the goaltender, and the unfilled shapes are the team the picture is drawn for.

The In-House Rules go lower still: they record that "the NGB" authorises rinks *below 56 m × 26 m* — shorter than the IIHF's 60 m and narrower than its 26 m minimum — in *"all fixtures"* those rules govern, though not the Elite League's, with two named venues restricted to U16 and below. [UK and England Rules](../foundation/uk_rules.md) has the detail. The instruction is the same: **look at the sheet you are actually standing on.**

## 2. Routes onto the ice — adults

### Step zero: can you skate?

If you cannot skate, learn first or alongside. You do not need to be good. The NHL/NHLPA Learn to Play programme says this for children, and it applies to adults too: participants are *"strongly encouraged to complete a 'Learn to Skate' program at a local rink before signing up."*

Public skating is the cheapest ice you will ever buy. Going twice a week for a month before you spend anything on gear is a good plan. See [Skating](../technique/skating.md) for what to work on — almost every adult skating fault is a straight-legs fault.

### The four things adults can sign up for

**1. Adult learn-to-play / "hockey 101" programmes.** A fixed block of sessions mixing instruction with a supervised scrimmage. They go by many names and are the best entry point for an adult beginner, because everyone else there is new too.

One sourced example: the Adult Safe Hockey League's **ASHL Hockey 101**, as listed on 1 September 2026. Each session runs to the same plan: a five-minute warm-up, team tactics and skill work, and a **30-minute guided scrimmage**. ASHL describes it as *"ideal for players looking to join the ASHL, players coming back to the game after a prolonged absence, or current ASHL players in a lower level looking to gain an edge on their opponent."*

**The blocks vary a lot, and the page is re-listed every season, so this is a dated snapshot, not a price list.** On **1 September 2026** its thirteen player listings ran from **4 sessions to 23** and from **CAD $148.00 to CAD $782.00**. Goalies were listed at **$0.00 in eleven of the thirteen**; two rinks charged them the same as skaters.

**Do not budget from these figures.** They show the *shape*: a handful of coached sessions ending in a game, often — not always — free or cheap if you will play goal.

**2. Stick-and-puck / drop-in / shinny.** Unstructured paid ice. No coach, no registration beyond a waiver, pay at the door. Covered in detail in section 4.

**3. An adult recreational league.** A season of scheduled games, usually in divisions. Some leagues place individual players on a team; others take only whole teams, so ask. ⚠️ **Ask in the same call whether it is a checking league.** Adult hockey is not uniformly non-contact: USA Hockey's checking ban is written for *"all **non-check** Adult classifications"* (Rule 604(a)), which is only some of them, and being a beginner does not put you in one. Britain has checking adult competitions too. [UK and England Rules](../foundation/uk_rules.md) and [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md#1-which-contact-rules-are-you-actually-playing-under) settle which you are in.

**4. A club that runs an adult section.** In England, the EIHA says *"Most clubs across England offer beginner sessions and learn-to-play programmes designed for adults and children of all ages"* and *"Most clubs are happy to arrange a trial session so you can experience the sport before making any commitment."* Asking for a trial is normal.

### The English route in, as England Ice Hockey itself describes it

**EIH publishes no "how to start playing" pathway.** It does sell four registrations, and three are for people not yet playing games — which tells you what a beginner is expected to do:

- **Trial** — a **28-day registration** for *"training sessions with the designated team/club"*, with *"training only insurance"*, at **most two per calendar year**. This is the formal "can I come and try it", and its existence shows that asking is normal.
- **Learn to Play (L2P)** — a **10-week registration** *"that allows an individual to play within a formal L2P scheme"*, covering *"EIH L2P events, club or rink led programmes"* and, where the club runs one, *"in-house game play"* delivered *"as structured learning activity by coaches within a single club environment."* It is England's version of the North American learn-to-play block, delivered by **the club or the rink**, not EIH.
- **Insurance only** — **season-long**, for *"training only"*, and also covers L2P. For someone skating with a club but not yet playing fixtures.
- **Standard registration** — **season-long**, and the one that lets you *"take part in all sanctioned activities"*: L2P, team training, development events, sanctioned training camps and competitive fixtures.

**EIH's Rules & Regulations do not tell you how to find any of this** — no session finder, club directory, learn-to-skate scheme or taster calendar. It is a governance document for clubs that already exist.

**So ring your local rink, then your national body.** The rink knows its public skating, its learn-to-skate class and which clubs use its ice, because it sells them the ice. The national body knows which clubs are affiliated. This document will not invent a session, a fee or a club name to fill the gap; section 10 has the starting points and the questions.

### A note on playing goal

If you are thinking of goaltending, say so early. Goalies are scarce at every amateur level, and the price often reflects it — most of the ASHL Hockey 101 listings above charged goalies nothing (one league's page on one date), though two charged full price. Clubs often own goal equipment and lend it. **Ask; do not assume it is free — and borrow everything except the mask.** [Equipment](../off-the-ice/equipment.md) treats a goalie mask as a helmet: you cannot see a crushed liner or a hairline crack, and you cannot know a shared mask's impact history. Borrow the pads, blocker, trapper and chest protector; buy your own mask. See [Goaltender](../positions/goaltender.md).

## 3. Routes onto the ice — children

**Before you choose where your child plays, find out whether checking is permitted at their age and division.** [Body Contact and Battles → 1. Which Contact Rules Are You Actually Playing Under?](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md#1-which-contact-rules-are-you-actually-playing-under) settles it, and separates body contact from body checking. British readers: see its [If you play in Britain](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md#if-you-play-in-britain) section.

### Try-it-once events

**Try Hockey For Free** (USA Hockey) is a one-session taster at hundreds of rinks. From its event site, retrieved 27 July 2026: the national dates are **3 October 2026 and 27 February 2027**, for ages **4 to 9**, and *"Most host locations will have loaner equipment, however, you are welcome to bring your own if you have it"* — **take your own helmet if you have one (see section 8).** And: *"Do I need a USA Hockey membership to Try Hockey? No!"* A waiver at registration covers the event. USA Hockey's *Come Play Youth Hockey* page — not the event site — calls it *"the world's largest introduction-to-hockey initiative, annually spanning more than 800 rinks nationwide."*

**Esso Fun Days** (Hockey Canada) is the equivalent introduction for girls and women. Hockey Canada's parent FAQ (its pathway page only names it) describes it as *"a hockey experience program that combines on-ice and off-ice instruction to give girls and women of all ages a first taste of organized hockey, with no fees and no obligations to sign up for a league."*

### Learn-to-skate

**Learn to Skate USA** runs at *"nearly 1,000 locations"* in the United States. In Canada it is **CanSkate**, through Skate Canada clubs; Hockey Canada's pathway page points parents to Skate Canada's club finder and to municipal programmes. In England and elsewhere, the rink usually runs the class.

Learn-to-skate is not a hockey programme and need not be finished first — but a child who can stand, glide and stop unaided will get far more out of their first hockey session.

### Learn-to-play

**NHL/NHLPA Learn to Play**, delivered with USA Hockey and NHL clubs, is the flagship North American entry programme. From its About page, retrieved 27 July 2026:

- It is for **first-time players aged 4 to 9** — genuinely first-time: *"The program is offered to new, first-time participants… those participants who have not previously been registered to USA Hockey a sanctioned youth hockey program."*
- It runs **approximately 4–8 weeks**, one lesson a week.
- **Head-to-toe equipment is provided**, for *"a nominal participation fee"* that *"may vary by market and facility."* Participants are *"properly fitted with equipment during an in-person fitting event"*, and *"All the equipment provided will be for participants to keep upon completion of the program."* It is not literally everything: the equipment page adds *"we recommend all participants purchase a mouth guard, neck guard and athletic supporter."* ⚠️ **Neither the About nor the Equipment page says the kit is new**, so if that matters — and for a helmet it does — ask the programme.
- Governing-body registration is included for the programme year. USA Hockey *"membership runs Sept 1-Aug 31 annually."*
- Registration is **online only** — *"phone and/or in-person registration at individual rinks will not be accepted"* — and popular sites have waiting lists.

**The First Shift** (NHL/NHLPA with Hockey Canada and Bauer) is the Canadian counterpart: Hockey Canada describes it as a *"6-week learn-to-play program for kids aged 6 to 10 that includes head-to-toe Bauer equipment."* **Reach it from Hockey Canada's pathway page, not a remembered web address** — see the note below.

> **Verification note:** the domain `thefirstshift.ca` does not reach the programme. On 27 July 2026 it redirected to an unrelated commercial site; on 1 September 2026 it served a domain registrar's parking page. `firstshift.ca` refuses automated retrieval. The description above is quoted from **Hockey Canada's** pathway page, which links to `firstshift.ca`.

### If your child is starting in England

England Ice Hockey's Rules & Regulations describe no taster event, learn-to-skate scheme or small-area-games mandate, so **this document cannot tell you what an English child's first session looks like.** What EIH does establish:

- **Junior hockey is mixed.** *"All junior leagues are mixed-gender"*, so there is no separate girls' ladder at junior level, and the age categories are unisex — [UK and England Rules](../foundation/uk_rules.md) has them.
- **The youngest category is Under 10, and you cannot be too late for it.** A U10 player must be *"under the age of 10 on 31 December in the year that the registration period commences"*, and where every other registration closes on **31 January**, *"those registering at U10… may register at any time."*
- **A cage, not a visor.** *"Face cages for all junior hockey games are mandatory, and must be worn as per the manufacturers stipulations. Cages must be tight against the player's chin at all times."*
- **Safeguarding sits on the club.** An affiliated club must *"[h]ave a Designated Safeguarding Lead if there are players aged under 18 in the Club"*. Ask who yours is — a club that cannot answer has a problem.
- **Junior games are filmed.** *"Every junior fixture, including challenge games, must be recorded"*, on an EIH-owned camera system, so recordings can go to disciplinary panels on request.

### Then: joining a team

After a learn-to-play block, the next step is a full season with a local association. In Canada that means the **Timbits U7** cross-ice programme or the **U9** half-ice programme; in the US, an **8U** or **10U** team. Both bodies keep the youngest on a smaller surface on purpose — Hockey Canada mandates cross-ice at U7 and half-ice at U9, and USA Hockey's recommended 8U experience is *"no full-time goalies, 7-9 players per team, station-based practices and cross-ice games."* The small surface is the point, not a compromise.

## 4. Drop-in, stick-and-puck and shinny — what the words mean

These three words are **conventions, not defined categories**, and rinks use them loosely. **The rink's own posted description is the authority** — and if it says nothing, ring and ask, because turning up to a full-contact drop-in expecting a skills session is how people get hurt.

| Session type | Usually has | Usually does not have |
|---|---|---|
| **Stick-and-puck** — usually open ice for individual practice | Full gear or at least helmet and gloves; many pucks; people shooting, stickhandling and skating on their own | Teams, a game, a goalie, referees |
| **Drop-in** — usually a pick-up game: you pay, you are given a jersey colour, you play | Full gear, goalies (sometimes), rolling shifts, a scoreboard nobody watches | Referees, standings, any obligation beyond the session |
| **Shinny** — usually informal pick-up hockey, indoor or outdoor; in much of Canada it is the standard municipal-rink term for pick-up | Varies enormously — some are no-contact, no-lift-the-puck, no-goalie sessions for all ages | Anything reliable enough to assume |

**The three questions that actually matter, and you ask them at the desk:**

1. **What gear is required?** Many stick-and-puck sessions require only a helmet; most drop-ins require full equipment and will turn you away without it.
2. **What is the standard?** Rinks often run separate beginner, intermediate and advanced drop-ins. Turning up to the wrong one is the most common beginner mistake and the most easily avoided.
3. **Is there contact, and is lifting the puck allowed?** Both vary, and both are house rules rather than rulebook rules.

Drop-in and stick-and-puck ice is also, as [Practice and Development](../off-the-ice/practice_and_development.md) puts it, *"usually the cheapest, highest-value ice you can buy"* — especially for a rec player whose team gets one slot a week.

## 5. Tiers and divisions — and how to work out where you belong

**The names vary by country, association and year, and there is no single ladder.** What follows is what two national bodies publish, so you recognise the *shape* of your own version.

### Age divisions

**Hockey Canada** (2025-26 chart, from its Age Divisions page): **U7 (Timbits)** six and under, **U9** seven and eight, **U11** nine and ten, **U13** eleven and twelve, **U15** thirteen and fourteen, **U18** fifteen to seventeen, **U21** eighteen to twenty, by *"Age on Dec. 31"* of the season's first year; for Timbits U7, *"local hockey associations have the option of registering four-year-olds."* Its parent FAQ states the rule: *"Players are placed into age categories according to what age they will be by December 31 of the current calendar year."*

**USA Hockey** (Youth/Girls classifications, *Parent Handbook 2025-26*): **8U** (8 and under), **10U** (9–10), **12U** (11–12), **14U** (13–14), **15 Only** (15-year-olds), **16U** (15–16), **18U** (17–18), and **19U** for girls only (17–19).

These overlap on purpose — a 15-year-old may fit 15 Only or 16U — and the birth-date rule is set by the national body's annual guide, not the playing rulebook. **Check this year's chart, not a sibling's experience two seasons ago.**

### Competitive levels within an age group

**Hockey Canada** publishes three, and defines them clearly enough to quote:

- **House League** (also called local league) — *"a recreational level of play. Most teams at this league level have 1-2 practices and one game per week… Registration is typically inexpensive, and away games are rarely hosted more than an hour's drive away. House league players are almost always given equal ice time regardless of skill or talent."*
- **AE (Alternate/Additional Entry)** — a middle tier **not every association offers**, for near-Rep players who do not make the Rep roster. Typically two 90-minute practices and two games a week, away games up to a 90-minute drive.
- **Rep (Representative)** — the association's top team for that age group, *"about twice the time commitment of house league hockey. Expect a minimum of one practice, one development session and two games per week."* Entry is by tryout, and *"Head coaches on AE and Rep teams have the exclusive right to decide which players will be offered spots."*

**USA Hockey associations** use a looser vocabulary — "house", "travel", "select", "Tier I", "Tier II", "AAA", "AA", "A", "B" in various mixes. **These labels are set by affiliates and local associations, not a single national ladder**, so one word can mean different things two states apart. USA Hockey's own parent materials give age classifications and no national tier table, so none is given here. Ask your association what levels it runs and what each means.

### The English structure, and where a beginner actually enters it

England has no tier-vocabulary problem of the American kind, because **EIH owns its leagues outright**: *"All EIH leagues are solely owned and operated by EIH."* It has **sections** instead, and the one you register into decides which rules of competition apply. EIH's player membership categories:

| Category | Who it is for |
|---|---|
| **NIHL** | *"a player who is eligible under the NIHL Regulations to compete in the National Ice Hockey League"* — the senior league structure below the professional Elite League |
| **WNIHL** | *"a player who is eligible under the WNIHL Regulations to compete in the Women's National Ice Hockey League"* |
| **Under 18/U19** and **Under 10** | Junior hockey, by age on 31 December |
| **Recreational** | *"a player who participates in the EIH Recreational Section"* — the adult game most beginners actually join |
| **Para** and **SEND** | Para (sledge) ice hockey under BPIHA regulations, and SEND club activity |

Two things to take from that table. First, **"Recreational" is a real section with its own registration and rules of competition, not a loose word for a beer league** — an adult beginner in England almost certainly comes in through it. Second, **the tiering happens below this.** EIH publishes no national ladder of divisions: *"EIH league structure is outlined in each section's rules and is subject to change from time to time"*, and *"[l]eague rules can be found in the Rules of Competition for each section."* Get your section's Rules of Competition from your club.

**Above the NIHL sits the Elite Ice Hockey League (EIHL)**, the professional tier. It is not one of EIH's categories or leagues; EIH's rulebook mentions it only as one of the "senior" fixtures, with the NIHL, whose ice takes priority when a game is rearranged. [UK and England Rules](../foundation/uk_rules.md) says which rule book each level runs on. For a beginner: **EIHL professional at the top, NIHL senior below it, and the recreational and junior sections where you will start.**

**On who may play where**, EIH sets three rules itself: *"EIH allow women and individuals that identify as non-binary to participate in senior National Ice Hockey League(s)"*; *"EIH allows male netminders U16 to play in the Women's National Ice Hockey League"*; and *"[a]ll junior leagues are mixed-gender"*. Play in more than one league and you pay once — *"the player is only required to purchase the lowest fee for the age group they qualify for."*

### Adult tiers

Adult hockey is tiered by **age bracket** and by **standard**, usually separately. USA Hockey's 2026 Adult National Championships show the pattern — men's **40+, 50+ Tier 1, 50+ Tier 2, 50+ Tier 3, 50+ Tier 4, 60+ Tier 1, 60+ Tier 2, 60+ Tier 3, 70+ and 75+**; women's **21+ Tier 1, 21+ Tier 2, 21+ Tier 3, 30+, 40+, 50+ Tier 1, 50+ Tier 2 and 60+**. USA Hockey says these events are *"recreational, non-check, and the only pre-requisite to register is everyone must be a current USA Hockey member."*

Local adult leagues use local labels — letters (A/B/C/D), numbers (Division 1 to 6) or words (Elite / Competitive / Recreational / Beginner). Hockey Canada's affiliated **Adult Safe Hockey League** says: *"Whether you're a beginner or an ex-pro, we play year-round and we've got a division for you."* Its size is quoted two ways — Hockey Canada says *"more than 65,000 players on the ice in 13 Canadian locations"*, the ASHL *"14 locations, more than 65,000 players."* That is a fair guide to how precise any of these numbers are.

### Working out which tier you belong in

There is no test for this and no source for one, so treat the following as **experience-based judgement, not sourced guidance**:

- **Start one tier below where your ego puts you.** Moving up mid-season is easy and welcomed. Moving down is awkward, and usually follows a bad injury or a bad month.
- **Watch a game at the level you are considering.** Twenty minutes in the stands beats any description. Watch whether players can stop on both sides, take a hard pass, and *change on the fly* — substitute while play is running, as hockey mostly does — without a collision.
- **Ask the league manager or club to place you.** They do it constantly, would rather place you right than lose you, and usually judge better than you.
- **Ask for a trial or a skills session before committing.** Standard practice at most clubs, and free at many.
- **Skating is the sorting variable**, not shooting or hockey sense. If you cannot stop on both sides and skate backwards, you belong in a beginner group. See [Skating](../technique/skating.md).

## 6. Registration — what it is, what it buys, what it costs

### What you are registering with

Organised hockey almost always means an individual membership with the national governing body, taken out **through your club or association**. **British readers: the body you register with changed on 30 June 2026** — England Ice Hockey and Scottish Ice Hockey memberships ended that day and are being reissued by Ice Hockey UK, so check the current process with your club rather than working from any published sequence, including the one below (see "If you are starting in Britain" in section 1). The EIHA's description still shows the *shape*: *"Registration begins with the individual joining or being associated with an affiliated club"*, the club submits it through an online portal *"including required documents (photo ID, proof of age etc)"*, and *"Once approved… the individual is covered by insurance and eligible to participate in sanctioned activities."*

**Registration years do not run from the date you pay.** USA Hockey membership *"runs Sept 1-Aug 31 annually"*, so joining in June may buy three months, not twelve. Ask before you pay.

### Registering with England Ice Hockey

England Ice Hockey's *Rules & Regulations* governs this, and it is more specific than most beginners expect:

**You register with EIH, but only your club can complete it.** *"All individuals must register directly with EIH and follow their EIH affiliated club's membership process. EIH registration is only confirmed upon successful completion of the registration process by the affiliated club selected and provision of the documentation required by EIH as outlined in this rule book."* Registration is *"open to everyone, however some clubs may limit their membership"* — and if a club declines you or makes an error, *"an individual may transfer to the correct club by notifying the registration secretary."* You normally register for **one club only**.

**The registration year runs 1 August to 31 July, with a deadline.** *"All registrations run from 1 August to 31 July unless otherwise noted. Players may register up until 31 January of the playing season, with the exception of those registering at U10 who may register at any time."* And: ***"Pro-rata discounts are not available."*** Registering in April buys the rest of a season at the price of a whole one.

> **Two registration years are in circulation and this document cannot reconcile them.** The Rules & Regulations say 1 August to 31 July, *"unless otherwise noted"*. The EIHA's Registration page describes the **recreational** section as *"a fixed annual insurance period (from 1 October to 30 September)"* which *"must be renewed each year; registration is not valid for 12 months from date of submission."* Both may be right, but ⚠️ **ask your club which year your registration runs on before you pay**, because it changes what you get for the money.

**What the fee buys, in EIH's words:** *"All registrations include: Insurance for EIH sanctioned competitions and activities. Affiliation fees to EIH, IHUK and IIHF as appropriate. Provision of core activities to the development of ice hockey in the UK… Enforcement of EIH Rule Book and policies to a consistently high standard."* **One payment covers your affiliation up to Ice Hockey UK and the IIHF.**

**The cost is not in the rulebook, and this document will not guess.** The fee is *"determined by the Board on an annual basis and published on EIH website"*, and varies by registration type and age. Two fixed rules: you may cancel within **14 days**, with a refund *"less a fixed fee of £5.00GBP for the period of registration and an administration fee of £20.00GBP"* — and **no refund** if you have already played in competition in that time. After 14 days there are **no refunds** *"except where there is non-delivery of the services offered by EIH or in exceptional circumstances, including but not limited to season-ending injuries or long-term illness"*, judged case by case and pro-rata if agreed.

**Register before the day of the game, not on it.** *"To be eligible for EIH-sanctioned competitions, players must be registered with EIH to the club and team they are playing for prior to the day of the fixture."* A team that plays an unregistered player is sanctioned.

**Before you retire in a huff:** *"A member who has retired is unable to reapply for EIH membership for a minimum of three months following the retirement date."*

### Why it exists: insurance and safeguarding

Beginners assume this is bureaucracy. It is not.

**Insurance.** Hockey Canada's parent FAQ says its plan *"costs $21.30 per participant per season and provides $20 million in general liability coverage, plus accidental medical and dental insurance, as well as accidental death or dismemberment, and directors and officers liability insurance, to all players, coaches, volunteers and referees registered with Hockey Canada."* For adults it lists **$20 million of commercial general liability including participant-to-participant liability, up to $1 million of accidental death and dismemberment cover, and up to $5,000 of accidental dental and medical insurance.** The ASHL adds that *"Injury reports must be completed in full no later than 90 days from the date of the injury"* and claims *"may take upwards of 6-8 weeks to process."*

**In England, the insurance is the registration.** *"All players, coaches, managers, team bench off-ice officials, off-ice officials and club committee members must be registered and insured through EIH and have met the minimum eligibility requirements outlined to be insured under EIH scheme."* Cover extends to *"any EIH affiliated club training session, any EIH sanctioned competition, including Challenge Games and tournaments, and any EIH run or sanctioned hockey camp"*; **a team travelling abroad needs separate EIH travel insurance.** Claims go *"directly to EIH's insurance broker via EIH Insurance Claim Form"*, and a personal-accident claim needs *"a doctor's certificate and appropriate evidence and witnesses"* — so get seen and get it written down on the night. **This document does not have the cover limits**; EIH publishes them separately and none was verified.

**Sanctioned versus unsanctioned.** Hockey Canada says unsanctioned leagues *"operate without a constitution, without bylaws, without insurance and with their own customized rulebook for game play"*, and it *"cannot hear appeals, insure players or ensure a consistently high standard of game play"* in them. The clause that catches families out: *"If you choose to register your child in a non-member league, your child will immediately lose all Hockey Canada membership privileges for the remainder of the hockey season."* And a player is *"ineligible if participating in non-sanctioned hockey after September 30"* for the rest of that season's sanctioned play. **Know which kind of hockey you are signing up for before you sign.**

**England draws the same line harder.** *"EIH participants are only insured through EIH insurance when the game or tournament has been sanctioned by EIH"* — an unsanctioned fixture is an uninsured one. And playing in an unrecognised competition is itself an offence: *"Any EIH-registered player or official participating in domestic or international leagues or competitions not recognised by EIH or IIHF will face a suspension from all authorised domestic or international competitions and severe financial penalties."* Clubs that lend their ice to such competitions *"may face a ban from EIH/IIHF recognised competitions."* **Before you join a midweek league someone set up at your rink, ask whether it is EIH-sanctioned.** If not, you are uninsured in it and may be risking the hockey you already play.

**Safeguarding obligations, including yours:**

- **USA Hockey:** SafeSport training is required annually, before first contact with minor participants, for *"all employees, coaches, officials, managers, volunteers and players 17 and older playing in an age category that allows minor age players"*; background checks every other year for adults with regular access to minors.
- **Hockey Canada:** the Respect in Sport parent programme is *"a condition of participation in most local hockey associations. At least one parent or caregiver in your household must maintain active RIS certification for your child to be eligible to play hockey."* It costs *"$12 for parents or $30 for coaches"*, takes six ten-minute modules for parents, and may need renewing every three to four years depending on your branch.
- **EIHA:** coaches and managers must *"meet additional criteria (DBS check if working with under-18s, first-aid, safeguarding training)."*
- **England Ice Hockey** puts the duty on the club, not the parent. An affiliated club must *"[h]ave a Head Coach who is an EIH licenced L2 Team Coach"* and *"a Designated Safeguarding Lead if there are players aged under 18 in the Club"*, and every member agrees to *"[b]e aware of and abide by EIH's Safeguarding Policies"* and its Code of Conduct, Social Media and anti-doping rules. *"[N]o person under the age of 18 can take control of the bench"*, and coaches and managers *"must display their licenses for the duration of the game."* **No EIH equivalent of Hockey Canada's compulsory parent course was found** — an absence in the source, not proof none exists; ask your club.

### What you will be asked for

Hockey Canada's list works anywhere: *"name, address, date of birth, hockey history"*, a provincial or territorial *"medical insurance number"*, and for a first registration *"a copy of your child's birth certificate and two documents proving your place of residence"* — a utility bill, tax assessment, school registration or insurance document. **Geographic boundaries** decide which association you join; Hockey Canada says its member branches set them and local associations keep them accurate.

**England has no published checklist and no geographic boundaries.** EIH requires *"provision of the documentation required by EIH as outlined in this rule book"* but does not list it for players. The EIHA's Registration page mentions *"required documents (photo ID, proof of age etc)"* — the closest thing to a list found, and a web page rather than a regulation. **Ask your club what to bring.** Instead of boundaries, clubs are tied to rinks: a national affiliated club *"should operate in the proximity of their rink"*, and needs *"a suitable venue for playing ice hockey on EIH approved venue list."* So choosing a club means choosing a rink, and nothing stops you picking one further away.

### When registration happens

Hockey Canada: *"Early registration typically opens in the spring or summer (between April and June), with main registration usually taking place during July and August. Evaluations or try-outs typically happen in September."* Associations set exact dates. **Youth registration happens months before the season** — decide in October and you may be late, but ring anyway.

**England's dates run earlier than you would guess.** Clubs must *"submit an expression of interest to enter a competition by 30 April prior to the start of the following season"* and re-affiliate *"at least 14 days prior to the opening of EIH individual registration window, normally set as the 1 July"*, a date EIH may move. **So the individual window normally opens around 1 July**, the year starts 1 August, and the door closes on 31 January for everyone except U10. February is not late for a *taster*; it is late for a *season*.

### What a season commits you to — time

Hockey Canada: *"Depending on the level of hockey your child plays, your time commitment could range from just 3 hours per week for House League up to 6-7 hours per week (plus travel time) for Rep."* It expects house-league away games within an hour's drive, Rep up to 90 minutes, and *"less than three hours per week round-trip"* for a typical schedule.

USA Hockey counts ice sessions per season (Parent Handbook): about **50–60 at 8U**; **95–100 at 10U** (75–80 practices, 20–25 game days); **160 at 14U and 16U** (120–130 practices, 40–50 games); and **200 for 18U males and 19U females**. **Practices far outnumber games at every level.** Under USA Hockey, a programme selling you mostly games is not following its own governing body; elsewhere, ask your association what it recommends.

For an adult, a rec season is usually one game a week plus any extra ice you buy. [Practice and Development](../off-the-ice/practice_and_development.md) has a realistic weekly plan.

### What a season commits you to — money

No figure is given here for your association, because an invented one would be wrong. Here is sourced context and the questions that get a real answer.

- **Hockey Canada's average is old, and it says so:** *"A 2011-2012 survey by Hockey Canada found that the average cost of hockey enrollment is $1,200 for registration and ice time."* That survey is fourteen years old. It tells you the cost runs to hundreds or thousands a season; do not budget from it.
- **Costs are set at the bottom layer.** Hockey Canada: *"Registration costs and tournament fees are set at the local association level. Equipment costs are set by manufacturers and retailers. Arena time costs are set by local arenas."*
- **Ask what the fee buys.** The USA Hockey Parent Handbook: *"Ask your local association what is included in return for your registration fees, and how the money is allocated toward ice time, officials, travel, and administration. Ensure these topics are covered during preliminary parent meetings so you understand your financial commitment for the season."*
- **Budget for extras.** Hockey Canada lists travel, tournaments, team clothing and end-of-season coach gifts as normal costs, often covered by fundraising or pooling.
- **Financial help exists and is underused.** Hockey Canada names **The Big Play** with Canadian Tire Jumpstart (*"up to $500 per child"*, first-come first-served), **KidSport Canada**, the YMCA's **YRASP** (*"up to $500 per child per year"*) and the **NHLPA Goals & Dreams Fund**, and local associations often run their own. Ask; nobody will offer.

## 7. Your first practice, first drop-in, first anything

### Before you leave the house

- **Confirm the session, the time and the gear requirement** — by phone or from the rink's own page, not from a friend's memory.
- **Pack the night before** and go through the bag. [Equipment](../off-the-ice/equipment.md) has a full pre-game checklist; the two items new players forget are a mouthguard and tape.
- **Bring water, a towel, and something to wear on the walk back to the car.** You will be soaked.
- **Bring the paperwork** if you have not registered online — waiver, membership number, or the confirmation email.

### When to arrive

**"On time" in hockey means dressed and ready before the stated time** — arrive **45 to 60 minutes before a game** and **20 to 30 minutes before a practice**. [Team Play and Culture](../off-the-ice/team_play_and_culture.md) covers this.

For a first session, allow longer. Learn to Play tells parents to *"arrive a minimum of 60 minutes prior to start time for your first on-ice session to provide enough time for your child to check-in and get dressed."* Dressing takes twice as long the first three times.

**In England the 60 minutes is written into the rules — for your coaches.** EIH requires *"[r]elevant representatives of each team"* — *"the team coaches or manager"* — to *"arrive at the game venue a minimum of 60 minute[s]"* before face-off, with any officials' meeting 40 minutes before. That is a guide to what your coach expects. One rule *is* about you, from the In-House Rules: **every player must be dressed and on the bench or the ice by the start of the second period to take part in the game**, and the officials cross off anybody who is not.

### The dressing room

[Team Play and Culture](../off-the-ice/team_play_and_culture.md) covers this — read its dressing-room section first. In short: take an unclaimed spot near the door and ask if it is someone's, keep your bag under the bench, say hello, listen more than you talk, and leave the goalie alone just before ice time.

**Nobody will comment on your gear or your skating.** They will comment, silently and permanently, on someone who takes up three stalls or who is still lacing skates when the ice is ready.

### Getting on the ice

- **Step on and skate.** A couple of easy laps in the direction everyone else is going, then stretch if you stretch. Do not stand at the gate.
- **Do not skate through the goalie's crease** — the painted blue semicircle in front of each net. Goalies clear it of snow and treat it as theirs. After the whistle this is custom; during play a narrower rule, goaltender interference, applies. See [Team Play and Culture](../off-the-ice/team_play_and_culture.md).
- **Shoot at a net, not at a person, and not at a goalie who has not signalled they are ready.** The signal is the goalie facing you, set, with the stick down. A goalie who is drinking, adjusting a strap or talking is not ready. This carries real injury risk and makes people angrier than any other breach.
- **Decide how you will meet the boards before a race for a loose puck puts you there** — you have several strides to choose on the way in, and no time on arrival. **Best: go in on an angle, not straight at the wall.** If you must meet it square on, get your skates parallel to the boards and take it on your forearm and hip — not the point of your shoulder, and not the whole shoulder if you can help it — head up, chin off your chest. If you are already facing the boards and cannot turn, get your stick and gloves up on the glass. ⚠️ **Never duck — a wall does not need an opponent to hurt you — and never turn your back to the checker.** Unexpected contact happens in non-checking hockey too. [Body Contact and Battles → Receiving a Hit Safely](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md#6-receiving-a-hit-safely) has the full posture — read it before your first session.

### How a drill line works

If you have never been in a coached practice, this confuses people most. **It is coaching convention, not a rule, and varies by coach and rink:**

1. **The coach explains and usually demonstrates once.** Get within earshot, or you will go the wrong way.
2. **Players form lines, usually in corners.** Join the shortest, not the one with the good players in it.
3. **You go on the whistle, or when the player in front of you is roughly halfway through.** Watch the first two groups before it is your turn — that is what the first two groups are for.
4. **Go at full speed and get out of the way at the end.** The single most common beginner error is finishing a drill and then standing where the next player needs to be.
5. **If you get it wrong, keep skating and rejoin the line.** Nobody cares. Stopping to apologise mid-ice causes the collision.

**On a whistle, stop and look at the coach.** Shooting pucks while someone talks gets you noticed for the wrong reason.

### Drop-in and pick-up etiquette

These are conventions, not rules, and vary by rink, but they are close to universal. **Experience-based: no governing body runs your local drop-in, so none states them.**

- **Short shifts, and change on the fly means *now*.** A **shift** is one stint on the ice between substitutions. Nobody manages drop-in ice time, so a 90-second shift is you taking someone else's turn. [Team Play and Culture](../off-the-ice/team_play_and_culture.md) covers the change: head to the bench when the puck is going the other way, call for your replacement, and the player coming on waits until you are within a stick's length of the boards.
- **Let faster groups play through.** At stick-and-puck especially, look before you cross the ice and do not skate through someone's drill.
- **One person's pucks are one person's pucks.** Collect your own, don't shoot into a group, and never shoot at the far net if someone is standing near it retrieving.
- **Match the intensity to the session, and to the least willing player in the battle.** Adult drop-in is usually non-contact, but that is a house rule that varies by rink — check at the desk. Nobody at a Tuesday drop-in is being scouted. The useful skills in [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md) — angling, stick lifts (lifting an opponent's stick to free the puck), body position — need no hit and annoy nobody.
- **Pass to the new player.** The person who does this on your first night is why you come back.
- **Get off promptly at the buzzer.** Ice time is rented by the minute and the next group is waiting at the gate.
- **Thank the goalie.** Two taps on the pads at the end. They paid the same as you to be shot at.

### Afterwards

Get your gear out of the bag as soon as you get home, every time. [Equipment](../off-the-ice/equipment.md) covers drying and why it matters beyond the smell.

## 8. The minimum equipment to start

[Equipment](../off-the-ice/equipment.md) is the authority on the full kit, fit, certification standards, and where to spend or save. **Read it before you buy anything.** This section covers only day one: what you need, and what you can avoid buying.

### What you need to be allowed on the ice

Requirements are set by the session, the league and the governing body, and they differ — so check your own league's before you buy. As a general shape, Hockey Canada's fitting guide lists: helmet, mouthguard, neck guard, shoulder pads, elbow pads, gloves, groin protection, hockey pants, shin pads, socks, skates, stick, practice jersey and a bag. **The certification standards for the helmet, cage and neck guard, and who must meet which, are in [Equipment](../off-the-ice/equipment.md). Follow that, not a shop assistant's summary.**

Stick-and-puck sessions often require far less — sometimes only a helmet and gloves. Ask the rink.

### What you need to be allowed on the ice in England

England Ice Hockey sets this out itself, and one item is the sharpest difference from North American hockey:

- **A neck guard, always, everywhere, at every level.** *"The EIH consider it mandatory that all players at all levels use an approved Ice Hockey Neck Guard/Protector whilst participating in all on ice activities."* That is **all on-ice activities**, not just games, and EIH covers England **and Wales**. The In-House Rules, adopted by IHUK and the BUIHA across all four Home Countries, make it mandatory for all players in all IIHF categories with **no warning**: an immediate 10-minute misconduct, and a second one puts you out of the game. The Elite League's own Rule 9.12 warns first, then gives a minor, and covers warm-ups and practice too. North American material teaches the opposite — USA Hockey exempts adults and Hockey Canada's requirement is narrower. **It is not optional for you.** [UK and England Rules](../foundation/uk_rules.md) has the enforcement detail, including what happens if it comes off in play, and [Equipment](../off-the-ice/equipment.md) the standard it must meet.
- **A cage if you are a junior** — it meets every British junior rule. England Ice Hockey's *Rules & Regulations* 24.5 makes face cages mandatory in all junior games; IHUK's junior leagues require full-face protection, cage or full visor, from U10 to U19 whatever the player's age; and the IIHF book's own floor at Under-18 is full-face protection of either kind. All players in Scottish recreational and university hockey, and in **all BUIHA (university) games in England and Scotland**, need **a half visor at minimum**. When you may move to a half visor is in [UK and England Rules](../foundation/uk_rules.md). **In women's hockey the date is the 18th birthday, not the category:** the In-House Rules make women's hockey adult from the 14th birthday (Rule 100.1) but allow no half visor before the start of the season after her 18th birthday (Rule 102). Whether one is allowed after that is less settled than it looks — UK and England Rules explains — and a full face satisfies every reading.
- **Everything to IIHF safety standards**, and everything except gloves, helmets, neck guards and skates *"worn entirely underneath the uniform"* — no elbow pads outside the jersey, no jersey tucked into the shorts, no shorts cut or torn in the thigh.
- **Full equipment, helmet included, in the pre-game warm-up** — *"must also be worn properly"*, in EIH's words.

**This is not the complete list** — only EIH's England-specific additions to the IIHF equipment rules. [Equipment](../off-the-ice/equipment.md) tells you what to buy, how it should fit and what is safe second-hand.

### What you can avoid buying at first

- **Programme-supplied kit.** NHL/NHLPA Learn to Play provides *"head-to-toe equipment"* and The First Shift includes *"head-to-toe Bauer equipment"*. Learn to Play's kit is the child's to keep: *"All the equipment provided will be for participants to keep upon completion of the program."* So it is issued to one child, not pooled, and a block costs the fee plus the mouth guard, neck guard and athletic supporter the programme tells you to buy separately. ⚠️ **No source consulted here says programme kit is new, and The First Shift publishes nothing about keeping it** — Hockey Canada's pathway page lists only what it includes, and `firstshift.ca` refuses automated retrieval. **So ask whether the helmet is new before the first session.**
- **Loaner pools.** Try Hockey For Free sites lend gear for the session, and a pool helmet has been on other heads with no record of what it stopped. ⚠️ **[Equipment](../off-the-ice/equipment.md)'s rule against second-hand helmets covers borrowed ones too, with no exception for a taster or a learn-to-play session.** USA Hockey's page settles the practical half: *"Most host locations will have loaner equipment, however, you are welcome to bring your own if you have it."* **So take a helmet if you have one, ask the host site if you do not, and make a helmet the first thing you buy.** No source settles whether a loaner helmet is safe for one supervised session, and no answer is invented here.
- **Club loan and hire.** The EIHA says *"many clubs have equipment available to loan or hire, which means you can try the sport without a significant initial investment."* [Equipment](../off-the-ice/equipment.md) also says ask the club first — many associations lend gear and run swaps, and goalie gear is often club-owned. **The one exception is the helmet:** no second-hand helmet, bought, borrowed or handed down, and a goalie mask counts as a helmet. You cannot see a crushed liner or a hairline shell crack, or know the impact history. Borrow the rest; buy your own helmet or mask.
- **Used gear, selectively.** [Equipment](../off-the-ice/equipment.md) lists what is safe second-hand. **What never comes second-hand includes helmets and goalie masks — bought, borrowed or handed down from a sibling alike.**

### The two things to get fitted in person

Skates and helmet. [Equipment](../off-the-ice/equipment.md) explains why: badly fitting skates make edges impossible to learn, and you will blame weak ankles when the problem is loose boots. Everything else can be bought online, used or borrowed.

## 9. What the first few weeks feel like

**Expect to be more tired than you predict.** Hockey is bursts of maximum effort, not steady exercise. [Conditioning and Recovery](../off-the-ice/conditioning_and_recovery.md) has the numbers: measured shifts run **30 to 80 seconds**, and 30 to 45 when well managed. Your first shifts will run longer because you do not yet know when to leave — the biggest reason new players feel destroyed after twenty minutes.

**Expect these specifically:**

- **Legs, not lungs, first.** The hockey stance loads the thighs and inner hips like nothing else. Groin and adductor strains are the classic hockey overuse injury — [Conditioning and Recovery](../off-the-ice/conditioning_and_recovery.md) covers them.
- **Cold hands and feet for the first ten minutes, then heat.** Rinks are cold and you will still finish soaked.
- **Sore feet and lace bite** in new skates. [Equipment](../off-the-ice/equipment.md) covers break-in and what to do about it.
- **Bruises you do not remember earning**, even in non-contact hockey.
- **A day-two ache in the hip flexors and lower back**, usually from the stance rather than an injury. Pain that is sharp, one-sided or lasts more than a few days is a question for a qualified medical professional.
- **You will barely touch the puck, and that does not mean you played badly.** Most of a shift happens away from the puck, at every level — [Core Principles](../foundation/core_principles.md) names this as one of the ideas that outlasts your position, team and league. Use the time between touches to look around: a glance while a pass is travelling costs nothing, one after it lands does. [Playing Without the Puck](../hockey-iq/playing_without_the_puck.md) and [Scanning and Anticipation](../hockey-iq/scanning_and_anticipation.md) cover what is measured and its limits.

**Ramp up gradually.** [Practice and Development](../off-the-ice/practice_and_development.md) says the classic way amateurs get hurt is not contact but a sudden jump in load — nothing for four months, then five sessions in week one. **That ranking is a coaching observation, not a measured one**; nothing cited here compares the causes of amateur hockey injury. The measured figures in [Conditioning and Recovery](../off-the-ice/conditioning_and_recovery.md) point elsewhere: in its collegiate women's meta-analysis, games ran at more than three times the injury rate of practices, and concussion was the most common injury type. Those findings concern setting and type, not cause, so they neither confirm nor refute the observation. **Do not read a load warning as a reason to treat collisions as the smaller risk.**

**If a concussion is suspected — yours or a teammate's — the player comes off immediately and does not return to play that day: not that shift, not that period, not that game.** "Suspected", not "confirmed": nobody at a rec-league rink can rule it out, a concussed player will tell you they are fine, and you do not have to be knocked out — the UK grassroots concussion guidance puts loss of consciousness at fewer than 10% of cases. ⚠️ **Any one of the red flags on the Concussion Recognition Tool (CRT6) means calling an ambulance, immediately** — neck pain or tenderness, a seizure, lost or double vision, loss of consciousness, increasing confusion or a deteriorating conscious state, weakness, numbness or tingling in more than one arm or leg, repeated vomiting, a severe or worsening headache, being increasingly restless, agitated or combative, or a visible skull deformity. Treat a symptom that is getting worse as a red flag too. **Assume a possible spinal injury in any head injury. Do not remove the helmet.** Do not move them, other than to keep their airway clear, unless you are trained to.

Short of a red flag, ⚠️ **a suspected concussion still means no driving and no return to contact until a medical professional clears them.** It also means no alcohol, recreational drugs or drugs not prescribed by their healthcare professional, not being left alone or sent home by themselves, and no activity with a risk of head contact, a fall or a collision until that clearance — symptoms clearing up is not the all-clear. [Conditioning and Recovery → Concussion](../off-the-ice/conditioning_and_recovery.md#concussion) has the full detail.

🇬🇧 ⚠️ **If you play in Britain, the UK concussion guidance for grassroots sport adds more red flags, and each is an ambulance call as well:** memory loss for events before or after the injury, an unusual change in behaviour, one weak or numb arm or leg, limb twitching or a player going rigid, trouble speaking, understanding or balancing, any suspicion of a skull fracture — a cut, bruise, swelling or severe pain at the site is enough — drug or alcohol intoxication, a history of brain surgery or a bleeding disorder, and a player on blood-thinning medication — even one who gets up and tells you they are fine. **In British grassroots hockey there is also a floor under the medical clearance: no return to competition before day 21, counting the day of the injury as day 0, and no training with a risk of head injury until you have been free of symptoms at rest for 14 days.** [UK and England Rules](../foundation/uk_rules.md#what-medical-cover-you-can-expect-at-the-rink-and-what-to-ask-for) carries the British list in full and the numbers to call.

### How long until you are actually playing

[Practice and Development](../off-the-ice/practice_and_development.md) gives the only honest answer, with its caveat: published adult-beginner timeframes **come from coaching providers and retailers, not research**. One such source offers roughly **6–10 hours of ice time** from clinging to the boards to confident forward skating, **3–6 months** to genuinely playing in a beginner league, and **one to two seasons** to comfortable hockey — but **there is no measured evidence behind those figures**, and people vary enormously.

The useful framing: **months for the basics, years for the rest, and progress comes in steps** — flat stretches where you feel stuck, then a sudden jump, more than once. That is a coaching observation, not a measured pattern.

### The questions to ask, in the order to ask them

Ring the club, association or rink. One call covers all of these:

1. **What programmes do you run for someone at my level / my child's age, and when does the next one start?**
2. **What does it cost, what does that include, and what is *not* included?** (Ice time, officials, jerseys, tournaments, travel.)
3. **What does registration involve, what documents do I need, and what dates does the membership year run?**
4. **What equipment do I need on day one, and do you loan or hire any of it?**
5. **What level is this session or team, and is there a trial or an assessment?**
6. **How much time is this per week, including travel?**
7. **Is there contact, and what are the house rules?**
8. **What do I need to do as a parent?** (Safeguarding certification, volunteering, driving rota.)

**Four more if you are in Britain**, each with an answer this document cannot give you:

9. **Is this session or league sanctioned, and am I insured in it?** In England, insurance follows sanctioning.
10. **Which registration am I buying — Trial, Learn to Play, insurance-only or standard?** They cover different activities and cost different amounts.
11. **Which registration year does it run on, and is any of it pro-rata?** EIH says pro-rata discounts are not available.
12. **Which section's Rules of Competition apply to me, and where do I get them?** They hold your roster sizes, tiering and suspension rules.

And one more: **"I'm completely new — who should I talk to?"** Amateur hockey runs on volunteers who are, in the USA Hockey Parent Handbook's words, *"your first point-of-contact for any questions and concerns."* Someone at most clubs has that job, and they want to hear from you.

## Common Mistakes

- **Buying kit before you have skated.** Go to a public skate, then a taster, then buy. Borrow before you buy where the club offers it — except the helmet.
- **Buying skates online without a fitting.** Get skates fitted in person: fit is checked with your foot in the boot, and a boot that is too big holds your skating back for as long as you own it. See [Equipment](../off-the-ice/equipment.md).
- **Turning up to the wrong drop-in.** Advanced drop-in is no place to learn to stop. Ask the standard; rinks run separate sessions so you can choose.
- **Overrating your level.** Take the lower of two tiers, and judge yourself on skating, not shooting. Too high buys a season of hanging on and a real chance of injury; too low buys a season of improving, and moving up is easy.
- **Treating registration as a formality.** Register through your club before your first game, and check the hockey is sanctioned before you sign. Registration is your insurance, safeguarding and eligibility. Hockey Canada says a child in a non-member league immediately loses all Hockey Canada membership privileges for the season, and a player in non-sanctioned hockey after 30 September is ineligible for the rest of that season's sanctioned play.
- **Joining a midweek league at your rink without checking it is recognised.** Ask whether it is sanctioned before you join. Under England Ice Hockey's last published rules — whose insurance Ice Hockey UK says continues unchanged through the merger — you are insured only in sanctioned hockey, and a registered player who plays in a competition EIH or the IIHF does not recognise faces suspension from all authorised competitions and severe financial penalties. That falls on the player, not just the club.
- **Missing the registration window.** Ask your association when it opens: youth registration runs months before the season — in Canada largely April to August — so October is late there; ring anyway. In England, under EIH's last published rules, the window normally opens around 1 July and closes on 31 January for everyone except U10.
- **Budgeting from a number found online.** Ask your own association. Fees are set locally, and the often-quoted Hockey Canada average is from a 2011-12 survey.
- **Taking a 90-second shift at drop-in.** That is someone else's turn. Short shifts, all night.
- **Shooting at a goalie who is not looking at you.** Wait until they are facing you, set, with the stick down. Shooting earlier carries real injury risk and is completely avoidable.
- **Racing to the wall with no plan for meeting it.** Choose on the way in: go in on an angle if you can; if not, skates parallel, forearm and hip to the wall — not the point of your shoulder, and not the whole shoulder if you can help it — head up, chin off your chest. Never duck and never turn your back to the checker, whether or not your session allows contact. See [Body Contact and Battles → Receiving a Hit Safely](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md#6-receiving-a-hit-safely).
- **Assuming a North American equipment exemption reaches you in Britain.** Wear a neck laceration protector in every game at every level, adults included — and at training too under England Ice Hockey's last published rules and the Elite League's. In IHUK, England, Scottish and university competitions there is **no warning** before the penalty; only the Elite League warns first. See [UK and England Rules](../foundation/uk_rules.md).
- **Putting yourself or a child in a second-hand helmet.** Borrow pads and gloves; buy the helmet new, and the goalie mask too. Bought, borrowed or handed down, you cannot see a crushed liner or a hairline shell crack, or know its impact history. **A taster's loaner helmet is borrowed too** — USA Hockey's Try Hockey For Free page says you are *"welcome to bring your own if you have it"*, so take one, and ask the host site if you have none. **Learn to Play's kit is the child's to keep, not pooled — but no source says it is new, so ask.** See [Equipment](../off-the-ice/equipment.md).
- **Assuming a session's label tells you whether you may hit or be hit.** Ask your coach or league secretary whether your league is a checking category before your first game. "Non-contact" at a drop-in is a house rule the desk can explain that night; your league's checking rule is set by your age group, league and country, in a rulebook or competition regulation, and nobody at the rink can change it. **That reaches adult beginners too** — being new does not put you in a non-checking category. See [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md#1-which-contact-rules-are-you-actually-playing-under).
- **Watching only the puck.** You will barely touch it, so use the gaps between touches to look around. See [Playing Without the Puck](../hockey-iq/playing_without_the_puck.md) and [Scanning and Anticipation](../hockey-iq/scanning_and_anticipation.md).
- **Not asking for help.** Ask a teammate, your coach or your club's volunteer contact: "I'm completely new — who should I talk to?" Someone's job is to answer that, and any teammate would explain most things in thirty seconds.

## Key Takeaways

1. **Find out whether your league allows body checking before your first game.** ⚠️ **Ask your coach or your league secretary, not the rink desk** — it is set by your age group, your league and your country. Almost everything else is local and changes yearly, so your club or rink answers most other questions. See [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md#1-which-contact-rules-are-you-actually-playing-under).
2. **Before any session, ask what gear is required, what standard it is and whether there is contact.** Arrive dressed 20 to 30 minutes before a practice, 45 to 60 before a game, and a full hour before your first session.
3. **Keep your shifts short and your eyes up, and never shoot at a goalie who is not ready** — facing you, set, stick down. In a drill, go full speed and clear out at the end; on a whistle, stop and look at the coach. When unsure, ask a teammate.
4. **Meet the wall on an angle if you can.** ⚠️ **If you cannot, skates parallel, forearm and hip to the wall — never the point of your shoulder — head up, chin off your chest; never turn your back to the checker and never duck, whether or not your session allows contact.** See [Body Contact and Battles → Receiving a Hit Safely](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md#6-receiving-a-hit-safely).
5. **Learn to skate first or alongside, then go in by stages:** public skating, a taster, a learn-to-play block, then a team. Work hardest on stopping on both sides and skating backwards — skating, not shooting, sorts players into levels.
6. **Borrow gear before you buy it — except the helmet.** Never a second-hand helmet, bought, borrowed or handed down; a goalie mask is a helmet, and a taster's loaner counts as borrowed. Learn to Play's kit is the child's to keep, **but nothing found says it is new, so ask before a child wears its helmet.**
7. **Start a tier below where your ego puts you** — moving up is easy, moving down is not. Expect months for the basics and years for the rest, with progress in steps: flat stretches are normal.
8. **Register through your club before your first game, and check the league is sanctioned before you join.** Registration is your insurance, safeguarding and eligibility, and the membership year does not run from the day you pay. In England you are insured only in sanctioned hockey, and an unrecognised competition can bring suspension and financial penalties. In Britain, ask which body you are registering with — memberships moved to Ice Hockey UK on 30 June 2026.
9. **In Britain, read every IIHF flag in this guide as yours, and look at the sheet you are standing on.** Every level runs the IIHF book with domestic amendments, and IIHF rinks may be 26 m to 30 m wide, so NHL distances may not be yours.
10. **In Britain, wear a neck laceration protector every time you go on the ice** — every game at every level, adults included, and training too under England Ice Hockey's last published rules and the Elite League's. ⚠️ **Do not count on a warning: in IHUK, England, Scottish and university competitions you are penalised on the spot**; only the Elite League warns first. **Put a junior in a cage, and keep a girl in women's hockey in full-face protection at least until the season after her 18th birthday.** [UK and England Rules](../foundation/uk_rules.md) has the rest.
11. **If a concussion is suspected — yours or a teammate's — the player comes off immediately and does not return that day.** No driving until a healthcare professional clears them, no alcohol or recreational drugs, not left alone for at least the first 3 hours (24 hours under the UK guidance), and not sent home alone. A concussed player will say they are fine, so it is never their call. ⚠️ **Neck pain, a seizure, lost or double vision, loss of consciousness, worsening confusion or drowsiness, limb weakness or tingling, repeated vomiting, a severe or worsening headache, increasing agitation or a skull deformity means an ambulance, immediately** — do not remove the helmet, and move them only to keep their airway clear; in Britain the list is longer. Nobody plays again without medical clearance, and in British grassroots hockey nobody returns to competition before day 21; see [Conditioning and Recovery → Concussion](../off-the-ice/conditioning_and_recovery.md#concussion).
