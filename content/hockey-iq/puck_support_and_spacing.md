# Puck Support and Spacing

> flagged where they change how you play. This is mostly tactics; the four rules that bite

## Key focus

**Give the carrier two options at different angles — a triangle, not a line.** Two teammates in the same lane are one option, because one stick covers both. A triangle survives the pass; a line has to be rebuilt after every one.

**Be the angle that is missing — behind, level with or ahead of the puck.** Behind is the outlet, level changes the side, ahead gains ground. In your own end the lateral option goes behind your net, never across the slot.

**Support from a distance the pass can beat.** Would a pass released now reach you before the defender does? Start at two to three stick lengths, 10 to 15 feet: tighter under pressure, longer in open ice. It is a rule of thumb, so let your coach adjust it.

**Skate to the space, not to the puck.** If you are not closest and your system has not sent you in, your job is the space the puck will come out into. To help a teammate pinned on the wall, get open nearby with your stick down.

**Make yourself available, not just unmarked.** Blade on the ice where you want the pass, call for it, and if the lane is blocked, move, even a metre. That is the receiver's job, not the passer's.

**Stay wide, and stretch the zone deep as well as high.** A wide winger holds a defender out there, and that keeps the middle open. In the offensive zone keep one player below the goal line and one at the blue line.

**Arrive late with speed, not early and still.** Let the carrier commit a defender, then go, and time it so the puck beats you over the blue line. In your own zone never delay: the carrier needs the outlet now.

**Pass and move.** The teammate you just passed to needs two options, and you are the nearest. Go again at once.

**Before you go below the puck, know who is above it.** One player stays back as insurance against the attack failing. Who that is depends on your system, so learn your team's version and know *who*, not what position.

**Defending, layer behind the first defender instead of joining them on the carrier.** Two players on one carrier are beaten by one pass. Stand behind and to the side, and tell them you are there. A double-team is a coaching call, so ask when.

## Overview

Most of what a teammate does happens without the puck. A carrier with two places to put it can beat pressure that would strip a better player with none. So your job is to be one of those two places, at an angle the other one is not.

Shape matters more than any single spot. A line is cut by one stick; a triangle is not, and it is still there after the pass. Distance is judged by time: the same twelve feet is safe in open ice and useless under a forecheck, where the defender arrives before the pass does.

The spacing error coaches name most often is going to the puck. It brings your checker and empties the ice the puck is about to come out into. Being unmarked is not enough either: you need a clear lane, a blade on the ice and a carrier who knows you are there.

Timing beats position, because a player arriving at speed is harder to cover than one standing still. Defending, the logic inverts: the second defender layers behind the first. Who stays above the puck, when to double-team and whether the neutral zone spreads or stacks are coaching choices, so find out what your team plays.

## 1. The central idea: hockey is a set of relationships

Beginners learn hockey as five jobs: centre does this, wingers do that, defence do the other. That gets you through your first season, then starts costing you games. **Your correct position is not a place on the ice. It is a distance and an angle from two moving things — the puck and your teammates.**

On a good team nobody stands where a whiteboard would put them. They keep adjusting, because the puck has moved.

### Spacing beats skill

```facts
Goal: Offer two options at different angles — the nearest defender physically cannot cover both
Risk: Zero options means winning a one-on-one duel, and even elite players lose that duel a lot of the time
Risk: One option is coverable — the defence takes away a single lane, and a good defender will
Read: The moment a second defender commits, a third option opens somewhere else
Key: Spacing is a large part of possession, not a footnote to skill — reasoning, not a measured result
```

This is arithmetic, not a slogan. A carrier under pressure decides in well under a second, and the decision is only as good as the options in front of them:

- **Zero options** — they must beat their man one-on-one or lose the puck. Even elite players lose that duel a lot of the time.
- **One option** — the defence can take it away by covering one lane, and a good defender will.
- **Two options at different angles** — the single nearest defender physically cannot cover both. Someone else has to come, and the moment a second defender commits, a third option opens somewhere else.

Average players who always offer two options give the defence a problem effort cannot solve. Good players who offer none ask every carrier to win a duel. Over sixty minutes the first team has the puck more. **Spacing is a large part of possession, not a footnote to skill** — this is reasoning, not a measured result, and no study is quoted for it. [Time and Space](time_and_space.md) covers what the carrier does with the options.

### Why this is the hardest thing to learn

Because it is invisible. Everyone sees the great pass; nobody claps the teammate whose positioning made it possible three seconds earlier. So most players never get feedback on spacing and never fix it. Give it to yourself: after a shift, ask where you were each time the puck moved, and whether the carrier could have used you. Section 12 shows how.

## 2. The support triangle

**The principle: the puck carrier should always have at least two passing options, at different angles and different distances.**

One option is coverable, so two is the minimum. And two options in the same direction are really one.

### Why a triangle and not a line

```facts
Never: String three teammates in a straight line — one stick blade in one lane kills two options at once
Action: Pull one of the three off the line into the middle, so the lanes diverge
Key: A triangle is the smallest shape where all three corners have two teammates at different angles
Goal: Keep a shape that survives the pass — a triangle rotates and stays connected, a line must be re-formed
Mindset: The triangle is not a formation you set up; it is a shape you keep while everything moves
```

Put three teammates in a line up the boards. One defender standing in that line is between the carrier and *both* receivers, so one stick kills two options. The line is the cheapest shape in hockey to defend.

Pull one of the three into the middle and you have a triangle. Three things become true:

1. **No single defender can be in both lanes.** Covering one means turning away from the other.
2. **Every player has two teammates at different angles.** Whichever corner gets the puck, the support is already there.
3. **The shape survives the pass.** After the pass you still have a triangle. A line has to be re-formed every time.

![](diagram:support-in-a-line)

![](diagram:support-triangle)

The third point matters most. The triangle is not a formation you set up; it is a shape you *keep*, rotating and stretching as the puck moves.

### The triangle is a minimum, not a maximum

```facts
Key: With five skaters you are not building one triangle, you are building overlapping ones
Position: Three players form the immediate support triangle around the puck
Position: A fourth provides width on the far side; a fifth sits above the play as the safety
```

With five skaters you build overlapping triangles: three around the puck, a fourth giving width on the far side, and a fifth above the play as the safety. Sections 5 and 6 cover those roles.

## 3. Support distance

Support has a working range. Outside it, you are decoration.

### Too close

```facts
Never: Support from inside a stick's reach of your teammate's checker — one body then guards two of you
Risk: Standing in the carrier's escape route shrinks their world when they need somewhere to turn into
Risk: Receiving from two metres away puts their forechecker on you before the puck settles
Key: Supporting too close moves the problem, it does not solve it
```

- **One defender covers both of you.** Within a stick's reach of your teammate's checker, you let one body guard two players and free a defender elsewhere.
- **You block the carrier's escape.** They need somewhere to turn into, and you are standing in it.
- **You inherit the pressure.** Take a pass from two metres away and their forechecker is on you before the puck settles.

So back off until one defender can no longer reach both of you.

### Too far

```facts
Risk: A long pass spends longer on the ice, giving every defender time to read it and get a stick in the lane
Risk: A long pass also arrives faster, often bouncing, and you receive it with a defender already closing
Convention: Larry Bruyere puts possession play on the short pass — "the 8-to-10-foot-or-less pass"
Key: Support does not end when the puck leaves — whoever passed to you now needs support from you
```

- **The pass takes too long.** Every defender has time to read it and get a stick in the lane. **Larry Bruyere**, a former USA Hockey Pacific District coach-in-chief, puts it plainly: *"generally, the 40-foot pass isn't a good option. The 8-to-10-foot-or-less pass is really what the small-area games are all about."* (He is quoted in **Mike Doyle's** article for USA Hockey, 30 March 2017; the words are Bruyere's, the piece is Doyle's.)
- **A long pass is harder to receive.** It arrives faster, often bouncing, with a defender already closing.
- **You are too far to help after the pass.** Whoever passed to you now needs support from *you*.

So close in until a pass released now would reach you before the defender does.

### Practical distances

**In short:** Start about two to three stick lengths from the carrier, closer under pressure, and judge it by whether the pass beats the defender.

```facts
Convention: Support distance defaults to roughly two to three stick lengths, 10 to 15 feet — a starting point, not a rule of hockey
Convention: Support from 8 to 10 feet when the carrier is pressured, stretching to 15 when they have time
Action: Shorten under heavy pressure, in your own zone, and below the offensive goal line
Action: Lengthen in open ice and above the offensive dots; in the neutral zone go longer and mostly lateral
Action: On wider international ice, check the rink first — distances scale, angles do not
Rule: In Britain the 2026-27 In-House Rules authorise rinks below 56 m × 26 m at every level except Sutton and Coatbridge, which are U16 only. Measure your rink rather than assuming it is wider
Key: The self-test is time, not distance — would the puck reach you before that defender does?
```

**No governing body has been found to set a support distance, and coaches differ.** The only published figure found is Bruyere's *"8-to-10-foot-or-less pass"* in the USA Hockey article above: one coach's rule of thumb written up by a staff writer, not a USA Hockey standard. The IIHF's *Coach Development Program* makes Support an offensive principle and asks only for *"close and quick support"*, with no distance in seventy-seven pages. ⚠️ **But one manual's silence is not a survey of every governing body**, so read this as "none found", not "none exists".

What follows is a widely taught rule of thumb, not a rule of hockey. Treat it as a starting point and let your coach adjust it.

**A useful default is two to three stick lengths — 3 to 5 metres, 10 to 15 feet.** A senior stick is about 1.5 metres, so you can judge it on the ice. That is close enough for a hard, flat, one-second pass and far enough that one defender cannot mark both of you.

![](diagram:support-distance-range)

**Support distance is not a gap.** Support distance is **teammate to teammate**. A **gap** is **defender to attacker**, and [Defending the Rush](../systems/defending_the_rush.md) owns it, in the same unit. Never carry a figure from one to the other.

**Bruyere's figure is tighter than this default, on purpose.** He is describing small-area, heavy-pressure play, the short end of the table below. Read the two as a range: **8 to 10 feet when the carrier is pressured, stretching to 15 when they have time.** Where they conflict, his sourced figure is the more defensible one.

**"International ice is wider" is only half true.** IIHF Rule 1.2 allows a sheet **26 m to 30 m wide**. At 30 m (about 98 feet) you have roughly thirteen feet more than an NHL rink, so the same shape covers more ground. At 26 m (about 85.3 feet) it is NHL width and nothing changes.

Then adjust:

| Situation | Adjust support distance | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Heavy pressure, carrier on the boards** | **Shorter** — 8 to 10 feet | The pass must be immediate. Short and safe beats good and late. |
| **Carrier has time, open ice** | **Longer** | You can afford the extra half-second, and it stretches the defence. |
| **Defensive zone** | **Shorter and tighter** | A turnover here is a chance against. See [Breakouts](../systems/breakouts.md). |
| **Neutral zone** | **Longer, and mostly lateral** | Width stretches their line, and you have room to receive. |
| **Offensive zone, below the goal line** | **Shorter** | Behind the net and in the low corners it is tight and fast. |
| **Offensive zone, above the dots** | **Longer** | Point players are far from the puck on purpose; that is why they are open. |
| **Wider international ice** | **Slightly longer — check the rink first** | Only at the wide end of the IIHF's 26-to-30-metre range. Distances scale; angles do not. |

**🇬🇧 In Britain this points the other way.** The 2026-27 In-House Rules allow rinks *"with a non-standard playing surface (below 56 m × 26 m)"* at all levels, **except** Sutton in England and Coatbridge in Scotland, which are allowed up to U16 only. That is short of the IIHF minimum on both dimensions, so your rink may be smaller, not wider. Measure it; [UK and England Rules](../foundation/uk_rules.md) has the detail.

**The self-test is time, not distance.** *If my teammate released the puck now, would it reach me before that defender does?* If not, you are decoration.

## 4. Support angles: behind, level and ahead

Distance tells you how far. Angle tells you what kind of option you are, and there are three.

### Support behind the puck — the outlet

```facts
Position: Between the puck and your own net, or at least further back than the carrier
Goal: Offer a guaranteed, low-risk reset into space the opposition has already vacated
Key: Support behind the puck is the option that exists when the level and ahead options are covered
Risk: With nobody behind the puck, the only answer to good pressure is a dump-out — a turnover you chose
Action: Whoever is lowest provides it — usually a defenceman or the centre in your own end
Action: Call for it; the verbal cue in most teams' vocabulary is "back"
```

You are between the puck and your own net, or at least further back than the carrier.

- **What it offers:** a safe reset. The pass goes backwards into space the opposition has left, so it is the least contested pass on the ice.
- **Why it is non-negotiable:** it is still there when the other two angles are covered. Without it, the only answer to good pressure is dumping the puck away — a turnover you chose.
- **Who provides it:** whoever is lowest. In your own end that is usually a defenceman or the centre; in the offensive zone, often the player who just came off the wall. Call **"back"** — see [On-Ice Communication](../foundation/on_ice_communication.md).

### Support level with the puck — the lateral option

```facts
Position: Roughly the same distance from your own net as the carrier, but across from them
Goal: Change the side — move the puck to the half where their strong-side coverage is thinnest
Read: In the offensive and neutral zones a pass across the face of people is normal and high-value
Never: Pass across the middle in your own defensive zone — an interception there is a point-blank chance
Action: In your own end, take the lateral option behind your own net, not through the slot
```

You are roughly the same distance from your own net as the carrier, but across from them.

- **What it offers:** a change of side. Most defences pressure strong-side, on the half of the ice the puck is on. A lateral pass moves it to the half where their coverage is thinnest and makes every defender reset.
- **The catch:** a lateral pass crosses in front of people. In the offensive and neutral zones that is a normal, valuable play. **In your own zone, never pass across the middle.** An interception in the slot is a point-blank chance for an opponent already facing your net. In your own end the lateral option goes *behind your net*.

### Support ahead of the puck — the attacking option

```facts
Position: Further up ice than the carrier
Goal: Gain territory — this is the only one of the three angles that gains ground
Risk: The ahead-of-the-puck pass is the most interceptable of the three angles, and if it fails you are behind the play and cannot recover
Rule: Both skates completely over the line before the puck completely crosses it is offside (NHL Rule 83)
Rule: A raised trail skate is onside under NHL, IIHF and PWHL rules, but USA Hockey 630(a) and Hockey Canada 6.11 require skate contact, and CARHA 72(c) settles it neither way — so drag the blade and you are onside wherever you play
Action: Time your entry rather than parking in the zone
```

You are further up ice than the carrier.

- **What it offers:** territory. It is the only one of the three that gains ground.
- **The cost:** it is the easiest to intercept, and if it fails you are behind the play.
- **The offside limit:** time your entry rather than parking in the zone. **Offside is both skates completely over the blue line before the puck completely crosses it.** Keep one skate on or behind the line, **with the blade on the ice**: a raised trail skate counts as onside under NHL, IIHF and PWHL rules, but USA Hockey and Hockey Canada require skate *contact*.

### The principle: all three at once

```facts
Goal: Have all three angles available simultaneously — that is the actual content of "good spacing"
Risk: All behind is safety with no attack; all ahead leaves no outlet; all level goes side to side and never up
Action: Ask which of the three angles is currently missing, then go and be that one
```

**A team needs all three angles at once.** That is what "good spacing" means.

![](diagram:support-angles)

All support behind the puck is safe and goes nowhere. All ahead has no outlet, so pressure turns it over. All level goes side to side and never up.

So when you skate to support, do not ask "am I open?" Ask **"which angle is missing?"** and go and be that one.

## 5. Above, level and below the puck

Coaches use these words to organise five players quickly, and you will meet them in [Defensive Zone Coverage](../systems/defensive_zone_coverage.md), [Forechecking Systems](../systems/forechecking_systems.md) and every backchecking instruction.

**Above the puck** means between the puck and *your own* net. **Below the puck** means the puck is between you and your own net. **Level** means neither.

Two things trip people up:

- **It is not about which end you are in.** "Above" always means goal-side of the puck, relative to the net you defend. A forward in the offensive corner is *below* the puck if the puck is at the point.
- **It flips on a turnover.** Players usefully below the puck in attack are stranded below it in defence. Only possession decides whether it is an asset or a liability.

### Somebody above the puck — the safety convention

```facts
Convention: Somebody above the puck, in every zone — a coaching convention, and benches suspend it on a called full-ice press, a delayed penalty or a 6-on-5
Goal: One player is not part of the attack; they are the insurance against the attack failing
Convention: Under this guide's standard system — low zone collapse, 2-1-2 forecheck — both defencemen hold the points unless one activates
Options: A 1-2-2 puts two forwards above as a layered wall; a single-high structure makes the weak-side winger the safety
Action: Before you go below the puck, know who is above it
Key: F1, F2 and F3 are roles defined by order of arrival, not fixed people
```

**Somebody stays above the puck.** In every zone one player is not part of the attack; they are the insurance against it failing. **That is a coaching convention, not a law.** Benches drop it on purpose for a full-ice press, a delayed penalty or 6-on-5 with your goalie pulled, because in each of those a counter-attack has stopped costing you anything. [Forechecking Systems](../systems/forechecking_systems.md) covers those three. Otherwise, assume it holds.

Who it is depends on the system:

- Under the **standard system** — a low zone collapse in your own end and a 2-1-2 forecheck — both defencemen hold the points in the offensive zone unless one deliberately activates, and the third forward supports high. **F1, F2 and F3 are roles by order of arrival, not fixed people.**
- Under a **1-2-2 forecheck**, the deepest forward is alone below the puck and two forwards sit above as a layered wall.
- In a **single-high** structure one defenceman may walk the line and the weak-side winger becomes the safety. Other teams insist both defencemen hold.

**Find out which your team plays.** The habit that works in every system: before you go below the puck, know who is above it. [Defender](../positions/defender.md) gives the defence pair's version. Under single-high that player is a **winger**, so know *who*, not what position.

## 6. Width and depth

**Depth** is spread up and down the ice; **width** is spread across it. Depth mostly happens by accident, as players string out along the play. Width has to be deliberate, and amateur teams rarely manage it.

### Why wingers stay wide

```facts
Position: Wide on the boards in the neutral zone, even when it feels useless — that feeling is the point
Key: Two defenders cannot both cover the middle and both cover the boards
Goal: Occupy a defender who has to stay wide with you, so the middle stays open for somebody else
Never: Drift inside to get closer to the play — your checker drifts in too and the middle closes
Risk: Drifting in gains you no option; it deletes one for a teammate
```

A winger on the neutral-zone boards often feels useless, far from the puck. That feeling is the point.

**Two defenders cannot cover both the middle and the boards.** A wide winger keeps a defender wide, out of the middle. The middle, the most valuable ice there is, is open *because* someone is standing somewhere else.

Drift inside to get closer to the play and your checker drifts in too, and the middle closes. You have gained no option and deleted one for a teammate. [Winger](../positions/winger.md) makes the same point.

### The counterintuitive rule: to help a battle, move away from it

```facts
Never: Skate into a board battle your teammate already controls — you crowd the exits and bring your checker
Action: Move to open ice about five metres away with your stick down, so the puck has somewhere to go
Read: Go in to win the puck when it is a genuine 50-50 scramble and nobody has it
Read: Stay out and offer an exit when your teammate has the puck and is trying to survive
Convention: Many teams assign a support player to arrive at the wall — find out if that is your job
Never: Arrive at a wall battle with your back to anyone coming at you, and never duck. Get your skates parallel to the wall instead, forearm and hip to the wall, head up, chin off your chest
Never: Hit the boards with the point of your shoulder. Keep the whole shoulder off them too if you can, and let the forearm and hip take the load instead
Never: Push or hit an opponent on the wall from behind — if their back is to you, go for the puck at their feet. It is checking from behind even in non-checking hockey, and in most books it ends your game
```

Your teammate is pinned on the boards in a battle. Every instinct says go and help.

**Usually, don't — not into the battle.** Your teammate needs to get the puck *out*. Skate into the pile and you add a body, bring your checker and crowd the exits. Move to open ice five metres away with your stick down and the puck has somewhere to go, and when it squirts free you have it with time.

![](diagram:support-a-battle-from-outside)

**The exception is real.** Go in to *win the puck* when it is a genuine 50-50 scramble and nobody has it, or when your team makes you the designated second player at the wall, as many do. Stay out and offer an exit when your teammate already has it. See [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md).

**When you do go in, how you arrive outranks winning the puck.** Come in on an angle, feet apart, knees bent. Get your **skates parallel to the wall** before contact and your **forearm and hip to the wall** — never the point of your shoulder, and not the whole shoulder if you can help it. Head up, chin off your chest, and **never turn your back to anyone coming at you, never duck.**

**And never push or hit the opponent on the wall from behind** — if their back is to you, go for the puck at their feet. A push from behind into the boards is checking from behind even in non-checking hockey. 50-50 pucks at the wall are where checking-from-behind and boarding injuries happen, and none of this is for fast arrivals only: USA Hockey warns that *"Even a light hit from behind could inflict a severe head or neck injury"*. Ducking puts your head into the boards first, which is how necks get broken, and USA Hockey says that injury *"can occur at walking speed."*

If you are already facing the wall and cannot turn, limit the damage: spread the load with your stick and gloves up on the glass. That is a fallback, not a position to aim for. [Body Contact and Battles §6, Receiving a Hit Safely](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md) is the full treatment.

### Depth means somebody deep and somebody high

```facts
Position: In the offensive zone, one player below the goal line and one at the blue line
Goal: Stretch the zone vertically so the defence must choose which end of it to defend
Never: Put all five between the dots and the goal line — that is a scrum with a net behind it, not an attack
```

In the offensive zone, depth means a player below the goal line *and* a player at the blue line, so the defence must choose which end to defend. Five players between the dots and the goal line is a scrum with a net behind it.

## 7. The "puck magnet" problem

**Of all the habits that separate beginner from intermediate hockey, this is the one coaches name most often** — an observation from coaching material, not a measured ranking.

On a young or new team the puck seems to have gravity. Ten players end up in a five-metre circle, the puck squirts out to whoever has someone outside the pile, and it all repeats twenty metres away.

### Why players do it

1. **The puck is where the action is.** Your eyes follow it, and your legs follow your eyes.
2. **It feels like effort.** Skating hard at the puck feels like trying; skating *away* from it feels like slacking. Coaches who reward visible effort train this in.
3. **Nobody wants to be the player who wasn't there.** Near the puck, at least you were involved.

### Why it is wrong

```facts
Risk: You do not arrive alone — you arrive as two players, in a space that was already crowded
Key: A teammate five metres away is an option; one metre away, inside the same checking bubble, is not
Risk: You vacate the empty space the puck is about to come out into, and it belongs to the other team
Risk: Converging leaves nobody above the puck, which turns a routine turnover into an odd-man rush
```

- **You bring your checker.** You arrive as two players, in a space already crowded.
- **You remove an option.** A teammate five metres away is an option; one metre away, inside the same checking bubble, is not.
- **You leave the space the puck is about to go to.** Pucks come out of piles into empty ice. If everyone is in the pile, that ice belongs to the other team.
- **It leaves nobody above the puck,** which turns a routine turnover into an odd-man rush the other way. See [Defending the Rush](../systems/defending_the_rush.md).

### How to train yourself out of it

```facts
Action: Every time the puck moves, ask "who is closest?" — if it is not you and your system has not sent you in, your job is the space
Key: How many players pressure is decided by your system, not improvised. Learn your role before you move
Action: Skate to where the puck will be available — up the wall or behind the net — not to where it is
Action: Use cross-ice and half-ice small-area games, where crowding loses you the puck immediately
Action: Ask to be filmed — spacing is the one thing you cannot self-assess in the moment
```

- **Ask one question.** Every time the puck moves, ask *"who is closest?"* If it is not you, your job is the space, not the puck.
- **Know how many of you are meant to pressure — it is not always one.** The habit to break is *everybody* converging. Under a **1-2-2** one forward pressures; under this guide's standard **2-1-2** two go deep on purpose and F3 reads behind them; man-to-man in your own end is different again. See [Forechecking Systems](../systems/forechecking_systems.md) and [Playing Without the Puck](playing_without_the_puck.md). The number is **decided, not improvised**, so learn your role before you move.
- **Skate to where the puck will come out, not where it is.** From a corner battle it emerges up the wall or behind the net, so go to one of those. See [Scanning and Anticipation](scanning_and_anticipation.md).
- **Use small-area games.** Cross-ice and half-ice games punish crowding at once, so the habit breaks itself. See [Practice and Development](../off-the-ice/practice_and_development.md).
- **Ask to be filmed.** You cannot see your own spacing in the moment.

## 8. Support in each zone

The principle is constant; the shape it takes is not.

### Defensive zone — the breakout triangle

**In short:** Give the retriever three short, safe options: the wall, the centre swinging low, and your partner behind the net, never through the slot.

```facts
Targets: The strong-side winger on the wall, roughly level with the faceoff dot — the short up option
Targets: The centre, swinging low through the middle and curling toward the same side
Targets: The defence partner on the far side, reached behind the net — never through the slot
Position: The weak-side winger higher and wider than everyone, for the width that stops one side being loaded
Priority: In your own end the safe outlet outranks the attacking option — support short, keep the angles safe
Action: Use the goalie as support — best view on the ice, no puck to look at
Rule: Firing it the length of the ice is icing: defensive-zone draw, no line change (NHL 81.4, which excepts only injury, a returning pulled goalie and a strength-changing penalty) and no timeout (87.1 — Rule 81 has no timeout provision); Special Teams covers the shorthanded exemption and its carve-outs
Rule: After an icing the NHL, IIHF and PWHL bar a change and a timeout; Hockey Canada bars the change (U18AAA, Junior, Senior at the Member's option) but allows the timeout; USA Hockey and CARHA bar neither. An injured player can still be replaced (NHL and IIHF 81.4, 87.1; PWHL 83.4, 89.1; HC 6.7(d))
Rule: USA Hockey grants the shorthanded icing exemption only from Youth 15-Only, Girls 16U, High School and Adult upward, so below those a shorthanded team can be called for icing (USA Hockey 624(b)(1))
Rule: Under NHL and IIHF rules the goalie may play the puck behind the goal line only inside the trapezoid, unless keeping skate contact with the crease (NHL Rule 1.8; 27.8, 63.2(viii); IIHF Rule 27.7) — the KHL reportedly too, but unverified here
Key: USA Hockey, Hockey Canada and CARHA have no trapezoid, so your goalie is a fourth breakout option but may not freeze it; the PWHL drops it but 65.2(viii) keeps the minor, so do not count on her there
Rule: In Britain keep your goalie inside the restricted area (IIHF 27.7): neither the Elite League's Casebook nor the In-House Rules switch it off, though England Ice Hockey's Rules & Regulations 22.3 call it "not currently enforced", its scope and standing unsettled
```

Your defenceman retrieves the puck behind your net. Before they turn, they need three options:

- **The strong-side winger on the wall**, roughly level with the faceoff dot — the short up option. See [Breakouts](../systems/breakouts.md).
- **The centre**, swinging low through the middle and curling to the same side — the option that turns a breakout into an attack.
- **The defence partner** on the far side, reached *behind the net*. Never through the slot.

The weak-side winger, higher and wider than everyone, adds the width that stops the defence loading one side.

**In your own end, support short and keep the angles safe.** Everywhere else the attacking option comes first; here the safe outlet does. A tidy exit is a win; a clever one that fails is a goal against.

![](diagram:defensive-zone-support)

**Give the retriever a short option, or the only play left is icing** — a faceoff back in your own end and, in the NHL, IIHF, PWHL and some Hockey Canada levels, a tired line that cannot change.

**The goalie is part of your support.** "Man on", "time", "reverse" — the calls in [On-Ice Communication](../foundation/on_ice_communication.md) — tell the carrier which option is really there. Under USA Hockey, Hockey Canada and CARHA rules your goalie can also be a fourth breakout option behind the net, but not one who sits on the puck for you. Under NHL and IIHF rules they may play it behind the goal line only inside the trapezoid.

**What it costs.**

- **Icing (NHL Rule 81):** play stops, the faceoff comes back to your end, and your line may not change before it. The only exceptions in Rule 81.4 are a substitution *"to replace a goalkeeper who had been substituted for an extra attacker, to replace an injured player, or when a penalty has been assessed which affects the on-ice strength of either team"* — so a hurt teammate can come off, a tired one cannot. Rule 87.1 bars a timeout too. The IIHF's 81.4 adds *"a Player with a broken skate"*, which its own Rule 82.1 does not carry; do not plan a change around it. CARHA bars no change after an icing, but icing it again and again on purpose to force faceoffs can be called as delaying the game there: a bench minor (Rule 55(a), Note 2).
- **The shorthanded exemption is not unconditional.** A shorthanded team may ice the puck without a whistle, but NHL Rule 81.6 withdraws that once a *major* has **expired** with nobody on the penalty box to come out, until the next stoppage. USA Hockey grants it only from Youth 15-Only, Girls 16U, High School and Adult upward (Rule 624(b)(1)), so below those a shorthanded team can be called for icing. [Special Teams](../systems/special_teams.md) sets out both, with the reason Rule 20.3 gives for the first.
- **Trapezoid:** under NHL and IIHF rules a goalie who plays the puck behind the goal line outside the trapezoid takes a two-minute delay-of-game minor (NHL Rules 1.8, 27.8 and 63.2(viii); IIHF Rules 1.8 and 27.7). The only exception is keeping skate contact with the crease; there is no teammate exception. The KHL is commonly said to match, but that is unverified here: no KHL rulebook was available.
- **No trapezoid:** the *USA Hockey Playing Rules 2025-29* (Rule 610), the *Hockey Canada Playing Rules 2026-2028* (Rule 10.1) and CARHA's book mark none and do not limit where a goalie plays the puck. **In the PWHL, do not count on it:** Rules 1.8 and 27.8 switch the trapezoid off (*"The PWHL does not apply a restricted area for goalkeepers"*), but Rule 65.2(viii) still writes the minor for playing the puck *"outside of the designated area behind the net"*.
- **Freezing is restricted everywhere.** USA Hockey Rule 614(c) makes it a minor to freeze the puck outside the *"goalkeeper's privileged"* area, which excludes the corners. Hockey Canada's Interpretation 3 to Rule 10.1(a) is stricter: once out of the crease *"they will not be allowed to freeze the puck. If they do, they will be assessed a Minor penalty. **No warning will be issued.**"* CARHA Rule 58(b) and PWHL Rule 65.2(i) also make a freeze behind the net, goalie out of the crease, a minor. How much rec, beer-league and youth hockey runs on the USA Hockey and Hockey Canada books is a question about leagues, and this document has no count for it.

**🇬🇧 In Britain, play as though the restricted area binds.** Nothing in the Elite League's Casebook switches it off — its Casebook adopts the 2026-27 IIHF book — and the In-House Rules leave IIHF Rule 27 unamended. England Ice Hockey's *Rules & Regulations* 22.3 call it *"not currently enforced"*, its scope and standing unsettled. **Keep your goalie inside the area and ask your league.** It costs nothing, and the alternative is two minutes. [UK and England Rules](../foundation/uk_rules.md) has the detail.

### Neutral zone — three lanes

```facts
Position: One player in each of the three lanes — strong-side boards, middle, weak-side boards
Convention: One player per lane is the usual default, a coaching choice rather than a law
Risk: Two players in the same lane can be covered by one defender — "same lane, same checker"
Options: A 1-3-1 regroup stacks the middle lane deliberately, to overload the seam and pull their defence in
Action: Ask whether your team spreads or stacks — identical for one stride, then opposite decisions
Technique: Different lane, different speed, on curved routes so you receive already facing forward
Goal: Stretch the ice with width, so their forwards and defence cannot squeeze the middle
```

The neutral zone has three **lanes**: strong-side boards, middle and weak-side boards.

**The usual default is one player per lane**, a coaching choice rather than a law. Two players in one lane can be covered by one defender: "same lane, same checker". The realistic alternative is a **1-3-1 regroup**, which stacks the middle lane on purpose to overload the seam and pull their defence inward. **Find out whether your team spreads or stacks** — the two look identical for one stride, then need opposite decisions.

Think **"different lane, different speed"**, so the defence cannot set one gap that handles everyone. Curve into open ice rather than skating straight, so you receive the puck already facing forward.

![](diagram:neutral-zone-lanes)

Width does the heavy lifting: stretched wide, their forwards and defence cannot get close enough to squeeze the middle. See [Neutral Zone Systems](../systems/neutral_zone_systems.md) and [Zone Entries](../systems/zone_entries.md).

### Offensive zone — below, level, above

```facts
Position: Someone below the puck, someone level with it and someone above it, at all times
Position: Below is behind or beside the net; level is the half-wall or slot; above is the point and often a third forward high
Key: Offensive-zone support is rotation, not position — the puck moves round the perimeter faster than anyone can skate
Action: When you leave one of those layers, someone fills it — the layer must stay occupied, not your spot
Convention: Rotation rules are a coaching choice and change who may go below the puck — ask which your team plays
```

In the offensive zone support becomes rotation, because the puck moves round the perimeter faster than anyone can skate.

Keep **someone below the puck, someone level with it and someone above it** at all times — section 4's three angles in offensive-zone terms. **Below** is behind or beside the net, the reset when the wall play dies. **Level** is the half-wall (the boards level with the faceoff dot) or the slot. **Above** is the point, often with the third forward high: the safety and the shot.

**When you leave a layer, someone fills it.** That is the rotation, and amateur teams skip it. The *layer* must stay occupied, not your spot.

[Offensive Zone Play §3, "Offensive Zone Structure"](../systems/offensive_zone_play.md) has the full treatment and the alternatives to the standard setup of an attack triangle with both defencemen on the blue line. Rotation rules are a coaching choice that changes who may go below the puck, so **ask which your team plays.**

## 9. Timing over position

Arriving at the right place at the right moment is worth far more than standing in it.

**A stationary open player is easy to cover.** Get there early and stop, and the defence can walk over and mark you.

**A moving player receives better.** You already have speed, so you attack at once. A stationary receiver in traffic is a turnover waiting to happen.

### Supporting late

```facts
Action: Delay deliberately, so you arrive with speed as the puck arrives
Technique: "Puck first, feet second" — let the carrier commit a defender before you launch
Goal: Cross the space at full speed while the defender is stationary with their head turned to the puck
Key: A late arrival with speed beats a set gap — by the time the defenceman reacts you are past the point where they could have set one
Rule: The puck must cross the blue line before or as your second skate does (NHL Rule 83 offside); under USA Hockey 630(a) and Hockey Canada 6.11 that trailing skate must also be touching the ice, so keep it down wherever you play
Never: Delay in your own zone — the carrier needs the outlet now, and a late arrival there is an absent one
```

The skill is **delaying on purpose so you arrive with speed as the puck does.**

The neutral-zone cue is **"puck first, feet second"**: let the carrier commit a defender before you launch. A half-second delay keeps you onside, lets the defender's head turn to the puck, and has you crossing the space at full speed while they stand still.

That is what "arriving late" means, and it is praise. **A late arrival with speed beats a set gap**, because you are past the defender before they can set one. The trailer on a rush, the defenceman jumping into the slot and the weak-side winger cutting to the back post all work this way.

Time your route so the puck beats you over the blue line. Keep one skate on or behind the line, **blade on the ice**, until it does: USA Hockey and Hockey Canada count only a skate in contact.

**When not to delay:** in your own zone, where the carrier needs the outlet *now* and a late arrival is an absent one.

### The moment the puck changes hands

```facts
Action: When your team wins the puck, the nearest player away from the pressure gives the winner an outlet at once — behind or level, blade on the ice, calling for it
Action: Everyone else gets wide and into their lanes rather than turning to watch the puck
Action: When your team loses it, the nearest player pressures the new carrier at once, the next one layers behind, and everybody else gets above the puck
Convention: Whether the nearest forward goes straight in or everyone recovers above the puck first is a coaching choice, so ask your bench which your team plays
```

Support is hardest in the second or two after the puck changes hands, because five players are still where the old possession wanted them.

**When your team wins it,** the nearest player away from the pressure becomes the outlet at once — behind or level with the winner, blade down, calling for it. Everyone else gets wide and into their lanes instead of watching. A player who wins a battle with nowhere to put the puck often loses it a second later.

**When your team loses it,** the nearest player pressures the new carrier before they can look up, the next one layers behind, and everybody else gets above the puck. That is the common version; some teams want everyone above the puck first and pressure only once they have numbers back. Ask your bench which yours plays.

## 10. Moving to create a passing lane

Players who complain nobody passes to them are usually confusing open with available. **Unmarked** means no defender is next to you. **Available** means the pass can be completed, and that needs three things:

1. **A clear lane.** No stick, skate or body between the puck and you. A defender ten metres away standing in that line takes you out of the play as surely as one marking you.
2. **A target.** Blade on the ice, flat, angled to receive. A raised stick is an invisible one.
3. **A carrier who knows you are there.** Their head is often down, so use your voice — "here", "middle", "back". See [On-Ice Communication](../foundation/on_ice_communication.md).

[Playing Without the Puck → Unmarked is not the same as available](playing_without_the_puck.md) covers the lane in more depth.

### It is the receiver's job, not the passer's

```facts
Mindset: Most players wait to be found; good support players go and make themselves findable
Action: If the lane is blocked, move — even a metre puts you the other side of the obstruction
Action: Move to the passing angle, not to the space — a small gap with a clean line beats a big unusable one
Action: When a forechecker seals the carrier on the boards, get below or behind them, where the seal does not reach
Key: Keep adjusting as the puck moves — most failed support is a player who found a good spot once and stopped
```

Most players wait to be found. Good support players make themselves findable:

- **If the lane is blocked, move — even a metre.** One stride sideways can clear the obstruction. You need to be *differently* placed, not more open.
- **Move to the passing angle, not to the space.** The biggest patch of empty ice is often one nothing can be delivered into. A small gap with a clean line is worth more.
- **Change the angle under pressure.** If a forechecker seals the carrier on the boards, the lane up the wall is gone. Get below or behind them.
- **Keep adjusting as the puck moves.** Most failed support is a player who found a good spot once and stopped. See [Playing Without the Puck](playing_without_the_puck.md).

## 11. Support when defending

**Attacking, the second player gives the carrier an option. Defending, the second player supports the first defender — not duplicates them.** Most defensive breakdowns come from getting this wrong.

### Layering, not converging

**In short:** When a teammate is already pressuring the carrier, support behind and to the side of them instead of joining them on the puck.

```facts
Never: Skate at the carrier your teammate is already pressuring — both of you are beaten by the same pass
Position: Behind and to the side of your teammate, covering the space and the passing lane they cannot
Risk: Two players on one puck means the other three cover four of them, and it is the free man who scores
Read: Which way your teammate is angling the carrier — stand where the carrier goes if they get beaten
Technique: Close enough to take over the coverage, far enough not to be beaten by the same move
Action: Talk — "I've got your back", "you have time", "man on"; the layer only works if they know it is there. Say "you have time" only when sure, or they take a hit unbraced
Convention: A double-team is a coaching decision, commonly allowed below your own goal line — ask where and when
Never: Arrive at a double-team at the wall with your back to anyone coming at you, and never duck. Get your skates parallel to the wall instead, forearm and hip to the wall, head up, chin off your chest
Rule: A push or hit from behind is checking from behind even in non-check play. It ejects in NHL, IIHF and PWHL 43; if intentional, in Hockey Canada 7.5(a) and CARHA 53(a). USA Hockey 608(a) starts at a minor plus misconduct; a hard one at the boards or head first ejects (608(b); Casebook 608 Situation 1)
Rule: Into the boards unable to protect themselves: a mandatory match in Hockey Canada 7.5(c) and CARHA 53(b), and in USA Hockey 608(c) with excessive force on a defenceless player — plan on one, and a likely multi-game suspension, for any reckless push (602(a))
Rule: Under USA Hockey a game misconduct also costs you your team's next game (404(b)), and at least two games when it comes from a second major in one game (403(b))
```

Your teammate is pressuring the carrier, and you are the second defender. You can:

- **Converge** on the same carrier. Now both of you are beaten by one pass, and an opponent is free somewhere.
- **Layer** behind and to the side of your teammate, covering the space and lane they cannot. You are the answer if they get beaten, and the reason the carrier cannot simply pass out.

The arithmetic is blunt: **two players on one puck means the other three are covering four**, and the free man scores.

To make the layer a real position:

- **Read which way your teammate is angling the carrier, and stand where the carrier goes if they get beaten.**
- **Be close enough to take over, far enough not to be beaten by the same move.** (The distance is below.)
- **Talk** — "I've got your back," "you have time," "man on." Say "you have time" only when you are sure: a false one sends your teammate into a hit unbraced. See [On-Ice Communication](../foundation/on_ice_communication.md).

![](diagram:layer-not-converge)

**Layering is almost always correct.** It sits inside every named defensive system. F1 pressures while F2 supports and reads the escape; in your own zone the next player covers the lane behind the pressure; a penalty-killing box (two forwards high, two defencemen low) or diamond (a single apex) covers the pass behind whoever steps up.

**The exception is a deliberate double-team**, a coaching decision rather than a default. Many teams send two players at a puck on the boards *below their own goal line*, where the net and boards contain a turnover. Under the standard coverage, the second player supports rather than doubling except there. **Ask your coach where and when you may double** — freelancing it is how backdoor goals happen.

**Going in does not change how you arrive:** on an angle, feet apart, knees bent, **skates parallel to the wall**, **forearm and hip to the wall** and never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, and **never turn your back to anyone coming at you, never duck.** **And never push or hit the opponent on the wall from behind** — go for the puck at their feet. A push from behind into the boards is checking from behind even where body checking is not allowed, and in most books it ends your game — under USA Hockey a forceful, head-first or reckless one draws a game misconduct, which also costs your team's next game (404(b)). [Body Contact and Battles §6, Receiving a Hit Safely](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md) is the full treatment.

### The same distance question, inverted

```facts
Position: Offset diagonally from the first defender, not directly behind them
Convention: The layering distance is the same two-to-three-stick-lengths range as support distance — near enough to arrive in about a second
Risk: Too close and one move beats you both; too far and the carrier skates through the gap between you
Convention: Whether you may hold the layer at all is system-dependent — ask how your team's coverage changes it
```

Too close and one move beats you both. Too far and the carrier skates through the gap. Aim to arrive in about a second without one deke beating you both — roughly two to three stick lengths, offset diagonally rather than directly behind.

**Whether you may hold that layer at all depends on your system** — zone, man-to-man or hybrid. [Playing Without the Puck → Layer, do not duplicate](playing_without_the_puck.md) sets out each and what to ask your coach; [Defensive Zone Coverage](../systems/defensive_zone_coverage.md) is the full treatment.

## 12. How to see it

Spacing is invisible until you look for it, then it is the most obvious thing on the ice.

### Watching a game

```facts
Action: Stop watching the puck — for one full shift, watch a single player who does not have it
Action: Count the options every time a player receives the puck; good teams average two or more
Action: Look at the shape, not the players — could you draw a triangle around the puck?
Action: Watch the far side; the weak-side winger is the best single indicator of spacing discipline
Action: Count how many are below the puck when possession changes — it predicts the next thirty seconds
Action: Watch the trailer on a rush, not the two who carried it in
```

**Stop watching the puck.** For one full shift, watch a single player who does not have it. It feels odd, and it is the fastest way to learn the game. See [How to Watch Hockey](../off-the-ice/how_to_watch_hockey.md).

Then:

- **Count the options.** Each time a player receives the puck, count the teammates who could take a pass. Good teams average two or more. Watch what happens when it is zero.
- **Look at the shape.** Spread at different depths and widths, or a clump? Could you draw a triangle around the puck?
- **Watch the far side.** The weak-side winger is the best single sign of spacing discipline: wide and high, or drifted in to watch?
- **Count how many are below the puck** when possession changes. It predicts whether the next thirty seconds are a rush against.
- **Watch the trailer on a rush.** The dangerous chance often comes from the third player arriving late.

### Self-checking on the ice

```facts
Key: One question, asked constantly — "where are my two options?"
Action: On the puck, name the two before you receive it; if you can only name one, buy time or move it on
Action: Off the puck, ask whether you are one of somebody's two options right now — if not, move
Action: After every pass, go again — pass and move is how the support triangle survives the pass
```

One question, asked constantly: **"Where are my two options?"**

- **On the puck** — ideally before it arrives — name the two. If you can name only one, buy time for a second, or move the puck to someone who has two.
- **Off the puck,** ask *am I one of somebody's two options right now?* If not, you are watching, not supporting. Move.
- **After every pass,** go again. The player you passed to needs two options and you are the nearest. **Pass and move** is how the triangle survives the pass.

## Common Mistakes

- **Skating to the puck instead of the space around it.** You bring your checker, crowd the carrier and empty the space the puck is about to reach. Unless you are closest or your system sends you in to pressure, go to open ice at an angle nobody holds.
- **Supporting from too close.** One defender now covers two of you, and you block the carrier's escape. Back off until one defender cannot reach both: eight to ten feet under pressure, more in open ice.
- **Supporting from too far.** The pass takes too long and a stick gets into the lane. Close in until a pass released now would beat the defender to you.
- **Two players in the same lane.** One checker handles both of you. If a teammate holds your lane, take a different angle rather than queueing behind them.
- **Standing still in the right place.** You got there early and the defence walked over and marked you. Hold off, then arrive at speed, except in your own zone, where the carrier needs the outlet now.
- **All support on one angle.** All behind the puck is safe but goes nowhere; all ahead has no reset. Look where your teammates are and take the angle nobody holds.
- **Passing across the slot in your own end.** An interception there is a point-blank chance for a player already facing your net. Go behind your net or up the wall.
- **Nobody above the puck.** Everyone chases, the puck turns over, and it is a rush the other way. Before you go below the puck, know who is above it, and if nobody is, it is you.
- **Wingers drifting into the middle.** It closes the space that made the middle valuable and puts you in your centre's lane. Stay wide unless your rotation sends you in or you are driving the net on a shot.
- **Piling into a board battle your teammate already controls.** You block the exit. Get open a few metres away with your stick down. If it is a genuine 50-50, or you are your team's designated second player, go in, but arrive safely. Get your skates parallel to the wall, forearm and hip to it, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, and **never turn your back to anyone coming at you, never duck.** **And never push or hit the opponent on the wall from behind** — go for the puck at their feet.
- **Two defenders on the same carrier when nobody called for it.** Both are beaten by one pass, and someone is unmarked. Layer behind the first defender instead. A called double-team, usually below your own goal line, is the exception. **Even then, never push or hit the opponent on the wall from behind** — go for the puck at their feet. A push from behind into the boards is checking from behind even in non-checking hockey.
- **Stick off the ice while "open".** No blade, no pass. Keep your blade on the ice where you want the puck.
- **Finding a good spot once and then stopping.** The puck moved, so your spot moved. Re-check your angle every time the puck moves.
- **Stopping after you pass.** The teammate you passed to now has one option fewer. Move again the moment the puck leaves your stick.
- **Assuming your team's rotation rules.** Who may go below the puck in the offensive zone is a coaching choice. Ask your coach who may go, and when.

## Key Takeaways

1. **Give the carrier two options at different angles, in a triangle.** Two teammates in one lane are one option, and a line has to be rebuilt after every pass; a triangle survives it.
2. **Judge support distance by time.** If a pass released now would beat the defender to you, you are in range. Start at two to three stick lengths, tighter under pressure. That is a rule of thumb — no governing body has been found to set one — so let your coach adjust it.
3. **Take the angle that is missing — behind, level or ahead of the puck.** You need all three at once. In your own end, the lateral pass goes behind your net, never across the slot.
4. **Before you go below the puck, know who is above it.** "Above" means goal-side of the puck, in every zone. Who stays back is a system choice; that somebody does is not, unless your bench gives it up on purpose.
5. **To help a teammate in a battle, usually move away; defending, layer rather than converge.** A called double-team is the exception; ask your coach when. If you do go to the wall, **never turn your back to anyone coming at you, never duck, and never push or hit the opponent from behind** — go for the puck at their feet.
6. **Wingers stay wide.** The middle only exists while someone holds the outside, and drifting in deletes it.
7. **Skate to the space, not to the puck.** If you are not closest and your system has not sent you in, your job is the space.
8. **Arrive late with speed.** Puck first, feet second — except in your own zone, where the carrier needs the outlet now.
9. **Make yourself available — that is the receiver's job.** Blade down, call for it, move to the passing angle and keep adjusting as the puck moves.
10. **Keep asking "where are my two options?", and go again after every pass.** When the puck changes hands, the nearest player becomes the outlet if you won it and the pressure if you lost it.

*Whether a governing body sets a support distance: the USA Hockey article above is the one that publishes a figure, and it publishes it as **Bruyere's quoted speech inside Doyle's piece** — refetched 14 September 2026, HTTP 200, no redirect, and the sentence located in the rendered body rather than in a comment or a script block. Against it: IIHF **Coach Development Program, Level I (Tactical)** — **Offensive Principle #3, "Support"** (page 20.3) and **§20.2.3 "Mid-Lane Support"** (page 20.9), which treat the subject at length and ask only for *"close and quick support"*: [PDF](https://www.hockeyeasternontario.ca/media/n4pf15hp/iihf_coachdev_off_tactics.pdf), sha256 `9b195d03…`, 750,071 bytes, 77 pages. **A governing body's coaching manual, and therefore convention only**, and its only date is **2002**, in the file's own metadata — the document prints none on its face. Searched throughout for a distance (every *feet*, *foot*, *metre*, *meter*, *yard*, *stick length*, and every number followed by a unit) and it states none for support; the one hit anywhere in it is a *"gap of 2.5-3 m"* in the §21 Shadow Drill, which is **defender-to-attacker**, not teammate-to-teammate. ⚠️ **One manual's silence is not a survey of every governing body**, and none of this makes the 10-to-15-foot default a standard.*

*Boards safety (how to arrive at a wall battle — [Body Contact and Battles §6, Receiving a Hit Safely](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md) is the full treatment): [USA Hockey — Heads Up Hockey Program Guide (PDF)](https://cdn4.sportngin.com/attachments/document/0138/6193/HUH_Program_Guide_FINAL.pdf) — Session 5, "Giving A Check", under its "Checking from behind" subheading, which is not the session's title, for *"Even a light hit from behind could inflict a severe head or neck injury"*; and "How spinal injuries happen in hockey" for *"A player doesn't have to be going at full speed for this to happen — it can occur at walking speed"* · [USA Hockey — Introduction To Body Contact (PDF)](https://cdn4.sportngin.com/attachments/document/55c1-2114751/IntroductiontoBodyContact.pdf) — §5 "Checking From Behind", carrying the same light-hit sentence, and "Contact Confidence" A, "Checked Into The Boards", for the fallback when you are already facing the wall: *"distribute the force of the impact over as wide a surface as possible, including putting your stick and gloves on the glass to cushion the blow"*. Located in the document itself on 30 August 2026. **Note the division of labour between the two quotes:** the walking-speed finding is stated of *"the head being slightly flexed (head down) while making head-on contact with the boards or goal post"*, and USA Hockey does not say it of a player who turns their back — the hazard of the turned back is that it makes the contact a hit from behind, which is the light-hit sentence. **Turning your back and ducking are both absolute here; only the ducking one has a published statement about speed behind it.** The walking-speed finding is also a restatement rather than USA Hockey's own research: the page credits *"research done among a wide range of hockey players"* and cites **Charles H. Tator et al., *Spinal Injuries Due To Hockey*, Canadian Journal of Neurological Sciences 11:34–41**, not read in the original here.*

*Women's professional, read in the primary text on 23 September 2026: [PWHL Official Rule Book 2025-2026 (PDF)](https://assets.contentstack.io/v3/assets/bltebdb4296e05d53db/bltb8440fe08a5e6dc5/69178cd0a582d7db321ad107/2025-2026_PWHL_Rulebook.pdf) — **Rule 27.8** (*"The PWHL does not apply a restricted area for goalkeepers"*), with **Rule 65.2(viii)**, read 14 October 2026, which still writes the minor for a goalkeeper who plays the puck *"outside of the designated area behind the net"*, so the book contradicts itself; **Rule 65.2(i)** (a goalkeeper's deliberate freeze outside her crease), **Rule 43** (Checking from Behind), **Rule 83.4** (no substitution before the ensuing face-off after an icing, repeated in Rule 84.1), **Rule 89.1** (no time-out to the defensive team following an icing) and **Rule 85.1 (Off-side)**, read 26 September 2026, which carries the NHL's vertical-plane test word for word — *"If a player’s skate has yet to break the “plane” prior to the puck completely crossing the leading edge, she is deemed to be on–side for the purpose of the off–side rule."* **The book renumbers from Rule 52 onward**, so the NHL's 81.4, 83.1 and 87.1 will not find them.*

*Adult recreational, CARHA-affiliated leagues only and never a general rec-hockey standard, read in the primary text on 26 September 2026: [CARHA Hockey Official Rule Book 2026 (PDF)](https://carhahockey.ca/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/RuleBk-2026e.pdf) (read in the 2020 printing; every rule cited here is unchanged in the 2026 book) — **Rule 53(a) and (b)** (Checking from Behind), **Rule 55(a)** and its **Note 2** (a bench minor for deliberately delaying the game, which names *"a SERIES OF ICINGS"*), **Rule 58(b)** (a goalkeeper falling on or holding the puck behind the goal line with their body entirely outside the crease), **Rule 72(c)** (Off-sides), *"A player is off-side when both skates are completely over the edge of the determining blue line involved in the play."*, and its **Note 1**, under which a player *"is on-side when either of their skates are in physical contact with, or on their own side of, the blue line at the instant the puck completely crosses the edge of that blue line, regardless of the position of their stick."* ⚠️ **The book never defines what "their own side" means for a skate off the ice** — "plane" appears nowhere in it, and neither Rule 72 nor its casebook Situation for that rule reaches a raised skate. So blade-down contact is expressly on-side there, while the lifted trail skate is settled neither way.*
