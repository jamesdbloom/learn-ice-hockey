## Key focus

**Know which of the four off-puck states you are in, and switch jobs the instant it changes.** Your team has the puck, they have it, it is loose, or it has just changed hands. Most bad off-puck play is the right job for the state before.

**Defending, watch your man rather than the puck.** Five teammates already watch the puck; nobody else watches your man. Keep them in your peripheral vision and take short looks at the puck. If you cannot see both in one glance, move.

**Stick in the passing lane, body between your man and your own net.** Lay the blade flat in the lane, never across them, which is a restraining foul. If you cannot do both in your own zone, net-side wins.

**Layer behind the pressure — never duplicate it.** One player pressures the carrier; the next sits behind them, taking away the pass the carrier wants. Two on one puck leaves an attacker free. A planned double-team or man-to-man coverage is the exception, so ask.

**On a loose puck, one player goes hard, one supports, nobody else joins — and call it.** Arrive at the wall with your skates parallel to it and your head up. Never turn your back to anyone coming at you, and never duck.

**Stick on the puck first, body second, and only on the player who has the puck — and find out before your first shift whether you may hit at all.** Younger age groups, girls' and women's play and whole leagues can be non-checking. There, take the ice, never the man.

**Backcheck to a man, not to a place.** Pick up the man arriving unmarked in the middle, unless your system sends the first forward at the carrier. **Never finish into anybody's back** — if you can see their numbers, let up. It causes life-altering injuries and in most books ends your game.

**Being unmarked is not the same as being available.** Show a flat stick, keep a straight line to the puck, and keep that line clear of sticks. Fix the line, then go when the passer's head comes up.

**Get open with a change of speed rather than top speed.** Defenders match speed but struggle to match changes of it. Coast to let your check settle, then go, or stop hard. That this beats top speed is coaching craft, not a measured finding.

**Drive the net, and finish every route you start.** A real drive pulls a defender with you and opens the ice behind; a glide moves nobody, and abandoned routes teach defenders to ignore you. Take the ice in front of the net, never the goalie.

## Overview

You spend almost all of your ice time without the puck. NHL tracking data — which the league itself calls unofficial — puts a forward at about forty seconds of possession in every twenty minutes of five-on-five, in bursts averaging 1.27 seconds. What you do without it decides what those seconds are worth.

**Off-puck play is four skills, not one.** What you do while your team has the puck is close to the opposite of what you do while they have it, and both differ from a loose puck and from the moment possession changes. Most players have a plan for one of the four.

**Being available beats being unmarked.** You need a flat stick, a straight line to the carrier, and no stick in that line. Go when the passer's head comes up, not when the space appears — and come back *toward* the puck rather than skating away and hoping.

**A loose puck needs a short plan: one goes, one supports, nobody else joins.** Who goes depends on your system. The race to the wall is a race into the boards: USA Hockey's *Heads Up Hockey* materials tie almost all on-ice cervical spine injuries to a head-down, head-on hit into the boards or a goal post, at walking speed as well as full. Arrive on an angle, head up and chin off your chest — never turn your back to anyone coming at you, and never duck.

**Defending without the puck is mostly about where you stand.** Watch your man, keep your stick in their lane and your body between them and your net, and layer behind the pressure instead of joining it. **Stick on the puck first, body second, and only on the player with the puck** — age group, league and even the opponent can take the hit away, so ask before your first shift. [If you are the one pressuring](#if-you-are-the-one-pressuring) sets out each book.

**Effort without the puck is the cheapest thing to improve.** Backcheck to a man, drive the net as if the puck is coming, and finish every route. The one thing effort must never buy is a hit on a player's back: when you are late on somebody facing away from you, take the lane or let up.

None of it requires a skill you do not already have.

## The central premise: you almost never have the puck

This is worth quantifying honestly, because the number is more extreme than most players guess.

### What the tracking data says

**In short:** Players hold the puck for only a few percent of their ice time, so most of your game is played without it.

The NHL installed puck and player tracking (PPT) in every arena for the 2021-22 season, and added an individual puck-possession model to its data stream in March 2023. A 2025 study from the University of Waterloo used that data across 1,194 games of the 2023-24 NHL season, covering 671 skaters, to measure how long individual players actually hold the puck at **5-on-5** — five skaters a side with both goalies in net. Note that 5-on-5 is not the same as even strength: 4-on-4 and 3-on-3 are also even strength, and the paper deliberately reports 5-on-5 only. Every figure below is 5-on-5.

The headline findings, all normalised to 20 minutes of ice time so that players with different roles can be compared:

| Measure | Forwards | Defencemen |
|---|---|---|
| Total puck possession per 20 minutes of ice time | **0:40** (95% CI 0:39–0:41) | **0:49** (95% CI 0:48–0:50) |
| Number of separate possessions per 20 minutes | 31.3 | 37.1 |
| Average length of one possession | 1.27 sec | 1.32 sec |
| Puck possession *in the offensive zone* per 20 minutes | 0:18 | 0:09 |

The study's own summary of that first row: players spend **about 3.3% to 4.2% of their ice time with the puck.**

Two more numbers from the same work, because they reframe what "having the puck" even means:

- The average individual possession lasts **1.29 seconds** across all skaters. In the offensive zone — where you most want to do something clever — it drops to **1.05 seconds**.
- The single highest total possession time in the league belonged to Quinn Hughes at **1:37 per 20 minutes**, followed by Cale Makar at 1:25 and Mathew Barzal at 1:21. Even the most puck-dominant player in the NHL holds it for under two minutes per twenty.

**USA Hockey's own puck-possession study** of the 2002 Olympic Winter Games and the 2002 Youth Tier I National Championships puts the **men's per-game average at 1:07** with the puck (1:01.4 in the men's gold-medal game itself). ⚠️ **Read those figures as the governing body's own report rather than as a study you can go and check.** USA Hockey publishes the whole thing as a single page of summary figures inside its *Small Area Competitive Games* handbook, and that handbook — the only place the study appears, as far as this document has been able to establish — states no method, no number of players and no definition of a puck touch. Different era, and no stated method to set against the tracking work above — what the two share is the order of magnitude.

The youth half of that study is more interesting than it first looks, and it has to be read bracket by bracket rather than as one number:

| Level | Game length | Puck possession per game |
|---|---|---|
| Olympic men (per-game average) | 60 min | **1:07.05** |
| Olympic women (per-game average) | 60 min | **1:12** |
| 14-and-Under nationals | 48 min | **1:06.02** |
| 16-/18-and-Under nationals | 51 min | **48.3 s** |
| 12-and-Under nationals | 45 min | **38.4 s** |

So the striking line — that the best youth players held the puck about as long as the best professionals — is true of the **14-and-Under** bracket and of that bracket only. The two levels either side of it are well under half the Olympic figure. The headline nobody should lose sight of is the one USA Hockey itself drew: across every level measured, from Olympic professionals down to 12-and-Under, **the maximum average a player possessed the puck in a single game was a minute and twelve seconds.**

### Putting it in your terms

Some arithmetic, all of which is mine rather than any source's — including the two inputs it starts from.

Across the 2024-25 NHL regular season, forwards who played at least 40 games averaged **15:17 of ice time per game**; defencemen averaged **19:31**. A separate sample of 8,325 shifts from 11 NHL games in that season gives a **mean shift length of 47 seconds and a median of 45**. **Neither of those is a published figure** — the ice-time averages are calculated from Hockey Reference's 2024-25 skater table, the shift lengths from the NHL's public shift-chart API across a small sample of games chosen for convenience, and neither is on the same footing as the peer-reviewed possession data above.

Take a forward with 15:17 of ice time. If the study's 5-on-5 rate held across all of it:

- They are on the ice for roughly **a quarter of the 60-minute game**.
- On the study's 5-on-5 rate, a forward with that ice time holds the puck for **about 30 seconds** — under **1% of the game clock**.
- That 30 seconds arrives as roughly **24 separate possessions**, each lasting a little over a second.
- Across about 19 or 20 shifts, that is **a bit more than one possession per shift**.
- In the offensive zone, with the puck, they have **about 14 seconds** for the whole game.

For a defenceman at 19:31, the equivalents are about **48 seconds** total and **roughly 9 seconds** in the offensive zone.

### What that means for how you practise

```facts
Key: Working only on what you do with the puck trains about 3.3% to 4.2% of your ice time — the rest is untrained
Key: The 3.3% to 4.2% figure is 5-on-5 only and normalised per 20 minutes of ice time, and the arithmetic joining it to a real shift is this document's own — the right shape, not decimals to defend
Risk: The untrained rest of your ice time decides whether your possessions happen in a dangerous place with your head up, or in a corner with two men on you
Mindset: Skill work is not wasted — a possession of a little over a second is a brutally short window in which to be unskilled
Action: Improve the fifteen minutes you spend on the ice without the puck — it is the most reliable way to get more out of your thirty seconds with it
```

If you spend every practice and every stickhandling session working on what you do with the puck, you are training **about 3.3% to 4.2% of your ice time**. That work is not wasted — those 24 possessions are where goals get created, and a little over a second is a brutally short window in which to be unskilled. But **the rest of your ice time** is currently untrained, and it is the part that decides whether those 24 possessions happen in a dangerous place with your head up, or in a corner with two men on you.

Put it the other way round. The most reliable way to get more out of your thirty seconds with the puck is to improve the fifteen minutes you spend on the ice without it.

> **Caveat, stated once and applying to all of the above:** the possession figures are 5-on-5 only and are normalised per 20 minutes of ice time. Real ice time includes power plays and penalty kills, where possession patterns differ. The NHL calls this dataset unofficial and it may differ from hand-labelled data. The arithmetic combining possession rates with ice time and shift length is mine, not the researchers'. Treat the numbers as the right *shape*, not as decimals to defend.

## The four off-puck states

```facts
Read: At any instant that you do not have the puck you are in one of four states — your team has it, they have it, it is loose, or it has just changed
Priority: When two states apply, take the one that names the more urgent job — a loose puck you can get to is State 3, whatever else is going on
Convention: The four states are a working sort, not a mathematical partition — states 3 and 4 genuinely overlap, and the value of the sort is that it changes what you look at and where you skate
Key: The state changes without warning several times a shift, and most bad off-puck play is a player still doing the right thing for the previous state
Key: Most players have a plan for one state only, which is exactly why States 3 and 4 are where the most improvement is available
```

Here is the organising idea for the rest of this document. At any instant that you do not have the puck, you are in one of four states, and each one asks something different of you:

| State | Who has the puck | Your job in one line |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Support** | Your team | Get open, create options, occupy defenders |
| **2. Defending** | Their team | If you are the closest, pressure the puck. If you are not, deny space, watch your man, and layer behind whoever is |
| **3. Scramble** | Nobody — the puck is loose | Win the race with body position, or protect what is behind you |
| **4. Transition** | Just changed, nothing settled | Recognise it first and act before the other side does |

**This is a working sort, not a mathematical partition.** States 3 and 4 overlap: a loose puck in the middle of a change of possession is genuinely both. When two apply, take the one that names the more urgent job — if there is a puck on the ice that nobody owns and you can get to it, that is State 3, whatever else is going on. The value of the sort is that it changes what you look at and where you skate, not that it carves the game into disjoint boxes.

Two other things about this framework matter more than the framework itself.

**The state changes without warning, several times a shift.** A blocked shot turns State 1 into State 3 in a tenth of a second. Most bad off-puck play is not a player doing the wrong thing for the state — it is a player still doing the right thing for the *previous* state. This is why [Scanning and Anticipation](scanning_and_anticipation.md) is the foundation under all of this: you cannot switch states you have not noticed.

**Most players only have a plan for one state.** Offensive players have a plan for State 1. Defensive players have a plan for the covering half of State 2. Almost nobody has a plan for States 3 and 4, which is exactly why those two are where the most improvement is available.

## State 1 — Your team has the puck and you don't

You are one of four teammates without the puck. Your job is to make the puck carrier's next decision easy. There are three ways to do that, and only one of them involves receiving a pass.

### Get open

Become a passing option. This is the obvious one and it gets its own full section below.

### Create options you never use

```facts
Goal: Be a credible option even when the puck never comes — one the carrier looks at and declines has still made a defender respect it
Key: Two credible options force a defender to choose; one option lets them sit in the lane
Position: Support is about being available in a different place, not about being close — the geometry belongs to Puck Support and Spacing
```

A passing option that the carrier *looks at* and declines still did its job, because a defender had to respect it. Two credible options force a defender to choose; one option lets them sit in the lane. This is the whole logic of [Puck Support and Spacing](puck_support_and_spacing.md) — support is about being available in a *different place*, not about being close.

### Occupy a defender

```facts
Action: Take a defender away from where the play is going to happen, whether or not the puck ever comes to you
Key: Being the wrong player to receive the pass does not excuse you from doing anything
Read: As the far-side winger with the puck on the opposite half-wall, whether you drift into the middle or hold width changes what the strong-side defenceman can do
```

Take a defender away from where the play is going to happen, whether or not the puck ever comes to you. Also below, in its own section, because it is the least understood work in hockey.

**The reason to think of these as three separate jobs:** on any given shift you may be the wrong player to receive the pass, and that does not excuse you from doing anything. If you are the far-side winger and the puck is on the opposite half-wall (the boards area roughly level with the faceoff dot), you may not touch the puck at all — but whether you drift into the middle or hold width changes what the strong-side defenceman can do.

## State 2 — They have the puck

Somebody on your team has to go to the puck, and the other four have to do something else. Both halves of that are off-puck work, and both are covered here — the pressuring job first, because everyone else's job is defined relative to it, and then the four players who are not pressuring, which is where amateur teams actually leak goals.

### If you are the one pressuring

**In short:** Angle the carrier toward your help, arrive under control, and tell your support you have the carrier so nobody guesses.

```facts
Goal: Take options away and force the carrier to decide early — winning the puck is a bonus, not the job
Action: Angle, do not chase. Approach on a curve that closes one side of the ice, so the only route left is the one your support is covering
Action: Steer them where your help is; angling a carrier into the middle while your support sits on the wall is worse than not pressuring at all
Technique: Arrive under control — close the last few feet with short strides, stick out front, in a position to change direction
Priority: Stick on the puck, body on the man while they still have it, in that order — take the body only where your league permits checking, and only when it does not cost you the lane behind you
Rule: Check whether you may hit at all — USA Hockey 604(a) bans body checking at 12U and below, in all girls'/women's and non-check adult classifications, and wherever a local governing body has prohibited it; Hockey Canada 7.3 bans it at U13 and below, in female hockey and any Member-approved division
Rule: USA Hockey 604(b) bars body checking any time a checking team plays a non-checking one, whatever your own division — so whether you may hit can change game by game
Rule: In Britain no rule book states a checking age; IHUK's junior rules make U10 and U12 non-checking. Women's play runs IIHF Rule 101.1 unamended, and Britain's Women's NIHL is non-checking at U16 and senior
Rule: In Britain the competition you are entered in decides whether you may body check, not your age — IHUK's junior and women's Rules of Competition give opposite answers at U16. Ask your league before your first shift
Rule: IIHF 101.1 allows body checking in women's hockey only with clear intent to play the puck or gain possession, save the rule's own exception — a step or glide into a player is at least a minor, up to a major and automatic game misconduct; never use the boards to pin or push her
Action: Talk, because you are the trigger — "I've got the carrier" tells your support where to stand, and silence makes four people guess
Convention: Who goes is a coaching choice — under the 2-1-2 and low zone collapse used throughout the nearest player pressures; under a 1-2-2 only one forward pressures at all, and under a trap almost nobody pressures deep
```

Roughly a fifth of the time it is you: **F1 on the forecheck, the first defender back on a rush, the strong-side defenceman in your own corner.** (**F1, F2 and F3 are roles defined by order of arrival, not positions** — whoever gets there first is F1.) The job is not to win the puck; winning it is a bonus. The job is to **take options away and force the carrier to decide early**, so that your four teammates are covering a narrower set of possibilities than they were a second ago.

- **Angle, do not chase.** Approach on a curve that closes one side of the ice, so the only route left is the one your support is covering. Skating straight at somebody gives them both sides and gives you nothing.
- **Steer them where your help is.** Your pressure and the second layer are one play, not two. Angling a carrier into the middle while your support sits on the wall is worse than not pressuring at all.
- **Arrive under control.** Full speed with your feet stopped is how you get beaten with one movement. Close the last few feet with short strides, stick out front, in a position to change direction.
- **Stick on the puck, body on the man — in that order.** **Only while they still have the puck:** once it has gone, let up, because a check after a pass or shot can be a [late hit](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md#the-late-hit--and-the-narrow-window-for-finishing-a-check). Take the body only when it does not cost you the lane behind you; a missed hit is a five-second absence. **And check whether you may hit at all** — see below. Where you may not, this bullet is angling, stick checks and body position: take the ice, never the man. Under Hockey Canada or CARHA hold only ice you already have, and in CARHA avoid the contact rather than lean into it.
- **Talk, because you are the trigger.** "I've got the carrier" tells your support where to stand. Silence makes four people guess.
- **Who goes is a coaching choice.** Under the standard system used throughout — a **2-1-2 forecheck** in their end and a **low zone collapse** in yours — the nearest player pressures and the second supports. Under a **1-2-2** only one forward pressures at all and the others hold the middle; under a trap, almost nobody pressures deep. Find out which you play. See [Forechecking Systems](../systems/forechecking_systems.md) and [Defensive Zone Coverage](../systems/defensive_zone_coverage.md).

**Whether you may hit at all.** Ask your association or league before your first shift, because the answer is not always your age.

- **USA Hockey** bans body checking at 12U and below, in all girls'/women's classifications and in non-check adult classifications. A local governing body *"may prohibit body checking in any classification"*, so a 14U or adult team can be non-check with nothing in the age list changing. And whenever a checking team plays a non-checking one, the whole game is non-check — so the answer can change game by game.
- **Hockey Canada** bans it at U13 and below, throughout female hockey and in any Member-approved division.
- **In Britain neither rule book states an age, so the competition you are entered in decides.** IHUK's Junior Rules of Competition run U10 and U12 non-checking and U14, U16 and U19 checking. The Women's National Ice Hockey League sets *"Full ice, non-checking, stop clock"* for the WNIHL and WNIHL U16 alike, while NIHL 1 and 2 are *"Full ice, checking, stop clock"* — so at U16 the junior and women's tables give opposite answers. The Elite League's Casebook sets no checking provision, and no other British document found here states one. Above junior hockey, ask your league and get the answer in writing.
- **British women's hockey runs IIHF Rule 101.1 unamended, and that is a restricted permission, not a licence to hit.** Contact is allowed only *"when there is a clear intention of playing the puck or attempting to 'gain possession' of the puck"*, subject to the rule's own exception. Never step or glide into a player, and never use the boards to pin her, push her in or eliminate her from the play. Read your competition's own rules too, because they can be narrower.

**What it costs.** Under USA Hockey a body check where checking is barred is a minor (604(c)). **604(d)** reads *"A major plus a game misconduct penalty shall be assessed to any player who recklessly endangers an opponent with a body check in a Competitive Contact category"*, with **604(e)** adding a match penalty at that tier — plan on one, and a likely multi-game suspension, if the check is reckless (**602(a)**). The bans themselves are USA Hockey **604(a)** and **(b)** and Hockey Canada **7.3**. Under IIHF 101.1, *"[a]ny move by a Player to step or glide into an opposing Player will be assessed at least a minor penalty (2') for an 'illegal hit'"*, up to a *"Major penalty (5') and automatic game misconduct penalty"*. CARHA **49(a)** penalises a player who does not avert body contact. See [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md) §1.

### If you are not the one pressuring

```facts
Position: Four of you are off the puck on defence at any moment, and only one teammate is on it
Risk: The four players away from the puck are where goals get conceded, because they either all chase the puck or all stand still
Key: The single organising question is "what is behind me, and who is it?"
```

Four of you are in this position at any moment, and this is where goals get conceded, because the four non-pressuring players either all chase the puck or all stand still.

The single organising question: **what is behind me, and who is it?**

### Watch your assignment, not the puck

```facts
Never: Watch the puck on defence — five teammates are already looking at it, and your man is the one thing nobody else is looking at
Technique: Keep your assignment in your peripheral vision while your eyes take short, repeated looks at the puck — not the reverse
Read: If you cannot see your man and the puck in the same glance, you are in the wrong place — adjust your position until you can
Convention: Coaches name puck-watching as the commonest amateur defensive error — an observation rather than a measured ranking, because no published work ranking amateur off-puck errors by how often each happens was found for this document
```

The defensive error coaches name most often in amateur hockey is watching the puck. Take that as coaching consensus rather than a measured ranking: no published work ranking off-puck errors by how often each happens was found for this document. [Scanning and Anticipation](scanning_and_anticipation.md) covers the gaze research hockey does have, and [Game Management](../systems/game_management.md) the studies that do measure amateur play.

Watching the puck feels responsible. It is not, because five teammates are already looking at it, and the man you are supposed to be covering is the one thing nobody else is looking at.

The technique is to keep your assignment in your **peripheral vision** while your eyes take short, repeated looks at the puck — not the reverse. If you cannot see your man and the puck in the same glance, you are in the wrong place: adjust your position until you can.

### Stick in the lane, body between man and net

```facts
Technique: Blade on the ice, flat, in the passing lane between the puck and your man — a stick held six inches up removes nothing
Position: Put your body between your man and your own net, not between your man and the puck
Never: Put your stick on a man who does not have the puck — the blade goes in the passing lane, and laid across them it is a restraining foul instead
Technique: Face up-ice enough to see the play, stay on the goal side of your man, and let your stick reach across into the lane
Priority: When you cannot both seal the lane and stay net-side, net-side wins in the defensive zone
Key: A pass completed twenty feet from your net is survivable; a player alone at the goalmouth is not
```

Two separate tools, and players routinely use only one.

- **Your stick takes away the pass.** Blade on the ice, in the passing lane between the puck and your man — **in the lane, not on them.** A stick lying flat in a lane removes a pass that a stick held six inches up does not.
- **Your body takes away the shot and the net drive.** Position yourself between your man and your own net, not between your man and the puck — **take the ice, not the body**, getting to the spot and holding it rather than stepping across into them.

![](diagram:stick-in-lane-body-net-side)

Doing both at once is a body-position problem: face up-ice enough to see the play, stay on the goal side of your man, and let your stick reach across into the lane. When they conflict — you cannot both seal the lane and stay net-side — **net-side wins in the defensive zone**, because a pass completed twenty feet from your net is survivable and a player alone at the goalmouth is not.

**Your man does not have the puck, and that limits both tools.** You may use body position to make their path to the puck longer, but not with your stick, not with your free hand, and not as cover for an otherwise illegal check (NHL and IIHF 56.1, PWHL 57.1). USA Hockey's Preface says the same — a skater keeping their own lane may *"force an opponent to take a less direct route to the puck"*, without a hand or arm. So the blade goes in the lane, not on them, and you take the ice by getting to the spot first, not by stepping across into them once they are moving.

**Under Hockey Canada or in a CARHA adult league, hold only the ice you already have.** Hockey Canada writes no such right, and CARHA's Note 2 gives it only to *"the defending players"*, who may *"stand their ground"* or *"shadow"* attackers. Both make it interference to impede an opponent *"not in possession of the puck"* (Hockey Canada 8.3, CARHA 66(a)). CARHA 49(a) also reaches a player who *"does not avert body contact with an opponent"* — a minor, or a major that ends your game and at least your next one (30(a), 32(d)).

### Layer, do not duplicate

```facts
Never: Go and pressure a carrier a teammate is already pressuring
Position: Be the second layer instead — behind and slightly to the side of them, in the space they would be beaten into
Key: Puck Support and Spacing has the layering distances
Convention: Whether you hold the layer is system-dependent — under the low zone collapse used throughout you sink toward the slot and cover areas; in man-to-man you may have to leave the layer to follow your check to the point; a hybrid is man low and zone high
Action: Ask your coach which coverage you play, and what the trigger is for switching
```

If a teammate is already pressuring the puck carrier, **do not go and pressure them too.** Position yourself as the **second layer** instead: behind and slightly to the side of them, in the space they would be beaten into.

**[Puck Support and Spacing → Support when defending](puck_support_and_spacing.md) has the geometry** — the converge-versus-layer choice, how far behind and how far off to the side to sit, what to read while you sit there, and when a deliberate double-team is the exception. The distances are set out there.

What belongs here is the part that is not geometry at all:

> **This is a system-dependent job.** In the **low zone collapse (zone) coverage** taken as the default throughout, the non-pressuring players sink toward the slot and cover *areas*, so layering behind the puck-pressurer is natural. In **man-to-man** coverage you stay with your assigned player wherever they go, which means you may have to leave the layer to follow them out to the point. In a **hybrid** scheme you play man-to-man low and zone high, so the answer changes depending on where you are standing. Ask your coach which one you play, and ask what the trigger is for switching. Full detail in [Defensive Zone Coverage](../systems/defensive_zone_coverage.md).

## State 3 — The puck is loose

Nobody has it. It is on the boards, in a corner, sitting in the slot after a save, or skittering out of a scrum. This is the state nobody teaches, and it recurs dozens of times a game.

### Who goes

```facts
Convention: Who goes is a coaching choice — under the 2-1-2 default the nearest two forwards go and the third reads; under a 1-2-2 one player goes and the other two hold the middle
Key: F1, F2 and F3 are roles defined by order of arrival, not by position — whoever gets there first is F1
Position: In your own zone under a low zone collapse, the closest player takes the corner, one teammate supports on the boards side and one covers the net front — never two
Action: One player goes hard, one player supports, nobody else joins — the rule that survives every system
Risk: Two players arriving at the same loose puck from the same angle is a turnover waiting to happen, because both are committed and neither is available
Action: If it is genuinely ambiguous, call it — "I got it", "yours", "help" — and the call resolves it
```

The honest answer is that this is a **coaching choice**, and the worst version is not knowing.

Under the standard forecheck used throughout — a **2-1-2** in the offensive zone — the first forward in (F1) pressures the puck and the second (F2) supports them, so on a loose puck in their end **the nearest two forwards go and the third reads**. Remember that **F1, F2 and F3 are roles defined by order of arrival, not by position**: whoever gets there first is F1, whether they are a centre or a winger.

Under a **1-2-2**, only one forward goes and the other two hold the middle, so on the same loose puck **one player goes and the others do not**. Under a **1-3-1 neutral zone** structure the read is different again. In your own zone, under a low zone collapse, the loose puck in the corner is taken by whoever is closest with **one teammate immediately supporting on the boards side and one covering the net front** — never two.

Find out which of these your team plays. Then, the rule that survives every system:

**One player goes hard. One player supports. Nobody else joins.** Two players arriving at the same loose puck from the same angle is a turnover waiting to happen, because both are committed and neither is available.

![](diagram:loose-puck-who-goes)

If it is genuinely ambiguous, **call it** — "I got it," "yours," "help" — and the call resolves it. Hockey's off-puck problems are disproportionately communication problems.

### Then how you arrive — covered elsewhere

```facts
Key: Deciding who goes is the off-puck question this document answers; how you arrive belongs to Body Contact and Battles, §10
Priority: Winning the puck never justifies arriving in any orientation other than the safe one
Technique: Come to the wall on an angle, feet apart and knees bent, skates parallel to it before contact, forearm and hip to the wall — not the point of your shoulder, nor the whole shoulder if you can help it
Technique: Head up, chin off your chest
Never: Turn your back to anyone coming at you, and never duck
Key: Squaring your chest to the boards is the fallback for when you cannot turn in time, not the target — then stick and gloves up, spread on the glass, head off it
```

Deciding *who* goes is the off-puck question, and it is the one this document answers. What the two who go should do when they get there is not: winning the race is not winning the puck. How you arrive, how you take body position, what the rules entitle you to and — above all — how not to get hurt all belong to **[Body Contact and Battles → Loose Puck Races and 50-50 Pucks](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md)**. It quotes the USA Hockey entitlement standard and the NHL interference rule that make inside body position legal, sets out why Hockey Canada and CARHA give you no such right, and carries "first to arrive, last to commit" with its limits.

**One thing from there outranks winning the puck, and this is the moment it applies.** A race to the wall is a race into the boards. USA Hockey's *Heads Up Hockey* materials, citing Tator et al., attribute *"almost all on-ice cervical spine injuries"* to a head *"slightly flexed (head down) while making head-on contact with the boards or goal post"*. They add that *"[a] player doesn't have to be going at full speed for this to happen — it can occur at walking speed."*

So arrive the safe way round. ⚠️ **Come to the wall on an angle, feet apart and knees bent, with your skates parallel to the boards before contact** and your **forearm and hip to the wall** — not the point of your shoulder, and not the whole shoulder if you can help it. Keep your **head up and chin off your chest**, **never turn your back to anyone coming at you, and never duck**. Squaring your chest to the boards is the fallback for when you cannot turn in time, not the target — and if you are caught that way, get your stick and gloves up and spread on the glass to take the blow, with your head off it. Winning the puck never justifies arriving any other way. [Body Contact and Battles → §10, Loose Puck Races and 50-50 Pucks](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md) covers it in full, and everything in its §6 applies at exactly this moment.

### What to prioritise when structure has broken down

**In short:** In a scramble, cover the net front first, then the shooter's stick, then clear the danger area, and only then look for the outlet.

```facts
Priority: The front of your own net first — if nobody is there, go there, because nothing else matters as much
Priority: Then take away the shooter's stick, not the puck: seal the blade, or lift it low on the shaft, well below the bottom hand, as the puck arrives, then let go. The lift goes up, not sideways
Priority: Then get it out of the danger area — anywhere is better than the slot as long as it stays in the rink, and this is the one moment an unglamorous clear is unambiguously correct
Priority: Then, and only then, look for the outlet — the exit pass before the danger is gone is how a scramble becomes a goal against
Rule: Do not clear it directly out of play, though — from your own zone that is a two-minute delay-of-game minor under the NHL, IIHF and PWHL (NHL Rule 63.2(iii)), and a deliberate one is a minor from any zone under 63.2(ii); off the glass and along it is fine
Rule: Do not rely on a skate clear under the IIHF — its Rulebook 63.2(III) names only "hand or stick" in 2025/26 and 2026/27 alike, but 63.2(II) reaches a deliberate one by foot from anywhere; its Handbook answers Yes to a kick over the glass (Situation 63.34, all three editions), so clear with the stick
Rule: Never aim a clear at the bench — over the glass behind it (NHL 63.2(iii), PWHL 65.2(iii)) or around it (IIHF 63.2(III)) is still the minor. Exempt: no-glass boards, a puck straight into the bench, one off a faceoff — but a draw aimed out is 63.2(ii)'s deliberate minor
Rule: Over the glass from your own zone is a minor whether you meant it or not only under the NHL, IIHF and PWHL (63.2(iii), 63.2(III), 65.2(iii)) — USA Hockey 610(c) penalises a deliberate one only, and Hockey Canada 10.1(v) and CARHA 55 reach only the goalie
Rule: Deliberately shooting or batting the puck out of play is a minor "from anywhere on the ice surface" — no zone limit in any book that writes it (NHL 63.2(ii), IIHF 63.2(II); USA Hockey 610(c); Hockey Canada 10.1(ii); PWHL 65.2(ii); CARHA 75(b)), and only the NHL and IIHF name leg, foot or arm too
Rule: The NHL, IIHF, USA Hockey and PWHL write the after-the-whistle one into the delay-of-game rule itself; Hockey Canada at Rule 11.1, preamble item (v), not 10.1. USA Hockey escalates: a misconduct (601(c)(10)), a game misconduct in protest (601(d)(7)) or toward an official or spectator (601(d)(9))
Action: The mirror image applies in their end — net front first, sticks free, and shoot from anywhere before you try to be clever
```

Sometimes there is no structure left — a scramble in front of your net, three players down, sticks everywhere. When you cannot work out the right play, work down this list in order:

1. **The front of your own net.** If nobody is there, go there. Nothing else matters as much.
2. **Take away the shooter's stick, not the puck.** Seal the blade, or lift it **low on the shaft, well below their bottom hand**, as the puck arrives — **then let go**, because hanging on to it is holding the stick. **The lift goes up, not sideways.** You do not need to win the puck to prevent the shot. Take the lift low in every league: at or near the hands it can be called hooking everywhere.
3. **Get it out of the danger area.** Anywhere is better than the slot, and this is the one moment an unglamorous clear is unambiguously correct. **Clear it hard and wide off the glass, or short enough to die in the neutral zone — never over the glass and out of the rink.** ⚠️ **Do not aim at the bench either, and under the IIHF clear with your stick, never your skate.**
4. **Then, and only then, look for the outlet.** Trying to make the exit pass before the danger is gone is how a scramble becomes a goal against.

The mirror image applies in their end: net front first, sticks free, and shoot from anywhere before you try to be clever. See [Risk Management](risk_management.md) for the general principle.

**What it costs.** ⚠️ **From your own zone, a puck shot or batted directly over the glass is a two-minute delay-of-game minor whether you meant it or not** under the NHL's **Rule 63.2(iii)**, the IIHF's **63.2(III)** and the PWHL's **65.2(iii)**. The carve-outs are narrow: boards with no glass above them, a puck shot straight into the bench, and one that leaves directly off a faceoff — but never aim a draw out, because the deliberate limb reaches that.

- **The bench is no exception.** The NHL and PWHL penalise a clear over the glass *"behind"* the bench and the IIHF over the glass *"surrounding"* it. The IIHF's Situation Handbook still calls the minor on a clear that lands in the bench; only a puck shot *"directly into the Players' Bench (where there is no glass)"* escapes (Situation 63.24).
- **Under the IIHF a skate clear counts.** Its 63.2(III) names only *"their hand or stick"*, but its Situation Handbook answers *"Yes"* to the minor for a kicked puck over the glass (Situation 63.34, and 63.19 in its current editions), and the deliberate limb, 63.2(II), names the foot outright.
- **A deliberate one is a minor from anywhere on the ice in all six books** — NHL 63.2(ii), IIHF 63.2(II), USA Hockey 610(c), Hockey Canada 10.1(ii), PWHL 65.2(ii) and CARHA 75(b).
- **After the whistle it can cost more.** USA Hockey climbs to a misconduct (601(c)(10)), or a game misconduct if done in protest of a call (601(d)(7)) or toward an official or a spectator (601(d)(9)). Hockey Canada prices it as unsportsmanlike conduct under Rule 11.1, where the referee may start at a misconduct.
- **USA Hockey, Hockey Canada and CARHA write no whether-you-meant-it limb for a skater.** USA Hockey makes an accidental one a last-play faceoff (631(d)); Hockey Canada 10.1(v) and CARHA Rule 55 reach the goalie alone.
- **A stick lift at or near the hands** is hooking under the IIHF on location alone (**Rule 55.1**), and under **USA Hockey 623** and **Hockey Canada 8.2(a), Interpretation 1** where it stops the opponent playing the puck. The NHL, the PWHL and CARHA reach a shaft used *"above the upper hand to hold or hook an opponent"* — a minor under NHL 55.2 and PWHL 56.2, and a double minor at the referee's discretion under CARHA. [Body Contact and Battles → Stick Checks](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md#3-stick-checks--legal-effective-and-under-used) carries the wording and the IIHF sentence that pulls against 55.1.

[Risk Management](risk_management.md) carries the full delay-of-game ladder and every IIHF Handbook situation on it.

## State 4 — Transition, possession undecided

Possession has just changed, or is in the act of changing, and neither team has organised. This is the most chaotic state, and the highest-leverage one, because **defensive structure takes several seconds to form and your opportunity exists only inside those seconds.**

### Why it matters so much

```facts
Key: Every organised defensive structure is designed and rehearsed, and against a set structure your options are limited
Read: In the two or three seconds after a turnover that structure does not exist yet — players facing the wrong way, defencemen up ice, forwards behind the puck
Key: A chance created in that window is created against no structure at all — and the window closes as the structure forms
Risk: The transition window after a turnover works against you the same way — two seconds of your own disorganisation is where you concede
```

Every organised defensive structure — a defensive-zone box or collapse, a neutral zone trap, a set forecheck — is designed and rehearsed. Against a set structure your options are limited. In the two or three seconds after a turnover, that structure does not exist yet. Players are facing the wrong way, defencemen are up ice, and forwards are behind the puck. A chance created in this window is created against no structure at all, which is why it is worth sprinting for — and why it closes as fast as it opened.

The same is true against you. Two seconds of your own disorganisation is where you concede.

### What to do off the puck in transition

```facts
Action: When you have just won it, turn and go immediately — start moving on the likelihood of possession, not the certainty of it
Risk: Skating hard in the wrong direction is a survivable error; being late means the window is gone
Action: Get width and get depth in the same instant — if everyone does the same one, the breakout dies
Never: All go — transition is exactly where the "one more forward joins the rush than should have" goal against comes from
Read: When you have just lost it, find the most dangerous man, not the puck — usually the man arriving late into the middle rather than the carrier; that ranking is coaching emphasis, not a counted one
Never: Everyone sprint to the middle — two wingers and a centre converging there leaves both outside attackers unmarked
Convention: Backcheck lane assignment is a coaching choice rather than a law — the standard one used throughout is centre in the middle lane, wingers on the outside; others use first man back takes the middle, or man-on-man from the puck outward
Action: Count — know whether it is a 2-on-1, a 3-on-2 or a 3-on-3, because the correct behaviour differs completely
```

**When your team has just won it:**

- **Turn and go immediately.** The single biggest transition error is the half-second of hesitation while you confirm your team actually has it. Start moving on the *likelihood* of possession, not the certainty of it. If you are wrong, you have skated hard in the wrong direction — a survivable error. If you are late, the window is gone.
- **Get width and get depth in the same instant.** Somebody has to stretch the ice so the defence has to retreat, and somebody has to be close for the short outlet. If everyone does the same one, the breakout dies. See [Breakouts](../systems/breakouts.md).
- **Do not all go.** Transition is exactly where the "one more forward joins the rush than should have" goal against comes from. Read [Risk Management](risk_management.md).

**When your team has just lost it:**

- **Find the most dangerous man, not the puck.** In transition the puck carrier is usually not the threat — the man arriving late into the middle is. **That the man arriving late into the middle is more dangerous than the carrier is coaching emphasis rather than a counted ranking**; [Defending the Rush](../systems/defending_the_rush.md) owns it and states the limit with it.
- **Sprint back on your lane, and make sure somebody has the middle.** Under the standard assignment used throughout — a coaching choice rather than a law — **the centre takes the middle lane and the two wingers take the outside lanes**. So "everyone sprint to the middle" is exactly wrong: two wingers and a centre converging on the middle leaves both outside attackers unmarked. What is always true is that *somebody* owns the middle and nobody assumes it is somebody else — if you are back first and the middle is empty, it is yours. Some teams formalise that as **first man back takes the middle**; others pick up **man-on-man from the puck outward**. [Defending the Rush](../systems/defending_the_rush.md) sets them out, along with the four named team backchecking systems that decide which assignment applies.
- **Count.** Your only real job while backchecking is to know whether it is a 2-on-1, a 3-on-2 or a 3-on-3, because the correct behaviour differs completely. [Defending the Rush](../systems/defending_the_rush.md) covers this in full.

![](diagram:rush-backcheck-lanes)

### Recognising it first

```facts
Key: Transition is won by whoever recognises the change of possession earliest, and recognition is a trainable habit
Action: Get your head up before the turnover happens, so you already know where the space is when it does
Risk: If you find out about turnovers by seeing the puck go the other way, you will always be second
```

Transition is won by whoever recognises the change of possession earliest, and recognition is a trainable habit — it is the payoff for scanning. If your head is up before the turnover happens, you already know where the space is when it does. If you find out about turnovers by seeing the puck go the other way, you will always be second.

## Getting open: the mechanics of separating from a check

Now the detail on State 1's most obvious job.

### Unmarked is not the same as available

```facts
Key: Unmarked means no defender is near you; available means the carrier can actually get you the puck — the second is the one that matters
Technique: Stick on the ice, blade flat, in a position to receive — the blade is your target, so give the passer something to hit
Position: Give a straight passing angle from the puck to your blade; if the carrier would have to bend the pass around a body, you are not available
Read: Look for a defender's stick lying flat across the only lane — nothing about your own position tells you it is there, and it leaves you unmarked and unavailable
Action: When you get open, look at the passing lane, not at your defender. If the lane is blocked, move three or four feet to open the angle
Action: Say something — a short call gives the carrier information their eyes may not have
```

This distinction is the whole section, so it comes first.

**Unmarked** means no defender is near you. **Available** means the puck carrier can actually get you the puck. They are very different, and the second is the one that matters. To be available you need all three of:

1. **A stick on the ice, blade flat, in a position to receive.** A stick held up in the air is a stick that cannot take a pass. The blade is your target — give the passer something to hit.
2. **A passing angle.** A straight line from the puck to your blade. If the carrier would need to bend the pass around a body, you are not available no matter how alone you are.
3. **No defender's stick in that lane.** Nothing about your own position tells you whether this one is true. You are ten feet from anyone, waving, and there is a defenceman's stick lying flat across the only line between you and the puck. You are unmarked and unavailable.

![](diagram:unmarked-but-unavailable)

The practical consequence: **when you get open, immediately look at the passing lane, not at your defender.** If the lane is blocked, move — often only three or four feet, changing the angle enough that the line opens. A small adjustment that opens the lane is worth more than a big burst that does not.

And say something. A short call gives the carrier the information their eyes may not have.

### Change of speed

```facts
Key: Defenders match speed but struggle to match changes of speed, because reacting costs them a beat
Action: Slow down, then explode — coasting for a stride invites the defender to close and settle, and the moment they settle you go
Never: Arrive at your top speed with nowhere to go — speed is only useful if it creates separation at the moment the pass comes
Technique: Stopping is a change of speed too — a hard stop while your check keeps going creates separation and leaves you facing the play
Convention: That a change of speed beats top speed is coaching craft, not a measured finding — the IIHF's coach-development manual and Cutting Edge Ice Academy both teach it, and neither measures it
```

Defenders match speed. They struggle to match *changes* in speed, because reacting costs them a beat. **That a change of speed beats top speed is coaching craft, not a measured finding** — the IIHF's coach-development manual and Cutting Edge Ice Academy both teach it, and neither measures it. See [Time and Space](time_and_space.md).

- **Slow down, then explode.** Coasting for a stride invites the defender to close and settle. The moment they settle, go. It runs against instinct, because slowing down feels like laziness.
- **Never arrive at your top speed with nowhere to go.** Speed is only useful if it creates separation at the moment the pass comes.
- **Stopping is a change of speed too.** A hard stop while your check keeps going creates as much separation as any burst, and it leaves you facing the play.

### Change of direction

```facts
Technique: Two steps one way, then hard the other — the defender's weight has to transfer before they can follow
Read: Attack their hips — break in the direction their hips are not facing
Options: Curl rather than stop-start when you want to keep options; a full stop makes you a stationary target and costs three strides to restart
Action: Come back toward the puck — you arrive with speed the defender cannot match from a standstill, and you shorten the pass
```

- **Two steps one way, then hard the other.** The defender's weight has to transfer before they can follow, and edge-work costs them more time than it costs you because you knew it was coming.
- **Attack their hips.** Break in the direction their hips are *not* facing. A defender whose hips are square to the boards cannot easily follow you to the middle.
- **Curl rather than stop-start when you want to keep options.** A tight turn keeps your feet moving and your head up; a full stop makes you a stationary target and costs you three strides to restart.
- **Come back toward the puck.** Amateurs often skate away from the carrier and hope. A player skating *toward* the puck arrives with speed the defender cannot match from a standstill, shortens the pass, and gives the carrier a target moving into space rather than away from it.

### Screens and picks — what is actually legal

**In short:** Hold the ice you already have and never step sideways into a player without the puck; that is a pick, and a penalty.

```facts
Rule: You are allowed the ice you are standing on and are not required to move in order to let an opponent proceed (NHL Rule 56.1 and IIHF Rule 56.1, Interference — the IIHF writes it in the plural, so it is the British rule too)
Rule: You may block an opponent's path provided you are in front of them and moving in the same direction (NHL Rule 56.1 and IIHF Rule 56.1, Interference, in the same terms)
Rule: Moving laterally without establishing body position and then making contact with a non-puck carrier is penalised as interference — NHL Rule 56.1 and IIHF Rule 56.1 write that sentence in the same words, so it is the British rule too
Rule: A pick — moving into an opponent's path without having body position — is an interference minor (NHL 56), and it is not capped there: NHL 56.4 and PWHL 57.4 allow a major on the degree of violence, and 56.5 and 57.5 add a game misconduct where it injures
Rule: Above the minor, IIHF 56.5 reaches a major plus a game misconduct where it recklessly endangers, Hockey Canada 8.3(b) on the degree of violence with nobody hurt, and CARHA 66(a) at the referee's discretion — both mandatory on injury (8.3(b), CARHA 66(e))
Rule: In USA Hockey non-check play — 12U and below, girls' and women's, non-check adult, or any other ban — a pick that lands as a body check is a 604(c) minor; if reckless, a mandatory major plus game misconduct (604(d)), and plan on a match (602(a), 604(e)) and a likely multi-game suspension
Rule: Where USA Hockey allows checking, Rule 625 stops at the minor, but a reckless pick reaches 640(g)'s major plus game misconduct through 640(d) (a check with no play on the puck, blade above the knees) and 602(a)'s match penalty, with a likely multi-game ban
Rule: Under NHL and IIHF Rule 56.1 you are always entitled to use body position to lengthen an opponent's path to the puck, without your stick, your free hand or an otherwise illegal check — Hockey Canada and CARHA write no such right
Rule: Hockey Canada and CARHA write no right to lengthen an opponent's path — both make impeding one not in possession interference (8.3; 66(a)), CARHA's Note 2 lets only defenders "stand their ground", and its 49(a) penalises failing to avert body contact
```

Using a teammate's body to separate from your check is legal within limits, and the limits are precise. The NHL rulebook (Rule 56, Interference) defines them, **and the IIHF's Rule 56.1 prints the same paragraph, in the plural, so a British reader is under these limits too.** The quotations below are the NHL's wording.

**USA Hockey writes the same permission in its Preface:** a skater *"is entitled to stand their ground and is not required to move if an opponent wishes to skate through that area of the ice"*, and *"may block an opponent so long as they are in front of the opponent and moving in the same direction"*. Its Rule 625 Note defines interference as impeding a non-puck carrier *"with no effort to play the puck, maintain normal foot speed or maintain an established skating lane"* — so keeping your lane and your foot speed is not what the rule is about.

**Body position** is defined as "the player skating in front of or beside his opponent, traveling in the same direction." From that definition follow the rules that matter to you:

- **You are allowed the ice you are standing on.** NHL Rule 56.1, and IIHF Rule 56.1 in the plural, state that a player "is allowed the ice he is standing on (body position) and is not required to move in order to let an opponent proceed." Simply being somewhere is not a penalty, even if it is inconvenient for an opponent.
- **You may block a path if you are in front and moving the same direction.** NHL and IIHF Rule 56.1 again: "A player may 'block' the path of an opponent provided he is in front of his opponent and moving in the same direction."
- **You may not step laterally into someone.** NHL and IIHF Rule 56.1 write this sentence in the same words: "Moving laterally and without establishing body position, then making contact with the non-puck carrier is not permitted and will be penalized as interference." This is the line most illegal picks cross.
- **A "pick" is a penalty.** NHL Rule 56.1 defines it as checking an opponent who is not in possession and is unaware of the impending hit — specifically "one who moves into an opponent's path without initially having body position, thereby taking him out of the play." The IIHF's 56.1 defines a *"pick"* in the same terms. It is an interference minor — ⚠️ **and not the ceiling: a violent, reckless or injuring pick can end your game in every book.** *What it costs*, below, sets out each one.
- **You may lengthen an opponent's path with your body.** NHL and IIHF Rule 56.1: "A player is always entitled to use his body position to lengthen an opponent's path to the puck," provided they do not use their stick to make themselves bigger, do not use their free hand, and do not use the position to deliver an otherwise illegal check. USA Hockey's Preface grants the same, to a skater keeping their own lane. **Not under Hockey Canada or CARHA:** neither writes that right, both make impeding a player without the puck interference (Hockey Canada 8.3, CARHA 66(a)), and CARHA 49(a) also reaches a player who *"does not avert body contact with an opponent"* — so there, hold only the ice you already have.
- **A stick used to impede without body position is hooking, not interference.** Different penalty, same underlying idea — NHL and IIHF Rule 56.1 both make it a hooking penalty instead.

**What it costs.** A pick is an interference minor everywhere, and every book reaches higher:

- **NHL and PWHL:** a major on the degree of violence (56.4, 57.4), and a game misconduct that is mandatory where that major injures (56.5, 57.5).
- **IIHF:** **56.5** reaches a major plus a game misconduct where it recklessly endangers, with nobody hurt.
- **Hockey Canada:** **8.3(b)** reaches a major and game misconduct on the degree of violence with nobody hurt, and makes it mandatory on injury.
- **CARHA:** **66(a)** lets the referee go straight to a major and a game misconduct, and **66(e)** makes the major mandatory where it injures. Either major ends your game and at least your next one (30(a), 32(d)).
- **USA Hockey where checking is barred** — 12U and below, all girls'/women's and non-check adult classifications, a game against a non-check team, or a local ban (**604(a)** and **(b)**): a pick that lands as a body check is a **604(c)** minor. **604(d)** reads *"A major plus a game misconduct penalty shall be assessed to any player who recklessly endangers an opponent with a body check in a Competitive Contact category"*, with **604(e)** reaching a match penalty, and a likely multi-game suspension, for that same reckless check.
- **USA Hockey where checking is allowed:** Rule 625 writes no tier above the minor, but it still reaches one from outside. **Rule 602(a)** is the clean fit — a match penalty for a player who *"recklessly endangers or attempts to injure any opposing player"*, tied to no named foul. **Rule 640(g)** is narrower: a major plus game misconduct for recklessly endangering through 640(b)–(f), and its nearest fit for a pick, **640(d)**, is a body check *"with no effort to gain possession of the puck and the blade of the player's stick is above the knees"*. Plan on a match and a likely multi-game suspension if the pick is reckless.

Do not read "no tier inside 625" as "two minutes or nothing".

### Running a legal route through traffic

**In short:** Run close past a teammate who is doing their own job so your check must go around, never to delay whoever pressures your carrier.

```facts
Action: Run close by a teammate so your check goes around, unless the check pressures your carrier; in Hockey Canada or CARHA, never past one parked to catch your check. Past them, shout, not into their spot
Key: What makes that route legal is who it delays — it must not be the player pressuring your own puck carrier, and "they were my check" is a rule of thumb rather than the test
Rule: No-contact screening of a forechecker off your puck carrier is named in three books, a minor in two — USA Hockey 625(a), "Providing a protective screen and limiting the opportunity for an opposing player to apply pressure to a teammate in possession or control of the puck", and Hockey Canada 8.3(a)
Rule: Screening a forechecker off your own puck carrier, even without contact, is not capped at a minor in a CARHA-affiliated adult league — CARHA Rule 66(a) names it in Note 2 and reads "A Minor penalty for interference or, at the discretion of the Referee, a Major penalty and a Game Misconduct penalty"
Rule: The NHL, IIHF and PWHL do not name the no-contact screen, but their 56.2 (PWHL 57.2) reaches restraining a forechecker and impeding anyone without the puck — only the right to ice you already stand on keeps a standing screen legal, not sliding across to keep it
Rule: Hockey Canada and CARHA give the side with the puck no right to its ice — both make impeding any opponent not in possession interference (8.3; 66(a)), and CARHA 66(a) Note 2 lets only defenders "stand their ground"
Rule: The NHL and IIHF differ here, and the IIHF's is the British one — its 56.1 opens with a general definition of interference the NHL has no counterpart to, and its 56.2 minor "shall be assessed" where the NHL's "can be imposed"
Never: Stop in a defender's path and take the contact, or drift sideways into a chaser
Key: "Screen" here means the goalie's sightline and nothing else — NHL Rule 56's words for skater-on-skater work are body position, blocking, picks and interference, so call yours traffic, or a route
Convention: Obstruction is called far less consistently at amateur level than in the NHL, in both directions — do not build your game on getting away with picks, and do not assume the referee will punish theirs
```

The practical, legal version of this for a player trying to get open: **run your route close past a teammate so that your check has to go around them.** You are not hitting anybody, your teammate is not moving into anybody, and the defender simply loses a stride navigating traffic. That is a **legal route through traffic**, and it is available on almost every faceoff and every net drive — **provided the man you delay is not the one pressuring your own puck carrier.** *"They were my check"* is a serviceable rule of thumb, but it is not the test: the books that name the offence key it to the teammate being protected rather than to whose check the delayed man is, and in your own end your check is very often the forechecker. **Under Hockey Canada and CARHA your teammate has no written right to that ice:** both make impeding any opponent who is not in possession of the puck interference (Hockey Canada 8.3, CARHA 66(a)), and CARHA's Note 2 gives the right to *"stand their ground"* to the defending side only. **So there, run past a teammate who is doing their own job, never one parked to catch your check.**

![](diagram:legal-route-through-traffic)

**Taking the forechecker off your own puck carrier is a different act from shaking your own check, and three books name it.** **USA Hockey Rule 625(a)** lists among the actions penalised as interference *"Providing a protective screen and limiting the opportunity for an opposing player to apply pressure to a teammate in possession or control of the puck."* **Hockey Canada Rule 8.3** says players of the team in possession *"may not run deliberate interference for the puck carrier"*. **CARHA** says the same in the Note 2 its Rule 66(a) hangs off — *"Players of the side in possession of the puck shall not be allowed to 'run' deliberate interference for the puck carrier"*. Hockey Canada's book never defines *running* interference and no Interpretation construes it, so read the rule yourself rather than leaning on any scope drawn for it here. So get open for your carrier instead.

**What it costs.** USA Hockey and Hockey Canada take a screen made with no contact no higher than a minor (625(a); 8.3(a)). Rule 625 writes no higher tier, USA Hockey's summary of penalties lists 625(a) under minors alone, and its routes above it (640(g), 640(h)) reach only body checks and contact after the whistle; Hockey Canada's 8.3(b) and 8.3(c) turn on the violence of the impact, injury or an attempt to injure. ⚠️ **CARHA's ceiling is not the minor:** 66(a) reads *"A Minor penalty for interference or, at the discretion of the Referee, a Major penalty and a Game Misconduct penalty"*.

**The NHL, IIHF and PWHL do not name the no-contact screen, and that is not the same as permitting it.** Their interference minor reaches restraining *"an opponent who is attempting to 'forecheck'"* (NHL and IIHF 56.2(ii), PWHL 57.2) by illegal means such as a hook, a hold or a trip, and its first limb reaches *"a player who interferes with or impedes the progress of an opponent who is not in possession of the puck"*. What keeps a standing screen legal is only 56.1's permission (57.1 in the PWHL): a player *"is allowed the ice he is standing on (body position) and is not required to move in order to let an opponent proceed."*

So the permission covers the ice you already occupy, not moving to keep occupying it. ⚠️ **Get to the spot before the forechecker does and stand still; sliding across to stay in front of them is interference.** The same paragraph penalises *"[m]oving laterally and without establishing body position, then making contact with the non-puck carrier"*, and its permission to *"block"* a path needs you *"in front of his opponent and moving in the same direction"*, which a player facing an oncoming forechecker is not. 56.1 is not tidy here either — it defines *"body position"* as skating *"in the same direction"*, then calls *"the ice he is standing on"* body position too — so a standing screen is not a margin to argue from.

**And the NHL and the IIHF are not one book here. The IIHF is the stricter, and the IIHF's is the British book.** Two differences. **IIHF Rule 56.1 opens with a general definition of the offence that the NHL writes nowhere**: *"A Player who obstructs or prevents an opponent 'without possession of the puck' from skating, receiving a pass, or moving about the ice freely is considered as 'interference'."* And the modal differs — the NHL's 56.2 says a minor *"can be imposed"*, the IIHF's that one *"shall be assessed"*. ⚠️ **So a referee who judges your screen to have impeded a forechecker has discretion under the NHL book and, on the wording, none under the IIHF's.** Find out which book your league plays under before you run this anywhere near your own puck carrier.

**That answers the legality question and stops, and the safety question is a different one.** The collision you are engineering is a defender at chase speed into a teammate who is standing still and facing away, and who cannot see them coming. ⚠️ **Give them a shout as you come, and run *past* your teammate rather than into the space they are standing in.** **That is a coaching caution, not a rulebook requirement** — the rules above are about what the officials will call, and nothing in them asks this of you.

**A word about the word.** Throughout this guide, **screen** means one thing: standing in the *goalie's* sightline. **None of these books uses the word for legal skater-on-skater work.** The NHL's and the IIHF's vocabulary for skater-on-skater obstruction is body position, blocking, picks and interference. The NHL uses *screen* once, as the heading over its goaltender-screening table, and the PWHL's only use is that same heading. CARHA writes the word nowhere, and the one place a book writes *"protective screen"* of a skater, USA Hockey Rule 625(a), it is naming the illegal act above. **That is a point about the word and not about what is legal:** standing on ice a skater wants to cross is permitted under the NHL and IIHF books and USA Hockey's Preface, as the rules above set out. Calling your route a "screen" blurs two rules that work completely differently, so this document calls it traffic, or a route.

The illegal version is stopping in a defender's path and taking the contact, or drifting sideways into a chaser. If the defender is *unaware* of you and you *move into their path*, that is the definition of a pick.

> **Rec and beer-league note:** obstruction is called far less consistently at amateur level than in the NHL, in both directions. Do not build your game on getting away with picks, and do not assume the referee will punish theirs.

### Timing your break

```facts
Key: Arriving open too early is the same as not being open — the defender has that second to recover
Action: Watch the passer, not the space
Read: Break when the carrier's head comes up, when the puck comes to the middle of their blade or their forehand, when they have just beaten a checker, or when their shoulders turn toward you
Technique: Hold, hold, hold, then go on their look — keep moving in a small way throughout, so your check stays honest
```

Getting open is as much about *when* you go as about where.

**Arriving open too early is the same as not being open.** If you break into space a second before your teammate is ready to pass, a defender has that second to recover, and by the time the carrier looks up you are covered again. Then you stop, the play dies, and you blame the passer.

The fix is to **watch the passer, not the space.** Break when:

- The carrier's head comes up.
- The puck comes to the middle of their blade, or they move it to their forehand.
- They have just beaten a checker and have a free second.
- Their shoulders turn toward you.

Concretely: **hold, hold, hold, and then go on their look.** Holding is not passivity, because you should be moving in a small way the whole time — a curl, a drift, a change of angle — to keep your check honest. It is the *committing* burst that waits for the passer.

This is also why the pass and the break should be thought of as one action shared between two players. See [Passing and Receiving](../technique/passing_and_receiving.md).

## Occupying defenders: the work nobody claps for

Some off-puck work exists purely to move a defender somewhere unhelpful. You will never get an assist for it and it is often the thing that made the goal possible.

### Drive the net

```facts
Action: Skate hard at the net without the puck — a half-hearted glide does not move anybody
Goal: Pull a defender with you, because leaving a man driving the goalmouth is unacceptable to any defenceman
Goal: Create a rebound and deflection threat, which changes the goalie's depth and attention
Key: The drive bends the defence toward the net and opens the ice behind you — the high slot and the point
Mindset: If you are going, go as if you expect the puck
Never: Arrive into the goal frame head first. Know where the posts are, and take a post or the boards with an arm or a leg instead. Head up, chin off your chest, and never duck
Rule: A net drive never licenses touching the goalie — avoiding it is on you (Hockey Canada 8.5), and a shove excuses contact only if you made a reasonable effort to avoid it (NHL and IIHF 69.1)
Rule: Charging a goalie can end your game in every book — mandatory under Hockey Canada (8.5(b)) and, in the crease or on injury, CARHA (52(b)), with at least a one-game ban (32(d)); under USA Hockey plan on a match and a likely multi-game suspension if reckless (602(a))
```

Skating hard at the net without the puck does three things at once:

- A net drive pulls a defender with you, because leaving a man driving the goalmouth is unacceptable to any defenceman.
- A net drive creates a rebound and deflection threat, which changes the goalie's depth and attention.
- A net drive bends the defence toward the net, which opens the ice **behind** you — the high slot (dots to the top of the circles) and the point.

![](diagram:drive-the-net-before)

![](diagram:drive-the-net-after)

The point of the drive is that somebody else scores: you pull a defender to the net, and a teammate shoots into the space they vacated. No source held here counts goals by type, so take that as the mechanism rather than a ranking of which goal is commonest. **The drive has to be genuine.** A half-hearted glide does not move anybody. If you are going, go as if you expect the puck.

**One thing the drive itself asks of you.** You are skating hard at a steel goal frame, with a goalie set in front of it and a defender coming with you, so **know where the posts are, and never arrive head first.** USA Hockey's *Introduction To Body Contact* puts it as an instruction: *"Hit the boards or goal posts with an arm, a leg, or anything but your head first."* Head up, chin off your chest, and **never duck**. Its *Heads Up Hockey* guide traces almost all on-ice cervical spine injuries to a head *"slightly flexed (head down) while making head-on contact with the boards or goal post"*, and records that it *"can occur at walking speed"* — so a crowded, slow net drive is not a safe one, in any league, checking or not.

**Drive at the net, not at the goalie.** The books put the onus on you to avoid them — Hockey Canada's 8.5 says *"The onus is always on the attacking player"* — and being shoved into them excuses you only if you tried not to be. [Whether you touch the goaltender](#whether-you-touch-the-goaltender) has each book's price.

### Screen the goalie

**In short:** Stand in the goalie's sightline with both skates off the crease line, stick on the ice, and never touch the goalie.

```facts
Rule: A no-contact screen from outside the crease keeps the goal (NHL Table 14, Situations 5C and 5E; the IIHF and PWHL tables agree). Contact, or a position that stops the goalie moving freely in their crease, can cost it (69.1, PWHL 71.1)
Rule: With no contact at all, NHL and IIHF Rule 69.3 disallows the goal for a significant position in the crease — a substantial part of your body there for more than an instantaneous period — that both obstructs the goalkeeper's vision and impairs his ability to defend his goal (the NHL's words)
Rule: The IIHF's own Situation Handbook does not apply 69.3's test — asked about a player "in the crease screening the goalkeeper", its Situation 69.1 answers "the goal shall be disallowed". The Handbook ranks below the rulebook, but do not screen from inside the paint
Rule: Under the IIHF, a hand, glove or stick held directly in front of the goalie's eyes is not judged under Rule 69 at all — it is unsportsmanlike conduct and a Rule 75 minor, and only if the referee witnessed it (IIHF Situation Handbook 2025/26 and 2026/27, Situations 69.3 and 75.4)
Rule: USA Hockey 625(b) is a flat position bar with three narrow carve-outs — no goal with an attacking player in the crease, whoever scored, unless the puck got there first, the goalie is out of the crease area, or a defender's interference is what put you there
Rule: USA Hockey 625(b)'s goalie-out carve-out is not the free pass it reads as — the Casebook, Rule 625 Situation 9, disallows the goal and adds an interference minor where the goalie was returning to the crease and the attacker prevented them from making a play
Rule: Hockey Canada 8.5 is not flat — the goal stands where a teammate outside the crease scores and the crease player stays passive; its Interpretation 3 disallows it only if that player plays the puck, interferes with the play, or impedes the goalie's vision or movements
Rule: Neither book reaches a no-contact screen set from outside the crease — USA Hockey writes no goalie-vision provision at all, and Hockey Canada's two references to the goalie's view both reach only a player standing in the crease (USA Hockey 625, Hockey Canada 8.5)
Never: Treat being shoved into the paint as cover for what you do next — the relief covers your position and nothing else, and getting straight back out of the crease is still on you
Key: A screen from outside the crease without contact keeps the goal, and contact with the goalie outside the crease can cost it in every book
Rule: Rule 69.4 reaches your position too (NHL and IIHF; PWHL 71.4), for the penalty rather than the goal — an attacker whose "deliberate actions" prevent a goalie who has played the puck outside their crease from returning to it "may be penalized" for goalkeeper interference — no contact needed
```

**The job is short: stand where the goalie's eyes are, outside the blue paint with both skates off the red line around it, stick on the ice for a tip or a rebound, touching nobody — and get out of the way at the last instant.** The how is in *To screen properly*, below; first, why the paint decides it.

Standing in the goalie's line of sight is **legal from outside the crease** — standing in the sightline there is not a penalty. Whether the goal counts is a separate question, and the answer depends on the paint, on contact and on your book.

**Under the NHL, IIHF and PWHL (Rule 69; PWHL 71):**

- **Inside the crease, position alone can cost the goal.** If an attacker *"establishes a significant position within the goal crease, so as to obstruct the goalkeeper's vision and impair his ability to defend his goal,"* the goal is disallowed (69.3). The rule defines that as a body, or a substantial part of it, in the crease *"for more than an instantaneous period of time"*. ⚠️ **But do not treat that as room to work in:** the IIHF's own Situation Handbook simply disallows a goal scored with a player *"in the crease screening the goalkeeper"* (Situation 69.1), and the official in front of you may be working from that answer.
- **Any contact you start with a goalie in the crease costs the goal** (69.3), save incidental contact in a rebound or scramble you are both going for (69.7).
- **Outside the crease and touching nobody, the goal stands.** The reference tables (NHL, IIHF and PWHL Table 14, the IIHF's Table 16 in 2025/26; Situations 5C and 5E) print *"Goal is allowed."*, even for a screener who impairs the goalie's ability to defend. What costs the goal is contact, or a position that stops the goalie moving freely in their crease (69.1); contact outside the crease, other than incidental, also takes it off the board (69.4).
- **Get out of the goalie's way back to the crease.** A goalie who has played the puck outside the crease and is kept from returning by an attacker's *"deliberate actions"* is interfered with, and that attacker *"may be penalized"* — ⚠️ **no contact needed** (69.4, PWHL 71.4).

**Work off the red line, not merely out of the blue paint.** The books disagree about the line. IIHF Rule 1.7 (*"The marked line belongs to the Goal Crease"*), USA Hockey's Note to 625(b) and the Hockey Canada and CARHA Glossaries count it as crease. The NHL, PWHL and IIHF reference tables allow a goal to an attacker planted *"on the crease line"*, so the IIHF answers the question both ways. Off the line holds in every book.

**USA Hockey, Hockey Canada and CARHA answer differently, so do not carry the above into a game under any of them.**

- **None of the three has a rule about screening a goalie's vision from outside the crease.** ⚠️ **But contact with the goalie outside the crease costs the goal in all three.** Hockey Canada disallows any goal where an attacker starts contact with the goalie *"regardless of whether the contact occurs inside or outside of the goal crease"* (8.5, Interpretation 1); CARHA disallows it where the attacker *"has physically interfered with the goalkeeper"* (66(b)); USA Hockey disallows a goal scored while an attacker was *"interfering with the goalkeeper"* (617(c)(4)).
- **USA Hockey is flat inside the paint.** *"No goal may be scored with an attacking player in the goal crease unless the puck has preceded the player(s) into the goal crease or the goalkeeper is out of the goal crease area"* (625(b)) — whoever shot it. The same rule moves the faceoff to the neutral zone *"any time an attacking player stands, holds their stick, or skates through the goal crease"* while your team has the puck in the zone and the goalie is in contact with the crease. Keep your skates and stick blade out of the paint and off its line.
- **"The goalkeeper is out" is not a licence.** USA Hockey's Casebook (Rule 625, Situation 9) lets you precede the puck into a crease the goalie is *"completely out"* of — but if they are trying to get back and you stop them making a play, *"the goal must be disallowed and an interference penalty shall be assessed."* ⚠️ **Never stand between a scrambling goalie and their net.**
- **Hockey Canada is not flat.** A passive player in the crease does not cost the goal when a teammate outside it scores, unless that player plays the puck, interferes with play or blocks the goalie's view or movements (8.5, Interpretation 3). No penalty follows without *"actual physical contact"*.
- **CARHA is flat like USA Hockey's** (66(b)), with the same relief for a player shoved in.
- **Shoved into the paint?** In USA Hockey and CARHA that does not take the goal off the board — ⚠️ **but get out anyway**, because the relief covers your position, not what you do next. Under USA Hockey, **625(a)(8)** is a minor for contact that interferes with the goalie's movement, and **607(d) Note 1** makes a deliberate hit charging.

The instruction does not change with the book; only the reason does.

**A hand, a glove or a stick held in front of the goalie's eyes is not a screen.** Under the IIHF it is unsportsmanlike conduct and a Rule 75 minor *"if witnessed by the Referee(s)"* (Situation Handbook, Situations 69.3 and 75.4, 2025/26 and 2026/27). ⚠️ **Screen with your body and your position. Waving something across their mask is two minutes.**

#### To screen properly

```facts
Position: Stand where the goalie's eyes are, not where the puck is going. Occupy the sightline between the puck and the goalie
Position: Keep both skates off the red crease line, not merely out of the blue paint — the crease edge is where the books split, and off the line is the answer that holds under all of them
Rule: IIHF Rule 1.7, the Note to USA Hockey 625(b) and the Hockey Canada and CARHA Glossaries all count the red crease line as crease, but the NHL, PWHL and IIHF reference tables all allow a goal on the line — the IIHF answers the same play in two directions, so keep both skates off the line
Never: Put a hand, a glove or your blade across the goalie's face — under the IIHF that is not a screen at all but a minor for unsportsmanlike conduct
Action: Keep your stick on the ice — a screen is a tip threat and a rebound threat, not a place to stand and watch
Technique: Move late — getting out of the way at the last instant, so the goalie picks the puck up a fraction too late, is more disruptive than a static body. Move where you can still see the puck
Technique: A defender you feel, not watch, moves you off a screen, so know where the near post is before the shot and take the shove braced — wide base, knees and hips giving — never against the goalie
Never: Meet a goal post head first, and never duck. Head up, chin off your chest instead. A head-down, head-first hit on a goal post is the same injury as one on the boards, and can happen at walking speed
Risk: A screen stands you in the shot lane at close range, upright, with a defender on your back and your eyes on the puck. No injury rate for screening is published in any source held here
```

- **Stand where the goalie's eyes are, not where the puck is going.** Your job is to occupy the sightline between the puck and the goalie.
- **Never put a hand, a glove or your blade across the goalie's face.** That is not a screen — under the IIHF it is a Rule 75 minor for unsportsmanlike conduct, judged outside Rule 69 altogether.
- **Keep both skates off the red crease line, not merely out of the blue paint.** Outside the crease and touching nobody, the screen is legal — but the crease *edge* is where the books disagree. Every book that settles the question in its rule text or glossary puts the red line inside the crease. Those are **IIHF Rule 1.7** (*"The marked line belongs to the Goal Crease"*), **USA Hockey's Note to 625(b)** (*"including crease lines"*), **Hockey Canada's Glossary** (*"The lines of the crease are considered part of the crease"*) and **CARHA's Glossary** (*"The lines which designate these areas are to be considered as part of the creases"*). ⚠️ **The NHL's rule text leaves it unsaid — but its Reference Table 14 answers it the other way**, allowing the goal to an attacker planted *"on the crease line"*, **and the IIHF's Appendix IV and the PWHL's own Table 14 print that same row, so the IIHF answers the question in both directions.** Inside the crease your position alone can cost the goal in every book, and off the line is the reading that holds whichever way the IIHF's is resolved. Work off the red line.
- **Do not stand still and watch.** A screen is also a tip threat and a rebound threat. Keep your stick on the ice.
- **Move late — and move where you can still see the puck.** Getting out of the way at the last instant, so the goalie picks up the puck a fraction too late, is more disruptive than a static body. Late is a timing instruction, not a reason to lose the shot: you cannot time an exit from a release you did not see.
- **Know where the near post is before the shot.** A defender is working under your arms to walk you off the spot, and with your eyes on the puck by design you can travel several feet without looking up. **Take the shove braced** — wide base, knees bent, weight low, knees and hips giving rather than a locked frame — and keep your **head up and your chin off your chest. Never duck.** *Heads Up Hockey* traces almost all on-ice cervical spine injuries to a head-down, head-on hit on *"the boards or goal post"*, and says it *"can occur at walking speed"*; its *Introduction To Body Contact* says *"Hit the boards or goal posts with an arm, a leg, or anything but your head first."* **None of this is scoped to checking leagues** — a box-out or a shove puts the same body in the same place.
- **The one body you must not take braced is the goalie's.** Give ground the moment a goalie leans into you, then re-set the screen. [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md) covers the net-front battle from both sides.
- **You are standing in the shot lane, upright — that is the job, and it is worth naming.** A shot blocker is coached to get close, stay square and keep their head out of the lane; a screener stands tall in it by design, with a defender on their back. A tip attempt also puts a blade in the lane a few feet from the shooter, and a puck off a blade can go up into your face — coaching craft, not a sourced finding. Tipping is a real skill and none of this is a reason to stop. It is a reason to know when the puck is released, and to have your face protection right rather than nearly right ([Equipment](../off-the-ice/equipment.md)). **No injury rate for screening or tipping appears in any source held for this guide**, so take this as geometry rather than a measured risk.

#### Whether you touch the goaltender

```facts
Priority: Where you stand and whether you touch the goalie are separate questions with different answers — clearing the blue paint settles only the first
Rule: Outside the crease, NHL and IIHF 69.4 and PWHL 71.4 penalise unnecessary contact, permit incidental only while the goalie plays the puck, after a reasonable effort to avoid it, or in a rebound or scramble you both play (69.7, 71.7); USA Hockey 607(d) Note 1 sends accidental contact to interference
Rule: Hockey Canada 8.5 requires an effort to avoid a goalie always, anywhere, and ejects if a foul on one injures (8.5(b)); CARHA penalises unnecessary contact (52(b) Note), impeding or deliberate contact anywhere (66(b)), a major at the referee's call, mandatory on injury (66(e)); a major ejects (30(a))
Rule: NHL and IIHF 69.3 and PWHL 71.3 disallow the goal for any contact you initiate with a goalie in their crease, incidental or otherwise, save incidental contact in a rebound or loose-puck scramble (69.7, PWHL 71.7) — and USA Hockey 607(d) Note 1 files even accidental contact under interference
Rule: NHL and IIHF Rule 69.7 and PWHL 71.7 disallow the goal where a goalie is pushed into the net together with the puck by an attacking player after making a stop, and appropriate penalties may follow as well — the rule needs all three of those conditions
Rule: NHL and IIHF Rule 69.7 and PWHL 71.7 let the referee allow the goal where a defender pushed or fouled you into pushing the goalie into the net with the puck — but 69.1 excuses shoved contact only if you made a reasonable effort to avoid it
Rule: Where the puck is under a player in or around the crease, deliberately or otherwise, NHL and IIHF Rule 69.7 and PWHL 71.7 allow no goal from pushing that player and the puck into the net — penalties follow if applicable, and a penalty shot if it was covered deliberately
Rule: Deliberate contact with a goalie, in or out of the crease, is at least a minor in every book (NHL and IIHF 69.2, PWHL 71.2, Hockey Canada 8.5(a), CARHA 66(b)); USA Hockey files it as charging, never a bare minor (607(d) Note 1)
Rule: Charging a goalie can end your game in every book — mandatory under Hockey Canada (8.5(b)) and, in the crease or on injury, CARHA (52(b)); under USA Hockey a reckless one is a mandatory major and game misconduct (607(b)): plan on a match and a likely multi-game suspension (602(a))
Never: Initiate contact with the goalie, inside or outside the crease — a screen never needs to touch anybody at all, and being outside the paint changes nothing about that
Key: "Outside the crease" is an answer about where you stand and no answer at all about whom you touch — on contact the books converge where on position they diverge
```

**Where you stand is one question and whether you touch the goalie is another: "outside the crease" is not a licence to bump them.** NHL and IIHF Rule 69.4 and PWHL 71.4 open with the restriction — a goalie *"is not 'fair game' just because he is outside the goal crease"* — and allow incidental contact only *"when the goalkeeper is in the act of playing the puck outside his goal crease"*, after a reasonable effort to avoid it. **So a goalie who has come out to challenge, or is set outside the paint with the puck elsewhere, is not covered at all.** ⚠️ **The other books do not hand it to you either.** **Hockey Canada Rule 8.5** says attackers *"must make an effort to avoid contact in all circumstances"*, anywhere on the ice. CARHA's Note to 52(b) says a goalie is not fair game outside the crease, and **Rule 66(b)** makes deliberate contact with one *"whether in or out of the goal crease"* a mandatory minor, or a major at the referee's discretion — which ends your game and costs at least your next one (30(a), 32(d)).

**USA Hockey Rule 607(c)** makes body checking or charging a goalie in the crease or **privileged area** a minor plus misconduct or a major plus game misconduct. The privileged area runs from the end-zone faceoff spots back to the end boards, so it takes in the ice behind the net but not the corners — and a screener in front of the net is inside it. **Rule 607(b)** makes the major and game misconduct mandatory once a charge recklessly endangers, and **607(d) Note 1** files accidental contact as interference and any deliberate contact as charging, so a deliberate bump is never a bare minor. ⚠️ **The privileged area is where the automatic penalty lives, not where the protection stops:** 607(d) and the Casebook's **Rule 607, Situation 5** both call for a penalty for unnecessary or avoidable contact *"even when outside the privileged area."*

**Situation 5 also says a goalie *"can be legally checked"* outside the privileged area** — ⚠️ **do not take that half until you know you are in a checking game.** USA Hockey bars checking at 12U and below, in all girls'/women's and non-check adult classifications (604(a)), and whenever a checking team plays a non-checking one (604(b)) — so it depends on tonight's fixture. In a non-checking game keep off the goalie altogether: 604(c)'s Note on competitive contact never says it reaches a goalie, and 607(d) Note 1 still files deliberate contact as charging. In a checking game, Situation 5's one permission is keyed to the puck: you may *"physically engage with the goalkeeper in an effort to gain possession of the puck"* only *"When the goalkeeper has possession of the puck"*. A check meant to intimidate or punish is still charging, and once they lose control any avoidable contact is penalised. **The common ground is the instruction: a goalie who is not dealing with the puck is not to be contacted, and a screen never needs to touch them at all.**

**And inside the crease there is no conditional permission at all — not in the NHL's book, the IIHF's or the PWHL's.** **Rule 69.3** (PWHL 71.3) disallows the goal for contact *"with a goalkeeper, incidental or otherwise, while the goalkeeper is in his goal crease"* — the IIHF writes *"a relevant contact"*. The one exception is **Rule 69.7** (PWHL 71.7): incidental contact is allowed *"In a rebound situation, or where a goalkeeper and attacking player(s) are simultaneously attempting to play a loose puck, whether inside or outside the crease"*. ⚠️ **That exception is a scramble you are both going for, not a screen.** A screener planted in the paint who bumps the goalie gets no help from it.

**Rule 69.7 has three more limbs, in the same terms in all three books.** It disallows the goal where a goalie *"has been pushed into the net together with the puck by an attacking player after making a stop"*, and penalties may follow as well. ⚠️ **That needs all three conditions** — into the net with the puck, after a stop, pushed by an attacker — so not every push on a goalie kills a goal. ⚠️ **It runs the other way too:** where a defender pushed or fouled you into the goalie, *"the goal can be permitted"* — **provided you made a reasonable effort to avoid the goalie** (69.1; CARHA's 66(b) gives a minor to a fouled attacker who *"fails to attempt to avoid making contact"*). And where the puck is under a player in or around the crease *"(deliberately or otherwise)"*, no goal can be scored by pushing that player and the puck in — penalties follow if applicable, and a penalty shot if the puck was covered in the crease deliberately. All three books also list the push-in among disallowed goals (NHL 78.5(ix), IIHF 78.5(IX), PWHL 80.5(ix)).

**So: keep off the goalie.** Under the NHL, the IIHF and the PWHL the only contact you are ever allowed is incidental — while the goalie is playing the puck outside the crease and you made a reasonable effort to avoid it (69.4, PWHL 71.4), or in a rebound or loose-puck scramble either of you is going for (69.7, PWHL 71.7). Hockey Canada asks you to avoid contact in all circumstances, anywhere on the ice. USA Hockey files even accidental contact with a goalie under interference (607(d) Note 1). The permission its Casebook writes — engaging a goalie who has possession of the puck in a checking game — is not one a screener can use, because 607(c) covers the crease and the privileged area, and the net front is inside it. **A screen never needs to touch anybody.**

**Do not skate into the goalie — a charge on one can end your game in every book.** Charging a goalie in their crease can reach a major plus a game misconduct in all of them, and deliberate contact short of a charge can get there too. ⚠️ **Under Hockey Canada that ejection is mandatory for any charge on a goalie** (8.5(b)), and under CARHA for a charge in the crease or one that injures anybody (52(b)), where a major also costs at least your next game (30(a), 32(d)). Under USA Hockey a reckless charge is a mandatory major and game misconduct (607(b)) — ⚠️ **plan on a match and a likely multi-game suspension** (602(a)). Under the IIHF the referee can eject you for a reckless charge with nobody hurt (42.4); whether its reckless-interference ejection (56.5) also reaches a goalie-interference call under 69.2, the book does not say. A charge meant to injure is a match penalty in most books. [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md#what-it-costs-to-touch-the-goalie) sets out each book's full price.

More detail from the other side in [Goaltender](../positions/goaltender.md), and on the crease rules in [Offensive Zone Play](../systems/offensive_zone_play.md).

### Be a decoy on the weak side

```facts
Position: The weak side is the side of the ice away from the puck — stand there with your stick down and your feet moving
Goal: Force the defence to keep somebody accounting for you; a player standing on the weak side watching does not
Action: Look like you are about to receive a pass — blade down, body open to the puck, one small step toward the seam
```

The **weak side** is the side of the ice away from the puck. A player standing on the weak side with their stick down and their feet moving forces the defence to keep somebody accounting for them. A player standing on the weak side watching does not.

The difference is small and entirely about intent: **look like you are about to receive a pass.** Blade down, body open to the puck, one small step toward the seam. That is what makes a defender stay.

### Stretch the defence for width

```facts
Goal: Defences want to defend a narrow rink and collapse toward the middle — your width is what stops them
Position: As the far winger in the offensive zone, hold your position near the boards; drifting into the middle to look busy lets the defence compress
Key: On a rush, a wide forward makes the defenceman respect the outside lane, widening the gap between the pair — and that gap is where the middle drive goes
Position: In the neutral zone, a stretched forward high near the far blue line pins a defenceman deep and shortens the ice your breakout has to cover
Convention: How much width a team wants in the offensive zone is a coaching choice — some want the far winger high and wide against the counter-attack, others want them crashing the back post on every shot; ask
```

The rink is 85 feet wide. Defences want to defend a narrow rink, so they collapse toward the middle. Your width is what stops them.

![](diagram:width-makes-the-middle-lane)

- In the offensive zone, if you are the far winger, **holding your position near the boards** keeps a defender out there with you. Drifting into the middle to look busy lets the defence compress, and compressed defences are much harder to score against.
- On a rush, a wide forward forces the defenceman to respect the outside lane, which widens the gap between them and their partner — and the gap between defencemen is where the middle drive goes.
- In the neutral zone, a stretched forward high near the far blue line pins a defenceman deep and shortens the ice your breakout has to cover. See [Neutral Zone Systems](../systems/neutral_zone_systems.md).

> **System note:** how much width a team wants in the offensive zone is a coaching choice. Some coaches want the far winger high and wide to protect against the counter-attack; others want them crashing the back post on every shot. Both are defensible and they are not compatible. Ask.

## Supporting the puck carrier: the receiver's half of the pass

```facts
Key: A completed pass has two authors — if you are waiting to be found, you are doing a quarter of your job
Action: Give a target — stick on the ice, blade flat, angled to receive, something specific to aim at rather than a general area
Position: Give a lane — be open along a line the puck can travel, and adjust your position to create it rather than expecting the puck to bend
Action: Give a time — arrive as the passer is ready, not before
Technique: Arrive facing where you intend to go, and open your body up-ice before the puck arrives
Action: Give information — "time", "man on", "reverse", "over"; one word said early is worth more than a perfect route. Say "time" only when sure — a false one sends a teammate into a hit unbraced
Options: When the carrier is pinned they need a close, safe, catchable option — being the boring five-foot outlet is a real job
```

[Puck Support and Spacing](puck_support_and_spacing.md) is the full treatment of where to be. This section covers only the thing that document does not: **your responsibility, as the man without the puck, for making the pass possible.**

A completed pass has two authors. If you are waiting to be found, you are only doing a quarter of your job. The receiver owes the passer:

- **A target.** Stick on the ice, blade flat, angled to receive. Give them something specific to aim at rather than a general area.
- **A lane.** Not merely being open, but being open *along a line the puck can travel*. Adjust your position to create the line; do not expect the puck to bend.
- **A time.** Arrive as the passer is ready, not before — see "Timing your break" above.
- **A next play.** Arrive facing where you intend to go. A player who receives a pass with their back to the play has taken a good pass and turned it into a battle. Open your body up-ice before the puck arrives.
- **Information.** "Time," "man on," "reverse," "over" — one word, said early, is worth more than a perfect route. Say "time" only when you are sure: a false one sends a teammate into a hit unbraced. See [On-Ice Communication](../foundation/on_ice_communication.md).
- **A soft target when they are under pressure.** When the carrier is pinned, they do not need a spectacular option forty feet away; they need a close, safe, catchable one. Being the boring five-foot outlet is a real job.

The general principle from [Time and Space](time_and_space.md) applies here in reverse: **you can give a teammate time by where you stand.** A support option in a good place buys the carrier the extra second they need to see the whole ice.

## Effort without the puck

Everything above is about knowledge. This section is about the part that requires none.

### Backchecking

```facts
Key: Backchecking is racing back toward your own end when possession is lost; forechecking is applying pressure in the opponent's end
Never: Decide for yourself mid-rush to chase the puck carrier. Cover a lane instead — the man arriving unmarked into the middle is usually the more dangerous: coaching emphasis, not a counted ranking
Options: Some teams do coach the first forward back to pressure the carrier with the defence holding inside — where that is the system it is an instruction, not an instinct, so find out which yours runs
Rule: ⚠️ **Never finish into anyone's back** — no book prices a hit that stays on the back at a bare minor, and in five books every such call ends your game, Britain's IIHF included (43.2, 43.3); USA Hockey keeps a minor plus misconduct for a push or pinch (Casebook 608, Situation 1)
Rule: ⚠️ **Under USA Hockey a forceful check from behind on a player at the boards ejects** — major and game misconduct or match (Casebook 608, Situation 1); a reckless one is a mandatory major, game misconduct and next game (608(b), 404(b)): plan on a match and a likely multi-game suspension (602(a))
Rule: ⚠️ **Every book but the IIHF can make a check from behind a match** — mandatory into the boards or net so they can't defend themselves (Hockey Canada 7.5(c), CARHA 53(b)), and for intent to injure (NHL, PWHL 21.1; Hockey Canada 7.5(c); CARHA 48(a); USA Hockey 602(a), with a likely multi-game ban)
Rule: At a club in England or Wales, outside the Elite League, a game misconduct for checking from behind or a head check, or a boarding major, is at least a one-game ban, in every league or age group you are registered in (IHUK DOPS C.1, s.11)
Risk: ⚠️ **A check from behind is a spinal-injury mechanism, not a tariff question** — Hockey Canada's Rule 7.5 preamble tells referees to be "aware of the tragic consequences of spinal injuries"
Never: Use your stick, body or free hand to restrain an opponent from behind, whether or not they have the puck. You must skate to regain position instead
Rule: Restraining an opponent from behind is interference where they do not have the puck (NHL and IIHF Rule 56.1), and holding or hooking where they do, since 54.1 reaches an opponent "whether or not he is in possession of the puck" and 55.1 sets no possession condition
Convention: Which lane is yours is a coaching choice — the one used throughout gives the centre the middle and the wingers the outside; alternatives are first man back takes the middle and man-on-man from the puck outward. Under every one, somebody owns the middle and nobody assumes it is somebody else
Action: Backcheck to a man, not to a place. Pick up a player and communicate that you have them
Risk: Skating hard to the top of the circles and stopping, having covered nobody, spends the energy and buys nothing
```

**Backchecking** is racing back toward your own end when possession is lost. It is the opposite of **forechecking**, which is applying pressure in the opponent's end. The two words are easily confused and mean nearly opposite things.

Backchecking well is mostly about two decisions, both made while sprinting:

- **Do not chase the puck carrier. Take your lane.** Resist the instinct unless your team has told you otherwise: some teams coach the first forward back to pressure the carrier while the defence holds inside, and then it is an instruction rather than an instinct. Otherwise take the lane. The dangerous man is usually the one arriving unmarked into the middle — coaching emphasis rather than a counted ranking — and covering a lane is worth more than catching the carrier from behind.
- **Know which lane is yours — a coaching choice.** Under the standard assignment used throughout, the **centre takes the middle lane and the wingers take the outside lanes**, so a winger takes the middle only as the first man back with nobody else there. Alternatives are **first man back takes the middle** and **man-on-man from the puck outward**; [Defending the Rush](../systems/defending_the_rush.md) sets them out, with the four named team backchecking systems that decide which applies. Under every one, somebody owns the middle and nobody assumes it is somebody else.
- **Backcheck to a man, not to a place.** Pick up a player and communicate that you have them. A backchecker who skates hard to the top of the circles and then stops, having covered nobody, has spent the energy and bought nothing.

#### Never finish a backcheck into a player's back

**Pressuring never means finishing into a carrier's back.** When the player you are catching is facing away from you, take the lane or let up; if you can see their numbers, pull up and play the puck. You are late, they are facing the boards with their head down, and the last stride becomes a check from behind. ⚠️ **That is a spinal-injury mechanism, not a tariff question.** USA Hockey's Casebook calls the ten feet nearest the boards a *"danger zone"*, where a player checked from behind *"may be at high risk of receiving a serious and possibly life altering injury"*. Hockey Canada's Rule 7.5 preamble tells referees to be *"aware of the tragic consequences of spinal injuries"* — picturing a player *"along the boards, with their head down looking for the puck"* who is *"hit from behind and driven head-first into the boards."*

**Nor may you restrain them from behind — skate to regain your position.** Behind an opponent who does *not* have the puck, NHL Rule 56.1 says you *"may not use his stick, body or free hand in order to restrain his opponent, but must skate in order to gain or reestablish his proper position in order to make a check"*. The IIHF's 56.1 says it word for word in the plural. That clause excludes the carrier, but holding and hooking reach them: NHL Rule 54.1 defines holding as restraining an opponent *"whether or not he is in possession of the puck"*, and 55.1 makes hooking using the stick to restrain an opponent, with no possession condition (the IIHF's 54.1 and 55.1 carry both). Skating is the only legal way back.

**What it costs.** ⚠️ **No book prices a hit that stays on the back at a bare two minutes, and none confines checking from behind to the boards.** For a hit that stays on the back, in the NHL, the IIHF, the PWHL, Hockey Canada and CARHA every call carries a game misconduct, so it ends your game (NHL 43.5, IIHF 43.3, Hockey Canada 7.5(e), CARHA 53(a)) — ⚠️ **in Britain too**, because the IIHF book has no minor for it (43.2). At a club in England or Wales, outside the Elite League, that game misconduct is at least a one-game ban, in every league or age group you are registered in ([UK and England Rules](../foundation/uk_rules.md#a-first-major-can-end-your-game-and-adults-are-not-exempt)). Into the boards or goal frame on a player who cannot protect themselves, Hockey Canada (7.5(c)) and CARHA (53(b)) make it a mandatory match. USA Hockey alone keeps a minor plus misconduct, for a push or a light pinch — but every forceful check on a player standing at the boards, back to the middle of the ice, is a major and game misconduct or a match (Casebook 608, Situation 1), and a reckless hit is a mandatory major and game misconduct that also costs your next game (608(b), 404(b)) — ⚠️ **plan on a match and a likely multi-game suspension** (602(a)). [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md#checking-from-behind-and-what-the-onus-does-and-does-not-mean) sets out each book's tiers, including a hit that reaches the head.

The books differ on the price. None of them differs on the play.

### The second effort

```facts
Key: The second effort is the play after the play — you lose a battle and, rather than gliding out of it, you turn and go again
Action: Go to the rebound rather than admiring your shot. Take one more stride when you get beaten wide
Read: The player who won the first battle believes it is over and stops playing it for a beat — that beat is available to whoever takes another stride
```

Roughly: the play after the play. You lose a battle, and rather than gliding out of it you turn and go again. You shoot, and rather than admiring it you go to the rebound. You get beaten wide, and rather than conceding you take one more stride.

Second efforts win pucks because the opponent has already relaxed. A player who has just won a battle believes it is over, and for a beat they stop playing it — and that beat is available to whoever is willing to take another stride.

### Finish your route

```facts
Action: If you commit to driving the net, arrive at the net, not on the goalie; if you break into a lane, complete the break even after the pass has gone elsewhere
Risk: Within a period defenders learn your drives are not real and stop respecting them, and you have lost the ability to occupy anybody
Risk: An abandoned route leaves you in an in-between place, neither supporting nor defending, and that is where odd-man rushes get conceded
```

If you commit to driving the net, **arrive at the net, not on the goalie.** If you break into a lane, **complete the break** even after the pass has gone elsewhere.

Half-finished routes are one of the off-puck failures coaches name most often in amateur hockey, and they cost twice. *(That is an observation rather than a measured one. Amateur play is measured in several ways — published work profiles the rule infractions a Bantam season produces and researchers have video-coded head contact in body-checking divisions of competitive minor hockey, both carried by [Game Management](../systems/game_management.md), while public tracking counts puck events such as zone entries and exits, which [Neutral Zone Systems](../systems/neutral_zone_systems.md) covers. None of those counts a route nobody finished, and no published work ranking off-puck errors by frequency was found for this document. Treat this as coaching consensus.)* First, defenders learn within a period that your drives are not real and stop respecting them, which means you have lost the ability to occupy anybody. Second, an abandoned route leaves you in an in-between place, neither supporting nor defending, and it is from those places that odd-man rushes get conceded.

### The honest point about effort

```facts
Mindset: Off-puck effort is the most visible thing about you and the easiest thing to change
Key: Your coach cannot easily see whether you made the right read, but can see exactly who backchecked, who finished the route and who watched
Key: "Compete", "hard to play against" and "reliable" are almost always descriptions of off-puck behaviour
Action: It requires no new skill. Backcheck hard into your lane, finish your route, take a second stride into a battle
Never: Skate hard in random directions — effort in the wrong place is just fatigue
```

**Off-puck effort is the most visible thing about you and the easiest thing to change.**

Your coach cannot easily see whether you made the right read. They can see, from the bench, exactly who backchecked, who finished the route, who got to the net front and who watched. When a coach describes a player as "compete", "hard to play against", or "reliable", they are almost always describing off-puck behaviour.

And it requires no new skill. You do not need better hands, a harder shot or faster feet to backcheck hard into your lane, finish your route, or take a second stride into a battle. It is the only part of hockey you can improve to a genuinely high standard between one game and the next. Most players never do, which is why doing it separates you so cheaply.

That is not a licence to skate hard in random directions. Effort in the wrong place is just fatigue. Everything in the four states above tells you where to spend it.

## Off-puck play by position

**In short:** Know your off-puck job: the centre supports low, the winger holds width, the defenceman controls the gap, and the goalie talks.

Brief, because the position documents cover each in full. Take the one line that applies to you.

Two words in the defenceman's line are easily confused. A **pinch** is stepping down from the *offensive* blue line to keep a puck in their end; **stepping up** is challenging an attacker forward, in the neutral zone or at your own line. Different decisions, in different halves of the rink.

| Position | Your off-puck job in one line | The characteristic error |
|---|---|---|
| **Centre** — [Centre](../positions/center.md) | Low support in your own end, middle-lane driver on the rush, and the player who reads which state the shift is in and tells everyone else. You are most often the **second layer** behind pressure. | Duplicating pressure instead of layering behind it. |
| **Winger** — [Winger](../positions/winger.md) | **Width and the point.** In your own end you usually have the opposing defenceman on your side — that is coverage, not puck pursuit. In their end your width is what stops the defence compressing. | Leaving your lane to go to the puck. |
| **Defenceman** — [Defender](../positions/defender.md) | Gap control (the distance you keep from an attacker), net-front coverage, stick in the lane, and the hold-or-pinch decision at the offensive blue line — a decision made entirely without the puck. | Both defencemen going to the same puck. |

**Goalie.** A special case, because you are off the puck essentially all game: your off-puck work is depth, angle, tracking and communication. You are also the only player who sees the entire ice on every play, and none of that reaches your team unless you say it. Call the loose puck, call the unmarked opponent behind the play, call the change.

**Where you may play the puck behind the net depends on the book.** The NHL and IIHF have the **trapezoid**, which restricts where you may play the puck behind the goal line (NHL Rules 1.8 and 27.8; IIHF Rule 27.7). The KHL is commonly said to have it too, though that is unverified here: no KHL rulebook was available to check. USA Hockey, Hockey Canada and CARHA have none. **The PWHL switches it off (Rules 1.8 and 27.8) but its Rule 65.2(viii) still writes the minor for a goalie who plays the puck *"outside of the designated area behind the net"*, so in the PWHL play as if it binds.**

**No trapezoid is not no restriction** — ⚠️ **a goalie taught otherwise takes a minor.** USA Hockey **Rule 614(c)** and CARHA **Rule 58(b)** make it a minor to freeze the puck behind the goal line with your body entirely outside the crease, and Hockey Canada's **Interpretation 3 to Rule 10.1(a)** is blunter: *"When a goaltender leaves their crease, they will not be allowed to freeze the puck. If they do, they will be assessed a Minor penalty. No warning will be issued."* So you may go and fetch the puck out there, but you may not sit on it. See [Goaltender](../positions/goaltender.md).

🇬🇧 **In Britain, stay inside the trapezoid until your league tells you otherwise.** The Elite League's Casebook adopts the 2026-27 IIHF book and the In-House Rules do not amend Rule 27, so 27.7 reads as live. England Ice Hockey's *Rules & Regulations* 22.3 (2024-25) call it *"not currently enforced"*, its scope and standing unsettled; the In-House Rules, 2025-26 and 2026-27 alike, do not mention it, and their Introduction says *"[u]nless otherwise stated, all rules will be enforced in line with the IIHF Rule Book"* ([UK and England Rules](../foundation/uk_rules.md)).

## How to train it

Off-puck play is trainable, but not by every drill: a line rush with no defenders trains none of it.

### Small-area games

```facts
Goal: Small-area games are the most efficient off-puck training available, because in a small space you cannot hide and you are constantly forced into one of the four states
Action: Ask for two-touch or one-touch — it removes the option of skating out of trouble, so the only way to succeed is off-puck movement by everybody else
Action: Make a goal count only with a net-front presence, which forces net drives
Action: Require a change of direction before receiving, which trains separation
Action: Play three minutes with no talking, then three with mandatory calls — the difference makes the value of communication self-evident
```

Games played in a reduced space — cross-ice, one zone, three-on-three, with modified rules — are the most efficient off-puck training available, because in a small space you cannot hide and every player is constantly forced into one of the four states.

USA Hockey's coaching material makes this case explicitly. Its *Teaching Concepts Through Small-Area Games* manual describes hockey as "an instinctive, largely unscripted game," and notes that players "will be forced to make decisions repeatedly while battling for space in confined areas". It identifies the second component of development, after skills, as **"'sense,' both with and without the puck"** — hockey sense being "the ability to read the play and react (or anticipate) appropriately." The manual quotes NHL coach Mike Sullivan: "Situations in hockey rarely occur the same way we draw them up in practice. We, as coaches, would like to make the game black and white. The fact of the matter is that the game, by nature, is gray."

Useful constraints to ask your coach for, or to impose in a shinny game:

- **Two-touch or one-touch.** Removes the option of skating out of trouble, so the only way to succeed is off-puck movement by everybody else.
- **A goal only counts with a net-front presence.** Forces net drives.
- **You must change direction before receiving.** Trains separation.
- **Silent play, then loud play.** Play three minutes with no talking, then three with mandatory calls. The difference is startling and it makes the value of communication self-evident.

### Watch yourself on video away from the puck

```facts
Action: Watch a filmed shift and never look at the puck. Watch only yourself
Read: Look for long glides, routes abandoned three-quarters of the way, standing still watching a battle, and facing the wrong way when the puck came free
Technique: Pick three shifts and write down how many times the state changed, how many of those changes you reacted to late, and one moment you were unmarked but not available
```

If any of your games are filmed, the single most valuable exercise available to you is this: **watch a shift and never look at the puck.** Watch only yourself.

You will see things you do not believe. Long glides. Routes abandoned three-quarters of the way. Standing still watching a battle you could have joined. Facing the wrong way when the puck came free. Almost every player's first honest off-puck video session is uncomfortable, and almost every player improves immediately afterwards, because the problems are obvious once seen.

A structured way to do it: pick three shifts, and for each one write down (a) how many times the state changed, (b) how many of those changes you reacted to late, and (c) one moment where you were unmarked but not available.

### Watch a live game and follow one player

```facts
Action: At a live game, or on a wide-angle feed, pick one player and watch only them for a full shift — not the puck
Read: Notice how much a good centre moves when nothing is happening, and how often they adjust position by a few feet in response to something you had not noticed
Key: A standard broadcast follows the puck and crops out exactly the four players you are studying, so live is better
```

The best off-puck education available for free: at a live game, or on a wide-angle feed, **pick one player and watch only them for a full shift** — not the puck. Two minutes of a good centre shows you how much they move when nothing is happening and how often they adjust their position by a few feet in response to something you had not noticed. Live beats a standard broadcast, which follows the puck and crops out exactly the four players you are studying. Full method in [How to Watch Hockey](../off-the-ice/how_to_watch_hockey.md).

### Self-check questions for the bench

```facts
Position: The bench is where off-puck improvement actually happens — thirty seconds and a clear view
Action: After each shift ask yourself one of the six questions — one, not all six
Key: Pick one question and use it for a whole game; six questions asked vaguely change nothing, one asked six times changes behaviour by the third period
```

The bench is where off-puck improvement actually happens, because you have thirty seconds and a clear view. After each shift, ask yourself one of these — one, not all six:

1. **Which of the four states was I in when the shift went wrong?**
2. **Was I ever unmarked but unavailable?** (No lane, no stick down, wrong angle.)
3. **Did I finish every route I started?**
4. **On the loose pucks, did I arrive with body position or just with speed — and did I arrive the safe way round: skates parallel to the wall, head up and chin off my chest, never turning my back to anyone coming at me, and my forearm and hip to the wall rather than the point of my shoulder?**
5. **When we lost it, how long did it take me to turn?**
6. **Did I duplicate a teammate's pressure instead of layering behind them?**

Pick one question and use it for a whole game. Six questions asked vaguely change nothing; one question asked six times changes behaviour by the third period.

## Common Mistakes

- **Training only the time you have the puck.** Train what you do without the puck too — small-area games, and watching your own shifts away from the puck. The 3.3% to 4.2% is 5-on-5 possession from NHL tracking data the league itself calls unofficial. Skill work is necessary, but it is not most of your game.
- **Assuming your age group settles whether you may body check.** Ask your association or league before the first shift. A league can bar checking in any division, and under USA Hockey a checking team playing a non-checking one plays the whole game non-check. In Britain your competition's regulations decide, not your age. Where checking is barred, defend with angle, stick and position: a hit there is a penalty, and under USA Hockey a reckless one is a mandatory major and gets you thrown out — plan on a match penalty and a likely multi-game suspension (602(a)).
- **Watching the puck on defence.** Keep your man in your peripheral vision and take short looks at the puck — if you cannot see both in one glance, move until you can. Watching the puck feels attentive, but nobody is then watching the man who scores.
- **Duplicating pressure instead of layering.** Put one player on the carrier and layer the next behind the first. Two on one carrier leaves a free attacker, and that attacker is the one who hurts you. Two planned exceptions: a **deliberate double-team below your own goal line**, where the net and boards contain a turnover, and **man-to-man coverage**. Freelancing either is the real error.
- **Being unmarked but unavailable.** Alone in space with your stick in the air, or with a defender's stick in the only lane, then blaming the carrier. Look at the lane, not your defender; if it is blocked, move the three or four feet that open it, then call for the puck.
- **Breaking too early.** Getting open a second before the passer is ready, being covered again when they look up, and stopping. Watch the passer, keep moving in small ways, and burst when their head comes up.
- **Skating away from the puck to get open.** Come back toward the carrier: it is shorter, faster and harder to defend.
- **Half-hearted net drives.** If you are going, go as if you expect the puck, and arrive at the net — never into the goalie — head up and knowing where the posts are. A glide occupies nobody, and within a period the defence stops respecting you.
- **Bumping the goalie because you were outside the crease.** Take the goalie's eyes, not their body — a legal screen never touches anybody, and being outside the blue paint settles where you may stand, not whom you may touch. Under the NHL, the IIHF and the PWHL, contact you start with a goalie in the crease takes the goal off the board. ⚠️ **The penalty runs separately from the goal:** deliberate contact is at least a minor in every book, in the crease or out, and a charge can end your game in every book — mandatory under Hockey Canada, and under CARHA in the crease or wherever it injures, with at least a one-game ban there. Under USA Hockey plan on a match and a likely multi-game suspension for a reckless charge (602(a)).
- **Screening with your head down and no idea where the post is.** Know where the near post is before the shot, and take a defender's shove braced, with a wide base and your knees giving — never lean on the goalie. Keep your head up and your chin off your chest, and never duck. USA Hockey's *Heads Up Hockey* guide names the goal post in how these injuries happen, *"at walking speed"* as well as full. **This holds in non-checking leagues too** — a box-out puts you in the same place as a shove.
- **Abandoning routes.** Complete the break even after the pass has gone elsewhere. Otherwise defenders learn to ignore you, and you end up stranded where odd-man rushes are conceded.
- **Both players going to the same loose puck from the same angle.** One goes hard, one supports, nobody else joins — and if it is ambiguous, call it. Otherwise both are committed and one opponent beats two of yours.
- **Racing to a loose puck square to the boards.** Come to the wall on an angle, feet apart and knees bent, with your **skates parallel to the wall**, head up, chin off your chest, and **forearm and hip to the wall — not the point of your shoulder.** **Never turn your back to anyone coming at you, never duck.** That outranks winning the puck. If you are already facing the wall with no time to turn, get your stick and gloves up and spread on the glass, head off it. Wall races are where checking-from-behind and boarding injuries happen ([Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md), §6).
- **Making the outlet pass before clearing the danger.** In a defensive-zone scramble, take the net front first, then the shooter's stick, then get the puck out of the slot, and only then look for the outlet.
- **Ending the pressure by putting the puck over the glass.** Aim the clear hard and wide *off* the glass, or short enough to die in the neutral zone, and never fire it out to buy a whistle or end a shift. ⚠️ **Over the glass from your own zone, direct and non-deflected, is a minor whether you meant it or not** under the NHL, the IIHF and the PWHL, and a *deliberate* one is a minor from anywhere in all six books.
- **Standing still on the weak side.** Keep your stick on the ice and take one step toward the seam — that is the difference between a decoy and a passenger.
- **Stepping laterally into a checker to block for a teammate.** Run your own route close past a teammate instead, so your check has to go around them — unless your check is the one pressuring your puck carrier. Stepping into a checker is a pick, an interference minor — **and not the ceiling**: a violent, reckless or injuring one can end your game, under USA Hockey plan on a match and a likely multi-game suspension for a reckless one (602(a)), and in non-check hockey a pick that lands as a hit is priced as a body check.
- **Taking the forechecker off your own puck carrier with your body.** Get open for the carrier instead. Shielding the carrier is interference — USA Hockey, Hockey Canada and CARHA name it outright, the other books' interference minor reaches it once you move into the forechecker's path, and CARHA can make even the no-contact version a major, your game and at least your next one. **Once your body meets the forechecker, two minutes is not the ceiling anywhere.**
- **Backchecking to a place instead of to a man.** Pick up a player and tell your teammates you have them. Sprinting to the top of the circles and stopping covers no one.
- **Finishing a backcheck into the back of the man you could not catch.** When they have their back to you, take the lane or let up — if you can see their numbers, pull up and play the puck. You are late, they are facing the boards with their head down, and the last stride becomes a check from behind. ⚠️ **No rulebook prices a hit that stays on the back at a bare two minutes, and in most books it ends your game** — and every book but the IIHF's can make it a match. **The reason is not the penalty sheet:** USA Hockey's Casebook calls the ten feet nearest the boards a *"danger zone"* where a player checked from behind *"may be at high risk of receiving a serious and possibly life altering injury"*. No league, checking or not, makes it legal.
- **Assuming the answer to "who goes?" is universal.** Ask your coach which forecheck your team plays. Under a 2-1-2 the nearest two forwards go in their end; under a 1-2-2 one does and the others hold the middle.

## Key Takeaways

1. **Train what you do without the puck — small-area games, and watching your own shifts away from it — not only stickhandling.** You have the puck for about 3.3% to 4.2% of your ice time, in possessions averaging 1.29 seconds, on 5-on-5 NHL tracking data the league itself calls unofficial. You never have it long enough to fix bad position.
2. **Know which of four states you are in, and change jobs the instant it changes:** your team has it, they have it, it is loose, it has just changed hands. Most bad off-puck play is the right job for the state before. When two apply, take the more urgent one.
3. **Find out before your first shift whether you may hit; where you may not, defend with angle, stick and position.** Take the ice, never the man — under Hockey Canada or CARHA only ice you already have. In IIHF women's hockey, never step or glide into a player or pin her on the boards.
4. **Make yourself available, not just unmarked.** A flat stick as a target, a straight line from the puck to your blade, and nothing in that lane — a defender's stick across it leaves you unavailable, and you cannot see that from your own position. Fix the lane, then call for it.
5. **Break on the passer's look, not the open space.** A second early is the same as never getting open. Go when their head comes up.
6. **Come back toward the puck.** Skating away is a common amateur habit; arriving toward the carrier is shorter, faster and harder to defend.
7. **Occupy a defender fully: drive, screen, stay live weak-side.** Run routes close past a teammate doing their own job; under Hockey Canada or CARHA, never past one parked to catch your check. Never take a forechecker off your own carrier: interference in three books, risky in the rest. Once your body meets the forechecker, two minutes is not the ceiling.
8. **On defence, layer behind pressure rather than duplicating it** — two players on one puck usually leaves somebody free. **Whether you hold that layer is a system question, not a law**, so ask your coach which coverage you play. [Puck Support and Spacing](puck_support_and_spacing.md) covers the geometry and the deliberate double-team exception.
9. **On a loose puck, one goes hard, one supports — and arrive safe before you arrive first.** Head-down hits into the boards or a post cause almost all on-ice cervical spine injuries, even at walking speed. Head up, chin off your chest — **never turn your back to anyone coming at you, and never duck.**
10. **Backcheck to a man, finish your route, take the second stride — and never finish a backcheck into a player's back.** Late on somebody facing away from you, anywhere on the ice, take the lane or let up. It causes life-altering injuries, every book can eject you for it, and it holds in every league, checking or not.
11. **Take the goalie's eyes, never their body.** Screen outside the paint, off the red line, touching nobody. Mind the posts, head up, never duck. ⚠️ **Deliberate contact is at least a minor, and a charge can end your game, in every book**: mandatory under Hockey Canada anywhere, and under CARHA in the crease or if it injures anyone, with at least a one-game ban.
12. **Clear the zone off the glass, never over it.** Aim it hard and wide along the glass, or short enough to die in the neutral zone. **From your own zone, straight over the glass is a minor, meant or not, under the NHL, IIHF and PWHL**; a *deliberate* one is a minor from anywhere in all six books.

- The **individual possession figures** come from a peer-reviewed conference paper (LINHAC 2025) using NHL puck-and-player-tracking data from 2023-24. The paper itself states that the NHL considers this possession dataset **unofficial** and that it "may differ from other datasets that track possession information (e.g., a hand-labelled dataset)." All of its figures are **5-on-5 only** and **normalised to 20 minutes of ice time**.

Two things follow, and both cut against the way these numbers are usually repeated. **1:07.05 is the men's per-game tournament average, not a Modano or Sakic figure** — those player names come from the gloss in **Harry Thompson's "The Power of Practice"**, a *USA Hockey Magazine* feature that reports the study rather than measuring anything itself. And **1:06.02 is the 14-and-Under bracket alone**, not youth nationals generally.
- The **ice-time averages** (forwards 15:17, defencemen 19:31 for players with 40+ games in 2024-25) are computed from Hockey Reference's 2024-25 NHL skater table, not a figure published by that site.
- The **shift-length figures** (mean 47 seconds, median 45) are computed from the NHL's public shift-chart API across a sample of **11 regular-season games from 2024-25 (8,325 shifts)**. That is a small sample chosen for convenience and it is not a league-wide published statistic.
- All **arithmetic combining** possession rates, ice time and shift length (the "about 30 seconds", "about 24 possessions", "about 14 seconds in the offensive zone" figures) is mine. It assumes the 5-on-5 possession rate holds across all ice time, which is an approximation.
- The **interference and pick rules** are quoted from the **NHL Official Rules 2025-2026**, Rule 56.1, and the **holding and hooking** definitions in the backchecking section from Rules 54.1 and 55.1 of the same book. **Note what Rule 56.1 does not cover:** its "Body Position" paragraph is written for *"an opponent, who does not have the puck"*, so it does not reach a player restraining the puck **carrier** from behind. That is holding under 54.1, which applies *"whether or not he is in possession of the puck"*, or hooking under 55.1, which sets no possession condition.

  The IIHF equivalents were read in the **2025/26 Rulebook (Version 1.1, July 2025)**: its 54.1, 55.1 and 56.1 carry the same wording in the plural, its 55.1 adding a sentence about the stick going against or near the opponent's hands. **56.1's "Body Position" paragraph and its "Pick" definition differ from the NHL's only in the plural pronouns, the capitalised *Player*, one added comma, and quotation marks around the IIHF's defined terms** — *"establishing body position"*, *"interference"*, *"body position"*, *"free hand"* — and the lateral-movement sentence is word for word identical bar two of those quotation-mark pairs. **The same paragraph is byte-identical in the 2026/27 Rulebook (Version 1.0, June 2026)**, so nothing here turns on which IIHF edition a British league is sitting on.
- **Rule 56.2 was read in full in both books for the route-through-traffic section.** Its second limb names restraining an opponent *"attempting to 'forecheck'"* — and both books define *restrain* as illegal means — for example a hook, a hold or a trip — rather than skating, so it does not reach a screen made with no contact; PWHL 57.2 carries the same limbs. Its first limb reaches *"a player who interferes with or impedes the progress of an opponent who is not in possession of the puck"* and its third *"any player who deliberately checks an opponent, including the goalkeeper, who is not in possession of the puck"*; the IIHF's (I) and (III) are the same limbs in the plural. ⚠️ **That a stationary screen falls outside those limbs because of 56.1's body-position permission is a reading of how the two rules fit together, and not something either book states** — the reading is that 56.1's express permission would have nothing left to operate on otherwise, since 56.1 defines *"restrain"* by the same verb, *impede*, that 56.2's first limb uses.

  **Two differences between the two books were read rather than assumed.** The IIHF's 56.1 opens with a general definition of interference, and the NHL's has no counterpart: three distinctive phrases from it appear nowhere in the NHL's book, which does carry the neighbouring *"strict standard on acts of interference"*, and the NHL's Glossary of Terms carries no interference entry. ⚠️ **That IIHF sentence is broken across a page in the PDF:** a running header — *"SECTION · RESTRAINING INFRACTIONS"* — sits in the middle of it, so search for each half separately; the sentence is in the 2026/27 edition in the same words. And the IIHF's 56.2 opens *"A minor penalty for 'interference' shall be assessed"* against the NHL's *"can be imposed"* — wording the NHL's own 2024-25 edition had as *"shall be imposed"*. **Whether a referee's practice differs with the modal is not something any book this guide covers says**, and nothing here claims it does; what is stated is the difference in wording.
- **The ceiling on a screen run for your own puck carrier was checked in every book that names the act.** **USA Hockey:** Rule 625 was read end to end and writes no tier of its own above 625(a)'s minor. The *Summary of Penalties* appendix lists *625(a) Interference* under **Minor Penalty** and nowhere else. The escalation clauses, 640(g) and 640(h), reach only *"actions falling under Rule 640(b, c, d, e or f)"*, read in full on 12 September 2026: **(b)**, **(d)**, **(e)** and **(f)** each require a body check, and **(c)** reaches *"avoidable physical contact with an opponent after the whistle"* — so none of the five describes a screen held during play.

  **Hockey Canada:** 8.3(b) is keyed to *"the degree of violence of the impact"* or to injuring an opponent, and 8.3(c) to an attempt to injure. **CARHA:** its Rule 66(a) Note 2 names the same act. Unlike the other two, it writes the route above the minor into the rule the Note hangs off: 66(a) is *"A Minor penalty for interference or, at the discretion of the Referee, a Major penalty and a Game Misconduct penalty"*. So CARHA is the one of the three whose ceiling for a no-contact screen is not the minor. **CARHA governs CARHA-affiliated adult leagues only**, so that is an answer for a rec-league reader in one and for nobody else. ⚠️ **None of that is a statement that a screen can never be penalised above a minor** — it is that neither book writes a route there for a screen made with no contact.

  **And Hockey Canada's *"may not run deliberate interference"* sentence is undefined in its own book:** it occurs once in the book, and the book's Glossary entry for *INTERFERENCE* enumerates the four lettered limbs of Rule 8.3 and omits it. The two Interpretations printed under Rule 8.3 are both about the goaltender — one about objects dropped at or near the crease, the other about a goaltender making physical contact. The nearest thing to a definition found anywhere is in CARHA's Rule 66(a), Note 2 — a book with no authority over Hockey Canada — where the same phrase is one half of an express contrast with defending players being *"entitled to 'stand their ground' or 'shadow'"*, which suggests an active route rather than standing still. **That is provenance for an idiom, not a construction of Hockey Canada's rule**, and it is why no scope is asserted for the sentence above.
- The **delay-of-game material** — in the loose-puck priority list, and in the Common Mistakes and Key Takeaways bullets on clearing the zone — is quoted directly from the rule books: NHL 63.2(ii) and (iii), IIHF 63.2(II) and (III), and PWHL 65.2(ii) and (iii). For USA Hockey it is 610(c) and 631(d), for Hockey Canada 10.1 with its clauses (ii) and (v), and for CARHA Rules 55 and 75(b). Two limits travel with it. **Whether the IIHF Rulebook and the IIHF Situation Handbook disagree about a puck kicked out over the glass depends on a reading neither book settles.** The wording of 63.2(III) says hand or stick; Situation 63.19 in the Handbook's **v1.1 (August 2025)** says the minor applies, reversing the Handbook's own **v1.0 (June 2025)**; and **Situation 63.34** puts the bare kick and answers *"Yes"* in **all three editions read**, so v1.0 held both answers at the same time.

  **Three Handbook editions were read for these situations:** v1.0 and v1.1 of 2025/26, and the **2026/27 Handbook (Version 1.0, June 2026)**, in which 63.19 is in the same words and answers *"Yes."* again and 63.34 and 63.29 are word for word as in both 2025/26 editions. The **2026/27 Rulebook** was read for 63.2(II) and (III) alone, both unchanged, (III) still naming only *"their hand or stick"*; nothing else in the 2026/27 books has been read for this document. **Neither situation cites a rule.** Nowhere is it stated whether the Handbook is applying **63.2(II)** — *"deliberately shoots or bats (using their hand, leg, foot, arm, stick etc.) the puck outside the playing area (from anywhere on the ice surface)"*, the one clause in the whole of Rule 63 that names the foot — or reading (III) more widely than its words. Every account of *why* the answer is Yes is inference about an unstated intention rather than evidence. Neither the reversal nor its reason is explained in any of them, and the Handbook's Foreword subordinates itself to the Rulebook.

  **Situation 63.29 was also read and runs the other way**, the Handbook there being *narrower* than 63.2(III): a goalkeeper's reactive stick save over the glass draws *"No. The goalkeeper was simply making a save and did not 'shoot' the puck over the glass. Rule 63.2. talks about deliberate, the save cannot be seen as falling under this criteria."* So the position is reported as conditional rather than resolved. **The bench material comes from the same Handbook** — Situation 63.24, read in the 2025/26 (v1.1) and 2026/27 editions and worded the same in both — and is subordinate to the Rulebook for the same reason. **Whether the NHL reads its own hand-or-stick wording the same way is not known here.** It writes *"using his hand or his stick"* where the IIHF writes *"using their hand or stick"*. The NHL's situation handbook is not held in this corpus and was not searched, so nothing said about the skate is a statement about the NHL beyond Rule 63.2(iii)'s own words.
- The USA Hockey material on small-area games is quoted from *Teaching Concepts Through Small-Area Games* (USA Hockey, 2014, written and compiled by Ken Rausch and Dan Brennan; flexxCOACH is credited in the manual for video and audio production only, not as a publisher).
- The **coaching content** — getting-open mechanics, the four-state framework, loose-puck priorities, the bench questions — is a synthesis of standard coaching practice and is **not** a rule of hockey. Where a specific assignment depends on a system (who pursues a loose puck, how much width a winger holds, man versus zone coverage) that is flagged in the text. Ask your coach.
- The **four states are a teaching device, not a partition.** States 3 (loose puck) and 4 (transition) genuinely overlap, and the four are not mutually exclusive.
- The **goaltender-interference wording** in the screening section is quoted from **NHL Official Rules 2025-2026, Rules 69.1 and 69.3**. The section's reading that a no-contact screen from outside the crease keeps the goal rests on the books' own reference tables: NHL Table 14, IIHF Table 16 and its Table 14 in 2026/27, and PWHL Table 14. Their Situations 5C and 5E answer both the "outside the crease" and the "on the crease line" cases with "Goal is allowed.", even where the screener impairs the goalkeeper's ability to defend. Rule 69.1's clause (1), impairment "either by his positioning or by contact", is read with those rows: outside the crease it bites on contact, or on a position that stops the goalkeeper moving freely within their crease. **None of those books states what status those tables have**, and both provide that Rule 69 is "enforced exclusively in accordance with the on-ice judgement of the Referee(s), but may be subject to a Coach's Challenge" — the NHL's wording; the IIHF's adds "a Video Review… or to a Coach's Challenge" too.

  **Rule 69.3's definition of a "significant position" is quoted rather than paraphrased on purpose**, because the ground reads far stricter without it: the rule confines itself to a body, "or a substantial portion thereof", in the crease "for more than an instantaneous period of time".

  **The IIHF prints that defining sentence word for word** in both its 2025/26 (v1.1) and 2026/27 editions, differing only in writing "their body" for "his body", so the test quoted here is the same test a British reader plays under. ⚠️ **The IIHF's own Situation Handbook does not apply that test, and it is quoted in the section above rather than left out:** its Situation 69.1 answers a player *"in the crease screening the goalkeeper"* with a flat *"The goal shall be disallowed, and no additional penalty assessed"*. It cites *"Rule 69.1 and Rule 69.3"* and asks nothing about duration, nothing about the goalkeeper's vision and nothing about their ability to defend. It is guidance, and the Handbook's own Foreword subordinates it — *"in case of any contradictions, the valid IIHF Rulebook is always the basis"* — so the narrowing above stands as a statement of the rule. It is stated rather than omitted because it is authority running the other way, and because the official in a British reader's game may be working from it.

  **The USA Hockey and Hockey Canada comparison in the same section is quoted from their own books.** The statement that neither book reaches a screen set from **outside** the crease is a search of both in full. USA Hockey writes no goaltender-vision provision at all, and Hockey Canada's only two mentions of a goaltender's view — in Rule 8.5's crease paragraph and its Interpretation 3 — both reach a player who is in the crease. That is a claim about screens set outside the paint only; inside it, Hockey Canada does judge impairment, and 8.5's own carve-out and Interpretation 3 are quoted in full above rather than summarised, because the first sentence of each reads as a flat bar and the second is what makes it not one.

  The separate USA Hockey *Official Rules and Casebook* was searched for the same thing and does not add one. Its only two uses of the word *screen* are Rule 625(a)(1)'s protective screen set on a **checker** and a sled-hockey pushers' provision, and it never speaks of a goaltender's vision, view or sight. None of the seventeen Casebook situations under Rule 625 reaches a screen at all — the nearest, Situation 8, turns on an attacker *standing in the crease*. So the USA Hockey negative rests on both volumes.

  **CARHA was searched in full for the same thing and writes none either** — it never uses the word *screen*, and every occurrence of *vision* in the book is part of *division* or *provisions*; its Rule 66(b) reaches the goalkeeper's movement only *"by actual physical contact"*, and its crease paragraph reaches a player standing in the crease. **The USA Hockey Casebook situations do carry a limit the printed rule does not, and it is quoted in full above:** Situation 9's proviso disallowing the goal, and adding an interference penalty, where the goalkeeper was trying to get back to the crease and the attacker prevented them from making a play. Hockey Canada prints its interpretations inside the playing rules, so both of its mentions were searched with them.
- **The red crease line** (the *To screen properly* passage and Key Takeaway 11): read in the primary texts on 18 September 2026 and quoted from them. **IIHF** Rule 1.7 — *"The marked line belongs to the Goal Crease"*, identical in the 2025/26 (v1.1) and 2026/27 editions. **USA Hockey**, the Note to Rule 625(b) — the crease area is *"all the space outlined by the semi-circular crease lines (including crease lines)"*, with its *Official Rules and Casebook* answering it directly under *The Rink* at Situation 4: *"Is the two inch red line outlining the goal crease considered to be a part of the goal crease? Yes."* **Hockey Canada**, its Glossary entry for GOAL CREASE — *"The lines of the crease are considered part of the crease."*

  **CARHA**, its Glossary entry for the creases — *"The lines which designate these areas are to be considered as part of the creases."* ⚠️ **The NHL writes no such sentence** — its Rule 1.7 says only that the area is *"outlined by the crease lines"* — but **Reference Table 14, Situation 5 E** allows the goal where an attacking player *"plants himself on the crease line or outside the goal crease, as to obstruct the goalkeeper's vision"*. **IIHF Appendix IV** prints that same row in its own pronouns, at **Table 16** in the 2025/26 edition and **Table 14** in 2026/27.

  **The PWHL is the NHL's shape too:** its Rule 1.7 says only that the crease area *"shall include all the space outlined by the crease lines"*, while its own **Table 14** prints the crease-line row and allows the goal. ⚠️ **So the IIHF book answers the same play in two directions**, its Rule 1.7 against its own Situation 5 E. ⚠️ **And only one of the four answers sits in rule text** — the IIHF's. USA Hockey's is a Note to a playing rule plus a Casebook situation, and Hockey Canada's and CARHA's are Glossary entries, Hockey Canada's own Rule 1.5(c) using the NHL's looser *"outlined by the crease lines"* wording. **Nothing in any of the books ranks a table against a note against a glossary.** The instruction to stay off the line follows those four answers and is deliberately more conservative than any of the tables.
- The **contact-inside-the-crease material** in the same subsection is quoted from **NHL Rule 69.3**'s first paragraph and **Rule 69.7**, from the IIHF's 69.3 and 69.7 in both editions, and from the **PWHL's Rules 71.3 and 71.7**. The IIHF writes *"a relevant contact"* where the NHL writes *"contact"* in that first paragraph, and its 69.7 is the NHL's sentence with quotation marks of its own around *"incidental contact"*. The NHL's 69.3 refers to 69.7 as an exception in terms. **Neither book's 69.7 is scoped to a screen.** The reading offered above — that a planted screener bumping the goalkeeper falls under 69.3 rather than 69.7 — rests on 69.7's own words about a rebound or a puck both players are simultaneously attempting to play, not on anything either book says about screens.

  **69.7 is quoted for all four of its limbs rather than the permission alone**, because the other three are limits a net-front player meets. The first is the disallowed goal where a goalkeeper is pushed into the net together with the puck by an attacking player after making a stop, with *"[i]f applicable, appropriate penalties will be assessed"*. The second is the relief in the sentence after it, where a defending player pushed or fouled the attacking player into causing that. The third is the last limb, on a puck under a player in or around the crease. The IIHF prints all four at the same number in both editions, differing only in small wording details and in layout. The wording details are its capitalised *Player*, its *"goal crease"* for the NHL's *"crease"*, its quotation marks around *"incidental contact"* and *"Penalty Shot"*, and its opening *"In the event"* for the NHL's *"In the event that"*. The layout difference is that it sets all four limbs off as separate paragraphs where the NHL prints three and keeps the relief inside the second; the reading of every limb is the same in both books.

  **The PWHL prints all four at its Rule 71.7 in the NHL's own words**, its only difference being the closing cross-reference to its own *"Rule 65.7 – Awarded Goal"* where the NHL names *"Rule 63 – Delaying the Game"* — the same rule under the PWHL's renumbering, which runs 63 → 65 and 78 → 80. **NHL 78.5(ix), IIHF 78.5(IX) and PWHL 80.5(ix) are cited only as cross-references**: all three state the push-in in a shorter form that drops *"by an attacking player"* and writes *"after making a save"*, and all three refer back to 69.7 — 71.7 in the PWHL — so 69.7's wording is what is relied on above.
- The **contact-outside-the-crease material** is quoted from **NHL Rule 69.4** and from **IIHF Rule 69.4** in both the 2025/26 (v1.1) and 2026/27 editions, where the conditional clause is word for word the same save for the IIHF's gender-neutral pronouns, its capitalised *Player*, and its quotation marks around *"incidental contact"* and *"reasonable effort"*. It is also quoted from **USA Hockey Rule 607(c) and 607(d)** with its Note 1 and Note 2, and from the **USA Hockey Official Rules and Casebook 2025-29** at **Rule 607, Situations 5 and 6**. It is quoted, too, from **Hockey Canada Rule 8.5**'s opening paragraph and its *Protection of the Goaltender* paragraph, from **PWHL Rule 71.4**, which prints the NHL's paragraph in the feminine, and from the Note to **CARHA Rule 52(b)**, which carries the *“fair game”* sentence with no permission written after it.

  Two limits are worth stating. **USA Hockey's position was reached by searching the concept rather than the phrase:** its books contain no sentence resembling the NHL's *"in the act of playing the puck"*, and the phrase appears nowhere in them. So the parallel rests on the Casebook's possession wording being read as answering the same question — which is a reading, not something either book says. And **USA Hockey 607(d) Note 1** — *"any accidental or unavoidable contact that occurs with the goalkeeper shall be penalized under the Interference rule"* — can be read either as penalising accidental contact outright or as routing it to the right rule once it is penalised; the book does not say which, and neither reading is asserted above. **Its second sentence carries no such ambiguity** — *"Any deliberate body contact or check that is delivered to the goalkeeper shall be penalized as charging"* — and that is the sentence the body relies on where it says USA Hockey's interference minor does not price a deliberate bump.
- **On the two rankings of amateur off-puck errors — puck-watching and half-finished routes.** Amateur play *is* published and counted, and by more than one method. Gilbert and Trudel's profile of rule infractions in Bantam hockey is a frequency breakdown of amateur **penalties** — what an official wrote down — and its figures and qualifications belong to [Game Management](../systems/game_management.md), which carries them. The same document carries a study that video-coded head-contact events in body-checking divisions of competitive minor hockey and found most of them within the rules, which is researcher-coded amateur behaviour that no official recorded, and a direct-observation study of aggression in ice hockey. **So amateur on-ice behaviour is not counted only where a whistle goes.** There is also measurement of *gaze*, where and when a defender looks, and of puck *events* such as turnovers and zone exits, mostly in professional leagues.

  **What was not found for this document is any published work that ranks off-puck errors by how often each happens**, so there is nothing to check the coaching claim against — and the reason is that nobody located here has done that count, not that the count is impossible. The nearest limit recorded anywhere in this corpus is narrower and about a different population: [Neutral Zone Systems](../systems/neutral_zone_systems.md) records that no published breakdown classifies a rush goal by which defensive assignment failed, and states that the tracking behind that is NHL data, one source of it a single hand-tracker's. Both rankings above are therefore attributed to coaches rather than stated as measured, and should be read that way.
- "**First to arrive, last to commit**" is a coaching heuristic with no known originator, not a law of hockey. The phrase and that caveat sit with the rest of the loose-puck material in [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md), §10.
