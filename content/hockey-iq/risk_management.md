## Key focus

**Change where you are standing before you decide — risk belongs to the location, not the play.** The same pass is brilliant in their corner and catastrophic in your own slot. A turnover in your own slot is catastrophic, in your own zone or the middle of the neutral zone high, at their blue line moderate, and in their corners nearly free — a coaching-consensus ranking, measured at only one point on the ice.

**Get the puck out of your own slot before you do anything with it, and never pass it — or skate it — across the front of your own goal.** Take one stride to the wall, go behind the net, or put it hard off the glass. Pass D-to-D *behind* your net, not through the slot. Send one defenceman to the puck and keep the other home, and say out loud who is who. These are coaching conventions: some teams allow a D-to-D high in the zone, and a strong-side overload puts both defencemen low. Ask where your coach draws each line.

**Stay on your skates to make a check, where your league allows checking.** Skate at them with your stick in the lane and take the option away. You cannot change direction in the air, and a carrier who reads the leap leaves you out of the play. **Leaving your feet to hit is also charging, by name, in all six books this guide covers.**

**Take a penalty to deny a certain goal, and almost never out of frustration or retaliation.** A minor gives up roughly a one-in-five chance of a goal — 21.1% across the 2025-26 NHL regular season — so it is a cheap trade against a goal and a terrible one against nothing.

**Prevent the breakaway four seconds earlier with your positioning — chasing and fouling from behind saves nothing.** A foul from behind on a breakaway draws a penalty shot in every book here, not a minor, and with your own net empty it is an awarded goal.

**When you have time, make the possession play; when you do not, get it out.** Off the glass buys two or three seconds but only moves you from catastrophic to high, so get organised the moment it lands. Shoot on net with traffic. Under pressure take the simple play, and in the last minute of a period take the safe one. In tracked data, carry-outs and pass-outs lead to an entry far more often than dump-outs.

**Play simple in your own end and creative in theirs.** A missed chance in their end costs you a chance; a turnover in your own end costs you a goal.

**Let the scoreboard set your risk level, not your temperament.** Leading by one in the third, take the safe option earlier; leading by three, take it down hard. Trailing late, take it up — pinch, activate, force the entry — because a goal against costs you little and a goal for is everything. Shorthanded, clear it hard, unless you have truly read a 2-on-1 the other way.

**Before you pinch, jump a lane or activate, ask three questions: do I have information or a feeling, who is behind me if I miss, and what does the scoreboard allow?** A pinch with your centre high is a good pinch even if you lose the puck, because the worst case is a 2-on-2. Two yeses and a green light, go; otherwise hold your position, which is often the right decision.

**Spend the four seconds after a turnover getting to the inside, not chasing the carrier.** Skate to the middle between the puck and your net, call "turnover" or "back", and finish the shift. That is this guide's default; **some teams send the first forward back at the carrier by design, so learn which yours plays.** Do not try to fix it — the second mistake is usually worse than the first. ⚠️ **And never solve the chase with your body** — a hit on a player's back ends your game in most books, and the prices are set out below.

## Overview

Every time you touch the puck you place a bet, and what you stand to lose depends more on where you are standing than on what you try.

**So change where you are standing before you decide.** One stride out of your own slot improves every option you have. The middle third is dangerous too, because a turnover there hands them an attack where they outnumber you. And losing the puck while it travels toward your own net is worse than losing it going the other way, because the turnover and the counter-attack become one event.

**Understand the "never" list rather than memorising it.** There are nine, each set out below with its reason: D-to-D behind your net, not through the slot; no blind backhand up the middle; north or behind the net, not across your own end; one defenceman to the puck, one home; no stickhandling in your own slot; no change with the puck in your own zone; feet on the ice to check, where checking is allowed; no forced pass at their blue line with nobody back; and off the glass, not over it. **Seven are coaching conventions with real exceptions** — on a strong-side overload some coaches put both defencemen low, and then somebody else must be home — **so ask where your coach draws each line. Two are rules: leaving your feet to hit, and putting the puck over the glass**, which the books price differently.

**Take a minor to stop a certain goal, and almost never out of frustration** — it gives up roughly a one-in-five chance of a goal. **And do not chase a carrier you cannot catch:** foul them from behind on a clear breakaway and every book here gives a penalty shot, not a minor (under USA Hockey the fouled team may take the minor instead).

**When you genuinely have nothing, take the hit and accept the faceoff that follows** — feet apart, knees bent, head up, never turn your back to the checker and never duck. Then do not compound it: the next play is a new play.

## The central idea: every play has an expected value

**Expected value** is the plain-English idea that a decision is worth *what you gain if it works, weighted by how often it works, minus what you lose if it fails, weighted by how often it fails.*

In hockey terms:

> **(chance the play works × what you gain) − (chance it fails × what you lose)**

You already do this. When you rim the puck around the boards instead of trying a cross-ice pass, you have just decided that a low-value certainty beats a high-value gamble. You made that calculation in about a third of a second and you did not notice yourself making it. **Making it conscious — in practice, on video, on the bench — is how you improve it**, because unconscious calculations do not get better on their own. They just get faster at being wrong.

### What the analytics say about the "gain" side

Public tracking work has put numbers on the upside of different puck decisions. Thibaud Chatel's "Hockey Decision Tree" at Hockey Graphs (March 2020), built from roughly **11,000 possession sequences** tracked manually by Chatel and his colleague Mathieu Brosseau in France's Ligue Magnus and Switzerland's NLA over three seasons, valued each type of play as *how often it leads to a shot attempt* multiplied by *the average expected goals of the sequences it appears in*.

**Expected goals (xG)** is a model estimate of how likely a given shot is to become a goal, based mostly on where it was taken from and under what circumstances. An xG of 0.10 means "shots like this go in about one time in ten."

| Play | Value of the play | How that value is built |
|---|---|---|
| Successful **high-danger pass** (a pass into the middle of the offensive zone) | ≈ **0.112 xG** | Leads to a shot attempt 87% of the time, and those sequences average 0.129 xG |
| Successful **controlled zone entry** (carrying or passing the puck over their blue line) | ≈ **0.040 xG** | Leads to a shot attempt 60% of the time, and those sequences average 0.067 xG |
| Successful **carry-out** of your own zone | ≈ **0.024 xG** | Leads to a shot attempt 37% of the time, and those sequences average 0.064 xG |
| **Failed dump-out** of your own zone | ≈ **0.003 xG** | Leads to a shot attempt 4% of the time, and those sequences average 0.076 xG |

Three things to take from that table:

1. **The gaps are enormous.** A completed pass into the middle of the offensive zone is worth roughly *thirty-seven times* a failed dump-out and *over four times* a clean carry out of your own end (0.112 against 0.024, which is Chatel's own phrasing). Not all good plays are equally good.
2. **Getting out of your own zone with the puck is worth about eight times getting out of it badly.** Possession compounds.
3. **These numbers only measure what you gain**, not what a failure costs you at the other end. Chatel said so himself in the 2020 write-up — *"I'm working on that."* — and his answer two years later, for one place on the ice, is under the risk map below. The cost side is what the rest of this document is about.

> **Honest caveat:** this data is from two European professional leagues, manually tracked. The direction of the findings is what matters, not the third decimal place. Do not quote these as NHL numbers.

### The cost side is invisible, and that is the problem

```facts
Key: When a risky pass works everyone sees it; when it fails, the goal arrives eight seconds later and looks like somebody else's mistake
Risk: The chain from your decision to the puck in your net is long enough that most players never connect the two
Action: Start connecting your own decision to the goal that follows it — that improves your decision-making faster than any skill you can train
```

When a risky pass works, everyone sees it. When it fails, the goal usually arrives eight seconds later and looks like somebody else's mistake — a missed coverage, a screened goalie, a bad bounce. **The chain of causation from your decision to the puck in your net is long enough that most players never connect the two.** Coaches connect it. Video connects it. Start connecting it yourself and your decision-making improves faster than any skill you can train.

## Risk is a function of location

**The same pass is brilliant in one place and catastrophic in another.** Risk is not a property of the play; it is a property of the play *and where you are standing when you make it.*

First, the vocabulary, because the whole map depends on it:

- **The slot** — the area in front of the net between the faceoff circles. The **high slot** runs from the faceoff dots up to the tops of the circles; the **goalmouth** is the immediate net front. This is the ice both teams want. Roughly **10–15%** of slot shots go in against **2–4%** from the perimeter — a coaching-level range whose source names no dataset. Close to half of all NHL goals came from the inner slot, on Sportlogiq tracking of three seasons at five-on-five that is not public and cannot be re-derived; **26.8%** fell within two stick-lengths of the net and **51.6%** within twenty feet, computed from the NHL play-by-play feed over 400 games of 2024-25, from the 2,498 goals in it that carry coordinates. [Shooting](../technique/shooting.md) owns the shot-location values; [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md) owns the counted distances. **Nothing cited here ranks the slot's conversion rate against every other area of the ice**, so take “the highest-percentage ice” as the shape those figures agree on, not a measured ranking.
- **The point** — the area just inside the blue line, where defencemen stand in the offensive zone. Not to be confused with the **half-wall**, which is on the boards level with the faceoff dot.
- **North** — toward the opponent's net. **South** — toward your own. **East–west** — across the ice.
- **Odd-man rush** — an attack where the attackers outnumber the defenders back, such as a 2-on-1 or 3-on-2.

### Why location dominates everything

The reason location matters so much is that **shot danger is largely about where the shot is taken from**. MoneyPuck's public expected-goals model — trained on more than 800,000 NHL shots from 2007-08 to 2014-15 — puts distance from the net and shot angle among its most important inputs, and in its **2015-16 test season the 15% of shots the model rated highest accounted for over half of all goals**. Location is the biggest part of the story rather than the whole of it: the model uses around fifteen inputs, including shot type, rebounds and the movement before the shot — which is why the cross-ice pass in the offensive-zone hierarchy below is so valuable.

Flip that around: **the space in front of your own net is worth defending out of all proportion to its size**, and any decision that risks putting the puck there has an outsized cost.

### The risk map

```facts
Read: Grade every area by one question — if I lose the puck here, how many seconds and how many bodies stand between the turnover and a shot on my net?
Convention: Grading the ice by turnover cost is coaching consensus reasoned from shot danger and recovery time, not a measured ranking — but one of the five areas, the offensive blue line, does carry a tracked turnover price, from one Swiss league at five-on-five
Risk: Your own slot is catastrophic — the opponent gets the puck where the counted scoring concentrates more heavily than anywhere else on the ice, with zero seconds of recovery time
Risk: Your own zone generally is high — the puck is 30–50 feet from your net and one pass from the slot
Risk: The middle of the neutral zone is high — dangerous and disorganised, and coaching material puts the making of odd-man rushes here; nothing published classifies them by the play that created them
Risk: The offensive blue line is moderate — the cost scales with how many of your players are ahead of the puck
Risk: The offensive corners and below their goal line are low — this is where you should be at your most creative
```

Every area of the ice can be graded by one question: **if I lose the puck here, how many seconds and how many bodies stand between the turnover and a shot on my net?**

| Where you are | Risk of a turnover | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **In your own slot** | **Catastrophic** | Not a chance against — very nearly a goal against. They get the puck where the counted scoring concentrates most heavily, goalie unset, defenders facing the wrong way. No recovery time. |
| **In your own zone generally** | **High** | Everyone on your team is already home, which helps, but the puck is 30–50 feet from your net and one pass from the slot. The margin for error is a stick length. |
| **In the middle of the neutral zone** | **High** | Counter-intuitive: a turnover here catches two or three of your players going the wrong way at speed. Your own zone is dangerous but organised; this is dangerous *and* disorganised. |
| **At the offensive blue line** | **Moderate** | A turnover here does not threaten your net at once, but starts a rush against a defence that was supporting the attack. Cost scales with how many players are ahead of the puck. |
| **In their corners and below their goal line** | **Low** | You are 150-plus feet from your net, all five teammates between puck and danger. They need a breakout, a neutral-zone crossing and an entry to threaten. **Be at your most creative.** |

**Three of those rows need a qualification.**

- **The slot row** rests on shares of goals rather than a conversion rate for that ice, as the vocabulary above sets out.
- **The centre-ice row** rests on coaching material, not a count: nothing published classifies odd-man rushes by the play that created them.
- **The offensive-blue-line row is the only one whose *turnover cost* has actually been priced — and the price cuts against the intuition the row encodes.** Chatel went back to the question in 2022 with *"over **56,700** 5v5 zone entries in our database, all tracked manually in Switzerland since 2019"*. After the puck is lost, *"all scenarios (dump or controlled entry, successful or failed) give up on around **0.06 or 0.07 xG** to the opponent"*, and *"a failed controlled entry is still the biggest threat, with a potential **0.015 xG** against you, but very close to a failed dump that has a potential **0.013 xG** against you."* That is the Swiss National League, five-on-five, manually tracked, on Chatel's own model, at one place on the ice. ⚠️ **He concluded the common wisdom overstates the blue-line risk** — *"nobody think about the dozens of possessions just handed over to the other team when you are dumping the puck"* — and at the moment of decision his numbers put the dump at **0.012 xG** against you and the controlled attempt at **0.008**. That compares a dump-in with a carry-in, not with the forced pass with nobody back that the "never" list rules out. Read it as an argument for entering in control where you can, and as a reason to keep this row at **Moderate**.

> **Honest caveat: that ordering is coaching consensus, not measurement.** The grades are reasoned from the shot-danger evidence and from how long a turnover takes to become a shot on your net. Only the offensive blue line has a tracked turnover price, from one Swiss-league dataset, and **nothing found ranks the five against one another.** One candidate **could not be read** — Schulte and colleagues' 2017 Markov game model for valuing actions and locations in ice hockey is closed-access, with no open copy indexed by Semantic Scholar or OpenAlex — so this means "not found, and one candidate unread", not "no such ranking exists". Trust the order of the rows more than the labels.

Laid over the ice, those five rows are one picture — and **the grade does not simply fall with distance from your own net.** The middle of the neutral zone is shaded as heavily as your own end.

![](diagram:the-risk-map)

**The shading does not survive black-and-white:** "their end", the only region shaded blue, prints darker than the faintest red band once the colour is gone, so on a printout take the order from the labels. **Nothing on the map is painted on a real rink** — the edges exist only to show each row as a patch of ice, and the blue region runs from their faceoff dots to the end boards, so it also covers the ice in front of their net, which the table's row does not name.

**One more limit: the map has no sense of direction**, and a turnover's direction changes its cost as much as its location does.

### Why north turnovers are so much worse than south turnovers

```facts
Key: A turnover has a direction, and the direction changes the cost more than the location does — coaching consensus rather than a measured comparison, since neither ranking has been counted
Risk: A north turnover — losing it while it travels toward your own net — makes the turnover and the counterattack the same event
Risk: A south turnover deep in their end separates the turnover from the counterattack by four or five seconds, which is enough to get organised
Action: Weigh the direction of a risky play as well as its location — a blind pass up your own wall is bad, the same pass in their end is a nothing play
```

A turnover has a direction, and the direction changes the cost more than the location does — **coaching consensus rather than a measured comparison**, on the same footing as the risk map above, since neither ranking has been counted. The mechanism is what to carry:

- **A "north" turnover** — you lose the puck while it is travelling toward your own net, or you lose it to an opponent already facing your net. Their momentum is toward your goal. Your momentum is away from it. Every body on the ice is instantly on the wrong side of the puck. The turnover and the counterattack are the same event.
- **A "south" turnover** — you lose the puck deep in the offensive zone, moving away from your net. Their momentum is toward you, which means it points at up to 178 feet of ice they have to cross while your five players get back — goal line to goal line on a 200-foot rink, because **NHL Rule 1.5** places each goal line 11 feet from the end boards. The turnover and the counterattack are separated by four or five seconds, which is enough time to be organised.

**Practical consequence:** the direction of a risky play matters as much as the location. A blind pass up the wall out of your own zone is a bad idea; the *same* blind pass in the offensive zone is a nothing play. This asymmetry is the reason the "never" list below is almost entirely about your own end.

## The "never" list — and why each one exists

**Read the reason, not the rule.** Every item below is a rule with a mechanism, and the mechanism is what lets you handle the case nobody warned you about.

**Two of the nine are named in a rulebook: number 9, the puck over the glass, and number 7, leaving your feet to hit, which all six books this guide covers write into the charging offence** (NHL 42.1, IIHF 42.1, USA Hockey 607, Hockey Canada 7.4, the Professional Women's Hockey League (PWHL) 42.1, CARHA 52(a)). The other seven are coaching conventions — near-universally taught, but conventions — and some carry a secondary penalty risk, noted where it applies.

### 1. Never pass D-to-D through the slot. Go behind the net instead — a coaching convention, not a rule

```facts
Never: Pass D-to-D through your own slot — an interception leaves the interceptor in the slot facing your goalie
Action: Take the D-to-D pass behind the net instead — a longer route by maybe eight feet, and half a second slower
Risk: A failure behind the goal line is a battle behind the net rather than a shot from twenty feet
Convention: Keeping D-to-D out of the slot is a coaching convention, not a rule — some teams allow a D-to-D exchange high in the zone, above the tops of the circles
Action: Find out where your coach draws the line on a D-to-D exchange
```

**D-to-D** means one defenceman passing to their partner. In your own zone, the two obvious routes are *through* the front of the net or *behind* it.

**Why not through.** A pass through your own slot travels across the highest-danger area on the ice, in front of the net, at knee-to-stick height, through the exact space where a forechecker is standing. If it is intercepted, the interceptor is already in the slot facing your goalie with the puck. That is not a chance against. That is a goal.

**What to do instead.** Take it behind the net. It is a longer route by maybe eight feet, it takes half a second longer, and a failure there produces a battle behind the goal line rather than a shot from twenty feet.

![](diagram:breakout-d-to-d)

*Coaching convention.* Some teams do allow a D-to-D exchange high in the zone, above the tops of the circles, far enough out that an interception is not a slot chance — [Rink Map](../foundation/rink_map.md) has that geometry. Know where your coach draws the line.

### 2. Never make a blind backhand pass up the middle of your own zone — a coaching convention, not a rule

```facts
Never: Make a blind backhand pass up the middle of your own zone — the worst tool, the worst information and the worst location in one motion
Risk: The backhand arrives slowly and imprecisely, and blind means you do not know whether the target is a teammate or an opponent
Action: Turn your shoulders and take the extra half-second to look
Options: Put it on the boards so a mistake becomes a battle, or use the glass if you genuinely have no time
Convention: No rule forbids the blind backhand up the middle — it is about as close to universally taught as coaching gets, and the claim that it is a top source of amateur goals is coaching observation, not measurement
```

A **blind pass** is one made without seeing the target — you throw it to where you assume someone is.

**Why not.** Three failures compound: the backhand is your least accurate and least powerful side, blind means you do not know whether you are passing to a teammate or an opponent, and the middle of your own zone is where an interception costs most — the worst tool, the worst information and the worst location in one motion. Coaches name it as one of the commonest sources of amateur goals against. **That is coaching observation, not measurement.** Failed exits are tracked and priced — the table earlier on this page, and the zone-exit tracking quoted later — but **no published breakdown found for this document ties a goal against to the defending mistake that let it in**; the two goal-breakdown sources read are both NHL, one a single hand-tracker's data, and [Neutral Zone Systems](../systems/neutral_zone_systems.md) sets them out ([Defensive Zone Coverage](../systems/defensive_zone_coverage.md) makes the same point). Every dataset named here is professional, and nothing cited ranks amateur mistakes against one another — ⚠️ **so let the reasoning persuade you, not the ranking.**

**What to do instead.** Turn your shoulders and take the extra half-second to look, or put it on the boards where a mistake becomes a battle rather than a chance. If you genuinely have no time, the glass is always there.

*Coaching convention.* No rule forbids it. It is as close to universally taught as coaching gets, and I am aware of no system that asks for it.

### 3. Never make a cross-ice pass in your own defensive zone — a coaching convention, not a rule

```facts
Never: Make a cross-ice pass in your own defensive zone — it crosses every forechecking stick and spends the longest of any pass in the air
Risk: If it is picked off, it is picked off by someone whose momentum already carries them toward your net
Risk: Even when it works it usually puts your teammate under immediate pressure facing the boards
Action: Move the puck north, or move it behind the net
Action: If the only outlet is on the far side, go D-to-D behind the goal line and then north — two low-risk passes beat one high-risk one
Convention: Nothing in the rulebook touches the cross-ice pass in your own zone — some teams accept it above the tops of the circles, almost nobody below them
```

**Why not.** An **east–west** pass in your own end crosses every forechecking stick on the ice, travels perpendicular to the direction you want to go, and if it is picked off it is picked off by someone whose momentum already carries them toward your net. It also takes the longest time in the air of any pass available to you, which means the most time for someone to read it.

There is one more cost people miss: even when it *works*, a cross-ice pass in your own end usually puts your teammate under immediate pressure facing the boards, because the forecheck slid across with the puck. You took a big risk to create a worse situation.

**What to do instead.** Move the puck north or move it behind the net. If the only outlet is genuinely on the far side, go D-to-D behind the goal line first and *then* north — two low-risk passes beat one high-risk one.

![](diagram:cross-ice-in-your-own-end)

*Coaching convention.* Nothing in the rulebook touches this. Some teams will accept a cross-ice pass above the tops of the circles, where an interception is not a slot chance; almost nobody accepts one below them.

### 4. Never both defencemen to the puck — a coaching convention, not a rule

```facts
Never: Send both defencemen to the same puck — one goes, the other stays home, because committing both puts 100% of your defensive-zone depth into a single 50/50 battle
Risk: Both defencemen to the same puck with nobody left in front of your net, and losing it leaves literally no one between the puck and your goalie but forwards facing the wrong way
Action: One goes and takes the puck — or the body, only where your league permits checking; the partner covers the space it will squirt into, staying between puck and net
Action: Say out loud which of you is going and which of you has the net
Convention: Sending both defencemen to the same puck has real exceptions — under a strong-side overload some coaches put both low along the boards and give the slot to a forward instead; that is one coaching account rather than anything counted, so ask what your team plays
Key: Somebody must be home; what varies is who — find out what your team plays
```

**Why not.** If both of you attack the same puck, you have committed 100% of your defensive-zone depth to a 50/50 battle. Win it and you gain nothing you would not have gained with one of you. Lose it and there is no one — literally no one — between the puck and your goalie except forwards who are facing the wrong way.

**What to do instead.** One goes, one supports. The one who goes takes the puck — **or the body, only where your league permits checking; where it is barred, take the ice they need and play the puck instead**, and see [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md) for which of those you are under. The partner reads the likely outcome and covers the space it will squirt into, staying between the puck and the net. Talk about it out loud — see [On-Ice Communication](../foundation/on_ice_communication.md).

![](diagram:never-both-defencemen-to-the-puck)

*Coaching convention, with real exceptions.* Under a **strong-side overload** some coaches put **both** defencemen low along the boards and hand the **slot** to a forward — one coaching account rather than anything counted, so a reason to ask, not a licence to assume. **Somebody must be home; what varies is who.** This guide's default is a **low zone collapse (zone) coverage**, with the strong-side defenceman pressuring and the weak-side defenceman holding the net front; [Defensive Zone Coverage](../systems/defensive_zone_coverage.md) sets out who has the net front under each system. Find out what your team plays.

### 5. Never force a low-percentage pass at the offensive blue line with no support back — a coaching convention, not a rule

```facts
Never: Force a low-percentage pass at the offensive blue line with no support back
Risk: The cost of the failure is not the loss of possession, it is the odd-man rush — one touch turns your five-man attack into their three-man rush
Read: If this pass is intercepted right now, how many teammates are behind the puck? Fewer than two, do not make it
Options: Chip the puck past the defender into the space behind them and race, or dump it into the far corner and forecheck
Risk: Low-cost is not free — on one Swiss-league sample a dump-in carried a larger immediate threat against you than a carry-in did, so dump when the pass is not on, not instead of a carry you could make
Convention: Forcing that pass is not a rule and genuinely negotiable — a team trailing late, or built to attack in waves, will accept the risk deliberately
```

**Why not.** The offensive blue line is where your attack is at its most stretched — your forwards are ahead of the puck, your defencemen have often stepped up to hold the zone. A turnover there converts your five-man attack into their three-man rush in one touch. **The cost of the failure is not the loss of possession; it is the odd-man rush.**

**What to do instead.** **Chip the puck past the defender into the space behind them** and race for it, or **dump it into the far corner** and forecheck. Both concede possession about 114 feet from your net, going south — the offensive blue line is 64 feet from their goal line (**NHL Rule 1.5**). **Low-cost is not free:** on the Swiss-league tracking quoted under the risk map, a dump attempt carried a *larger* immediate threat against you than a carry-in — small figures, quoted there with their scope — because the dump is so often handed straight back. These are the answer when the pass is not on, not a reason to give up a puck you could have carried in. See [Zone Entries](../systems/zone_entries.md) for which to pick.

**The test to run:** *if this pass is intercepted right now, how many of my teammates are behind the puck?* If the answer is fewer than two, do not make it.

*Coaching convention.* Not a rule, and genuinely negotiable: a team that is trailing late, or one built to attack in waves, will accept this risk deliberately. See the game-state table below.

### 6. Never stickhandle in your own slot — a coaching convention, not a rule

```facts
Never: Stickhandle in your own slot — stickhandling buys time, and there is no time to buy and nothing worth buying it for
Risk: Head down, surrounded, in the highest-danger area — you either gain nothing or concede everything
Action: Take the puck out of the slot first — one stride to the wall, behind the net, or a hard shot of it off the glass
Key: Change the location first, then make the decision
Convention: "Never stickhandle in your own slot" is not a rule — about as close to unanimous as coaching advice gets, but it is still advice
```

**Why not.** Stickhandling is a play that buys time. In your own slot there is no time to buy and nothing worth buying it for. Your head is down, you are surrounded, you are in the highest-danger area on the ice, and the only outcomes are "you get out of it having gained nothing" or "you lose it having conceded everything."

**What to do instead.** Take the puck out of the slot before you do anything with it — one stride to the wall, behind the net, or a hard shot of it off the glass. **Change the location first, then make the decision.**

*Coaching convention.* Not a rule. It is about as close to unanimous as coaching advice gets, but it is still advice.

### 7. Never leave your feet to make a check

```facts
Never: Leave your feet to make a check — you cannot change direction in the air — and never body check at all where your league bars it
Risk: A carrier who reads it makes you miss with one movement, and you are out of the play for four or five seconds
Action: Skate, stay on your feet and keep your stick in the lane — take away the option rather than the puck — hit only where your league permits checking
Rule: All six books here write the leap into the charging offence by name — NHL 42.1, IIHF 42.1, USA Hockey 607, Hockey Canada 7.4, PWHL 42.1, CARHA 52(a)
Rule: A charge can end your game in every book here, and an injury can make it mandatory — NHL and PWHL 42.5 and EIHL Casebook 42 (a major that hurts the face or head), Hockey Canada 7.4(b) and CARHA 52(a) (any injury); USA Hockey 607(b) if reckless, and plan on a match (602(a))
Rule: Hockey Canada 7.4(c): charge an opponent "in such a way that they are unable to defend themselves", or charge to injure, and it is a match penalty — no injury needed
Rule: Hockey Canada Rule 7.4 Interpretation 1 defines the jump as the feet leaving the ice before contact — skates that come off after contact, during an otherwise legal check, are not charging — that is Hockey Canada's own interpretation, and no other book here writes it
Rule: IIHF Rule 48.1(IV) makes jumping to deliver a blow to the head or neck a separate offence — 48.2 sets the floor at a minor for any illegal check to the head or neck, and 48.3 is the ceiling, where the referee may assess a major and an automatic game misconduct for reckless endangerment
Technique: Blocking a shot is a separate question, and the coached default is a standing block — close with your feet, not by lunging, head out of the lane, and never turn your back or sideways
Convention: On a 2-on-1 the slide is system-dependent — where your job is holding the passing lane, sliding early abandons it; some teams coach a timed slide across the lane as the method itself, so ask which yours plays
Key: The penalty risk of leaving your feet is real and primary, and the positional cost sits on top of it — down is out of the play
```

**Why not.** You cannot change direction in the air. A puck carrier who reads it makes you miss with one movement, and you are now on the ice behind the play, out of it entirely, for four or five seconds. The upside is small — best case you break up one play — and the downside is that you have removed yourself from the defensive structure at the precise moment it needed five players.

**What to do instead.** Skate. Stay on your feet, keep your stick in the lane, take away the option rather than the puck. A defender on their feet with a good stick beats a defender sliding with a great one. **A body check is only for a league that permits checking; where it is barred, this is still how you close a carrier down — feet on the ice, stick in the lane, no hit.**

*Part coaching convention, part rule — and the two halves are worth separating.*

- **Leaving your feet to check someone *is* itself named as an offence, whatever you may have been told.** All six books this guide covers name the leap in their charging rule. **NHL Rule 42.1** penalises a player who *"skates, **jumps into** or charges an opponent in any manner"*; **PWHL 42.1** uses the same sentence, and **IIHF 42.1** — the book British hockey plays under — makes *"**jumps to check an opponent**"* the first branch of its definition. **USA Hockey 607** includes *"**leaving one's feet (jumping)** into the opponent to deliver a check"*, **Hockey Canada 7.4** lists *"**Jumps to check an opponent.**"* first, and **CARHA 52(a)** reaches *"any player who runs or jumps into, or charges an opponent"*. **Hockey Canada alone defines the leap:** Interpretation 1 to 7.4 makes it a jump when *"a player's feet leave the ice **prior to** making body contact with their opponent"*, and not when they come off *"**after** contact is made with their opponent, during an otherwise legal check"*. ⚠️ **The offence is the takeoff, not the follow-through** — be on your skates at the moment of contact. No other book here spells that out, so do not carry it into a game played under them as settled.
- **A charge can end your game in every book here,** ⚠️ **so do not read the NHL's "minor or major" as its ceiling.** Under the **NHL and the PWHL**, a major for a charge that injures the face or head carries a game misconduct that *"shall be imposed"* (Rule 42.5 in both; the **EIHL Casebook** prints the same at its own Rule 42), and a deliberate attempt to injure is a mandatory match penalty under **Rule 21.1** in both, whatever 42.4's *"may"* says. **Hockey Canada 7.4(b)** makes a major and game misconduct mandatory when an otherwise-minor charge injures, any charging major carries the game misconduct (7.4(e)), and **7.4(c)** is a match penalty for charging an opponent *"in such a way that they are unable to defend themselves"* or trying to injure. **CARHA 52(a)**: ⚠️ **"If injury results, a Major penalty and a Game Misconduct penalty shall be assessed"**; its discretionary major ejects you even when nobody is hurt (Rule 30(a)), and a charge meant to injure is a match with no discretion (**48(a)**). **USA Hockey 607** starts at a minor plus a misconduct and makes a major plus game misconduct mandatory for a charge that *"recklessly endangers an opponent"* (607(b)) — and plan on a match there (602(a)). The **IIHF** lets the referee give a major and game misconduct for a reckless charge (42.4). Treat the penalty risk as **real and primary**, on top of the positional cost below.
- **Blocking a shot or a pass is a separate question, and the coached default is a *standing* block, not a slide.** [Body Contact and Battles → Shot Blocking](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md) covers the method: get close, square your padded surfaces to the puck, keep your feet on the ice and your head out of the lane, hands behind your body, never turn your back or sideways, and don't lead with your stick, because a puck off the blade goes upward (coaching craft rather than anything measured). **Close with your feet, not by lunging** — a slide that misses leaves you out of the play with no way to recover. A sliding block is a choice for a specific moment, and wrong when you are screening your own goalie. **On a 2-on-1 it depends on your system:** where your job is the passing lane, sliding early abandons it; some teams coach a timed slide across the lane as the method itself. Either way, slide at the *pass*, never sprawl at the *shot*. See [Defending the Rush](../systems/defending_the_rush.md).
- **And even with no penalty in prospect, down is out of the play.** That reason applies on every leap, including the ones no referee punishes, on top of the penalty risk.
- **Leave your feet near an opponent's head and it stops being a charging question.** **IIHF Rule 48.1(IV)** makes it a separate offence — *"A Player who **jumps (leaves their skates)** to deliver a blow to the head or neck of an opponent"*. ⚠️ **The floor is a minor (48.2), and the referee may give a major and an automatic game misconduct where the player *"recklessly endangers their opponent"* (48.3)** — so a jump at the head the referee does not judge reckless is still not free.

### 8. Never change with the puck in your defensive zone, or when the play is coming at you — a coaching convention, not a rule; the rulebook governs how you change, not when

```facts
Never: Change with the puck in your defensive zone, or when the play is coming at you
Risk: A line change is the most vulnerable four seconds your team has — the main risk is the opponent attacking four-on-five
Action: Change when the puck is deep in their end, when your team has clear possession in the neutral zone, or at a stoppage
Mindset: If you are tired and stuck in your own zone, you are not tired enough to change. Get the puck out first
Rule: A departing player must be within five feet of the bench and out of the play before the substitute steps on, or it is a bench minor for too many men (NHL 74.1; PWHL 76.1)
Convention: No rule forbids changing on the fly because the moment is tactically bad — 74.1 governs only how the exchange is made. The rules that do bar a change are triggered by an event at a stoppage, such as your own icing, and not by the danger
```

**Why not.** A **line change** is the most vulnerable four seconds your team has: for part of it you have players who are effectively not in the play. Under NHL **Rule 74.1** and **PWHL 76.1**, a departing player must be within five feet of the bench and out of the play before the substitute steps on, and a bench minor for **too many men** follows if the two overlap in any meaningful way. But the penalty is not the main risk — the main risk is that the opponent attacks four-on-five into a zone where you have no margin.

**What to do instead.** Change when the puck is deep in *their* end, when your team has clear possession in the neutral zone, or at a stoppage. If you are tired and stuck in your own zone, **you are not tired enough to change.** Get the puck out first, then change on the fly as the play moves north. See [Game Management](../systems/game_management.md).

*Coaching convention, with a rule attached to one failure mode.* No rule forbids changing **on the fly** because the moment is tactically bad; Rule 74.1 governs only *how* the exchange is made. The rules that do bar a change are triggered by an event at a stoppage, not by danger: your own icing (NHL and IIHF 81.4, PWHL 83.4), your own goalkeeper freezing a puck the other team shot in from their side of the centre red line (NHL and IIHF 63.8, PWHL 65.8), and, in Hockey Canada's Junior and Senior divisions, an unintentional puck out of play (6.4(a)). The icing and goalkeeper-freeze bars still let you replace an injured player.

### 9. Never shoot the puck over the glass — from your own zone it is a delay-of-game minor under the NHL, IIHF and PWHL, and a deliberate one is a minor from anywhere

```facts
Rule: Shooting or batting the puck directly out of the playing surface from your defending zone is a minor, except where there is no glass (NHL Rule 63.2(iii); IIHF 63.2(III); PWHL 65.2(iii))
Rule: Wider than the NHL's strict-liability limb: a deliberate shoot-out of the playing area is a minor with no zone limit under the NHL and IIHF (63.2(ii)), USA Hockey (610(c)), Hockey Canada (10.1(ii)), CARHA (75(b)) and PWHL (65.2(ii))
Rule: A shoot-out after the whistle is named by the NHL and IIHF at 63.2(ii), USA Hockey at 610(c) and PWHL 65.2(ii); Hockey Canada at 11.1 preamble item (v) Unsportsmanlike Conduct, not Rule 10.1; USA Hockey escalates it too — 601(c)(10) a misconduct, 601(d)(7) a game misconduct in protest of a call
Rule: "Directly (non-deflected)" is the operative wording — a puck deflected off a stick, a glove or a body on its way out is not a penalty (NHL Rule 63.2(iii))
Rule: What counts is where the puck was when you hit it, not where it crossed the glass (NHL Rule 63.2(iii))
Rule: Over the glass at the bench is penalised — "behind" it under NHL 63.2(iii) and PWHL 65.2(iii), "surrounding" it under IIHF 63.2(III); only a puck shot straight in where there is no glass escapes — clearing the glass into the bench is a minor (IIHF Handbook 63.24)
Rule: Directly off a faceoff, NHL and IIHF 85.1, PWHL 87.1 and the 63.2(iii) Note assess no penalty — none says whether that covers one put out on purpose, and 63.2(ii) reaches a deliberate one anywhere; USA Hockey, Hockey Canada and CARHA write no draw exemption
Rule: Do not rely on a skate clear under the IIHF — its Rulebook 63.2(III) names only "hand or stick" in 2025/26 and 2026/27 alike, but 63.2(II) reaches a deliberate one by foot from anywhere; its Handbook answers Yes to a kick over the glass (Situation 63.34, all three editions), so clear with the stick
Rule: USA Hockey 610(c) is deliberate-only and 631(d) makes an accidental one a last-play faceoff; Hockey Canada 10.1(ii) is deliberate-only but not skaters-only — its 6.3(e)(i) Note 1 sends "any player of either team" to that limb — and 10.1(v) writes no deliberateness word into the goaltender's limb
Rule: Hockey Canada 10.1(v) and CARHA 55(a) put a minor on a goaltender with no deliberateness word, but both exempt the accident — Interpretation 5(i) and Note 1 free a puck put out where there is no glass, or one that "touches the glass or deflects off a player or official"
Rule: USA Hockey 610(c) reaches "any player or goalkeeper" who deliberately shoots or bats the puck outside the playing area — the deliberate-clear limb is not skaters-only under that book
Rule: Even where no penalty applies, the faceoff goes to the nearest spot in the zone the puck was shot from (NHL Rule 85.1) — and in Hockey Canada's Junior and Senior divisions an accidental one also costs you your line change (6.4(a))
Action: Off the glass and out — hard, low and along the boards so it stays in; if you are panicking, aim lower than feels right
Action: Read your own league's book — under CARHA's rec rules a skater's puck-over-glass is a minor only when deliberate, and that is one association's rules, not a rec-hockey standard
```

**This is an actual rule, not a convention.** NHL **Rule 63.2(iii)** imposes a minor penalty on any player who "shoots or bats (using his hand or his stick) the puck directly (non-deflected) out of the playing surface from his defending zone, except where there is no glass." 🇬🇧 **IIHF Rule 63.2(III)** — a British reader's rule, not a North American footnote — and **PWHL 65.2(iii)** say the same. Four details matter on the ice:

- **"Directly (non-deflected)" is doing real work.** If the puck clips a stick, a glove, a shoulder or a teammate on the way out, there is no penalty — which is why so many pucks that leave the rink are waved off.
- **The determining factor is where the puck was when you hit it,** not where it crossed the glass. Touch it inside your blue line and it goes out, and you are going to the box.
- **Over the glass at the bench is penalised.** The NHL and PWHL say glass *"behind"* the bench; the IIHF says *"surrounding"* it, which appears to reach the sides as well — an apparent widening that no source found here settles. Only a puck shot straight **into** the bench where there is no glass escapes: the IIHF Situation Handbook gives a minor for one that clears the glass and drops into the bench (Situation 63.24). ⚠️ **So the bench is not a target** — aim at it under pressure and you are aiming at the penalty you were trying to avoid.
- **Never aim a draw over the glass.** NHL and IIHF **Rule 85.1**, the **63.2(iii) Note** and PWHL **87.1** assess no penalty when the puck goes out directly off a faceoff, but none says whether that covers one put out on purpose, and the deliberate-clear rule reaches one *"from anywhere on the ice surface"* — so do not test it. USA Hockey, Hockey Canada and CARHA write no draw exemption at all.

**The second limb is the one most players have never heard of.** **Rule 63.2(ii)** — **IIHF 63.2(II)** carries the same clause in the plural — assesses the same minor *"on any player who deliberately shoots or bats (using his hand, leg, foot, arm, or stick, etc.) the puck outside the playing area (from anywhere on the ice surface) during the play or after a stoppage of play"*. ⚠️ **So outside your defending zone you are safe only if it was an accident** — a deliberate flip out of the neutral or attacking zone to kill a shift, or a puck fired away in temper after the whistle, is two minutes. **All six books reach the deliberate clear, with no zone limit:** PWHL 65.2(ii), USA Hockey 610(c), Hockey Canada 10.1(ii) and CARHA 75(b) as well. The PWHL's shorter list — *"(using her hand or her stick)"* — is no licence to kick it out, because its 65.2(i) reaches a player who deliberately causes a stoppage with *"her stick, skates or body"*.

Under the IIHF, ⚠️ **do not clear it with your skate either.** The Rulebook's 63.2(III) names only *"their hand or stick"*, but the Situation Handbook answers *"Yes"* — a minor — for a defender who kicks the puck over the glass (Situation 63.34, in all three editions held here), and since v1.1 also for one who kicks at a pass and sends it off the foot over the glass (Situation 63.19, reversing v1.0's *"No"*). Neither Situation cites a rule, so **whether the Handbook is applying 63.2(II)'s foot clause or reading (III) more widely is stated nowhere.** It turns on whether *"deliberately"* describes the kick or the puck leaving the ice; neither book defines it, and the IIHF's own material points both ways. The Handbook is narrower elsewhere — it excuses a goalkeeper's reactive stick save over the glass (Situation 63.29) — and it ranks itself below the Rulebook: *"in case of any contradictions, the valid IIHF Rulebook is always the basis"*. None of these differences is explained. **None of it is a gap to play into: clear with the stick**, which is right on every reading. *(The NHL's situation handbook is not held here and has not been checked.)*

**After the whistle, every book reaches the act, though not all in the delay-of-game rule.** The NHL, the IIHF, USA Hockey (610(c)) and the PWHL write it there; CARHA 75(b) and Hockey Canada 10.1(ii) do not. **Hockey Canada puts it under Unsportsmanlike Conduct** — Rule 11.1's item (v), *"Shooting the puck out of the playing area, after the whistle"* — a minor at the referee's discretion (11.1(a)), then a misconduct (11.1(d)) and a game misconduct (11.1(e)) for persisting, and each carries a Note saying the referee need not give the lower penalty first. ⚠️ **So under Hockey Canada too, the puck fired away in temper can end your night.** Rule 10.1(a)'s minor for a player *"deliberately delaying the game"* reaches it as well. Either way, assume the two minutes are there.

Under USA Hockey ⚠️ **the temper shot after the whistle is the one that gets you thrown out.** 610(c)'s minor is the floor. **601(c)(10)** makes it a **misconduct** when not done in protest; in protest of a call, **601(d)(7)** makes it a **game misconduct**; in the direction of an official or a spectator, **601(d)(9)** is a game misconduct, and the Note to 601(d) puts a **match penalty** above that where it recklessly endangers them. **601(a)(4)** adds an unsportsmanlike minor for an avoidable shot after the whistle even when the puck stays in, and 601(a) escalates for persisting. The misconduct is ten minutes unless your affiliate has set a shorter time for shorter periods — Rule 404(a)'s Note authorises that, but not for adults. USA Hockey does not say which of 610(c) and 601(c)(10) governs a given puck, and none of the Casebook situations under 601 addresses it. **The NHL and the IIHF write no escalation aimed at this act**, so one puck over the glass after the whistle is 63.2(ii)'s two minutes. ⚠️ **Persisting is another matter:** IIHF 75.4(I) and 75.5(I) make a misconduct and then a game misconduct of persisting in conduct already penalised, and NHL 75.4(v) states the same progression as the usual course. Neither book has ruled on the puck case.

**The PWHL writes the NHL's strict-liability limb; USA Hockey and Hockey Canada do not.** **USA Hockey 610(c)** reaches *"any player **or goalkeeper**"*, but only one who deliberately puts the puck out, and **Rule 631(d)** makes an accidental one a last-play faceoff in the zone it was shot from — so under that book the panicked clear costs you a defensive-zone draw, not two minutes. **Hockey Canada 10.1(ii)** is deliberate-only, from anywhere, and ⚠️ **it is not skaters-only:** the Glossary counts the goaltender as a player, and Rule 6.3(e)(i) Note 1 sends *"any player of either team"* to it — so a goaltender who throws the puck over the glass with the glove is inside it. **10.1(v)** then puts a minor on a goaltender who *"shoots or bats the puck directly out of the playing surface, with their stick"*, with no deliberateness word, and Interpretation 5 lists the goaltender's escapes — no glass, or a puck that *"touches the glass or deflects off a player or official"* — without intent among them. ⚠️ **Whether an accidental stick clear is therefore a penalty is a reading the book does not resolve**, so if you keep goal under Hockey Canada, do not count on it being free.

**In Hockey Canada's Junior and Senior divisions an accidental clear costs you your line change.** **Rule 6.4(a)**: where a defending player other than the goaltender *"unintentionally shoots, bats, throws, kicks, or otherwise directs the puck outside the playing surface, no penalty will be assessed, but the defending team may not make a line change"* — and tired legs are usually why you cleared it. A deliberate one is still 10.1(ii)'s minor (its Note 1). Interpretation 1 confines it to pucks last legally touched in the end zone, so a clear that deflects off a teammate in the neutral zone allows the change. **Outside Junior and Senior it does not apply**, and nowhere in the book is a skater's accidental clear a penalty. ⚠️ **"Junior and Senior" is a stream, not an age.** Hockey Canada's other bucket is *"minor and female hockey"*, the book never says which side senior female play falls on, and Rule 7.3 leaves *"any other divisions approved by a Member"* — so ask your Member which rules you are under rather than reading it off your age.

**Rec-league note:** in a rink with no glass along a stretch of boards, the rule does not apply there — and whether the strict-liability limb reaches you at all depends on your league's book. **CARHA**, one adult recreational book on hand, writes the puck-out-of-play minor for the **goaltender only**, at **Rule 55(a)** — *"A Minor penalty shall be assessed to any goalkeeper who shoots or bats the puck out of the playing surface with their stick"*, with no deliberateness word — and its other clause, **Rule 75(b)**, is deliberate-only. ⚠️ **75(b)'s *"any player"* includes the goaltender** under CARHA's Glossary, and it is the only clause reaching a puck **thrown** out; whether 55(a) displaces it for a goaltender's stick clear, the book does not say. **So for a CARHA skater an accidental clear over the glass out of your own end is a stoppage and a faceoff, not a penalty.** 55(a)'s Note 1 gives the goaltender the same escapes as the other books — no glass, or a puck that *"touches the glass or deflects off a player or official"*. That is one association's published rules, read here to show the limb is not universal — not evidence of how common either arrangement is. Read your own league's rule sheet. Even where there is no penalty, NHL **Rule 85.1** puts the faceoff at the nearest spot *in the zone the puck was shot from*, so you have bought yourself a defensive-zone draw for nothing.

**What to do instead.** Off the glass and out — hard, low, and along the boards so it stays in. The difference between a clear and a penalty is often a few degrees of stick blade. If you are panicking, aim lower than feels right.

## The "almost always right" list

```facts
Action: Off the glass and out when you are in trouble — it moves you from catastrophic to high, not to safe, so get organised once it lands
Action: Shoot on net with traffic — the worst realistic outcome is an offensive-zone faceoff or a puck retrieved in the corner
Action: Take the simple play under pressure — the give-and-go you can make with three seconds becomes a giveaway with one
Action: Put the puck deep when your line is tired — tired players make late decisions, and late decisions in your own end are how goals happen; that chain is reasoning, not a measured effect
Rule: A puck put in from your own side of the centre red line and frozen by their goalie leaves the faceoff in their zone and denies them a line change — NHL and IIHF Rule 63.8, whose only exceptions in that paragraph are an injured player and a penalty that changes either team's on-ice strength
Action: Take the safe option in the last minute of a period — give them nothing to walk off with
Priority: When you have time, make the possession play; when you do not, get it out — the differentiator is possession, not method
```

Every one of these is a low-variance play: modest upside, very small downside. **You will never be the story of the game for making them, and that is the point.**

- **Off the glass and out when you are in trouble.** You concede possession in the neutral zone — roughly 65 to 110 feet from your net, since your own blue line is 64 feet from your goal line (**NHL Rule 1.5**). The risk map grades that zone **High**, so this moves you from **catastrophic to high**, not to low, buying distance and the two or three seconds your five players need to get set. It is still overwhelmingly worth making — but the puck is landing where coaching material says odd-man rushes are made, so get organised rather than assuming the danger has passed.
- **Shot on net with traffic.** A shot that reaches the net with a body in front produces rebounds, deflections and scrambles, and the worst realistic outcome is a faceoff in the offensive zone or a puck retrieved in the corner. A pass attempted instead of that shot can be intercepted and go the other way. See [Shooting](../technique/shooting.md).
- **The simple play under pressure.** When you are being closed down, the difficulty of every option rises but the difficulty of the *simple* option rises least. The give-and-go you can make with three seconds becomes a giveaway with one.
- **Puck deep when your line is tired.** Tired players make late decisions, and late decisions in your own end are how goals happen — coaching craft reasoned from what fatigue does to judgement, not a measured effect; [Conditioning and Recovery](../off-the-ice/conditioning_and_recovery.md) sets out the physiology. There is a rule-based bonus too: under NHL **Rule 63.8** — 🇬🇧 and IIHF 63.8, the same rule at the same number — if you shoot the puck into their end **from your own side of the centre red line** and their goalie freezes it, the faceoff is in *their* zone and **the defending team may not change lines**, except *"to replace an injured player, or when a penalty has been assessed which affects the on-ice strength of either team"*. You have trapped their unit out there while you get fresh legs.
- **The safe option in the last minute of a period.** A goal conceded in the final minute leaves you no time to answer before the break, and the other team gets to walk off with it. The last 60 seconds of a period is when the cross-ice pass in your own end goes from "bad idea" to "unforgivable."

**The honest counterweight.** "Safe" is a default under pressure, not a philosophy. Alex Novet's work at Hockey Graphs, on Corey Sznajder's tracking of roughly **230,000 zone exits** — about half the games of the 2016-17 and 2017-18 NHL seasons, not both in full — found that **carry-outs and pass-outs led to a subsequent zone entry almost nine times out of ten, while dump-outs succeeded only about one time in five** — and that when a team *kept possession* on a dump-out, its success rate rose to the same roughly 89%. The differentiator is possession, not method. **When you have time, make the possession play; when you do not, get it out.**

## Decision hierarchies by zone

This is the most practical section here. Each list is a **priority order**: run down it and take the first option that is genuinely available. The point of a hierarchy is that it removes deliberation — you are not weighing five options, you are checking four boxes in order and stopping at the first "yes."

**The prerequisite.** None of this works if you start looking when the puck arrives. You need one or two shoulder checks *before* you receive it, so that by the time it is on your blade the list is already down to two options. That is what [Scanning and Anticipation](scanning_and_anticipation.md) is for. The hierarchy is the decision; the scan is the information.

### Defensive zone — you have just retrieved the puck, forecheck arriving

```facts
Priority: Quick-up → Up → Wheel → D-to-D → Reverse → Rim → Glass, taking the first option genuinely available
Key: The defensive-zone breakout hierarchy is for a retriever with no time to read, not a ranking of breakout plays
Action: Quick-up first — first look up ice, then down; the window closes before you finish checking the rest of the list
Read: Reverse beats a forechecker who has over-run you; rim beats one who is sealing you against the wall
Never: Take D-to-D through the slot. Go behind the goal line instead
Action: Nothing there? Take the hit — skates parallel, feet apart, knees bent, forearm and hip to the wall, head up, chin off your chest, never turn your back to the checker. Protect the puck, eat the faceoff
Never: Take the wall on the point of your shoulder, or on the whole shoulder if you can help it — and never duck: chin off your chest
Never: Solve a defensive-zone jam by putting the puck over the glass
```

This is the same order taught in [Defender](../positions/defender.md) — **Quick-up → Up → Wheel → D-to-D → Reverse → Rim → Glass** — with the reasoning attached. **It is a hierarchy for a retriever under pressure, not a ranking of breakout plays:** [Breakouts](../systems/breakouts.md) is the authority and is explicit that which breakout you run is chosen by reading the forecheck in front of you, not picked off a list of favourites. Use this list when there is no time to read anything.

![](diagram:breakout-up)

1. **Quick-up.** *Did we just win this puck, with their forwards still below it and no shape formed?* → Move it up ice on the first touch. [Breakouts](../systems/breakouts.md) calls this the lowest-risk high-reward play available, precisely because the two or three seconds after a retrieval are the least organised the opposition will ever be. It is first on the list because the window closes before you finish checking the rest of it. **First look up ice, then down.**
2. **Up.** *Is a teammate open going north on my side of the ice, with no opponent between us?* → Pass it. The fastest way out, and — with the carry-out — one of the two exits that keep possession.
3. **Wheel.** *Do I have a clear lane to skate it out myself — around behind the net and up the far side, or straight up my own side, with nobody between me and the neutral zone?* → Go. On the zone-exit tracking cited earlier, a carry-out and a pass-out both lead to a subsequent zone entry almost nine times out of ten, so a carry beats a pass only when the lane is genuinely clear.
4. **D-to-D.** *Is my defence partner open with time?* → Take it across **behind the goal line**, never through the slot. This changes the side of the ice and forces the forecheck to reset its angle.
5. **Reverse.** *Has the forechecker over-committed and skated past me?* → Send it **back against the flow** into the space they just left, to a partner or centre arriving behind you. A **reverse** and a **rim** are opposite plays chosen by the same read: reverse beats a forechecker who has over-run you; rim beats one who is sealing you against the wall. Getting this read wrong is one of the most common defensive-zone turnovers there is.
6. **Rim.** *Can I send it hard onward around the boards to my winger's side?* A **rim** goes onward around the boards, with the flow of play. A 50/50 puck on the wall is an acceptable outcome; it is a battle, not a chance.
7. **Glass and out.** *Can I get it off the glass and out?* → Do it. No shame in this at all.
8. **Nothing?** → Take the hit, protect the puck against the boards, and eat the faceoff — and **take the hit properly**: go into the wall on an angle if you can, skates parallel to it, feet apart and knees bent, forearm and hip to the wall — never the point of your shoulder, and not the whole shoulder if you can help it — head up and chin off your chest, **never turn your back to the checker**, and never duck. If the wall arrives with you already facing it and no time to turn, get your stick and gloves up on the glass, arms out, to spread the load; [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md) covers receiving a hit safely. Or ice it — the draw comes back to **your own** zone, and under the NHL, the IIHF and the PWHL you cannot change either (Rule 81.4 in the first two, 83.4 in the PWHL). USA Hockey writes no such bar (its Rule 624 never mentions changing), and Hockey Canada applies one only *"in categories of U18AAA, Junior, and at the option of the Member in Senior"* (Rule 6.7(d)); wherever it applies, an injured player can still be replaced. Icing here is bad, but a penalty and a power play is worse. **Never solve this by putting it over the glass.**

### Neutral zone — you are carrying through the middle third

![](diagram:centre-nz-get-it-to-the-outside)

```facts
Priority: Stretch pass behind their defence → winger with speed on the wall → middle lane with support → gain the line with control → chip or dump
Read: Anyone behind their defence? That is the highest upside available here, and it travels away from your own net
Action: Move it to a winger with speed going north and follow — north passes to moving targets are the safest productive play in this zone, which is coaching consensus rather than a counted ranking
Risk: Take the middle lane only with support — a turnover there creates an odd-man rush going the other way
Never: Carry back into the middle at your own blue line, or make an east–west pass across centre ice — go back as a regroup instead, giving the puck to a defenceman and across D-to-D
Convention: Naming the turn-back and the east-west pass as prime manufacturers of odd-man rushes is coaching consensus, not a counted comparison — no public data ranks odd-man rushes by the play that created them
Options: Nothing there? Chip the puck past their defenceman into space and race, or dump to the far corner and forecheck
```

1. **Is anyone behind their defence?** → Stretch pass or area pass into that space. Highest upside available in the neutral zone, and it travels away from your own net.
2. **Is a winger with speed available on the wall going north?** → Move it and follow. North passes to moving targets are the safest productive play in this zone — *coaching consensus reasoned from the risk map above, not a counted ranking* — which is why they rank above anything through the middle.
3. **Is the middle lane open with a teammate supporting?** → Take it, but only with support, and knowing you are choosing the highest-risk square on the risk map above. The middle of the neutral zone is graded **High** *specifically because* a turnover there creates an odd-man rush going the other way.
4. **Can I gain their blue line with control, with a teammate arriving?** → Carry wide, protect the puck on the outside, and drive the line.
5. **None of the above?** → Chip the puck past their defenceman into space and race, or dump to the far corner and forecheck.
6. **Never at any point in this list:** turn back into the middle of the ice at your own blue line with the puck on your stick, or make an east–west pass across centre ice. Coaching material names both as prime manufacturers of 2-on-1s against — *coaching consensus; no public data ranks odd-man rushes by the play that created them.* **That is not a ban on going backwards.** A **regroup** is the same retreat done properly: give the puck back to a defenceman, and keep the D-to-D flat and hard, never blind across the middle with a forechecker between you. [Neutral Zone Systems](../systems/neutral_zone_systems.md) owns it, and [Defending the Rush](../systems/defending_the_rush.md) lists neutral-zone turnovers as one of five ways an odd-man rush gets manufactured.

### Offensive zone — you have the puck below or at the tops of the circles

![](diagram:oz-half-wall-options)

```facts
Priority: Lane to the net with traffic → pass into the middle → puck to the net for a rebound → cycle → point with a screen → chip back deep
Key: Failure in the offensive zone is cheap and success there is worth more than anywhere else — the offensive zone is where you get to be dangerous
Action: Shoot the moment there is a lane to the net with a body in front of the goalie
Read: Rank by value times availability — but when the seam into the middle is genuinely there, it outranks everything
Action: Under pressure with support below you, take it behind the net or to the corner and cycle
Key: "The point is open" means a lane to the net, not just a lane to the defenceman
Never: Force it through their blue line on the way out — losing the puck in their corner is free, losing it at their line is a rush against
```

**The offensive zone is where you get to be dangerous.** The risk map says failure here is cheap; the expected-goals data says success here is worth more than anywhere else. Both point the same way.

1. **Is there a lane to the net with a body in front of the goalie?** → Shoot. Now.
2. **Is there a pass into the middle available — the slot, the backdoor, or across the middle of the ice?** → Make it. Chatel's tracking valued it highest of the four plays it priced, at about **0.112 expected goals** — manually tracked in **two European professional leagues**, so take the ranking, not the decimals. A pass across the middle forces the goalie to move laterally and reset. **It ranks second only because a lane to the net with a body in front is available far more often**, a shot that misses costs almost nothing, and a middle pass that misses is the most common way an offensive-zone possession becomes a rush the other way. Rank by value *times* availability; when the seam is genuinely there, it outranks everything.
3. **Can I get the puck to the net for a rebound or deflection with traffic?** → Do it. Low-quality shots with a body in front beat high-quality passes that never happen.
4. **Am I under pressure with support below me?** → Take it behind the net or to the corner and cycle. You keep the puck, you keep them in their end, and you keep tiring them out.
5. **Is the point open with a clear lane and a screen set?** → Back to the point. But "open" means a lane to the *net*, not just a lane to the *defenceman*.
6. **Nothing at all?** → Chip it back deep and re-forecheck. **Do not force it through their blue line on the way out.** Losing the puck in their corner is free; losing it at their blue line with your defence pinched is a rush against.

## Risk and game state

```facts
Key: The correct risk level is not a personality trait — it moves with the scoreboard and the clock
Action: Leading by one in the third, take risk down and reach for the safe options earlier than usual; leading by three, down hard
Action: Trailing late, take risk up — pinch, activate, force the entry; not raising your risk when trailing is not being safe
Action: Shorthanded — fewer skaters because of a penalty — down and clear it hard — the one exception is a genuine 2-on-1 the other way
Action: On the power play, up inside their zone and down at the blue line — a shorthanded rush against four attackers is the worst-value event on a power play
Rule: NHL regular-season overtime is five minutes of 3-on-3 sudden death (Rule 84.1); playoff overtime is full 20-minute periods at 5-on-5 (Rule 84.5)
Convention: In 3-on-3 the convention is to be patient, to attack only with numbers, and to regroup rather than force an entry
Action: Empty net and trailing, risk up to the maximum — the only bad decision is a slow one
Action: Leading with their net empty, risk down to the minimum — but shoot it rather than hunting for a better play
Rule: With their net empty, a foul denying you the chance is an awarded goal, not a penalty shot (NHL 25.1, 57.4; IIHF 25.3; PWHL 58.4) — USA Hockey 616(b), Hockey Canada 4.12(a) and CARHA 36(a)(3) keep the from-behind test, though CARHA's broad 36(a)(4) may reach a foul from the front
```

The correct risk level is not a fixed personality trait. **It moves with the scoreboard and the clock.** Full treatment lives in [Game Management](../systems/game_management.md); here is how it maps onto the decisions above.

| Situation | Adjust risk | What actually changes |
|---|---|---|
| **Leading by one, third period** | **Down** | A goal against gives them the game back; a goal for only extends a lead you already have. Reach for options 4–6 in the defensive-zone hierarchy earlier than usual. |
| **Leading by three** | **Down hard** | Nothing you can do with a creative play is worth what a comeback would cost. Manage the puck, forecheck, get changes. |
| **Trailing late** | **Up** | The arithmetic inverts. A goal against when you are down two costs you almost nothing; a goal for is worth everything. Pinch, activate, force the entry, take the low-percentage pass. |
| **Tied, early** | **Neutral** | Play the hierarchies straight. |
| **Shorthanded** | **Down, with one exception** | You have fewer skaters because of a penalty, so a turnover in your own end is worse than usual. Clear it and clear it hard. The exception is a genuine 2-on-1 the other way. |
| **On the power play** | **Up in their zone, down at the blue line** | You can afford creativity inside their zone. You cannot afford a turnover at the offensive blue line: a shorthanded rush against four attackers is the worst-value event on a power play. |
| **Overtime (NHL regular season)** | **Situation-dependent, high stakes** | Five minutes of **3-on-3**, sudden death (**NHL Rule 84.1**). Every turnover in that much space is an odd-man rush, and there is no next shift to fix it. |
| **Last minute of a period** | **Down** | See the "almost always right" list. Give them nothing to walk off with. |
| **Empty net, you are trailing** | **Up to the maximum** | There is no such thing as too risky. The only bad decision is a slow one. |
| **Empty net, you are leading** | **Down to the minimum, but shoot it** | Take the shot rather than hunting for a better play. **A foul on you here can be an awarded goal rather than a penalty shot, and which one depends on your rulebook.** See below. |

**Four of those rows carry more than a row can hold.**

- **Trailing late.** A player who does not raise their risk when trailing is not being safe, they are being unhelpful.
- **Shorthanded.** A genuine 2-on-1 the other way while shorthanded is one of the highest-value plays in hockey, and a shorthanded goal is worth chasing when you have actually *read* it — which is the anticipation-versus-gambling test in the next section, not a licence to leave early.
- **Overtime.** In the 3-on-3 the convention is to be patient, to attack only with numbers, and to regroup rather than force an entry — a convention rather than anything in the book. And **playoff overtime is a different game**, written at **NHL Rule 84.5**: full 20-minute periods at 5-on-5, sudden death. That is regulation hockey with the cost of a mistake set to maximum.
- **Empty net, you are leading.** Under **NHL Rules 25.1 and 57.4**, **IIHF Rule 25.3** and **PWHL 58.4**, if you have possession in the neutral or attacking zone with no defender between you and the empty net and you are fouled, the goal is simply *awarded* — not a penalty shot. ⚠️ **USA Hockey 616(b) and Hockey Canada 4.12(a) keep the from-behind test there**, so under those two books a skater fouling you from the front is not an awarded goal. CARHA keeps it at 36(a)(3) too, but its broader 36(a)(4), where the goal *"will be awarded"*, may reach a foul from the front.

## Anticipation versus gambling

Jumping a passing lane, pinching at the offensive blue line, activating as a defenceman into the rush — these are all risks, and they are all necessary. Hockey played with no risk is hockey that never scores. The question is never *should I take risks*; it is **is this one anticipation or a gamble?**

Definitions first, because these three get conflated:

- **Pinch** — stepping *down* from the **offensive** blue line to keep a puck in their zone.
- **Step up** — challenging an attacker forward, in the neutral zone or at your own blue line.
- **Activate** — a defenceman joining the attack below the top of the circles in the offensive zone.

### The distinction

```facts
Key: Anticipation is acting on information; gambling is acting on hope — they look identical from the bench when they work
Read: You have information when the carrier's hips and shoulders turn toward the receiver, they look twice, and the receiver's stick is already presented as a target
Read: You counted their other options and there are none, or you have seen this player make this exact pass already tonight
Mindset: You are gambling if the honest answer is "it felt like a pass was coming"
```

**Anticipation is acting on information. Gambling is acting on hope.** They look identical from the bench when they work. They are completely different decisions.

You are anticipating if you can answer *why* before you move — the carrier's hips and shoulders turned toward the receiver, they looked at the receiver twice, the receiver's stick is already presented as a target, you counted their other options and there are none, or you have seen this player make this exact pass already tonight. The full cue list is in [Scanning and Anticipation](scanning_and_anticipation.md). You are gambling if the honest answer is "it felt like a pass was coming."

### The second test: who is behind you?

```facts
Read: Before you jump a lane, pinch or activate, ask not only "am I right?" but "what happens if I am right and I still miss it?"
Key: A pinch with your centre high in the zone behind you is a good pinch even if you lose the puck — the worst case is a 2-on-2
Risk: The same pinch with your centre below the goal line is a bad pinch even if you win the puck — you were one bounce from a 2-on-1
Priority: Three questions in half a second — do I have information, not just a feeling? is someone behind me if I miss? what does the scoreboard allow?
Action: Two yeses and a green light from the scoreboard question, go; otherwise hold your position, which is itself a decision
```

Even a well-read risk is a bad risk if nobody is covering the space you left. **Before you jump a lane, pinch or activate, the question is not only "am I right?" but "what happens if I am right and I still miss it?"**

A pinch with your centre high in the zone behind you is a good pinch even if you lose the puck, because the centre becomes the third defender and the worst case is a 2-on-2. The same pinch with your centre below the goal line is a bad pinch even if you win the puck, because you were one bad bounce from a 2-on-1 and you did not know it.

![](diagram:pinch-centre-high)

![](diagram:pinch-centre-below-the-goal-line)

Those two pictures are the same picture twice, differing only in where the centre is; what the pinching defenceman can see of the puck and the man on the wall is identical in both.

**So the full test, in three questions you can run in half a second:**

1. **Do I have information, not just a feeling?**
2. **If I miss, is someone behind me?**
3. **What does the scoreboard say about how much I can afford?**

Two yeses and a green light from the third: go. Otherwise, hold your position — which is itself a decision, and often the right one.

## The asymmetry of hockey mistakes

```facts
Key: A missed offensive chance costs you a chance; a defensive-zone turnover costs you a goal
Risk: Ten creative plays in their end with eight failures leaves you clearly ahead; ten in your own end with eight failures puts some in your net
Priority: In your own end simple is usually right — the downside is enormous and the upside of complexity is small
Priority: In their end creative is usually right — the downside is nearly zero and the upside is the most valuable event in hockey
Mindset: The players people call "smart" are not the ones who take the fewest risks, but the ones who take them in the right end of the rink
```

Here is the structural fact that makes hockey different from most team sports:

> **A missed offensive chance costs you a chance. A defensive-zone turnover costs you a goal.**

Those are not the same size. Not remotely. If you attempt ten creative plays in the offensive zone and eight fail, you have lost eight chances you never had in the first place and gained two you would not otherwise have had — you are clearly ahead. If you attempt ten creative plays in your own zone and eight fail, some of those eight are in your net.

At roughly **3.1 goals per team per game** in the 2025-26 NHL regular season (8,086 goals in 1,312 games), a single conceded goal is around a third of a team's normal scoring output. **One avoidable defensive-zone turnover can be worth more than an entire period of good offensive work.**

**This is the whole reason for the two-speed philosophy:**

- **In your own end: simple is usually right.** Not because simple is virtuous, but because the downside is enormous and the upside of complexity is small. The best possible outcome of a fancy play in your own zone is a clean breakout — which the boring play also achieves, most of the time.
- **In their end: creative is usually right.** Not because creativity is virtuous, but because the downside is nearly zero and the upside is the most valuable event in hockey.

The players people call "smart" are almost never the ones who take the fewest risks. **They are the ones who take their risks in the right end of the rink.**

## Penalty risk as a decision

A penalty is a decision like any other, and it has a price you can actually look up. **What follows is the arithmetic of that price** — what a minor costs, what a penalty shot is worth, and which fouls are not priced as minors at all. The game-state half of the topic — which score-and-clock situations make a penalty unforgivable, and what the published evidence actually says about why amateur players take them — belongs to [Game Management, "Discipline as Game Management"](../systems/game_management.md#discipline-as-game-management), and is not repeated here.

**In the 2025-26 NHL regular season, teams scored 1,595 power-play goals on 7,555 opportunities — a league-wide conversion rate of 21.1%** (aggregated across all 32 clubs from the NHL's public team power-play report; see Sources). Teams on the power play also conceded 205 shorthanded goals, about 2.7% of opportunities. So the honest arithmetic:

> **Taking a minor penalty hands the opposition roughly a one-in-five chance of a goal, netting out to something a little under one goal against for every five penalties you take.**

Against ~3.1 goals per team per game, that is a meaningful piece of a game, given away.

### When a penalty is genuinely worth taking

```facts
Read: Is the thing you are preventing more likely to become a goal than the one-in-five chance you are handing over?
Action: Take the penalty to deny a certain goal — a 21% chance against you, the league-wide NHL power-play rate for 2025-26, beats a 100% chance every time
Rule: A foul from behind on a breakaway is a penalty shot, not a 21% minor, in all six books here (NHL 57.3; IIHF and PWHL 24.8; USA Hockey 616(a), where the fouled team may take a minor instead; Hockey Canada 4.11(a); CARHA 86(c)) — with your own net empty, an awarded goal instead
Options: Late in a game, a minor that breaks a rush which would tie or win it is a defensible trade
Options: Stopping play when you are pinned and exhausted with no change available is real, effective and universally disliked
Never: Retaliate, send a message, or stand up for a teammate after the whistle — and in the first period take nothing at all except to deny a certain goal, with forty minutes still left to pay for it
```

Rarely, and less often than players think, because the test is unforgiving: **is the thing you are preventing more likely to become a goal than the one-in-five chance you are handing over?** Three cases pass it.

- **Denying a certain goal.** If the choice is a goal or a penalty, take the penalty: a 21% chance beats a 100% chance every time. **But make it a foul that costs a minor, not one that hauls him down from behind — and with your own net empty there may be no minor to take.** From behind on a clear chance it is a penalty shot (under USA Hockey, diagonally from behind too). With your net empty, the NHL, IIHF and PWHL award the goal for any foul that denies the chance, from behind or not, and CARHA may too, so do not foul him from the front and assume a penalty is all it costs. And no book caps a hook that drags him down at a minor: one that injures him is a major and an ejection in the NHL, PWHL, Hockey Canada and CARHA books, and one that recklessly endangers him is a major and a game misconduct under USA Hockey 623(b) — plan on a match there (602(a)) — and the IIHF's referee may give the major and game misconduct too (55.3).
- **Late in a game, to break a rush that would tie or win it.** A minor with 30 seconds left when you lead by one is a defensible trade: they get a fraction of a power play, not a two-minute one.
- **Stopping play when you are pinned and exhausted with no change available.** Cynical, effective, and universally disliked. It is a real tactical option and it is also a good way to lose your teammates' goodwill — see [Team Play and Culture](../off-the-ice/team_play_and_culture.md).

Note what is *not* on that list: retaliation, sending a message, standing up for a teammate after the whistle, and — with one exception — anything at all in the first period, when there are forty minutes left in which to pay for it. **The exception is denying a certain goal**, which is not a clock judgement at all: a 21% chance beats a 100% chance in the first minute as much as in the last. For the situations where a penalty is not merely unprofitable but catastrophic — leading late, already killing one, already on the power play, either side of a 6-on-5 — see the "when it absolutely isn't" list in [Game Management](../systems/game_management.md#when-it-absolutely-isnt).

### The critical exception nobody accounts for: fouls that are not priced as minors

```facts
Rule: A penalty shot needs four cumulative conditions — the foul from behind, in the neutral or attacking zone, on a player in possession and control or who clearly would have obtained it, with no opponent between him and the goalkeeper, denying a reasonable chance to score (NHL Rule 57.3)
Rule: Playing the puck first and tripping the opponent in the process is a minor, not a penalty shot (NHL 57.3, IIHF 24.8, same words)
Rule: USA Hockey 639(a)'s Note is scoped "under this rule" and turns on poke- or hook-checking, but its Casebook writes the wider version — no penalty at all where you clearly played the puck "with the focus solely being on the puck" (639, Situation 2)
Rule: Whether that relief reaches the 616 penalty shot is this guide's reading of two rules together, not a USA Hockey ruling — no situation in either volume joins 639(a)'s Note to Rule 616, and 616 Note 1's "would normally be deemed a penalty" reads either way
Rule: Two limits sit on that USA Hockey puck-first relief — you stay responsible for your stick after the check, and it reverses where "the player loses possession of the puck as a result of being tripped – then a penalty must be called" (639, Situation 2)
Rule: USA Hockey's puck-first relief does not save a slide: leaving your feet and sliding into any opponent, carrier or not, and causing them to fall is tripping there "regardless of who gains possession of the puck", the one exception being knees dropped to block a shot (639, Situation 4)
Rule: Only the NHL's and PWHL's tripping rules stop at a minor — IIHF 57.3 and 57.4 reach a major and a major plus game misconduct, CARHA 86(a) the latter; USA Hockey 639(b) makes that pair mandatory when reckless, plan on a match (602(a)); Hockey Canada 8.6(b) mandatory on injury
Rule: A trip meant to injure is a mandatory match even where the tripping rule stops at a minor — NHL and PWHL 21.1, CARHA 48(a), USA Hockey 602(a); Hockey Canada's 8.6(c) bars one but Appendix D's Note 1 requires one, so plan on it; the IIHF writes no match, its ceiling 57.4's major and game misconduct
Rule: With your own goalkeeper pulled, a foul denying the chance is an awarded goal under the NHL, IIHF and PWHL — none of their awarded-goal clauses carries the "from behind" requirement (NHL 25.1 and 57.4, cited as a pair; IIHF 25.3; PWHL 58.4)
Rule: USA Hockey and Hockey Canada keep the "from behind" test even with the net empty — 616(b) awards the goal only via the "fouled from behind provisions", and 4.12(a) routes through 4.11(a)(i), whose criterion reads "tripped or fouled from behind, or fouled by the goaltender"
Rule: CARHA keeps the "from behind" test at 36(a)(3), but 36(a)(4) also awards the goal where a defender "interferes by means of their body, stick, or any other object with the puck or the puck carrier", which may reach a foul from the front
Key: A penalty shot beats a power play but by less than the phrase suggests — 28.8% over eleven NHL seasons, computed for this document
Action: Prevent the breakaway four seconds earlier with your positioning — chasing from behind saves nothing, and saves nothing at all with your net empty
```

Some fouls do not cost you the 21.1% league-wide power-play rate, because they do not produce a power play at all. Two families matter, and each has a worse version when your own goalie is out of the net. This section covers the first — fouling a breakaway; [covering the puck in your own crease](#covering-the-puck-in-your-own-crease) is the second, and has its own section below because it has a free option the first does not.

**1. Fouling a breakaway from behind costs you a penalty shot, in every book here.** The IIHF and the PWHL award it at 24.8, Hockey Canada at 4.11(a), CARHA at 86(c) and USA Hockey at 616(a), where the team you fouled may take a minor instead; the tests differ at the edges, as set out below. Under NHL **Rule 57.3** — the tripping rule, which the hooking, holding, slashing and interference rules all cross-reference for this purpose — a penalty shot is awarded when all four of these are true:

1. The infraction took place in the neutral or attacking zone — that is, over the puck carrier's own blue line.
2. The infraction was committed **from behind**. 🇬🇧 **The IIHF and Hockey Canada add the goalkeeper.** IIHF **Rule 24.8(II)** excepts *"a foul committed from the front by the goalkeeper on a 'breakaway' situation"*, and Hockey Canada **4.11(a)(i)** reads *"The puck carrier is tripped or fouled from behind, **or fouled by the goaltender**."* So under those two books a goalkeeper who comes out and fouls a breakaway from in front is not saved by this criterion. **USA Hockey does not**: its Casebook gives a goalkeeper who deliberately trips a player going around him a minor, because *"[t]his situation does not call for a penalty shot because the player has not been fouled from behind"* (Rule 639, Situation 3). The NHL's silence is a difference in wording, not a licence. ⚠️ **But USA Hockey's test is wider on the angle:** 616(a) reaches a player *"fouled from behind, or diagonally from behind by an opponent"*, and Note 1 glosses that as *"beyond the fouled player's peripheral vision"*. What it awards is *"a penalty shot/**optional minor penalty**"*, and Rule 406(a) gives the choice to the team you fouled. **That 406(a) reaches 616(a) is this guide's reading of the two together**; on either reading the choice is theirs, so it never makes the foul cheaper.
3. The player was in possession and control (or, in the referee's judgement, clearly would have obtained it) and was denied a reasonable chance to score. Getting a shot away does not automatically rule it out — the rule says a penalty shot still applies if the foul denied a *"more" reasonable* scoring opportunity.
4. There was **no opposing player between them and the goalkeeper**.

Two riders sit inside the penalty-shot rule itself. **The first: if you play the puck first and trip the opponent in the process, it is a tripping minor, not a penalty shot** — **NHL 57.3 and IIHF 24.8 say so in the same words.** **USA Hockey writes it differently in each of its two volumes.** Its playing rule, 639(a)'s Note, says *"no penalty shall be assessed **under this rule** if, in the opinion of the Referee, the player was clearly hook-checking or poke-checking the puck for the purpose of gaining possession."* Rule 616, which awards the penalty shot, has no carve-out of its own, but its Note 1 defines *"fouled from behind"* as an infraction *"that would normally be deemed a penalty"* — so where 639 leaves no penalty, there is arguably nothing for 616 to act on. ⚠️ **That is this guide's reading of the two rules together, not a USA Hockey ruling** — no situation in either volume joins them, and Note 1 reads equally well as classing the *kind* of action — **so do not defend as though the penalty shot has been ruled out.** The referee must also judge that you were *"clearly"* checking the puck, and a hook or a hold never enters 639 at all. USA Hockey's Casebook then goes wider than the NHL: no tripping call where the defender *"has clearly attempted to and then played the puck with the focus solely being on the puck"* (639, Situation 2) — no penalty at all, where the NHL only downgrades to a minor. ⚠️ **Two limits matter more than the permission:** you stay responsible for your stick after the check, and *"if the player loses possession of the puck as a result of being tripped – then a penalty must be called"*. **Nor does touching the puck first save a slide:** Situation 4 rules that *"When a player leaves their feet and slides into **an opponent**, thereby causing them to fall, a penalty for tripping must be called regardless of who gains possession of the puck"* — carrier or not — the one exception being a player who drops to their knees to block a shot. **Hockey Canada writes the carve-out wide:** 8.6's Interpretation 2 assesses no penalty where a falling or sliding player *"contacts the puck prior to contacting the body of their opponent"*. ⚠️ **But 8.6 does not reach a slide called as clipping or slew-footing** — it hands those to 8.7 and 8.8, and both reach a **match penalty**.

And ⚠️ **do not price a reckless trip at a minor anywhere outside the NHL's Rule 57 and the PWHL's Rule 58.** IIHF 57.3 and 57.4 reach a major and a major plus game misconduct; **USA Hockey 639(b) makes that pair mandatory** where you recklessly endanger the player, and plan on a match there (602(a)); Hockey Canada 8.6(b) is mandatory where it injures; and CARHA 86(a) lets the referee give a major and game misconduct for any trip. A trip meant to injure is a mandatory match under NHL and PWHL 21.1, CARHA 48(a) and USA Hockey 602(a). Hockey Canada contradicts itself — 8.6(c) says *"No Match penalty may be assessed for tripping"*, while its Appendix D Note 1 requires one *"for any deliberate attempt to injure"* — so plan on the match. **The second rider:** if the attacker has already beaten the goalie and is fouled with the open net in front of him, it is *still* only a penalty shot, because *"no goal can be awarded since the goalkeeper is still on the ice"* (NHL 57.3 and IIHF 24.8, identical words).

**2. With your own goalie pulled, that same foul is an awarded goal.** The referee stops play and simply gives them the goal. There is no shot to save and nothing to be gained.

**Two NHL rule numbers govern it, and they are a pair.** **Rule 25.1** is the general awarded-goal rule — goalkeeper off, an attacker with possession and control in the neutral or attacking zone *"without a defending player between himself and the opposing goal"*, prevented from scoring by an infraction — and **Rule 57.4** is the operative clause for tripping and the fouls that cross-reference it. Rule 25.3's **Table 10** lists the seven infractions that produce an awarded goal with the net empty, tripping among them — *"fouling from behind – including hooking, holding, slashing, etc."* — and the index prints both numbers together: *"Control of the puck — awarded goals … 25.1, 57.4."*

**One difference cuts against what most players assume.** The "from behind" requirement is in the **penalty-shot** test, not in either awarded-goal clause: 25.1 and 57.4 ask only that the attacker was fouled with nobody between him and the empty net. Table 10's row label still reads "fouling from behind", so treat this as a wording gap rather than settled practice — but on the text, the empty-net goal is easier to trigger than the penalty shot. ⚠️ **That holds for the NHL, the IIHF (25.3) and the PWHL (58.4), not for USA Hockey or Hockey Canada, and CARHA leaves it open.** **USA Hockey 616(b)** awards the goal only through the *"fouled from behind provisions"*, and **Hockey Canada 4.12(a)** routes it through 4.11(a)(i)'s from-behind test. **CARHA 36(a)(3)** keeps the from-behind test, but **36(a)(4)** also awards the goal where a defender *"interferes by means of their body, stick, or any other object with the puck or the puck carrier"*, and the book does not say whether that reaches a foul from the front. **So stepping across from the front awards a goal under the NHL, the IIHF and the PWHL, not under USA Hockey or Hockey Canada — and under CARHA it may.**

### Covering the puck in your own crease

```facts
Rule: Five of six print the permission in rule text — NHL and IIHF 67.2, PWHL 69.2, Hockey Canada 10.2(a)(iii) and CARHA 61(a), the last two adding a no-advantage test. USA Hockey alone prints none; 618(a) leaves the catch-and-drop unpenalised outside the crease, its Casebook says so. Nothing to trade
Never: Hold a caught puck, carry it, turn with it or throw it — that is the act the books reach: a penalty in three, a USA Hockey stoppage outside the crease, the penalty shot in your own crease
Rule: What buys the penalty is the conduct after the catch, not the height — holding it, taking a stride, turning to avoid a check, or throwing it (NHL and IIHF 67.2(I); Hockey Canada Interpretation 4 to 10.2(a)(iii); USA Hockey Casebook 618, Situation 1)
Rule: Under USA Hockey a CAUGHT puck held in a closed hand outside the crease is a stoppage, not a penalty — but covering a loose puck on the ice with your hand, or picking it UP, is a minor there, and in the crease the penalty shot or optional minor (618(a), 614(a) Note, 614(b))
Rule: Picking the puck UP off the ice outside the crease with the hand while play is in progress is a minor — a different act from a hand merely closed on the puck, and the NHL, the IIHF and USA Hockey write it alike (67.2(III); 618(a) second paragraph)
Rule: A skater covering a puck that is itself in your crease concedes a penalty shot (NHL 67.4); five books make the puck's location the test (NHL and IIHF 63.6, PWHL 65.6, USA Hockey 618(a), CARHA 58(c)); Hockey Canada gives it there too (4.11(a)(v)) and also where you are in the crease (10.2(a)(v))
Rule: Puck outside the paint, you inside: a minor, not the shot, under NHL and IIHF 63.6, PWHL 65.6, USA Hockey Casebook 614 Sit. 1 and CARHA 58(a), (c); Hockey Canada gives the shot for a puck in the crease (4.11(a)(v)) and for you in it (10.2(a)(v)-(vi)): price the shot, or the goal with your net empty
Rule: With your net empty the same act is an awarded goal — NHL and IIHF 67.5, Hockey Canada 10.2(a)(vi), PWHL 69.5 and CARHA 58(c) Note 1 on the bare substitution, though CARHA 36(b) adds conditions; USA Hockey 614(b) and 618(a) award it only where it "prevented an obvious and imminent goal"
Rule: The catch is free; the hold is not. Height only sets the price — the crease runs four feet up to the top of the goal frame (NHL 1.7, 2.1), so above the crossbar is a minor and knee high in the paint the penalty shot (IIHF Situations 63.27 v 63.15, 67.4, worked applications, not rule text)
Never: Reach higher to hold a puck above the crossbar — once you are holding it, height only selects which penalty you pay, not whether you pay one; knocking it straight down is free at any height
```

**Start with the free option: catch it and put it straight down.** Catching a puck out of the air and immediately dropping it is free in every book here. NHL and IIHF **Rule 67.2**: *"A player shall be permitted to catch the puck out of the air but must immediately place it or knock it down to the ice."* **PWHL 69.2** prints the same sentence, and Hockey Canada **10.2(a)(iii)** and CARHA **61(a)** excuse a player who closes a hand on the puck and immediately drops it without gaining or trying to gain an advantage. **USA Hockey's playing rules print no permission sentence; its Casebook does** (Rule 618, Situation 1): *"[p]rovided the player catches the puck and drops it to their stick in one continuous motion, play should be allowed to continue."* It is the same permission in practice, but a casebook interpretation rather than rule text.

**Under USA Hockey a caught puck held outside the crease is a stoppage, not a penalty — but never cover a loose puck on the ice with your hand.** **618(a)** stops play for a player who *"closes their hand on the puck and does not immediately drop the puck to the ice"*, and keeps its minor for one who *"picks the puck up from the ice with their hand(s)"*. ⚠️ **A hand closed over a loose puck on the ice is a minor too** — **614(a)**, whose Note says *"the use of hands to make the puck unplayable should be penalized promptly"*. NHL 67.2(iii) and IIHF 67.2(III) give the same minor for picking the puck up. ⚠️ **In the crease, a hand that closes and does not open is the penalty shot or optional minor** (618(a), 614(b)). **Knock it straight down where you stand and there is no penalty in any book here.** Everything below is the price of the other choice.

**What costs you is the conduct after the catch, not its height:** skating with it to avoid a check or gain ground (NHL and IIHF 67.2(i)); throwing it, manoeuvring around a player or taking a stride while holding it (Hockey Canada Interpretation 4 to 10.2(a)(iii)); or holding it *"for any length of time"*, taking *"one or two strides"*, throwing it or faking the drop (USA Hockey Casebook 618, Situation 1). None of them names a height.

**Now the price.** A skater who *"falls on the puck, holds the puck, picks up the puck, or gathers the puck into his body or hands from the ice in the goal crease area"* concedes a penalty shot — **NHL Rule 67.4**. ⚠️ **In five of the six books it is the puck that has to be in the crease, not you.** NHL and IIHF **63.6**: *"If the puck is in the crease, penalty shot. If the puck is outside the crease and gathered into the body of a player (other than the goalkeeper) who is inside the crease, minor penalty."* **PWHL 65.6** prints that word for word, and **CARHA 58(c)** gives the shot for falling on, holding, gathering or picking up the puck *"while the puck is within their goal crease"*, with the plain minor at 58(a). **USA Hockey rules the same way** in its Casebook — the puck's location *"at the instant it is covered or held is the determining factor"*, so a slide that carries you into the crease with the puck covered outside it is a minor (Rule 614, Situation 1). ⚠️ **Hockey Canada is the exception, so do not carry the softer half into it.** Its **10.2(a)(v)** escalates the hand and body limbs to the shot *"when the infraction occurs inside the offending team's goal crease"*, which could mean you or the puck; 4.11(a)(v) points at the puck, but nothing settles it. **So with you in your crease and the puck outside it, price the penalty shot under Hockey Canada — and with your net empty, the goal (10.2(a)(vi)).**

**Pulling the puck out of the paint does not save you.** USA Hockey's Casebook gives the penalty shot to a player lying outside the crease who *"places their hand on the puck and pulls it out of the crease and into their body"*, though under USA Hockey a puck **batted** out and into their body is a minor rather than the shot (Rule 614, Situation 4); Hockey Canada's Interpretation 5 to 10.2(a)(v) makes a scoop-out by a player outside the crease a minor too, because *"The puck was not actually covered in the crease"*. Under USA Hockey the team you fouled may take a minor instead of the shot (Rule 406(a)) — ⚠️ **that choice is theirs, so the price to you is whichever is worth more to them.**

**And the crease is a volume, not a footprint** — ⚠️ **its ceiling sets which penalty you pay, never whether you pay one.** Every book here runs it up to the top of the goal frame, four feet up (NHL 1.7 and 2.1; IIHF 1.7; Hockey Canada 1.5(c); the Note to USA Hockey 618(a); PWHL 1.7; CARHA 5(c)). The IIHF Situation Handbook gives a minor for a puck caught **above the crossbar** in the paint (Situation 63.27) and the shot for one taken **knee high** (Situations 63.15 and 67.4) — but in both the defender turned and released it, and that conduct is what produced a penalty at all; the height only selected which one. Neither is a reason to reach higher. (The Handbook ranks itself below the Rulebook, so these are worked applications, not rule text.)

**Carrying it somewhere better does not cure it either.** In IIHF Situation 67.6 a defender with his own net empty grabs the puck at waist height and drops it **outside** the crease, and the ruling is *"an Awarded Goal to Team B."* The permission is to put the puck down **immediately**, not to relocate it first.

**So the trade is only on the table once you have already failed the free option**, and then only when the puck is in the blue paint below the crossbar. Against a puck that was going in anyway, it is a trade worth making. But with your goalie replaced by an extra attacker, **Rule 67.5** awards the goal outright — you have prevented nothing. ⚠️ **Five of the six books award it on the substitution alone, CARHA in one of its two clauses** — NHL and IIHF 67.5, PWHL 69.5, and Hockey Canada 10.2(a)(vi) whenever you are in the crease (4.12(a) with the puck in it). ⚠️ **USA Hockey conditions it**, awarding the goal only where the act *"has prevented an obvious and imminent goal"* (614(b); 618(a) says the same in other words). ⚠️ **CARHA writes it both ways, so play to the strict answer: with your net empty, any cover in your own crease is the goal.** Its 58(c) Note 1 reads *"If the goalkeeper has been removed from the ice, then a goal is awarded"*, while Rule 36(b) awards it only *"when a shot has been taken (control no longer applies)"* and the cover prevents *"an obvious and imminent goal."* Do not count on the referee reading the narrower one.

### Slew-footing: the one the carve-outs never reach

```facts
Rule: NHL 52.2 a mandatory match; USA Hockey 639 at least a major and game misconduct, and plan on a match if reckless (639(c), 602(a)); Hockey Canada 8.8 a double minor rising to a match, and 7.5, which ejects, once pushed into the boards (7.6 or 7.7 on the head: up to a match, a must if defenceless)
Rule: 8.8 is not Hockey Canada's only slew-foot rule — 7.5's preamble names a player who "kicks" or "pulls" an opponent's feet out from behind and then pushes them into the boards, and 7.5(c)'s match is mandatory where they cannot protect themselves; on the head it is 7.6(c) (7.7(c) in Junior and Senior)
Rule: Propelled from behind into the boards or goal, unable to protect or defend themselves: a mandatory match in Hockey Canada (7.5(c) off the head, 7.6(c) or 7.7(c) on it) and CARHA 53(b), whose Note tells referees "not to substitute other penalties when a player is checked from behind in any manner"
Rule: IIHF 52.2, the British reader's, is a major plus an automatic game misconduct, framed "The Referee shall, at their discretion, assess" — and Rule 52 writes no lesser tier beneath it: 52.1 defines the foul, 52.2 is its only penalty clause
Rule: On slew-footing the PWHL matches the NHL at the top of the six books here — PWHL 53.2 and NHL 52.2 are each one sentence, a mandatory match penalty with no lower tier
Rule: CARHA puts the slew-foot and the ordinary trip in one rule — 86(b) is a match penalty for a slew-foot *"from behind"* at the referee's discretion, and 86(a) prices a trip by the foot at a minor or, also at the referee's discretion, a major and a game misconduct
Rule: A slew-foot meant to injure is a mandatory match penalty under CARHA 48(a), whatever 86(b)'s discretion says, and USA Hockey 602(a); Hockey Canada 7.5(c) does the same for any check from behind meant to injure, boards or not — 7.6(c) or 7.7(c) if it reaches the head
Rule: Slew-foot meant to injure: plan on a Hockey Canada match even if no one is hurt — Appendix D Note 1 says one "shall be assessed for any deliberate attempt to injure"; that it overrides 8.8(c)'s discretion is a reading, as 7.1(c) excludes acts covered elsewhere
Rule: If your foot came at him, do not count on CARHA 86(b)'s discretion — 48(c) makes a match mandatory for a player who "attempts to kick or deliberately kicks" an opponent; that it reaches a slew-foot is a reading, as CARHA's casebook rules one under 86(b)
Rule: Hockey Canada's puck-first tripping carve-out does not reach a slide called as clipping or slew-footing — 8.6 hands those to 8.7 and 8.8, which carry no carve-out and both reach a match penalty
Rule: USA Hockey's puck-first relief does not reach it either — 639(a)'s Note excuses only a player "clearly hook-checking or poke-checking the puck", which a slew-foot is not, and 639(a) assesses the minor "except slew footing"
Never: Use your leg or foot to knock an opponent's feet from under them, or push their upper body back while your leg does it — that is slew-footing, a mandatory match penalty in the NHL (52.1, 52.2)
```

**4. Slew-footing is not a bare minor in any of these books, and no puck-first carve-out reaches it.** Taking a player's legs from under them puts them on their back with nothing to break the fall — that is this guide's reading; the **NHL and IIHF definitions (52.1)** both end on *"fall violently to the ice"*. Each carve-out misses it for its own reason. **USA Hockey's** 639(a) Note excuses only a player *"clearly hook-checking or poke-checking the puck"*, which a slew-foot is not, and 639(a) assesses its minor *"for any of the actions described above, **except slew footing**"*. **Hockey Canada's** 8.6 hands it to 8.8, which writes no carve-out; the **NHL, IIHF and PWHL** put it in a rule of its own (52, or 53 in the PWHL). **CARHA** prints a puck-first Note directly beneath its slew-footing clause, but the Note excuses *tripping* while falling or sliding, and CARHA's slew-foot is a *"kicking or leg dragging motion from behind"* — ⚠️ **that it therefore does not reach a slew-foot is this guide's reading of the two sentences together; CARHA does not say so.**

**What it costs, harshest first:**

- **NHL 52.2 and PWHL 53.2:** ⚠️ **a mandatory match penalty**, with no lower tier.
- **IIHF 52.2:** the referee *"**shall**, at their discretion, assess a major penalty and an automatic game misconduct penalty"*, and Rule 52 writes no lesser tier.
- **USA Hockey 639:** *"The minimum penalty to be assessed for slew footing is a major plus game misconduct penalty"*, and a match is mandatory for an attempt to injure (602(a)).
- **CARHA 86(b):** a match penalty *"At the discretion of the Referee"*, for a slew-foot *"from behind"* — a narrowing the others do not write. ⚠️ **Do not read that discretion as a way out.** 86(a) still lets the referee give a minor, or a major and game misconduct, for any trip by the foot; **48(a)** makes a match mandatory for a deliberate attempt to injure; and **48(c)** makes one mandatory for a player who *"attempts to kick or deliberately kicks"* an opponent. That 48(c) reaches a slew-foot is this guide's reading — CARHA's casebook rules one under 86(b) — **so if your foot came at him, do not count on the discretion.**
- **Hockey Canada 8.8:** starts lowest — 8.8(a) is a double minor, which does not eject, and 8.8(b) and (e) bar the major and game misconduct — but **8.8(c) is a match penalty**, at the referee's discretion on the violence and mandatory where it injures. **Meant to injure, plan on the match even if no one is hurt:** Appendix D Note 1 says one *"shall be assessed for any deliberate attempt to injure"*. ⚠️ **That the Note overrides 8.8(c)'s discretion is this guide's reading**, because the rule it cites, 7.1(c), reaches acts *"not otherwise covered within the playing rules"*.

**Take his feet from behind and then push him into the boards,** ⚠️ **and under Hockey Canada it is checking from behind, which ejects at its floor.** Rule 7.5's preamble names a player who *"‘kicks’ or ‘pulls’ an opponent’s feet out from behind and then pushes the player into the boards"*. **7.5(a)** is a mandatory minor plus game misconduct, and **7.5(c)**'s match is mandatory once he goes *"from behind into the boards or goal frame, in such a way that the player is unable to protect or defend themselves"* — or, boards or not, for any player who *"deliberately attempts to or deliberately injures an opponent by checking them from behind"*. If it reaches the head it is priced as head contact, because Rule 7.6 *"supersedes any other rules"* (7.7 in Junior and Senior), and it is a match where they could not protect or defend themselves; a hit that drives them head first into the boards or goal frame gets the harsher of the two prices. ⚠️ **CARHA 53(b) is a mandatory match too**, where a player is *"propelled in any manner from behind into the boards or goal net"* and *"unable to protect or defend themselves"*, with a Note telling referees *"not to substitute other penalties when a player is checked from behind in any manner."*

**What a penalty shot is actually worth.** It is a better scoring opportunity than a two-minute power play — a clean, unopposed attempt from centre ice against a goalie who must stay in the crease until the puck is touched. **Across the eleven NHL regular seasons from 2015-16 to 2025-26, penalty shots converted at 28.8% — 137 goals on 476 attempts** (computed from the NHL's skater penalty-shot report; see Sources). Set that against the 21.1% league-wide power-play rate above and the gap is real, but narrower than the phrase "penalty shot" makes it sound. The two most recent seasons both sit below that eleven-season average, and each was close to or beneath its own season's power-play rate: **21.9% in 2024-25 against a 21.6% league-wide power-play rate — roughly level; 17.1% in 2025-26 against 21.1% — clearly below.** Do not read that as a trend. Penalty shots are rare enough — between 27 and 59 a season league-wide over that span — that any single season bounces around wildly, so the eleven-season number is the one to use.

**So the practical rule:** if you are chasing from behind and the only way to stop them is a foul, you are probably not saving anything, and if your net is empty you are saving nothing at all. **The time to prevent the breakaway is four seconds earlier, with your positioning.** See [Defending the Rush](../systems/defending_the_rush.md).

### The penalties that are not trades at all

```facts
Key: Most amateur penalties look like habits rather than decisions — coaching observation, not measurement, since what has been counted is the type of infraction and not the decision behind it
Risk: Stick fouls that are really skating fouls — a hook because you stopped skating twenty feet ago, a trip because your feet quit
Risk: Retaliation — a slash after losing a battle, a cross-check after the whistle, an answer to something the referee missed
Action: Fix the stick penalty in conditioning and the retaliation penalty in the mental game — reasoning, not a measured effect
Key: Use frustration as a lens for fixing your own game, not as a fact about the population — no published study codes penalty motive
Rule: Embellishing to draw a call is its own minor under IIHF 64.2, the book British hockey runs on — the IHUK In-House Rules 2026-27 make it "2 plus 10", a minor plus a ten-minute misconduct, while the EIHL keeps the bare minor and can fine you on top through its Department of Player Safety
```

Most penalties in amateur hockey are not tactical trades at all — nobody weighed the odds and decided to take them. **That is coaching observation rather than measurement**: what has been counted is the *type* of infraction, not the decision behind it. It is still the useful way to look at your **own** penalties, because habits are addressable. Two of them account for most of what coaches see:

- **Stick fouls that are really skating fouls** — a hook because you stopped skating twenty feet ago, a trip because your feet quit and your stick took over.
- **Retaliation** — a slash after losing a battle, a cross-check after the whistle, an answer to something the referee already missed.

**The stick penalty is almost always a skating problem, and the retaliation penalty is almost always a composure problem.** One is fixed in conditioning, the other in the [Mental Game](../off-the-ice/mental_game.md). **Both of those are reasoning, not a measured effect.** [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md) sets out the one study found that tested fitness against penalty minutes, and its endurance measures came back at essentially zero.

**Be careful how strongly you state the second one.** That *most* amateur penalties are frustration penalties is **half measured and half folklore**; the working is in [Game Management, "Frustration penalties"](../systems/game_management.md#frustration-penalties). That they are mostly **aggression-type infractions** is measured — Gilbert and Trudel coded 850 penalties across 55 Bantam games and found 62.4% were minor aggression penalties. That most are **frustration-driven** has no measurement behind it, because no published study codes penalty *motive*, and two findings (McFaul et al. 2026 on head-contact events; Kirker et al. 2000 on observed aggression) point the other way. **Use frustration as a lens for fixing your own game, not as a fact about the population.**

**One deliberate act belongs on this list for the opposite reason: it *is* a decision, and the odds being played for are not the odds on offer.** Embellishing a foul to buy a call is a minor in its own right under the IIHF book British hockey runs on — Rule **64.2**, *"A minor penalty shall be imposed on a Player who attempts to draw a penalty by their actions (“diving / embellishment”)"*, worded identically in its 2025/26 and 2026/27 editions. The Elite League's casebook sets out the arithmetic: called one way or the other, *"Team A in scenario 1 plays shorthanded and in scenario 2, Team A, has a power play."* So you are staking a power play you hoped to win against one you hand over, on a judgement you do not control. ⚠️ **And the offsetting call is not the default:** that casebook tells its officials *"We would prefer that on a play where you feel a player dived to try and persuade you into making a call, that you ONLY take the player who dived"*. **The price also differs inside British hockey**: *"2 plus 10"* under the IHUK In-House Rules 2026-27, and in the Elite League the bare minor with a fine available afterwards — *"DOPS has the ability to fine players"*, whether or not a diving penalty was called on the ice. [Rules Primer](../foundation/rules_primer.md) carries both tiers in full. **What none of this tells you is how often a dive is spotted**, which is the number the decision actually turns on, and no book read here publishes it.

## Playing to the score — and to your level

```facts
Key: The right risk profile in a Tuesday-night beer league is not the right risk profile in a competitive one
Risk: Support is less reliable — in a team that does not practise, the give-and-go you can see is one your teammate cannot
Risk: Recovery is worse — a rec-league turnover frequently meets nobody at all, so the cost side of every calculation is higher, not lower
Risk: Goaltending is more variable both ways — get pucks to the net, and treat your own turnovers as costing more
Action: Tighten nothing in your game except your own end and you will win more games. Try things in the offensive zone instead
Key: Risk level is a dial, not a switch — a big lead, a tired bench or a backup in net all shift you toward the simple play
```

An honest section, because most hockey advice pretends everyone plays in the same league.

**The right risk profile in a Tuesday-night beer league is not the right risk profile in a competitive one.** The reasons are not about talent, they are about the structure of the game:

- **Support is less reliable.** The high-value plays in the tracking work above — the controlled entry, the high-danger pass; manually tracked in two European professional leagues, so a direction rather than an NHL measurement — depend on someone being where you expect them. In a team that practises twice a week, they are. In a team that does not practise, the give-and-go you can see is one your teammate cannot.
- **Recovery is worse.** In a good league a turnover meets a backcheck. In a rec league a turnover frequently meets nobody at all, which means the cost side of every one of your calculations is *higher*, not lower.
- **Goaltending is more variable.** Which cuts both ways: shots from distance go in more often, so getting pucks to the net is worth relatively more; but so do the chances you concede, so turnovers cost relatively more too. Both effects push toward the same conclusion.

**The blunt version:** most amateur teams lose far more goals to unforced turnovers than they gain from creative plays. If you tightened nothing in your game except *your own end*, you would win more hockey games. The creativity is not the problem, and nobody is asking you to stop trying things. **They are asking you to try them in the offensive zone.**

The same logic applies within a game and within a season. A team with a big lead, a tired bench, or a backup goalie in net should shift its whole distribution toward the simple play. A team that is being outplayed and needs to change the game should shift the other way. **Risk level is a dial, not a switch, and knowing where to set it is a skill in itself.**

## Recovering from a bad decision

You will make bad decisions. Everyone does, at every level. **What separates good players is almost entirely what happens in the next four seconds.**

### The immediate response

```facts
Action: Turn and skate to the middle of the ice between the puck and your net — become the extra defender
Action: Get to the inside and take away the middle; deny the pass to the slot and a scoring chance becomes a shot from the outside
Action: Talk — one word, "turnover", "back", "I'm high", reorganises four teammates who are still reading the play
Never: Foul the puck carrier from behind. Backcheck hard instead — a trip, hook, hold or slash invites a penalty shot
Never: Check from behind — if you can see their numbers, pull up and play the puck; they cannot see you coming
Rule: A hit that stays on the back has no minor in NHL, IIHF or PWHL 43.2 — a major and game misconduct (NHL and PWHL 43.3, 43.5; IIHF 43.3 if reckless, the referee's call); only USA Hockey 608(a) can leave you in the game — never if head first or forceful at boards; if reckless, plan on a match (602(a))
Rule: Hockey Canada 7.5 and CARHA 53 start a hit on the back, or head first, at a minor and game misconduct — a major on violence, a must if hurt, a match into boards if defenceless; under Hockey Canada one on the head is head contact (7.6; 7.7 Junior, Senior): a minor up to a match, a must if defenceless
Rule: USA Hockey's Casebook requires a minor plus a misconduct for a push "not near the boards" or a minimal-contact "pinch"; a major plus game misconduct or a match "must be called" for a forceful check on one "standing along the boards" (608, Situation 1); for a reckless one, plan on a match (602(a))
Rule: Under USA Hockey a check from behind still ends your game where it "recklessly endangers an opponent, or causes them to go head first into the boards or goal frame" — 608(b) mandates the major plus game misconduct, and plan on a match (602(a)); its Casebook adds a forceful check at the boards
Rule: Hockey Canada's minor for a check from behind is its floor, not its ceiling — off the head, 7.5(c) is a match where the hit puts an opponent "into the boards or goal frame, in such a way that the player is unable to protect or defend themselves"; on the head, 7.6(c) (7.7(c) in Junior and Senior)
Rule: A hit from behind meant to injure is a mandatory match penalty, boards or not — Hockey Canada 7.5(c), or 7.6(c) on the head (7.7(c) in Junior and Senior), NHL and PWHL 21.1, USA Hockey 602(a) and CARHA 48(a); CARHA 53(a) allows one on any hit from behind
Options: Some teams coach the opposite by design — the first forward back pressures the carrier while the defence holds inside — so find out which yours plays rather than improvising it
Never: Change while the play is live because you are embarrassed. Finish the shift instead
```

1. **Turn and skate.** Under the lane-and-man systems this guide teaches, skate not toward the puck but to the middle of the ice between the puck and your net, and become the extra defender. Chasing the carrier from behind achieves nothing and invites a penalty shot (all four NHL 57.3 conditions, set out above, must hold before one is awarded). **And do not solve the chase with your body: if you can see their numbers, pull up and play the puck.** A hit that stays on a player's back has no minor under the **NHL** or the **IIHF** — Rule 43.2 in each, the IIHF calling it *"serious and dangerous"* — so it is a major plus a game misconduct, mandatory under the NHL (43.3, 43.5) and, where the referee judges it reckless, under the IIHF (43.3). **Hockey Canada** starts it at *"[a] Minor penalty and a Game Misconduct penalty"* (7.5(a)), the game misconduct compulsory for a minor and a major alike (7.5(e)); 7.5(b) adds a major on the violence of the impact, mandatory where an otherwise-minor check injures; and **7.5(c) is a match penalty** where the hit puts an opponent *"into the boards or goal frame, in such a way that the player is unable to protect or defend themselves"*, or wherever you deliberately try to injure by checking from behind, boards or not. A deliberate attempt to injure by any means is a mandatory match under NHL and PWHL 21.1, CARHA 48(a) and USA Hockey 602(a) too. **Under Hockey Canada a hit from behind that reaches the head is priced as head contact**, because Rule 7.6 *"supersedes any other rules"*, bar fighting. In minor and female hockey (7.6) that is a minor if accidental and a double minor if intentional, a third in one game being an ejection; the referee may make it a major and game misconduct on the violence, and **must** if the player is hurt or for a cross-check above the shoulders; and it is a match if they could not protect or defend themselves or you tried to injure them. In Junior and Senior (7.7) it may be called as roughing, charging or another penalty at a minimal degree of violence, is a minor plus a ten-minute misconduct at a moderate one, and a major and game misconduct at a high one, for a cross-check to the head, or where the head hits the glass, ice or boards and is injured — and a match on the same two grounds. A hit that drives them head first into the boards or goal frame gets the harsher of the two prices: Hockey Canada's checking-from-behind rule names that very hit, so its minor plus game misconduct, and its mandatory match where they could not protect or defend themselves, apply as well. **USA Hockey writes the only tier in the six books that stops short of an ejection** — 608(a)'s *"minor plus a misconduct penalty"* — **and this hit does not belong in it.** 608(b) makes the major plus game misconduct mandatory for a check from behind that *"recklessly endangers an opponent, or causes them to go head first into the boards or goal frame"*. Its Casebook (608, Situation 1) keeps the minor plus misconduct for a push on an opponent *"not near the boards"* or *"minimal body contact"* producing a *"'pinch' against the boards"*, and says the major plus game misconduct, or the match, *"must be called"* *"In every instance where a player forcefully checks an opponent who is standing along the boards (back toward the middle of the ice)"* — no recklessness needed — and for any reckless check from behind, boards or not. It names the *"danger zone"* as *"the area approximately ten feet out from the boards."* With excessive force on a defenceless opponent the match is mandatory (608(c)); short of that, plan on a match for any reckless check from behind (602(a)). **So never price a check from behind at USA Hockey's minor.** [Rules Primer](../foundation/rules_primer.md) owns the full comparison. **Some teams do coach the opposite by design** — the first forward back pressures the carrier while the defence holds inside; [Defending the Rush](../systems/defending_the_rush.md) sets out both, and the mistake is improvising it rather than the pressure itself.
2. **Get to the inside.** Your job now is to take away the middle, not to win the puck back. Deny the pass to the slot and you have turned a scoring chance into a shot from the outside.
3. **Talk.** One word — "turnover", "back", "I'm high" — reorganises four teammates who are still reading the play. Silence is the second mistake.
4. **Finish the shift.** Do not change while the play is live because you are embarrassed. That is how a giveaway becomes a giveaway plus a 4-on-5.

### Do not compound the mistake

```facts
Key: The most damaging thing about a bad decision is the decision that tries to fix it
Never: Force a low-percentage play to make up for a giveaway — a pinch you have not read, a cross-ice pass, a hit you leave your feet for
Risk: The play you force to make up for a giveaway is nearly always worse than the giveaway, because the first was an error of execution and the second an error of judgement made on purpose
Mindset: The next play is a new play — its expected value has nothing to do with what just happened
Key: If the boring option was right before your turnover, it is still right after it
```

**The most damaging thing about a bad decision is the decision that tries to fix it.** The pattern is universal and it looks like this: you give the puck away, you feel it, and thirty seconds later you force a low-percentage play to make up for it — a pinch you have not read, a cross-ice pass, a hit you leave your feet for.

The second mistake is nearly always worse than the first, because the first was an error of execution and the second is an error of judgement made on purpose.

**The discipline is: the next play is a new play.** Its expected value has nothing to do with what just happened. If the boring option was right before your turnover, it is still right after it. Working on that separation is squarely the subject of the [Mental Game](../off-the-ice/mental_game.md).

## Common Mistakes

- **Treating risk as a personality rather than a location.** Be extremely safe in your own slot and extremely dangerous in the opponent's, in the same shift. Players describe themselves as "a safe player" or "a creative player", and both are wrong: risk belongs to where you are, not to who you are.
- **Only counting the risks that failed visibly.** Judge decisions by the odds you faced, not the outcome you got. The cross-ice pass in your own end that got through is not evidence the play was good; it is evidence you got away with it.
- **Making the decision after receiving the puck.** Scan before it arrives — once it is on your stick your options have halved. The hierarchy is just what you do with what the scan told you.
- **Stickhandling out of trouble instead of relocating out of it.** One stride to the wall changes the risk of every option on your list. Do that before you decide, not instead of deciding.
- **Passing or carrying the puck across the front of your own net.** Take the D-to-D behind the net, carry it to the wall, and move the puck north or behind the net rather than across your own end: an interception in your own slot is a shot from the slot before your goalie is set. Some teams accept a D-to-D or cross-ice pass above the tops of the circles; almost nobody accepts one below them.
- **Chasing the puck carrier from behind after a turnover, where your system did not ask for it.** Turn and skate to the inside and become the extra defender: you will not catch them, and a foul from behind on a breakaway is a penalty shot. Where pressuring the carrier *is* the system, use your feet and your stick — if you can see their numbers, pull up and play the puck. ⚠️ **Neither foul is a cheap minor:** a hit on a player's back ends your game once called in most books and can in all six, and a trip or slew-foot can end it too. **Recovering from a bad decision** and **The critical exception nobody accounts for**, above, give each book's price.
- **Both defencemen going to the same puck with neither knowing who has the net.** Say out loud, before either of you moves, who takes the puck and who has the net. On a **strong-side overload** some coaches put both of you low and give the slot to a forward — what never varies is that somebody is home before you go.
- **Panicking the puck over the glass.** Aim lower and put it out along the boards, or take the hit and the faceoff. It is the most avoidable minor in hockey. ⚠️ **And do not carry one book's price into another:** under the NHL, the IIHF and the PWHL a direct, non-deflected clear out of your own zone is a minor even by accident, a *deliberate* one is a minor from anywhere in all six books, and a goaltender under Hockey Canada or CARHA should not count on an accident being free.
- **Changing lines because you are tired rather than because the situation allows it.** Get the puck out first, then change as the play moves north or at a stoppage. The play dictates the change, not your lungs.
- **Not raising your risk level when trailing late.** Take it up — pinch, activate, force the entry. Playing "responsibly" while losing 3-1 with four minutes left is a slow-motion concession.
- **Compounding.** Treat the next play as a new play: turn, get to the inside, talk and finish the shift. A bad decision followed by a worse one to fix it costs more goals than the first mistake.

## Key Takeaways

1. **Every time the puck comes to you, weigh the play as a bet before you make it** — the chance it works times what you gain, against the chance it fails times what you lose. Make the calculation conscious.
2. **Risk belongs to the location, not the play.** Change your location before you decide — one stride out improves every option. **Never pass the puck — or skate it — across the front of your own goal:** get it out of your slot first, to the wall, behind the net or off the glass.
3. **Rank the ice by what a turnover there costs, and play to it:** your own slot catastrophic, your own zone high, the middle of the neutral zone high — where odd-man rushes are made — their blue line moderate, their corners nearly free. The ranking is coaching consensus; only the offensive blue line has a tracked cost, from one Swiss league, so trust the order more than the labels.
4. **North turnovers are far worse than south turnovers — coaching consensus reasoned from the mechanism, not a measured comparison.** Losing the puck while it travels toward your own net makes the turnover and the counterattack one event.
5. **Know the "never" list by its reasons, not by heart.** Take the D-to-D behind your net; turn your shoulders rather than throw a blind backhand up the middle; move the puck north or behind the net, not across your own end; send one defenceman and keep the other home; do not force the pass at their blue line with nobody back; do not stickhandle in your own slot; do not change with the puck in your own zone. **Those seven are coaching conventions with exceptions** — on a strong-side overload some coaches put both defencemen low (one coaching account) — so ask where your coach draws each line. **Two are rules in all six books:** stay on your feet to check where checking is allowed, and ⚠️ **put the puck off the glass, not over it** — a deliberate clear out of play is at least a minor anywhere, and under the NHL, the IIHF and the PWHL so is a direct one out of your own end, even by accident.
6. **With time, make the possession play; without it, get the puck out.** In tracked NHL data — one tracker, about half the games of two seasons — carry-outs and pass-outs lead to an entry nearly nine times in ten, dump-outs about one in five. The difference is *possession*, not method.
7. **Before you jump a lane, pinch or activate, answer three questions: do I have a read, is someone behind me if I miss, what does the scoreboard allow?** Two yeses and a green light from the scoreboard, go; otherwise hold your position. Acting on a read is anticipation; acting on hope is gambling.
8. **Play simple in your own end and creative in theirs.** Hockey's mistakes are asymmetric: a missed chance in their end costs you a chance; a turnover in your own end costs you a goal.
9. **Take a penalty to deny a certain goal, and almost never out of frustration.** A minor is roughly a one-in-five chance of a goal against, so it is a cheap trade against a certainty and a terrible one against nothing. **Prevent the breakaway earlier with your positioning; once a carrier is past you, do not foul them from behind** — that is a **penalty shot** in every book here (under USA Hockey the fouled team may take a minor instead), and an **awarded goal** if your net is empty. ⚠️ **And keep your feet out of theirs:** a trip can cost you your game in several books, and a called slew-foot ends it in most books and can in all six.
10. **Do not compound — and never solve the chase with your body.** The next play has nothing to do with the last: turn, skate to the inside, talk, and finish the shift. If you catch a carrier from behind and can see their numbers, pull up and play the puck. ⚠️ **A hit on a player's back ends your game once called in most books, and can in all six;** meant to injure it is a match in most, and into the boards when they cannot protect themselves, in several.
11. **Your risk level moves with the scoreboard and the clock.** Leading, take it down — hard once the lead reaches three. Trailing late, take it up: pinch, activate, force the entry. Shorthanded, clear the puck hard unless you have truly read a 2-on-1 the other way. See [Risk and game state](#risk-and-game-state) for the power-play, overtime and empty-net cases.
