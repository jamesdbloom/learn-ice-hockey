## Key focus

**Judge a shift by the room you had when the puck arrived, not by whether your move worked.** A look over your shoulder before you receive arguably buys more than any move. ⚠️ **The goaltender is the exception**: tracking a live shot, they never look away — see [Goaltender](../positions/goaltender.md).

**Read closing speed, not distance.** A defender three feet away who has overrun you gives you time; one twenty feet away closing hard gives you none. Read their hips: a player accelerates where the hips face.

**When the play ahead is not there, turn away from the closing defender and hold the puck a second or two for support** — a tight turn, puck on the outside. Never in your own end, where a lost puck is a goal against.

**Glide, look, explode.** Separation comes from changing speed, not from top speed: a defender can match a steady pace, and the moment they change speed is when they can least change direction. This is coaching craft, not a measured finding.

**Attack the seam between two defenders, never straight at one.** Make two players decide who takes you. Put the puck behind a defender, pass to the ice a teammate is skating into, and move to empty ice rather than to the puck.

**Without the puck, get open with a change of direction, not a race.** Stop when your check expects you to go. Break when the carrier's head comes up; if you are early, curl and come again.

**Defending, take the middle away first and put your blade in the lane before the pass.** When beaten, concede the outside. When you can't win the pass, press their blade as the puck lands — not a chop. Some coaches want the wall taken in certain spots; ask.

**Defending the rush, close your gap early so the carrier cannot look up** — but every foot you take from in front of them goes behind you. Read the hips, not the puck. [Defending the Rush](../systems/defending_the_rush.md) has the distances.

**Angle, don't chase: close on a curve that leaves the carrier only the wall, then take the puck, not the player.** Where checking is barred or limited, veering, stepping or gliding into them, adding a hip, shoulder or arm, or pinning them is a penalty. In CARHA, avoid contact altogether.

**Go to pressure only with a second layer behind you, on the same trigger as your teammates.** Alone, you move the space behind you rather than taking it. This guide assumes a 2-1-2 forecheck; find out what yours plays.

## Overview

Hockey has one currency: time and space — the room you have when the puck reaches you, and the moment you have to use it. Every tactic in this guide either earns more of it or takes it off the other team.

**Why grade yourself on room, not hands.** Two players with the same hands and shot: one produces, one doesn't. The difference is almost never the skill. The first receives the puck with a metre and a half of clean ice and a head already up.

**Why closing speed, not distance.** Your time is how far away the nearest dangerous opponent is, divided by how fast they are closing — a way of thinking, not something anyone measures. That is why an overrun forechecker three feet away is no threat, and a defender twenty feet away closing hard is.

**Look before the puck arrives, not after.** Your clock starts when the puck touches your stick, so a look while the pass is travelling is free. Nobody has ranked it against a move; what has been measured is that scanning beats not scanning. ⚠️ **It reverses for the goaltender**, who never looks away from a live puck — see [Goaltender](../positions/goaltender.md).

**Why turn away, change speed and attack seams.** Each one gives the defender a decision instead of a target. Turning away buys your support the time to arrive. A change of speed beats a defender who has matched your pace. A seam makes two defenders decide who takes you.

**Why defending is the same idea reversed.** Every defending habit here acts before the puck arrives: a gap closed early, a blade already in the lane, an angle instead of a chase, pressure with a second layer already behind it. Acting after the puck arrives is a gamble, and a missed gamble leaves you out of position.

And one question every time you come off the ice: did I have more time than I thought? The honest answer is almost always yes.

## 1. The central idea: skill is what you do with time and space

```facts
Key: Skill is what you spend; time and space are what you earn
Goal: Create time and space with every move — a move that beats nobody and opens nothing is a habit, not a skill
Action: Grade yourself on the room you had when the puck came to you, not on your hands
Action: Look over your shoulder before you receive, turn away from pressure, put your stick in a passing lane
Mindset: Hockey IQ is a learnable habit, not a mysterious gift — and it is learnable faster than skating
```

Here is the reframe. **Skill is not what makes players good. Skill is what they spend.** Time and space are what they earn.

Take two players with identical hands, shot and top speed, on the same line. One produces; one doesn't. The first receives the puck with a metre and a half of clean ice and a head already up. The second receives it with a stick on their hip and no idea what is behind them. Same skill, different result, because only one had something to spend it on.

**Garrett Szeremley**, writing for the coaching resource [PV Elite](https://www.pvelitehockey.com/bulletin/timeandspace), puts it bluntly: **every move in hockey has one purpose, which is to create time and space.** A toe drag that beats nobody and opens nothing is a habit, not a skill. A shoulder fake that moves a defender's hips six inches and opens a lane *is* a skill, even if it looks like nothing on a highlight reel.

Three things follow.

- **Grade yourself on your room, not your hands.** After a shift, ask how much space you had when the puck came to you, and what you did to earn it.
- **The cheapest improvements are time-and-space ones.** Looking over your shoulder before you receive costs nothing and buys more than a year of stickhandling in the garage. So does turning away from pressure — anywhere but your own end, where you take one look and move it. So does putting your stick in a passing lane.
- **Hockey IQ is not a mysterious gift.** It is the habit of making room for yourself and denying it to others, and it is learnable faster than skating is.

## 2. What "time" actually means

```facts
Key: Your time is the distance to the nearest threatening defender divided by their closing speed — a teaching model, not a measured quantity
Read: Closing speed, not distance — twenty feet closing hard is less time than three feet travelling away
Read: The hips, not the stick — a player accelerates where the hips face
Read: Feet or glide — a defender in full crossovers is committed and can be beaten by going the other way
Read: How many are arriving — one closing opponent you solve with a move, two you solve by getting rid of the puck
Action: Reverse the puck back against the flow into the ice an over-skated forechecker has just vacated
Never: Turn your back to the checker on a reverse, or duck — he is on your hip. Skates parallel to the wall, forearm and hip to the wall, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest
```

Beginners hear "you've got time" and picture seconds on a clock. That is the wrong model, and it will get you hit.

**A working model: your time is the distance to the nearest threatening defender divided by how fast they are closing on you.** The second half matters more than players expect. It is a teaching model, not a measured quantity, but it predicts the situations that surprise people.

Two cases catch players out.

**You can have time with an opponent three feet away.** A forechecker who has over-skated you and is travelling *away* has a closing speed of about zero. To reach you they must stop, turn and rebuild speed — three or four tenths of a second at least, a coaching estimate (no study was found that puts a number on it). That is the moment for a **reverse**: send the puck back against the flow into the ice they just left. (A *reverse* goes back against the direction of play; a *rim* goes on around the boards with it.) ⚠️ **You play a reverse at the wall with that forechecker still on your hip, so never turn your back to the checker, and never duck.** Get your **skates parallel to the wall** and take contact with your **forearm and hip to the wall**, not the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest. [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md) §6 covers it and outranks anything here.

**You can have no time with an opponent twenty feet away.** A defenceman skating backwards at you with a tight gap, on balance, stick already in your lane, is closing at maybe fifteen feet per second. You have a bit over a second, and some of it is spent because the puck is on your backhand. The "twenty feet" made you feel safe. It wasn't.

![](diagram:breakout-reverse)

**So read closing speed, not distance.** Look at three things when you scan:

- **Which way are their hips pointed?** A stick can be anywhere, but a player accelerates where the hips face. Gap control is built on the same read.
- **Are their feet moving or are they gliding?** A gliding defender can change direction fast. One in full crossovers is committed, and you beat them by going the other way.
- **How many are arriving?** One closing opponent you can beat with a move. Two from different angles you beat by moving the puck, because whatever space you open against the first, the second fills.

See [Scanning and Anticipation](scanning_and_anticipation.md) for the habit that makes these reads automatic.

## 3. Creating time and space for yourself

Six of these seven tools need no skating speed and no hands. That is the point.

### Deception — defenders react to what you show them

```facts
Key: Space is created in the defender's mind before it exists on the ice — show them the wrong option
Options: Fake shot, look-off, head and shoulder fake, or skating at one shoulder to open the other
Technique: Fake shot — weight onto the back leg, shoulder dropped, stick starting back; it only sells if you sometimes really shoot. In CARHA, keep the backswing within fifteen inches — never a wind-up
Rule: CARHA 79(a) makes a backswing "more than fifteen inches" behind the puck a slap shot and a minor, a major if it injures, which ejects under 30(a); 79(b) adds a minor for a fake slap shot used to intimidate
Technique: Look-off — head and eyes hard to one option, then deliver to another, and only on a pass you have already surveyed
Read: A deceived defender pivots their hips, reaches with the stick or shifts weight — every one is a positional loss you can spend
Never: Fake when you have no time — two closing forecheckers and your back to the boards means the simple play
```

Space is created in the defender's mind before it exists on the ice. A defender cannot cover every option, so they cover the one they believe is coming. **Show them the wrong one.**

- **The fake shot.** Load your weight onto the back leg, drop the shoulder, and start the stick back. A shot-blocker drops a knee or turns a hip; a goalie sets. Both have stopped moving sideways, so the pass across or the step around is open. It only works if you sometimes really shoot from there. **Deception is credit, and you build it by occasionally paying up.** **In a CARHA league, keep the backswing within fifteen inches of the puck — sell a wrist shot, never a wind-up.** That book decides a slap shot by the backswing, so a longer one is a penalty, and an ejection if it injures; a fake slap shot used to intimidate is a penalty too.
- **The look-off.** Turn your head and eyes hard to one option, then deliver to another. It works on goalies and defenders because head and eyes are the earliest cue they can read. You are not looking where you pass, so use it only on passes you have already surveyed: look, look away, pass.
- **The head and shoulder fake.** The smallest move here. A hard head-and-shoulder dip to your forehand side while carrying wide moves a defender's shoulders and, half the time, their weight. You need them leaning the wrong way for a fifth of a second, not beaten.
- **Showing one option to open another.** Skate at a defender's inside shoulder and they close the middle, which opens the outside. Skate at the outside shoulder and they widen, which opens the middle. Make them commit before you decide.

![](diagram:show-one-shoulder-open-the-other)

The [Cutting Edge Ice Academy](https://www.edgeiceacademy.com/article/how-deception-creates-space-without-speed) coaching material (the name in its own footer; its domain reads `edgeiceacademy.com`) describes what a deceived defender does: they pivot their hips, reach with the stick, or shift weight in response to a false cue. Each is a positional loss you can spend.

**One warning.** Deception costs a beat. With no time — two closing forecheckers, back to the boards — fake nothing and make the simple play.

### Delay and curl-back — turning away from pressure

```facts
Action: Turn away from the pressure, curl back, and hold the puck for a second or two while your support arrives
Position: The offensive zone and the offensive blue line area — including a zone entry where you are first man in with no support
Technique: A tight turn with the puck on your outside hip, body between puck and defender, feet still moving
Goal: Synchronise the play — a forward one stride late becomes a second attacker if you give him a second and a half
Read: Somebody has to take the depth role for the delay to work; then the defence must step or fall back, and either answer creates something
Never: Delay in your own defensive zone, or when you already have numbers
Mindset: Be willing to look, for a second, like you are going backwards
```

**You have to practise this, because it runs against instinct: every instinct says "go forward."**

The play: you carry into contested ice and the option ahead isn't there. Instead of forcing it, **turn away from the pressure** — a tight turn or curl back toward your own end, puck on the outside of the turn — and hold it for a second or two while your support arrives.

The picture shows the hardest version: arriving at the line alone against two defenders, support still coming.

![](diagram:entry-delay-curl)

Why it works:

- **It buys your teammates the one thing they need: time to get there.** A forward one stride late is useless; given a second and a half, he is a second attacker. You are *synchronising* the play, not delaying it. See [Puck Support and Spacing](puck_support_and_spacing.md).
- **It slows the defence with you.** **Greg Revak**, writing at [Hockey's Arsenal](https://hockeysarsenal.substack.com/p/zone-entry-middle-delay) on the middle delay at a zone entry, adds the detail the short version drops: *"if a player away from the puck takes the depth role, then the puck carrier can utilize the space up high to challenge the defense."* Somebody has to go deep. Then the defence faces Revak's question — *"Are you going to step or fall back?"* Step up and they leave space behind them; fall back and they concede a clean entry and a shot from the top of the circles.
- **It keeps the space behind the defence in the bank.** That is the most valuable ice there is, and you burn it the moment you attack into it alone. Delay until a teammate can get there with speed.

The physical skill is a tight turn with the puck on your outside hip, body between puck and defender, feet still moving — see [Puck Handling](../technique/puck_handling.md) and [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md). The mental skill is being willing to look, for a second, like you are going backwards.

**Where delay belongs.** In the offensive zone and at the offensive blue line, and on a zone entry when you are first in with no support. **Never in your own defensive zone**, where a lost puck is a scoring chance, and not when you already have numbers — a 2-on-1 at speed is worth more than a 3-on-2 attacked slowly.

### Changing pace — why coaching material puts a change of speed above top speed

```facts
Action: Glide, look, explode — the glide buys the scan, the scan tells you where to go, the explosion goes there
Key: Separation comes from the difference between your speed and theirs, not from your absolute number
Technique: Two or three hard crossovers out of a glide produce more separation than ten strides of continuous hard skating
Read: The moment a closing defender changes speed is the moment they are least able to change direction
Never: Skate hard the whole shift — you never have a gear left and you never have your head up
Action: Use the change of speed off the puck too — drift, let your check relax, then break
Convention: That a change of speed beats top speed is coaching craft, not a measured finding — the IIHF's coach-development manual and Cutting Edge Ice Academy both teach it, and neither measures it against top speed or against a defender's timing
```

Defenders don't defend speed, they defend *rhythm*. A player skating hard in a straight line at constant speed is easy to match: the defender picks a speed and holds it.

**This is coaching craft, not a measured finding.** The IIHF's *Coach Development Program, Level I* lists **change of pace** among the *"Skating Fakes"* a puck carrier uses one-on-one: a carrier *"varying the speed of attack"* *"may be able to deceive or force a defender to adjust speed and position very quickly."* The **Cutting Edge Ice Academy** material cited above goes further, under the heading *"Changing Pace Is More Dangerous Than Constant Speed"*: *"[a]ccelerating after hesitation creates separation without needing elite top-end speed"*. **Neither measures anything.** No study was found that ranks a change of speed against top speed, or against a defender's timing. Take the technique as taught and hold the ranking loosely — even the IIHF writes only *"may be able to"*.

**Deceleration is a weapon.** Glide, and the defender closing on you must choose: close harder and risk you going around, or ease off and give you room. Either way, the moment they change speed is the moment they can least change direction.

**Then explode.** Two or three hard crossovers out of a glide produce more separation than ten strides of steady hard skating. The counts are illustration, not measurement, but the reason is not: separation comes from the *difference* between your speed and theirs. Two players flat out at the same speed stay the same distance apart forever.

So: **glide, look, explode.** The glide buys the scan, the scan tells you where to go, the explosion goes there. Skate hard all shift and you never have a gear left or your head up.

It works without the puck too, and is worth more there. A winger who drifts, lets their check relax, then breaks is far harder to cover than one who sprints constantly. See [Playing Without the Puck](playing_without_the_puck.md).

### Using the net and the boards

```facts
Position: Behind your opponents' net — attackable from only two sides, with the whole offensive zone laid out in front of you
Key: Nothing behind the goal line is a scoring chance against you, so a mistake there is cheap
Action: Behind your own net you are pivoting, not surveying — one look, one decision, move it
Technique: Skates parallel, puck on the far side — one side to defend. Forearm and hip to the wall, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the checker, never duck
Priority: The wall posture outranks every other instruction in this section. Read Body Contact and Battles in full
Goal: Use wall possession to survive pressure until support arrives, then move it — not to create offence by itself
Risk: The boards remove half your options too
```

Two structures on the ice cannot be skated through. Treat both as teammates.

**Behind the net is the safest pivot in hockey.** Behind your opponents' net with the puck, you can be attacked from only two sides, you see the whole offensive zone, and a defender who commits to one side gives you the other. Nothing behind the goal line is a scoring chance against you, so a mistake there is cheap. It is the one place you can stop, lift your head and choose.

The picture shows two of the options in front of a carrier behind their net.

![](diagram:oz-behind-the-net)

Behind your own net it is *not* safe: a turnover there is a wraparound or a walk-out. There you are pivoting, not surveying — one look, one decision, move it.

**The boards are a wall nobody can attack you through.** Get your skates parallel to the wall with the puck on the far side of your body, and a defender can only come from one side. That halves the angles you defend, and it is why puck protection on the wall is a real possession skill. It is also why the **half-wall** — the boards level with the faceoff dot — is a power-play position: the wall covers one side, the corner is behind, the whole zone is in front.

**Take contact along your side, forearm and hip to the wall, never the point of your shoulder — head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the checker, never duck — and know who is arriving before the puck does.** Never stand square to the wall digging at the puck with your head down: that is the exact picture Hockey Canada's checking-from-behind rule describes. [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md) §6 covers it and outranks anything here.

The trade-off: the boards remove half your options too. Use the wall to survive pressure until support arrives, not to create offence by itself.

### Attacking into space, not at people

```facts
Never: Skate directly at a defender — you hand them a stationary problem they can win by doing nothing
Action: Attack the seam, the gap between two defenders that neither has clearly claimed
Goal: Force a communication and a decision between two players rather than a reaction from one
Action: On a rush, drive the ice between the two defencemen rather than at the outside shoulder of one
Action: On a zone entry, put the puck into the space behind the defender rather than at the defender
Targets: The space your teammate is skating into, never their current blade — a stationary stick freezes them
Action: In the offensive zone, move to the empty ice rather than to the puck
```

The commonest waste of an odd-man rush is skating straight at a defender. You hand them a stationary problem they can win by doing nothing.

**Attack the seam** — the gap *between* two defenders that neither has clearly claimed. Now two players must talk and decide, and communication under pressure is where teams break. If both take you, someone else is open two-on-one. If neither does, you're through.

![](diagram:attack-the-seam)

Same idea across the whole sheet:

- On a rush, drive the ice between the two defencemen rather than at the outside shoulder of one.
- On a zone entry, put the puck into the space behind the defender rather than at the defender. See [Zone Entries](../systems/zone_entries.md).
- On a pass, hit the space your teammate is skating into, not their current blade. A pass to a stationary stick freezes them; a moving player is hard to defend.
- In the offensive zone, move to empty ice, not to the puck. Four players converging on one puck turns five-on-five into five-on-two against yourselves.

### Scanning before you receive

```facts
Key: Knowing your out before the puck arrives buys you more time than any move you will ever learn — a ranking nobody has measured, and it does not hold for a goaltender tracking a live shot
Key: What is measured is only that scanning beats not scanning — one study of pass receptions, and it never ranked scanning against a move; the figures and their limits are in Scanning and Anticipation
Action: Shoulder-check as the pass is being made, not after — two looks are better than one
Read: Your clock starts the instant the puck touches your blade, so arrive already decided
Technique: Catch the puck with your hands and your peripheral vision, not by staring at it
```

**Knowing your out before the puck arrives buys you more time than any move you will ever learn.** **That is a ranking nobody has measured**, though, so be exact about what stands up. Hockey has one study that counted scanning before a pass reception, and it found that players who scanned succeeded more often than players who did not. It never ranked scanning against a move. [Scanning and Anticipation](scanning_and_anticipation.md) gives the figures and their limits. ⚠️ **And it assumes you are receiving a pass, not tracking a shot** — a goaltender facing a live puck gains nothing by looking away from it; see [Goaltender](../positions/goaltender.md).

Why it works: your time with the puck starts when it touches your blade. Spend the first half-second finding out where everyone is, and you have spent most of your budget on information that was free while the puck was travelling. A player who scanned first arrives already decided.

The habit is a shoulder check while the pass is being made, not after. Two is better than one. You won't miss the puck: you catch it with your hands and peripheral vision, not by staring at it.

[Scanning and Anticipation](scanning_and_anticipation.md) covers this habit in full.

### Getting open

```facts
Action: Separate from your check with a change of direction, not a race. Stop when they expect you to go, or cut across their momentum
Read: Break when their head turns to the puck, and time it to the carrier's head coming up rather than to your own eagerness
Action: If you are early, curl and come again rather than stand still waiting
Action: Present a target — a stick on the ice in a clear lane, plus your voice, plus movement
Rule: NHL Rule 56.1 — you are "allowed the ice he is standing on" and are not required to move to let an opponent proceed, but moving into an opponent's path without initially having body position is a pick and an interference minor
Rule: USA Hockey 625 names the pick and writes no tier above the minor inside it, but other rules reach an ejection, including 604(d)'s major plus game misconduct for a reckless pick made as a body check where checking is barred, 640(g) via 640(d), stick blade above the knees, and 602(a)'s match penalty
Rule: The pick is not capped at a minor — with nobody hurt, NHL 56.4, PWHL 57.4 allow a major, IIHF 56.5 a major and game misconduct if reckless, Hockey Canada 8.3(b), CARHA 66(a) that pair at the referee's call, mandatory on injury: major and game misconduct (8.3(b)), an ejecting major (66(e), 30(a))
Options: Three legal ways to occupy a defender — ice you got to first (in CARHA, go round them), a rub on your own real route, or a no-contact screen of the goalie from outside the crease
Rule: A no-contact screen from outside the crease keeps the goal (NHL, IIHF 2026/27 and PWHL Table 14, Situation 5); contact, or a position that stops the goalie moving freely in the crease, can still cost it (69.1, PWHL 71.1)
Rule: USA Hockey 625(b) bars the goal with an attacker in the crease whoever scored — unless the puck got there first, the goalkeeper is out of the crease area, or a defender put them there, which answers for position only, not contact; Situation 9 voids it plus a minor if you impeded a returning goalie
Rule: Hockey Canada 8.5 allows a teammate's goal scored from outside the crease if the player standing in it stays passive and clear of the goaltender's view and movements — and its bar does not apply at all where the puck is already in the crease area
```

Without the puck, nobody hands you space. You make it.

- **Separate from your check with a change of direction, not a race.** You rarely out-skate a defender in a straight line, but you beat them by stopping when they expect you to go, or cutting across their momentum. Go when their head turns to the puck — every defender's eyes leave their man eventually.
- **Time your break to the carrier's head, not your own eagerness.** Open a second before the carrier can look up, and you are covered again by the time they do. Break when their head comes up. If you are early, curl and come again.
- **Present a target, then move.** A stick on the ice in a clear lane, plus your voice, plus movement. See [On-Ice Communication](../foundation/on_ice_communication.md).

**On picks — get a teammate open by going somewhere yourself, never by stepping into a defender's path.** Hockey is **not** basketball: there is no legal pick or screen on a skater. The NHL's interference rule (56) bars using your body to block an opponent who does not have the puck, and USA Hockey's (625) names the "pick" and the "block" as the offence. **The test is movement, not stillness.** NHL Rule 56.1 says *"[a] player is allowed the ice he is standing on (body position) and is not required to move in order to let an opponent proceed"*. What it penalises is the player who *"moves into an opponent's path without initially having body position, thereby taking him out of the play."*

What *is* legal is narrower:

- **Occupying space you got to first.** Skating your route to the puck or the net, if a defender runs into you, that is generally on them: you are entitled to the ice you are standing on. What makes it a penalty is *moving into their path* once they are coming. **Not in a CARHA league:** its Rule 49(a) penalises a player who *"stands in front of an opponent for the purpose of making contact, and/or does not avert body contact with an opponent"*, so there go round them, and do not wait for them to run into you.
- **A "rub" or "traffic"** — skating your own line, at your own speed, to a real destination through ice a defender also wants. If they have to go around you, that is the rub. **Slow down or bend your line into their path and it is a pick.** The referee asks whether you were playing the puck or the man. A rub is never a way to shield your own carrier from a chasing defender — USA Hockey, Hockey Canada and CARHA each write that out as interference.
- **Screening the goalie is legal from outside the crease, and it is the one true screen in hockey.** Stand in the goalie's sightline, off the crease's red line, without touching them. Inside the crease you can lose the goal in every book. ⚠️ **An empty crease is not a free crease:** get in the road of a goalie scrambling back and you can be penalised with no contact at all, and deliberate contact with a goalie can get you thrown out of the game — in USA Hockey (if reckless), Hockey Canada and CARHA (in the crease, or if it injures) a charge on the goalie always does. Section 6 sets out what each book charges.

**What a pick costs.** Interference is a power-play penalty at least, and that is not the ceiling. With nobody hurt, the NHL (56.4) and PWHL (57.4, 57.5) allow a major on the degree of violence, and Hockey Canada (8.3(b)) the same on *"the degree of violence of the impact"*, mandatory where it injures. The IIHF (56.5) reaches a major and game misconduct where it recklessly endangers. CARHA (66(a)) lets the referee go straight to a major and game misconduct, with a major mandatory where it injures (66(e)), and a CARHA major ejects (30(a)). USA Hockey's Rule 625 writes nothing above the minor itself, but other USA Hockey rules reach a pick:

- **Where body checking is barred** — 12 and under, every girls' and women's classification, non-check adult hockey, or wherever your league bars it — a pick with your hip, shoulder or torso is an illegal body check under **Rule 604**: a minor (604(c)), a major and game misconduct for a player who *"recklessly endangers an opponent with a body check in a Competitive Contact category"* (604(d)), and a match penalty available (604(e)).
- **At any level**, **640(g)** gives *"a major plus a game misconduct penalty … to any player who recklessly endangers an opponent as a result of any actions falling under Rule 640(b, c, d, e or f)"*, and **640(h)** a match penalty the same way. A pick reaches it through **640(d)**: a body check *"with no effort to gain possession of the puck"* where *"the blade of the player's stick is above the knees"*. **640(b)** rarely fits: it is *"(except Adult Male Classifications)"* and covers an opponent *"no longer in control of the puck"*, not one who never had it.
- **Rule 602(a)** makes a match penalty mandatory for a player who *"recklessly endangers or attempts to injure any opposing player"*, and ties it to no named foul, so it reaches a reckless pick directly.

## 4. Taking time and space away

Everything above, run in reverse. Defending is shrinking someone else's budget, and it comes down to a handful of habits: a gap closed early, an angle instead of a chase, your blade in a lane before the pass, the middle taken away first, and pressure only with a second layer behind it.

### Gap control — the defenceman's version

```facts
Key: Gap is the distance between you and the puck carrier — the single biggest lever a defenceman has over an attacker's time
Read: Watch the hips, not the puck — hips tell you where they are actually going
Risk: A tight gap removes their time but puts space behind you, exactly where a chip-and-chase or a give-and-go wants to go
Risk: A loose gap protects the space behind you but hands them a full head-up survey and a shot in stride
Convention: Gap distances are a coaching starting point, not a law — published coaching pages disagree with each other, and the only page found that anchors a distance to the centre red line prints two stick lengths there, not a stick and a half. Take them from Defending the Rush
```

**Gap** is the distance between you and the puck carrier. It is the biggest lever a defenceman has over an attacker's time.

There is no free option:

- **A tight gap** takes the attacker's time — they cannot look up or pick a corner. But it puts space *behind* you, exactly where a chip-and-chase or a give-and-go wants to go.
- **A loose gap** protects the space behind you but hands the attacker a full head-up survey and a shot in stride.

Published coaching pages put a good gap through the neutral zone at **roughly two to three stick lengths**, and other coaching material gives it differently. You will also hear a stick and a half at the red line and **one stick length** at your blue line. ⚠️ **Treat all three as a starting point, not a law: a second page puts two stick lengths at the blue line, and the only page found that anchors a figure to the red line prints two stick lengths there, not a stick and a half.** The right gap depends on your backward speed against their forward speed, and on whether they have support coming. [Defending the Rush](../systems/defending_the_rush.md) gives the numbers in full, with the same limit. One to one and a half stick lengths in the neutral zone is a *tight* gap, not the default — see [Neutral Zone Systems](../systems/neutral_zone_systems.md).

The read is the one from section 2: **watch the hips, not the puck.** See [Defending the Rush](../systems/defending_the_rush.md) for the full treatment, including odd-man rushes.

### Angling — steering with your route, not your body

```facts
Action: Close on a curve that removes the middle of the ice, leaving the attacker only the option you want them to have
Goal: Steer them to the boards — you are not chasing them and you are not hitting them
Never: Veer out of the line you picked to make contact. Your route is part of the test, so contact at the end of an angle is a body check even when it is light
Key: An angle is geometry, so it does not depend on you being faster; you arrive where they will be, on the side you chose
Rule: The PWHL allows body contact while angling, but its Rule 52.1 makes it an illegal hit to extend an arm, shoulder or hip after angling, to leave your skating lane to make contact, or to make deliberate contact with opposite-directional force; any step or glide into an opponent is at least a minor
Key: The mechanics live in Body Contact and Battles — route, closing speed, where your stick goes, and what the angle ends in under checking and non-checking rules
```

**Angling means taking a route that leaves the attacker only the option you want them to have.** You are not chasing them and you are not hitting them. You close on a curve that removes the middle, so the only place left is the boards.

Why it beats chasing, which is the reason it belongs in a document about time and space: a chase is a race you might lose, and then you are behind the play. An angle is geometry, so it works whether or not you are faster — you arrive where they will be, on the side you chose. It also works in every league, because steering somebody is not touching them. ⚠️ **But only while you hold the line you picked: pick it early, keep hip, shoulder and arm out of it, and play the puck — and in a CARHA league do not touch them at all.**

**The mechanics are in [Body Contact and Battles → Angling](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md)** — your route against theirs, momentum back toward your net, closing speed, not crossing over as you close, where your stick goes, and what the angle ends in under checking and non-checking rules. That section quotes USA Hockey's definition and Seth Appert's coaching directly; learn it there.

**What it costs where checking is barred or limited.** Your route is part of the test, not just your hands. Under those books the same contact is a body check if you veered into it, and can be legal only if you held your line, were playing the puck, and put no hip, shoulder or arm into it.

- **Hockey Canada** Rule 7.3 — at U13 and below, throughout female hockey and in any Member-approved division — counts it as body-checking *“when a player leaves their skating lane to make contact”* or *“extends an arm, shoulder, or hip after angling an opponent”*, and allows only incidental contact *“in pursuit of the puck on the ice in the same direction”*.
- **USA Hockey**'s Casebook clears the angler in a Competitive Contact category only if they keep their skating lane, use no overt hip, shoulder or forearm, and go for the puck (Standard of Play, Situation 15).
- In **IIHF** women's hockey, ⚠️ **the move itself is the foul** (Rule 101.1) — *“[a]ny move by a Player to step or glide into an opposing Player will be assessed at least a minor penalty (2') for an ‘illegal hit’”* — and the same rule bars using *“the boards to make contact with an opponent to eliminate her from the play, push her into the boards, or pin her along the boards”*. That reaches women's hockey in Britain at every age, adult included.
- **The PWHL** lets you angle with your body, but its Rule 52.1 makes it an illegal hit to extend an arm, shoulder or hip after angling, to leave your skating lane to make contact, or to make deliberate contact with opposite-directional force; its penalty list makes *"[a]ny move by a player to step or glide into an opposing player"* at least a minor.
- **CARHA is strictest:** its Rule 49(a) penalises a player who *“does not avert body contact with an opponent”*. That is a minor; a double minor if it puts them into the boards (49(b), counting as two of the three penalties that put you out of the game under 32(a)); a major at the referee's discretion, always a major where injury results from intentional contact or contact into the boards; and a match penalty open to the referee for a serious incident. A CARHA major puts you out of that game (30(a)) and at least the next one (32(d)). **So in a CARHA league, steer them without touching them.**

### The active stick — free space denial

```facts
Action: Point your stick at a lane or a threat, not resting on the ice between your feet
Key: A blade placed in a lane before the pass prevents passes that are never even attempted
Risk: A stick jabbed at the puck after the pass is a gamble that pulls you out of position when it fails
Technique: One hand on the stick to extend reach when closing space, two hands in a battle or to lift a stick
Technique: Keep the blade flat on the ice — a lane blocked at ankle height is blocked, a blade waving at hip height is not
```

**Putting your stick blade in a passing lane costs nothing and removes an option.** It is arguably the best return on effort in defensive hockey, and the first thing tired players stop doing.

**That ranking is coaching opinion, and hedged where it comes from.** The page this section follows — [RinkHive](https://training.rinkhive.com/2026/03/16/defensive-zone-coverage-habits-positioning-stick-responsibility/), under *"Stick Responsibility: The Most Undercoached Skill in Hockey"* — says *"[s]tick positioning is **arguably** the single most impactful and undercoached defensive habit in the game, from mites to the minors."* "Most impactful" is not quite "best return on effort", and nobody has measured either.

The principle is RinkHive's: *"Your stick's primary job in the defensive zone is to read and intercept — to sit in passing lanes and deny options before the puck arrives."* Its rule of thumb: *"[y]our stick blade should always be pointing at a passing lane or a puck threat, not resting on the ice between your skates"*, because *"[a] well-placed stick takes away a pass that never even gets made."* The carrier sees a blade and looks elsewhere, which costs them time. A stick jabbed at the puck *after* the pass RinkHive calls *"a gamble"*: *"[i]f it misses, you've given the puck carrier an easy move to the net."*

**The rest of this section is not RinkHive's and has no source behind it.** One hand on the stick to extend reach when closing space, two hands in a battle or to lift a stick. Keep the blade flat on the ice: a lane blocked at ankle height is blocked; a blade waving at hip height is not.

### Pressure with support — why lone pressure creates holes

```facts
Key: Lone pressure does not take space away, it moves it — behind you
Action: Send pressure only with a second layer, positioned to take the puck if the first forces a rushed play and to cover the ice they left
Key: F1/F2/F3 are roles defined by order of arrival, not by position — whoever gets there first is F1
Convention: The house default here is a 2-1-2 forecheck and a low zone collapse — F1 pressures, F2 takes the first escape option, F3 holds the high middle
Options: A 1-2-2 holds two back and F1 steers rather than hunts, denying the neutral zone; a 1-3-1 trap concedes more and denies harder
Action: Find out which forecheck your team plays before your first shift, and go on the same trigger as everyone else
Risk: If you go and your teammate doesn't, you have made a hole rather than closed one
```

One player pressuring the puck with nobody behind does not take space away. **It moves it.** The pressuring player leaves the ice they came from, and if they are beaten, that ice is a clean lane and your team is a man short behind the puck.

Pressure only takes space away with a **second layer** — a teammate placed to take the puck if the first forces a rushed play, and to cover the ice the first player left.

![](diagram:layer-not-converge)

This is a system question, so name your system. **The house default throughout is a 2-1-2 forecheck and a low zone collapse (zone) defensive-zone coverage.** In a 2-1-2, F1 pressures, F2 takes the first escape option, F3 holds the high middle — and **F1/F2/F3 are roles by order of arrival, not position: whoever gets there first is F1.** A 1-2-2 sends one forechecker to steer rather than hunt and holds two back, conceding possession behind their net but denying the neutral zone. A 1-3-1 trap concedes more and denies harder.

The point is not which is right. **If you go and your teammate doesn't, you have made a hole rather than closed one** — so find out what your team plays before your first shift, and go on the same trigger as everyone else. See [Forechecking Systems](../systems/forechecking_systems.md) and [Defensive Zone Coverage](../systems/defensive_zone_coverage.md).

### Take away the middle first

```facts
Priority: The middle before the outside, in every zone — the middle has more options radiating out of it
Read: Shots from the slot go in at a far higher rate than shots from the wall, and a carrier in the middle can attack either side
Action: When you are beaten and have to concede something, concede the outside — steer to the wall and defend the inside shoulder
Key: For a defenceman at the offensive blue line, an attacker going around you on the outside is a manageable mistake; through you on the inside is a scoring chance
Convention: Middle before outside is a strong general principle rather than an absolute — some coaches ask for the wall taken away in specific spots, and some penalty kills overload one side, so ask
```

Not all space is equal. **The middle is worth more than the outside** in every zone, because more options radiate out of it.

- In the defensive zone, the middle is the slot, and shots from the slot go in at a far higher rate than shots from the wall.
- In the neutral zone, a puck carrier in the middle can attack either side; a puck carrier on the wall can attack one.
- Even in the offensive zone, killing the middle forces play to the perimeter, where a shot has to beat a screen, a stick and a goalie who can see it.

So when you are beaten and must concede something, **concede the outside.** Steer to the wall, defend the inside shoulder, and trade a wide shot for a dead slot pass. For defencemen at the offensive blue line: an attacker going around you on the outside is a manageable mistake; through you on the inside is a scoring chance.

This is a strong general principle, not an absolute — some coaches want the wall taken away in specific spots, and some penalty kills overload one side. Ask.

### Deny the reception, not the pass

```facts
Never: Lunge at the passing lane — passes are fast and you are guessing
Action: Arrive as the puck does, on the receiver with your stick in their receiving lane or on their blade as the puck lands — a press, not a chop
Priority: Defend the receiver's next action, not the pass. Take away their forward option and the pass gained them nothing
Goal: The result of an interception without having to guess right, and without being out of position when you are wrong
Key: A receiver forced to catch a puck while being closed on loses their scan — a whole second of blindness you created without touching anybody
```

You will spend a career trying to intercept passes and mostly failing, because passes are fast and you are guessing. There is a better version.

**Arrive as the puck does.** Instead of lunging at the lane, take a route that puts you on the receiver as the puck reaches them, stick in their receiving lane or on their blade as it lands — a press, not a chop. The result is the same as an interception, no clean possession, without guessing right or being out of position when you're wrong. The timing keeps it legal: pin the stick of a player still waiting for the pass and you are interfering with someone who does not have the puck. A hard chop can be called a slash, and knocking the stick out of their hands is interference too.

![](diagram:deny-the-reception)

Two ideas make this work:

- **Defend the receiver's next action, not the pass.** Take away their forward option as they receive and the pass gained them nothing: they have to turn back with you on them.
- **A receiver being closed on loses their scan.** They have to look at the puck — a whole second of blindness you created without touching anybody.

That is what "taking away time" means: not preventing the play, but making it arrive with no room attached.

## 5. Where time and space live — a map of the ice

![](diagram:strong-side-and-weak-side)

```facts
Position: Behind the net — no shot exists from there, so it is under-defended, and any defender who commits to one side gives you the other
Position: The weak side — defences slide toward the puck, so the far side is always the last to be covered
Position: The half-wall, the boards level with the faceoff dot — a wall on one side, sightlines to the whole zone, awkward for a defender to attack
Position: The seams between two defenders, in any zone — space by ambiguity rather than by distance
Action: With the puck, move it toward space; without the puck, move yourself toward where the space will be, usually the weak side
Never: Plan to stickhandle in the slot. Plan instead on arriving with no time, for one touch, a tip or a rebound
Never: Treat the strong-side corner as a playmaking zone. Win the battle, then move the puck out of it
Never: Slow down with the puck in the middle of the neutral zone — a carrier who slows there gets swarmed; it is ice to cross with speed or without the puck
```

Learn this like a map: it tells you where to go with the puck and without it.

**Places space usually exists:**

- **Behind the net.** No shot exists from there, so it is under-defended, and any defender who commits to one side gives you the other. The best pivot point on the ice.
- **The weak side.** The side away from the puck. Defences slide toward the puck — that is what a zone collapse *is* — so the far side is covered last. That is why an east-west pass so often beats a north-south one: you outrun the slide.
- **The point.** The blue line. Defended last because it is furthest from the net. The space is real but it is the least dangerous on the sheet — space to *use*, not to admire.
- **The half-wall.** The boards level with the faceoff dot. A wall on one side, sightlines to the whole zone, and awkward to attack because a defender has to come out. Home of the main puck-mover on a power play — in a **1-3-1** there are two half-wall players, one point, one bumper in the middle and one net front.
- **The seams.** Between two defenders, in any zone. Space by ambiguity rather than by distance.

**Places space almost never exists:**

- **The slot** — the ice in front of the net between the faceoff circles. Every defensive system is built to fill it. That's why it's valuable, but plan to arrive with no time: one touch, a tip or a rebound, never a stickhandle. The **high slot** runs from the dots up to the top of the circles; the **goalmouth** is the immediate net front. They are different jobs.
- **The strong-side corner.** Where the puck is, and where two, three or four players converge. A battle zone, not a playmaking zone: win it, then move the puck out. See [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md).
- **The middle of the neutral zone.** Both teams' structures pass through it and every trap is built to fill it. A carrier who slows there gets swarmed. Cross it with speed, or don't cross it with the puck.

So: **with the puck, move it toward space** — behind the net, weak side, point, half-wall. **Without it, move toward where the space will be**, usually the weak side, where the puck will need you next. See [Puck Support and Spacing](puck_support_and_spacing.md).

**Three moments where reading the space decides the play:**

- **A breakout pass is coming to you on your own half-wall and a forechecker is arriving.** Look before it lands. If he is closing hard, chip it past him up the boards or bump it to a teammate swinging under you. If he has over-skated you, turn into the space he left. Which outlet your team wants first is a breakout choice, so ask.
- **You are carrying through the neutral zone and the defence is backing off.** Don't slow down in the middle. Attack a seam or go wide with speed. If nothing is there and support is coming, delay at their blue line; if not, curl back and regroup, or chip it past them and chase. Some teams want it deep every time, others want possession entries, so find out.
- **You have the puck on the offensive half-wall with time.** Look across before you look along: the weak-side seam, the backdoor and the far dot first, then up the wall — the near slot will be full. The moment a defender commits to you, pass to the ice he left.

## 6. How the rules create space

```facts
Rule: Offside — both skates must completely cross the blue line before the puck does, and one skate on or behind keeps you onside (NHL and IIHF 83.1, PWHL 85.1)
Rule: A trail skate lifted but held back over the neutral zone is onside under NHL and IIHF 83.1 and PWHL 85.1; USA Hockey 630(a) and Hockey Canada 6.11 need skate contact, so keep the blade down there, and CARHA 72(c) settles it neither way
Action: Drag a trail skate at the line instead of braking early — keeping the blade down is the only version that is onside in every book
Rule: Delayed offside is recoverable — ⚠️ but USA Hockey 630(d) grants it only at **Youth 15-Only and Girls 16U and up, High School and Adult, below which the whistle is immediate**; the NHL, IIHF and Hockey Canada set no floor, and all attackers must tag the blue line by skate, together (NHL 83.3(i))
Rule: Every book here whistles a delayed offside for touching the puck or going for a loose puck; NHL and IIHF 83.3 add forcing the carrier deeper and being about to hit him, and USA Hockey 630(d) adds pressure that need not drive him deeper — so never close on the carrier expecting a whistle first
Rule: Hybrid icing (NHL 2013-14, IIHF 2014) — Rule 81.1 makes the determining factor "which player would first touch the puck", with the end-zone dot only the decision deadline; too close to call is icing
Rule: Under the NHL and IIHF books, outside the trapezoid the goalie may not play the puck behind the goal line — NHL 27.8 and 63.2(viii) for the penalty, Rule 1.8 in each book for the dimensions, which differ between them; skate contact with the crease is the sole exception
Rule: USA Hockey, Hockey Canada and CARHA print no trapezoid, and none frees the goalie — a freeze behind the goal line, body entirely outside the crease, is a minor (USA Hockey 614(c)(1), CARHA 58(b)); Hockey Canada: any freeze out of the crease, a minor, no warning (Interp. 3 to 10.1(a) v)
Rule: The PWHL switches the trapezoid off (Rules 1.8, 27.8) but its 65.2(viii) still writes the minor for a goalie who plays the puck "outside of the designated area behind the net" — so plan for theirs to come out, and have yours play as if it binds
Action: Dump wide of the trapezoid so a defenceman must retrieve it with your forechecker arriving — how much rec and youth hockey marks one is a question about leagues, and this document has no count
Rule: The restricted area is IIHF Rule 27.7, not the NHL's 27.8; nothing the Elite League publishes switches it off, and England Ice Hockey's Rules & Regulations 22.3 call it "not currently enforced" — in any British competition, plan for their goalie to come out
Rule: A no-contact screen outside the crease keeps the goal — NHL, IIHF 2026/27 and PWHL Table 14, Situation 5; USA Hockey has no goaltender-vision provision and Hockey Canada's two view references are crease-only; blocking a goalie's way back can still be penalised
Rule: Every book can take a goal scored with you in the crease — NHL/IIHF 69.3, PWHL 71.3 for a vision-blocking position; USA Hockey 625(b) flatly, three narrow carve-outs; CARHA 66(b) unless the puck is there; Hockey Canada 8.5 unless a teammate scores and you stayed passive
Rule: Keep your skates off the red crease line, not just out of the paint — the IIHF (Rule 1.7), USA Hockey (625(b) Note), Hockey Canada and CARHA (their Glossaries) count the line as part of the crease
```

The rulebook is not just a list of things you may not do. It is a set of geometric limits that make space and stop teams destroying it. Read it that way and it is easier to remember and to use.

**At the blue line, drag a trail skate rather than braking early, and keep its blade on the ice.** Brake early and you give away free attacking space. A trail skate in contact with the ice, on or behind the line, is onside under every book here — the only version that is.

**Offside prevents cherry-picking.** No attacker may enter the zone ahead of the puck, so nobody can park behind the defence and wait. **Both skates** must completely cross the blue line before the puck does; one skate on or behind the line keeps you onside.

**An airborne trail skate depends on your book.** A skate raised over the neutral zone is onside under the NHL, IIHF and PWHL — PWHL Rule 85.1 carries the NHL's sentence in the same words, *"a 'plane' of the blue line which shall extend from the leading edge of the blue line upwards"*. **USA Hockey (Rule 630(a)) and Hockey Canada (Rule 6.11) require skate *contact*, so keep the blade down if either is your book.** Hockey Canada's preamble says *"[o]nly the player's skate(s) that are in physical contact with the ice surface will be used in determining an off-side"*, and its Interpretation 3 rules one skate above the line, off the ice, **OFF-SIDE**. CARHA never defines the *"own side of"* in its Rule 72(c) Note 1 and never uses the word *"plane"*, so it settles the airborne skate neither way.

**When the linesman's arm goes up for a delayed offside, get every attacker out of the zone, tag up and come back in — and never close on the defending puck carrier expecting a whistle to stop you first.** Where your book allows it, keeping the possession is worth the few seconds.

A **delayed offside** — linesman's arm up, no whistle yet — ends when **every** attacker clears the zone together, by skate contact with the blue line (*"at the same instant"*, NHL 83.3(i); *"simultaneously"*, USA Hockey 630(d)(2)), or when the defending team moves the puck into the neutral zone. **⚠️ First find out whether your book gives you a delayed offside at all.** USA Hockey grants it only *"For all Youth 15-Only levels and above, all Girls 16U levels and above, High School and Adult classifications only"* (Rule 630(d)); **below those, the whistle is immediate.** A player taught to tag up there re-enters after play has stopped, into a defenceman who heard the whistle, stood down and is not braced. The NHL, the IIHF and Hockey Canada (6.11(b)) set no such limit.

**What brings the whistle while the arm is up differs by book.** Under NHL and IIHF Rule 83.3, and PWHL Rule 85.3, play stops if an attacker **touches the puck**, **tries to gain possession of a loose puck**, **forces the defending carrier further back into his own zone**, or **is about to make physical contact with him** — you need not touch anything. USA Hockey (630(d)) stops it only where an attacker touches the puck or *"attempts to gain possession of the puck or continues to apply pressure to the defending puck carrier"*: no contact limb, and a pressure limb *wider* than the NHL's. Hockey Canada (6.11(b)(ii)) and CARHA (72(e)) stop it only for touching the puck or trying for a loose one. ⚠️ **So under USA Hockey, Hockey Canada and CARHA nothing stops play because you are about to hit the carrier. Do not close on him expecting a whistle** — he is still playing the puck, and not braced for you. [Zone Entries](../systems/zone_entries.md) sets the books side by side.

**Ice the puck only when you have to: after your own icing the faceoff comes back to your end — in some books with the same tired players on the ice.** Know which kind of icing your league plays before you chase your own dump-in.

**Icing prevents clearing forever.** Without it, a leading team would simply fire the puck the length of the ice on every touch. Under **hybrid icing** — NHL from 2013-14, IIHF from 2014 — the race is to **the puck**, and the end-zone dot is only the **decision deadline**. Rule 81.1: *"the determining factor is which player would first touch the puck, not which player would first reach the end zone face-off dots"*, decided by the instant the first player's skate reaches that dot. **A race too close to call is icing.** Some youth and rec leagues still play no-touch (automatic) icing and a few touch icing; find out which, because it decides whether chasing your own dump-in is worth anything. **⚠️ Only the defensive-zone draw after your icing is close to universal** — and under USA Hockey and CARHA even that moves to the neutral zone if you ice it during a delayed penalty against the other team (USA Hockey Rule 624(a) Note; CARHA Rule 65, Note 1).

**What else your icing costs, by book.** Under the NHL, IIHF and PWHL your team **cannot change lines** (Rule 81.4; PWHL 83.4), though an injured player can be replaced, and **cannot take a timeout** (Rule 87.1: *"No time-out shall be granted to the defensive team following an icing…"*; PWHL 89.1), so a tired unit is trapped. **USA Hockey imposes neither**: Rule 624 has no substitution bar, 204(a) gives both benches their change at every stoppage, and 636(f) limits timeouts by timing only. That change is the ordinary five-second one, with a warning and then a delay-of-game minor for undue delay (204(a)). **Hockey Canada applies the line-change bar only *"[i]n categories of U18AAA, Junior, and at the option of the Member in Senior"*** (Rule 6.7(d)), and even there allows the timeout — but *"during that time out, the team in violation of icing will not be permitted to make player substitutions"* (6.7(d)(iii)).

**When icing is waved off.** A shorthanded team can usually ice the puck — **though not in every book, and not unconditionally.** NHL Rule 81.6 withdraws that once a *major* has **expired** with nobody on the penalty bench to come out, until the next stoppage (Rule 20.3). USA Hockey grants it only *"For all Youth 15-Only and above, Girls 16U and above, High School and Adult classifications only"* (Rule 624(b)(1)), so below those a shorthanded team cannot ice the puck at all. [Special Teams](../systems/special_teams.md) sets out both. Icing is also waved off from a faceoff, if the puck goes in the net, if the opposing goalie plays it or leaves the crease and moves toward it (NHL — **USA Hockey does not wave off for goalie movement**), or if a linesman judges an opposing skater could have played it. Under the NHL, IIHF and PWHL (Rule 81.1; PWHL 83.1), to beat a potential icing the puck must touch the centre red line **on a stick**, not a skate. Get it past the red line on your stick and you are safe in every book that divides the ice there — but some CARHA leagues divide it at the blue lines, so ask your league.

**Where a trapezoid is enforced, dump the puck wide of it, so a defenceman has to retrieve it with your forechecker arriving; where it is not, expect their goalie to come out and play it.**

**The trapezoid limits the goalie.** It exists to stop goalies acting as a third defenceman and killing the forecheck. Outside it, the goalie may not play the puck behind the goal line: a two-minute delay-of-game minor under **Rules 27.8 and 63.2(viii)**. **The sole exception is the goalie keeping skate contact with the crease** — there is no teammate-based exception. If your league has no trapezoid, wide dumps are worth much less. See [Zone Entries](../systems/zone_entries.md).

**Its size.** **NHL Rule 1.8** sets it per post: seven feet outside each crease (eight feet from each post) at the goal line, widening to eleven feet from each post at the end boards — which works out to **22 feet wide along the goal line, 28 feet along the end boards, 11 feet deep**. (Cite 27.8 for the penalty only: its own dimensions are stale pre-2014 wording and disagree with 1.8. See [Rules Primer](../foundation/rules_primer.md).) **Do not convert those numbers for the IIHF book.** IIHF Rule 1.8 prints *"The outside dimension of the marking along the goal line is 6.80m and along the boards is 8.60m"* — close to the NHL's widths — but its goal line sits **4.0 m** from the end boards (Rule 1.5) against the NHL's 11 feet (3.35 m), so the IIHF trapezoid is roughly **two feet deeper**. That changes where a puck is retrievable, and a conversion hides it.

**Who enforces it.** The **NHL** and **IIHF play**, on very different footings. The IIHF's announcement of 5 June 2021 said it would *"introduce a new Rule Book for the 2021/22 season"*, listing *"A restricted area for goalkeepers behind the net"* — that dates the IIHF's adoption. **The NHL's commonly given 2005-06 date is in no rulebook held here** (its Rules 1.8, 27.8 and 63.2(viii) carry no date), so treat it as received rather than checked. The KHL is commonly said to have it too, **unverified here: no KHL rulebook was available to check.**

**Three books print no trapezoid — USA Hockey, Hockey Canada and CARHA — and the PWHL switches it off without finishing the job.** PWHL Rule 1.8 reads *"No restricted Goalkeeper's Area, which will be known as a 'trapezoid' will be enforced"*, and Rule 27.8 says the same, **but its Rule 65.2(viii) still writes the minor for a goalkeeper who plays the puck *"outside of the designated area behind the net"*** — the book contradicts itself, so against a PWHL goalie, plan for her to come out. The words *"trapezoid"*, *"restricted area"* and *"designated area"* appear nowhere in USA Hockey's playing rules or Casebook, nor in CARHA's book; *"trapezoid"* and *"restricted area"* appear nowhere in Hockey Canada's, whose one *"designated area"* is where Rule 5.4(b) stations a Goal Judge. **How much rec, beer-league and youth hockey that adds up to is a question about leagues, and this document has no count for it.**

But ⚠️ **no trapezoid is not the same as no restriction.** USA Hockey **Rule 614(c)** makes it a delay-of-game minor for a goalkeeper to fall on or gather the puck where *"(3) The puck is outside the boundaries of the ”goalkeeper’s privileged” area"* or where *"(1) The puck is behind the goal line and their body is entirely outside of the goal crease"*. Hockey Canada's **Interpretation 3 to Rule 10.1(a)**, clause v, is blunter: *"When a goaltender leaves their crease, they will not be allowed to freeze the puck. If they do, they will be assessed a Minor penalty. No warning will be issued."* CARHA (Rule 58(b)) and the PWHL (Rule 65.2(i)) likewise make a goalie's freeze behind the net, out of the crease, a minor. So their goalie may fetch a dumped puck but may not sit on it out there.

If you are the goalie, ⚠️ **do not take clause v on its own.** Clause ii of the same Interpretation permits coming out *"to cut down the angle and catch the shot or, after stopping the shot, cover the puck"*, and **Hockey Canada nowhere says which of the two governs a cover made after a save outside the crease.** Treating ii as the exception is a reading, not a Hockey Canada ruling, though it is how the NHL and IIHF print the same pair at 63.2(vi).

**🇬🇧 In Britain, keep your own goalie inside it and plan as though theirs may leave it.** Nothing the Elite League publishes switches off Rule 27.7 (not the NHL's 27.8) — its Casebook adopts the 2026-27 IIHF book. But England Ice Hockey's *Rules & Regulations* 22.3 say the restricted area is *"not currently enforced"* in its own competitions, and **neither the 2025-26 nor the 2026-27 In-House Rules mention it at all** — though their Introduction says *"[u]nless otherwise stated, all rules will be enforced in line with the IIHF Rule Book"*, which points the other way. The published documents do not settle it. So plan as though their goalie **may** come out, ask your league, and look behind the net for painted lines — a hint, not a ruling, since IIHF 27.7 turns on *"the position of the puck"*, not on the markings.

**At the net, screen the goalie from just outside the crease, off its red line, without touching them — and get out of the road of a goalie scrambling back.**

**The crease protects the goalie — and by extension the most valuable space on the ice.** The NHL crease is **8 feet wide at the goal line and 6 feet deep at its deepest point** (Rule 1.7); [Rink Map](../foundation/rink_map.md) has the full geometry. **Screening — standing in the goalie's sightline outside the crease, without contact — is legal**, so the rule protects the goalie's body, not their view. ⚠️ **But keep your skates off the red crease line, not merely out of the blue paint** — the IIHF (Rule 1.7), USA Hockey (the Note to 625(b)), Hockey Canada and CARHA (their Glossaries) count the line as part of the crease. The NHL's and IIHF's Situation 5E allows a goal with an attacker on the line, so off it is the only ice safe under every book.

**Why the screen is legal.** NHL Table 14 and IIHF 2025/26 Table 16 (Situations 5C and 5E) allow the goal and disallow only the in-crease rows; the PWHL's Rule 71 is the same rule as their Rule 69; and USA Hockey and Hockey Canada have no rule that reaches a screen set from outside the crease at all. Stop the goalie moving freely in the crease, by position or contact, and the goal can still go (69.1; PWHL 71.1).

**Inside the crease, every book can cost you the goal.** NHL and IIHF Rule 69.3 and PWHL Rule 71.3 disallow it when you establish a significant position in the crease that blocks the goalie's view, with no contact at all; the IIHF's 69.1 also lets the referee stop play if you do not leave at once. USA Hockey Rule 625(b) disallows any goal scored with an attacker in the crease unless the puck got there first, the goalie was out of the crease or a defender put you there, and its Casebook voids a goal scored with even a stick or a skate in the paint while the goalie is in it (Rule 625, Situation 10). CARHA Rule 66(b) and Hockey Canada Rule 8.5 both say an attacker may not stand in the crease unless the puck is there: CARHA disallows a goal scored while you do, and Hockey Canada lets a teammate's goal from outside stand only if you stayed passive and out of the goalie's view and movements.

**If you are pushed in, get your feet out or get clear of the goalie at once.** USA Hockey's relief in 625(b) covers your position — the whistle and the disallowed goal — and says nothing about a penalty: **625(a)(8) is still a minor for *"physical contact, using their stick or body, in a manner that interferes with the movement of the goalkeeper, unless otherwise specified in the rules."*** Hockey Canada's Interpretation 2 to Rule 8.5(a) requires *"an effort by the attacking player to avoid making contact with the goaltender"*, and where none is made *"they must be penalized for interference with the goaltender"* — the defender who pushed you penalised as well, not instead. CARHA gives a minor to an attacker who is interfered with or tripped and *"fails to attempt to avoid"* contact with the goalkeeper, in or out of the crease (66(b)).

Outside the crease, ⚠️ **stay off the goalie too, and never get in the road of one scrambling back.** The NHL, IIHF and PWHL tolerate only incidental contact you made a reasonable effort to avoid (Rules 69.1 and 69.4; PWHL 71.1 and 71.4), and 69.4 and 71.4 also penalise an attacker whose *"deliberate actions"* stop a goalkeeper who has played the puck outside the crease from returning — a clause that names no contact at all.

Hockey Canada requires attackers to *"make an effort to avoid contact in all circumstances"* (Rule 8.5), and says a goaltender *"is not ‘fair game’ just because they are outside their goal crease"*. Its goaltender-interference statute is contact-only (the Note to Interpretation 3 of Rule 8.5(a)), but a goaltender who has not played the puck is not in possession by its own Glossary, so moving into his path is a minor under its ordinary interference rule, 8.3(i), with no contact needed. CARHA's interference rule, 66(a), reaches impeding any opponent who is not in possession of the puck. USA Hockey files even accidental contact with a goalkeeper as interference (607(d), Note 1), and its Casebook takes the goal and adds an interference minor if you stop a returning goalie from making a play (Rule 625, Situation 9); only its reach to the no-contact version is unsettled, and unsettled is not permission.

**What contact with a goalie costs.** Under USA Hockey any deliberate body contact with a goalkeeper is charging — a minor plus a misconduct or a major plus a game misconduct (607(a) and 607(d), Note 1) — and a reckless one is a mandatory major and game misconduct (607(b)). Plan on a match penalty for it too: USA Hockey's general Rule 602(a) says one *"shall be assessed"* to any player who *"recklessly endangers or attempts to injure"* an opponent, though 607(e) says only that one *"may also be assessed"*.

Hockey Canada 8.5(b) says a major and a game misconduct *"will be assessed to any player who charges the goaltender"*, in or out of the crease; other contact that interferes with the goaltender is at least an 8.5(a) minor, a major and game misconduct where the referee judges it violent, mandatory where it injures, and trying to injure one is a match penalty (8.5(c)). CARHA 66(b) makes deliberate contact with a goalkeeper *"whether in or out of the goal crease"* a minor the referee may raise to a major, and a CARHA major puts you out of the game (30(a)); a charge on a goalkeeper *"within the goal crease"*, or one that injures, is a mandatory major and game misconduct (52(b)). [Rules Primer](../foundation/rules_primer.md) carries the full comparison.

**The offside and icing lines together define the shape of the ice.** In the NHL the blue lines sit 75 feet from the end boards and 50 feet apart, and every neutral-zone system in hockey is built around that 50-foot corridor.

## 7. How space changes with the score, the clock and the rink

```facts
Read: A team protecting a lead concedes the point and the wall and takes away the middle — take the space that is offered
Never: Force the middle against a team protecting a lead; that is how you generate a counter-attack against yourself
Action: Walk the line, shoot for tips and work below the goal line — pull them out of shape rather than through it
Read: With the goalie pulled, six attackers create a genuine spare man and a lot of room at the blue line, and clearing options become worth an enormous amount
Key: Fatigue and rink size move the same variable — closing speed; a tired player's effective gap is bigger than their actual one
Read: "Olympic ice" is no longer a reliable synonym for wide ice — Beijing 2022 was played on the North American surface, so check the venue
Action: On wide ice, more delay, more east-west, more patience before entries; on narrow ice, decide earlier and use the walls
Action: Skate a warm-up lap and notice how far it is from the half-wall to the far dot — that number is your time budget
Rule: The IHUK In-House Rules 2026-27, Rule 1, authorise rinks below 56 m by 26 m — short of IIHF Rule 1.2's minimum on both counts — in all the fixtures they govern; the rules never mention the Elite League. In those fixtures, assume narrow, short ice until you have measured
```

Space is not a constant. Four things move it.

**The score.** A team protecting a lead, especially late, gives up space it doesn't need and takes away space that matters — fewer players below the puck, a passive 1-2-2 or a neutral-zone trap, everything funnelled outside. So as the trailing team you will find **room at the point and on the wall, and almost none in the middle.** Forcing the middle is how you give them a counter-attack. Take what's offered — walk the line, shoot for tips, work below the goal line — and pull them out of shape rather than through it. See [Game Management](../systems/game_management.md) and [Risk Management](risk_management.md).

**The clock.** In the last two minutes of a one-goal game the trailing team pulls the goalie and space distorts: six attackers make a genuine spare man and plenty of room at the blue line, while the defending team's clears become worth an enormous amount because the net is empty. And in the last thirty seconds of a shift, space shrinks for you personally because your legs are gone — a tired player's *effective* gap is bigger than their actual one, which is the real argument for short shifts.

**Fatigue and the rink both change the same thing: closing speed.** Anything that slows how fast defenders arrive gives everyone more time.

**Rink size.** This matters more than most players expect.

- The **NHL sheet is 200 by 85 feet** (about 61 by 26 m), corner radius 28 feet.
- The **traditional international sheet is 60 by 30 metres** (about 197 by 98 feet) — nearly the same length, roughly 13 feet wider. The current IIHF rulebook permits a range of widths (commonly given as 26 to 30 m), and top events have gone narrow: **the 2022 Beijing Olympics were played on the North American 200 × 85 ft surface (about 61 × 26 m)** — the IIHF's own tournament report says so. So "Olympic ice" no longer reliably means wide ice; check the venue.
- **🇬🇧 In the British games the In-House Rules govern, it points the other way.** The 2026-27 In-House Rules authorise rinks *"with a non-standard playing surface (below 56 m × 26 m)"* for all levels, **except** two named rinks permitted only up to U16 — Sutton in England and Coatbridge in Scotland — and the scope line reads *"This rule applies to all fixtures"*, meaning the fixtures those rules govern; they never mention the Elite League, so they settle nothing about its rinks. That is short of IIHF Rule 1.2 on **both** dimensions, so expect smaller and shorter, not wider. Measure your rink; [UK and England Rules](../foundation/uk_rules.md) has the detail.

What extra width does: the length is the same, so the change is all sideways. On a wide sheet the wall is further from the middle, so a forechecker who commits to the boards takes longer to recover, wall defenders are further from the slot, and east-west puck movement gains more than north-south. The game rewards skating, puck movement and patience, and punishes a forecheck that misses.

On the narrow NHL sheet, the opposite: space compresses, the forecheck arrives sooner, and quick decisions and physical play pay. **If you move between sheets:** on wide ice, more delay, more east-west, more patience before entries. On narrow ice, decide earlier, use the walls, and value possession behind the net over possession in the corner.

**In your rec league:** most rinks are close to NHL size in North America and closer to international elsewhere, but plenty of older barns are neither. Skate a warm-up lap and notice how far it is from the half-wall to the far dot. That distance is your time budget.

## 8. How to develop this

```facts
Action: Play small-area games — less space per player means more touches, more decisions per touch and less time to make each one
Action: If your team doesn't run them, run them yourself — three-on-three in one zone, two nets, no offside, first to five
Action: When you spectate, don't watch the puck. Pick one player and watch only them for a full shift, including the twenty seconds they don't touch it
Read: Watch a defenceman's gap from the moment the attacker gets the puck — most of it is decided before the attacker reaches the red line
Action: After every shift, ask "did I have more time than I thought?" — the honest answer is almost always yes
Key: Panic is the default state of an inexperienced player, and panic spends time you actually had
Action: Get one more look before you receive, and when you don't know, take the turn away from pressure rather than the pass into it — but in your own end never hold it: one look, one decision, move it
Mindset: Trust "time" when you hear it — a teammate or the goalie can see what you can't
```

Time and space is a trainable habit, not a personality trait. Three things build it fastest.

**Small-area games.** Cross-ice and half-ice games are the standard development tool — [USA Hockey](https://www.usahockey.com/smallareagames) publishes a full library through its Coaching Education Program: an age-banded *Small Area Competitive Games Handbook*, from 8-and-Under upward, plus a video series. **Take that as a library, not a curriculum** — nothing on the page or in the handbook says USA Hockey's curriculum is built around them. Less space per player means more touches, more decisions per touch and less time for each. The handbook gives the same reason: the games *"are designed to provide an environment to help players in their ability to make quick decisions"*, with players *"challenged to read and react quickly under pressure."* Train your decisions under harsh conditions and a full sheet feels roomy.

If your team doesn't run them, run them yourself: three-on-three in one zone, two nets, no offside, first to five. It is also the most fun hockey there is.

**Watch for it when you spectate — and don't watch the puck.** Pick one player and watch only them for a full shift, including the twenty seconds they don't touch the puck. You will see how much of what looks like skill is arrival: getting to open ice before the puck, checking the shoulder twice before receiving, curling back when nothing's there. See [How to Watch Hockey](../off-the-ice/how_to_watch_hockey.md).

Two specific things to look for:

- **Watch a defenceman's gap on a rush** from the moment the attacker gets the puck. Most of it is decided before the attacker reaches the red line.
- **Count how often a good centre looks over their shoulder** in the ten seconds before a breakout pass. It is more than you think, and it is the whole trick.

**Ask yourself one question after every shift: "did I have more time than I thought?"**

The honest answer, for almost every developing player, is **yes**. Panic is the default state of an inexperienced player, and panic spends time you actually had. Every rushed pass, blind rim and puck flipped to nobody was a moment with more time than it felt.

The fix is not "be braver," which nobody can act on. It is:

- **Get one more look before you receive**, so you know the answer before the puck arrives.
- **When you don't know, take the turn away from pressure** rather than the pass into it. Delay costs a second; a pass into pressure is a turnover. **Except in your own end**, where holding it is how the turnover happens — one look, one decision, move it.
- **Trust "time" when you hear it.** If a teammate or the goalie says nobody's on you, believe them — they can see what you can't. See [On-Ice Communication](../foundation/on_ice_communication.md).

Track it for ten games and the habit takes. It is the fastest improvement available to almost any amateur.

## Common Mistakes

- **Grading yourself on your hands instead of your room.** Judge how much room you had when the puck arrived. Skill with no space produces nothing.
- **Reading distance instead of closing speed.** Read how fast they are closing and where their hips face. Twenty feet closing hard is less time than three feet going the wrong way.
- **Skating hard the entire shift.** Glide, look, explode. At one speed all shift you have no gear left, no head up, and you are easy to match.
- **Attacking at a defender instead of into a seam.** Drive the ice between two defenders so they have to decide who takes you. Skate straight at one and they win by standing still.
- **Turning into pressure instead of away from it.** Turn away so your body is between the defender and the puck. Turn into them and the puck is where they can reach it.
- **Delaying in your own defensive zone.** Take one look and move it. Delay is an offensive-zone tool; in your own end it is how goals go in against you.
- **Scanning after you receive.** As a skater, look while the pass is still travelling. Look after it arrives and you have spent half your time on information that was free.
- **Breaking into open ice before the carrier can see you.** Break when their head comes up. Break early and you are covered again before they can use you.
- **Pressuring the puck with nobody behind you.** Go when a teammate is set as the second layer, on your team's trigger. Alone, you just move the space behind you.
- **Chasing the carrier instead of angling.** Close on a curve that leaves them only the wall, hold your line, and take the puck, not the player. A chase is a race you might lose.
- **Lunging at the pass instead of arriving with it.** Arrive on the receiver as the puck does and press their blade as it lands — a press, not a chop. A lunge is a guess, and a miss leaves you out of position.
- **Moving into a defender's path to spring a teammate.** Get a teammate open by going somewhere yourself: screen the goalie from outside the crease without contact, or skate your own line to a real destination. Hockey has no legal pick on a skater. Moving into their path is interference. Standing still on your own ice is protected, except in CARHA, which penalises not avoiding contact. Two minutes is the floor: a violent or reckless pick can be a major and an ejection in most books, and a reckless one is a match penalty in USA Hockey; where checking is barred, a hip or shoulder pick is an illegal body check.
- **Treating a shove into the crease as a licence.** Get your feet out, or get clear of the goalie, the moment you are pushed in. A shove can save your goal, but failing to avoid the goalie is still a penalty. Deliberate contact can end your game, and in USA Hockey (if reckless), Hockey Canada and CARHA (in the crease, or if it injures) a charge on the goalie always does.
- **Standing still on the half-wall or at the net front.** Keep moving. A stationary player is covered by a defender who also gets to watch the puck.
- **Letting the stick go to sleep.** Keep the blade in a passing lane. It is the cheapest thing you do all shift, and the first thing you drop when tired.
- **Forcing the middle against a team protecting a lead.** Take what's given: they are conceding the outside on purpose.
- **Assuming a goalie who has left the crease to play the puck is fair game as long as you never touch them.** Stay out of the lane of a goalie scrambling back, touching or not. Most books can penalise it with no contact at all, and USA Hockey takes the goal and gives you a minor if you stop them making a play. Go around them.

## Key Takeaways

1. **Time and space are hockey's currency, and skill is what you spend.** Time is distance divided by closing speed — a way of thinking, not a measured quantity. Read hips, not distance.
2. **Show the wrong option, and change speed.** Against a set defender, fake a shot, look off or dip a shoulder — and sometimes really shoot, or nobody buys it. Against one matching your pace, glide, look, explode.
3. **Buy your time before the puck arrives.** Look over your shoulder while the pass travels. When the play ahead is not there, turn away and hold it a second for support — **never in your own end.** ⚠️ **It reverses for the goaltender**, who never looks away from a live shot.
4. **Attack space, not people.** Drive the seam between two defenders and pass to the ice a teammate is skating into. Without the puck, get open with a change of direction, and break when the carrier's head comes up.
5. **Use behind the net and the boards as pivots.** Behind their net, stop and survey; behind your own, one look and move it. On the wall, side-on: skates parallel, forearm and hip to the wall, head up, never your back to the checker, never duck. Move it when support arrives.
6. **Defending the rush, close your gap early and angle rather than chase.** Read the hips. Close on a curve to the wall, hold your line, take the puck, not the player. Where checking is barred or limited — younger age groups, women's hockey, non-contact adult leagues — veering, stepping or gliding into them, adding a hip, shoulder or arm, or pinning them is a penalty; in CARHA, avoid contact altogether.
7. **When beaten, concede the outside and keep the middle.** Steer them to the wall and defend the inside shoulder.
8. **Deny the reception, not the pass.** Blade in the lane before the pass. When you cannot win it, arrive as the puck does and press their blade as it lands — not a chop or a pull. Pin the stick of someone still waiting and it is interference.
9. **Never pressure without a second layer.** Find out whether your team forechecks 2-1-2, 1-2-2 or something else, and go on the same trigger as everyone. This guide assumes a 2-1-2.
10. **Use the rules as geometry.** Keep a trail skate down at the blue line. Dump wide of an enforced trapezoid; in any British competition, plan for their goalie to come out. Screen from outside the crease, off its red line, without contact. Never block a goalie scrambling back — touching or not; most books call it interference, and USA Hockey takes the goal too.
11. **After every shift, ask: "did I have more time than I thought?"** The answer is almost always yes.
