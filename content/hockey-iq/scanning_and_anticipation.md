## Key focus

**Take at least one look before the puck reaches you, and have your eyes back on it when it lands.** The seconds while a pass travels are free looking time. A goaltender is the reverse: once a shot is released, never look away.

**Decide while the puck is still travelling.** Name your first option and your bail-out in a word — *"up the wall"*, *"reverse"* — and let the last look cancel a stale plan. A second-best play made now beats the perfect play made late.

**Scan in this order: pressure on you, your outlets, the danger you are responsible for, then open space.** Keep the puck as your anchor and spend extra looks on people. Know your first option, support option and safe out before the puck arrives.

**Defending, scan for bodies, not the puck.** On the backcheck, look over your inside shoulder more than once and count opponents. In your own end, every time the puck moves, ask who scores if it goes to the net right now.

**Use the free moments: the puck on the far side, the instant your pass leaves your blade, every stoppage.** Find the seam behind you and the body at your net front. After you pass, stop watching the puck. At a whistle, check who is out, the score and the clock.

**Read a puck carrier from the chest and hips, not the head, stick or puck.** Hips turned and outside foot planted means they are going that way. Do not bite on fakes, and if a carrier beats you, recover position rather than chasing.

**Read a passer by the front shoulder and blade, and a shooter by the hands and weight.** Pressure a passer who received without looking. A shooter with weight still back is not shooting yet — get your feet into the lane, head out of it, rather than lunging.

**Play where the puck will be next.** Arrive at the net moving, just outside the crease and off the goaltender, blade out of the paint, expecting the rebound. In a battle you will probably lose, go to where the loose puck will squirt out.

**Anticipate; do not guess.** Your position should still be useful if you are wrong. Jump a passing lane only on a real cue, with support behind you, arriving with your feet — never as the last player back or in your own zone.

**Get your head up off the puck.** You cannot scan while watching the puck. Keep it out in front, and train with a drill that fails if you look down, such as calling out a partner's fingers while stickhandling.

## Overview

Players who seem to see the whole ice have usually just looked already, while the puck was somewhere else.

**Timing is what matters.** A look taken while a pass travels to you costs nothing, because your eyes have no other job. A look taken after the puck lands costs possession time, and the picture changes while you look. So a calm player and a rushed player usually differ in when they looked, not in their hands. **Goaltenders are the exception**: they look off the puck before a shot, then track it all the way in — see [Goaltender](../positions/goaltender.md).

**Looking at all is what has been measured.** In 2,545 receptions by Swedish professionals, the next action succeeded 78.8% of the time after a look and 70.1% without. The study cannot say how often to look, and its authors say so. Clear the bar of one look, then work on what you see.

**The puck finds itself; people do not.** Everyone is skating toward the puck, so it needs no look of its own. Spend your looks on your pressure, your outlet and the body at your net front — the one that actually scores. (The anchor idea comes from eye-tracking five footballers, not hockey.)

**Read the part of a body that cannot lie.** A head, shoulder or stick can fake for free; shifting the body's weight is how a player really changes direction. That evidence is from rugby. A pass is easier to read than a carry, because it has to be aimed.

**The best read is where the puck goes next** — and which battles you will lose. The player who concedes a lost battle and takes the loose puck ends up with it.

**A good read still works when it is wrong.** A jump that works creates a chance; one that misses in your own end concedes one, so the bar is well above a coin flip. And none of this is possible if you must look down to control the puck.

## The central idea: vision is a habit, not a gift

```facts
Key: Players who look like they see the whole ice have almost always simply already looked
Action: Look while the puck is being retrieved, again as the passer's head comes up, and once more just before it arrives
Priority: Looks taken while the puck is travelling are free; looks taken after it arrives are paid for in possession time — except a goaltender once a shot is released, who then never looks away
Goal: Decide before the puck arrives — a decision started afterwards takes about as long as a forechecker needs to reach you
Mindset: Scanning costs no fitness, no ice time and no talent — the whole input is remembering to turn your head
```

Consider two players receiving the same pass on the half-wall — the boards area level with the faceoff dot in the offensive or defensive zone, roughly halfway between the goal line and the blue line.

**Player A** watches the puck travel toward them the whole way. The puck arrives. *Now* they lift their head, find that a forechecker is two feet away, panic, and throw the puck up the boards into nobody.

**Player B** looked over their inside shoulder while the puck was still being retrieved behind the net, looked again as the passer's head came up, and took a last glance a heartbeat before the puck arrived. They knew about the forechecker before the puck was even passed. They received the puck already turning away from the pressure, and moved it to the support they had seen three seconds earlier.

From the bench, Player B looks quicker, calmer and more skilled. Their hands are not better. **They took two or three brief looks, at moments when looking was free.** Copying that costs no fitness, no ice time and no talent.

![](diagram:scan-before-a-wall-reception)

Three lines explain *when* the habit pays:

- **Information gathered while the puck is coming is free.** Your eyes have nothing else to do.
- **Information gathered after the puck arrives is expensive.** You pay for it with possession time, and the picture changes while you look.
- **A decision made before the puck arrives is instant.** One started after it arrives takes about as long as a forechecker needs to reach you.

## Part 1 — Scanning

### The mechanics of a scan

```facts
Technique: A scan is a head turn, not an eye flick and not a chin dip — your chin should pass over your shoulder
Technique: A scan is a snapshot, not a stare. Take the photograph and process it afterwards
Action: Check both shoulders, not just your favourite; the surprise lives on the side you were not planning to use
Never: Let a scan cost you a stride. Scan while gliding, while crossing over, while pivoting
Key: The last look is always back at the puck
```

A scan is a **head turn**, not an eye flick and not a chin dip.

- **Turn far enough to actually see.** Your chin should pass over your shoulder. Moving only your eyes shows you your own shoulder pad and the frame of your cage or visor, which cut your side vision more than people admit.
- **It is a snapshot, not a stare.** Take the photograph and process it afterwards. **Nobody has published a measured duration for a hockey scan.** The hockey study below counted a head turn only if it lasted at least 0.2 seconds — a floor, not a typical length — and the football study's 242-millisecond *fixation* measures something else. The usable test: if you are still looking a second later, you have stopped playing.
- **Check both shoulders.** Most players favour the side they plan to pass to. The surprise lives on the other side.
- **Keep skating.** A scan that costs you a stride is one you will stop taking. Scan while gliding, crossing over and pivoting — all of it is trainable.
- **The last look is always at the puck.** Still looking away when it arrives trades a good decision for a missed reception.

### What the research actually says

**What to take onto the ice: look at least once before every reception, and do not chase a count.** Berg and colleagues (*Journal of Sports Sciences*, 2025) hand-coded 22 televised games from Sweden's top men's and women's leagues, the SHL and SDHL, and measured **2,545 puck receptions** by **88 professionals aged 16 to 36**. It is the primary evidence here; the football research below only corroborates it.

**What Berg measured:**

- A mean of **1.48 scans (SD 1.01) in the five seconds before receiving the puck** — **about 0.30 a second, or one every 3.4 seconds**. That rate is arithmetic on Berg's mean, not a figure the paper prints.
- **At least one scan in only 61%** of receptions; 71% had none or one. So **players took no scan at all in 39% of receptions** — again subtraction, since the paper reports only the 61%.
- **Scanning went with better outcomes.** The next action succeeded **78.8%** of the time after a scan and **70.1%** without — *"a difference between the two types of sequences of 8.7% (95% CI = [5.3%, 12.1%])"*. Controlling for opponent pressure, body contact and time in the game, scanning still predicted success (β = 0.12, 95% CI [0.07, 0.17]).
- **It worked the same for everyone.** Neither gender (β = 0.09, 95% CI [−0.56, 0.61]) nor position (β = −0.31, 95% CI [−0.90, 0.32]) changed the effect. Women scanned slightly more than men (1.55 against 1.39), and defencemen more than forwards (1.60 against 1.43).
- **Two other things predicted success.** No *close combat* — Berg's term for body contact around the reception — (β = −0.18, 95% CI [−0.22, −0.13]), and no opponent within a stick length (β = −0.11, 95% CI [−0.16, −0.06]). **Berg's team never rank these against scanning; the comparison here is read off their coefficients, not taken from the paper.** Getting out of a body-on-body battle is worth more than looking (0.18 against 0.12). Being unpressured is worth about the same as looking — the intervals all but coincide. And looking is what you can do when nobody is on you.

Three details of the method matter. The window was **five seconds**, not football's ten, because hockey changes faster. A scan was a **clear head turn away from the puck held for at least 0.2 seconds** — not an eye flick. And **"success" is a criterion the authors set with the Swedish Ice Hockey Association, not a neutral metric**: a pass counts if a teammate could receive it, a shot if it went towards the net, stickhandling if control lasted three seconds, a dump-in if a teammate recovered it within five. It is a reasonable definition, but it is not a count of goals or kept possession.

### Corroboration from football, and what does not transfer

The football (soccer) research is larger, older and better controlled, and it points the same way. Treat it as support for the principle; the hockey figures above are the ones that apply to you.

**The headline study.** Jordet, Bloomfield and Heijmerikx ("The Hidden Foundation of Field Vision in English Premier League Soccer Players", MIT Sloan Sports Analytics Conference, 2013) coded 1,279 situations involving 118 Premier League midfielders and forwards from close-up broadcast footage. Better players looked around more before receiving, and looking more went with completing more passes — most strongly for midfielders passing forward. **"Better" here means having won a major individual award** such as FIFA World Player of the Year; those players averaged 0.33 searches a second against 0.27 for the rest.

**The follow-up, with better controls.** Jordet and colleagues (*Frontiers in Psychology*, 2020) followed 27 players at one Premier League club through 9,574 possessions in 21 home matches:

- They averaged **0.44 scans a second** (SD 0.30) — roughly **3 scans in the 10 seconds before receiving**.
- Central midfielders scanned most, forwards least.
- They scanned slightly more before **successful** passes (0.46/s) than unsuccessful ones (0.40/s).
- Controlling for ability and pass difficulty, the authors called it a **"small, positive role"** for scanning — a real effect, not a dramatic one.

**The counter-evidence.** Caso, McGuckian and van der Kamp ("No evidence that visual exploratory activity distinguishes the super elite from elite football players", *Science and Medicine in Football*, online 2024, print 2025) compared 18 award-winning Champions League players with 18 elite teammates matched by team, match and position. They found **no significant difference in scanning or performance**, though players scanned more before their final reception than the one before.

It is tempting to conclude that scanning is **a threshold to clear rather than a dial to crank**. **That goes further than the evidence, and Berg's team explicitly decline it**: because they coded scanning as yes-or-no, *"we cannot say whether a higher scanning frequency would be more beneficial compared to a lower one."* And Caso compared two groups who were both already elite.

So the honest position: **going from no scan to at least one is measured, and in professional hockey it is worth 8.7 percentage points of action success. Going from two scans to four has not been measured in hockey, and football's frequency effect is small.** If you take one look every five seconds, the evidence for you is strong. If you already scan constantly, working on the *quality* of what you see is the better bet — but that is reasoning, not a finding.

**One more finding changes how to read all this.** Aksum, Magnaguagno, Bjørndal and Jordet (*Frontiers in Psychology*, 2020) put eye-trackers on players in real 11-a-side matches and analysed 2,832 fixations. Most fixation time went on **the player with the ball**; open space was the *least* fixated category. Fixations averaged 242 milliseconds. So elite footballers do not "stop looking at the ball and look at the space" — they keep the ball as an anchor and add quick snapshots around it.

**Weigh that carefully: the sample was five male footballers**, aged 17 to 23, watching a ball carrier, not on skates. No hockey study has done the same — Berg counted head turns, and hockey's on-ice gaze work (Martell and Vickers, 2004) measures *when* gaze settles in a live defensive-zone task, not where looking time goes. It is here because it is the closest evidence there is. Treat it as a reason to doubt "don't look at the puck", not as proof.

**What does not transfer from football to hockey:**

- **Speed and rink size.** An NHL rink is 200 by 85 feet against a pitch of about 115 by 75 yards, and skaters are far faster than runners. Your picture goes stale sooner, so hockey scans need to be *more recent*, not necessarily more numerous.
- **Where the puck sits.** A puck is out in front on a stick, higher in your view than a ball at your feet, so head-up control is easier in hockey and the excuse is weaker.
- **You can skate backwards.** A retreating defenceman already faces the play; their problem is checking *behind* them.
- **The boards.** You can play the puck off the wall, so "no option" is much rarer.
- **Line changes.** A scan from forty seconds ago may describe five people now on the bench.
- **The numbers.** Do not chase 0.44 scans a second — that is a football rate over ten seconds. Hockey's figure is **1.48 scans in five seconds** (0.30 a second on the arithmetic), and its authors say their data cannot tell you whether more is better.

**A coaching benchmark the measurement contradicts.** Several hockey coaching resources — Better Hockey's is the clearest — recommend scanning **every one to two seconds off the puck**, with a last look in the final half-second before receiving, and say untrained players start at about one scan every four or five seconds.

**Berg's data contradict that benchmark.** Professionals averaged **one scan per 3.4 seconds** before receiving, on the arithmetic, and, on the complement of the paper's 61%, took **no scan at all in 39% of receptions**. "Every one to two seconds" is two to three times the measured professional rate — and the "untrained" rate of one every four or five seconds is close to what professionals actually did.

One caveat in the benchmark's favour: Berg measured only the five seconds before a reception, while the benchmark describes all off-puck play. But that is the window where looking is cheapest and most useful. Use the benchmark, if at all, as a nudge to look more often — not as a number anyone has shown to be right.

### When to scan

```facts
Priority: The highest-value moment to scan is the one to three seconds while the puck is travelling to you
Action: Look while the puck is on the far side of the ice — nothing is being asked of your hands
Action: On the backcheck, look over your inside shoulder and count opponents, not pucks
Action: Before receiving on the wall take an early look and a late one — the early one tells you your options, the late one whether they still exist
Never: Turn your back to a checker or duck. Angle in, feet apart, knees bent, skates parallel to the wall, forearm and hip on it — never the shoulder tip, ideally not the whole shoulder — chin off your chest
Action: Scan during stoppages — who is out, who takes the draw, which defence pair, where your bench is, the score and the clock
Action: Break the reflex of following your own pass; the moment the puck leaves your blade you are an off-puck player
Convention: The scan counts in this section are coaching craft — nobody has validated an optimal number for any of these moments
```

Ranked by value. **The scan counts below are coaching craft**: nobody has validated an ideal number for any of these moments, for the same reason Berg's team cannot say whether more scanning beats less. They make the ranking concrete enough to practise, and a coach's different numbers are equally well founded.

1. **Before the puck comes to you.** The highest-value moment. As soon as the puck is likely to reach you — a teammate retrieving it, a teammate's head coming up, a loose puck you are closest to — you have one to three seconds when your eyes have no other job. Take two or three looks, finishing with your eyes back on the puck. ⚠️ **This is written for the player about to receive a pass.** A goaltender has the opposite job once a shot is released, and does not look away from it — see [Goaltender](../positions/goaltender.md).
2. **While the puck is on the other side of the ice.** The most wasted time in hockey, and your hands are free. Find who is near you, the seam behind you and the net-front threat. Players who do this "always seem to be in the right place".
3. **On the backcheck.** A **backcheck** is racing back to your own end after losing the puck; a **forecheck** is pressuring in the *opponent's* end. Watch only the puck on the way back and you arrive with no idea who came with you. Look over your inside shoulder — the one nearer the middle of the ice — at least twice, and count *opponents*, not pucks.
4. **Before you receive on the wall.** Coaches single this one out — an emphasis, not a counted ranking: half your escape routes are boards and glass, and one blind side is behind you. Take two looks — an early one for your options, a late one to see whether they still exist. **⚠️ And looking is only half of it: the eyes do not save you if the body is wrong.** A wall reception means contact with the boards, and your posture decides the cost. **Never turn your back to the checker, and never duck.** Come in on an angle, feet apart, knees bent, **skates parallel to the wall**, forearm and hip to the wall, head up, chin off your chest — not the point of your shoulder, and not the whole shoulder if you can help it. Already facing the wall and cannot turn? That is damage limitation, not a position to aim for: stick and gloves up and spread on the glass, the impact spread as wide as you can, no head contact at all. Full treatment in [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md); the same override sits on the "man on" call in [On-Ice Communication](../foundation/on_ice_communication.md).
5. **During a stoppage.** Free information almost nobody takes. Details below.
6. **Immediately after you pass.** Your eyes follow your pass by reflex; break it. Once the puck leaves your blade you are an off-puck player, and your next job — support position, or the net — depends on what you see now. See [Puck Support and Spacing](puck_support_and_spacing.md).

**The same window opens when you go back for a dumped puck.** As a defenceman, look over both shoulders before you reach it: where the forechecker comes from decides whether you take it behind the net, reverse it to your partner or move it up the wall. A partner calling *"man on"* or *"time"* gives you the same look for free.

**Scanning during a stoppage** costs nothing but attention:

- Who they have on the ice, and whether it is their top line.
- Who is taking the draw and which way they usually turn.
- Whether their defence pair is the mobile one or the stay-at-home one — this decides whether you can beat them wide.
- Where your bench is relative to the play, which is where you are changing to.
- Whether the last change belongs to the home team, which tells you whether the matchup you are looking at is deliberate.
- The score, the time, and the penalty clock, which change what risk is acceptable. See [Game Management](../systems/game_management.md) and [Risk Management](risk_management.md).

### What to look for, in priority order

```facts
Key: Beginners scan for the puck and stop; experienced players keep the puck as their anchor and spend every extra look on people and space
Priority: Scan in this order — pressure on you, then your outlet options, then the danger you are responsible for, then the space you could move into
Read: Which side pressure arrives on, and how fast it is closing — their speed and angle matter more than their distance
Options: Know your first option, your support option and your safe out before the puck arrives
Read: In your own end, ask who scores if the puck goes to the net right now — usually the net front or the high slot
Convention: Your eyes follow your system — the house-default low zone collapse alternates puck and nearest danger, man-to-man does not; find out which you play
Convention: The "anchor on the puck carrier" cue is imported from eye-tracking five footballers — a strong hint, not a hockey measurement
```

The divide is not *whether* you look at the puck but what you look at **around** it: **beginners scan for the puck and stop there; experienced players keep the puck as their anchor and spend every extra look on people and space.** The puck is the easiest thing on the ice to find — everyone is skating toward it — so it needs no look of its own. What needs one is what will not announce itself. The "anchor" evidence is the football eye-tracking study above — five footballers watching a ball carrier — so treat it as a strong hint from another sport, not a hockey measurement.

Take the information in this order, because it is the order in which it can hurt or help you.

**1. Pressure on you.** Who is closest, how fast are they coming, and from which side?

- **Which side matters more than the distance.** A checker arriving on your forehand side takes your best play away; one arriving on your backhand side leaves your strong hand free.
- **Their speed decides your play, not their distance.** A forechecker fifteen feet away at full speed is more urgent than one eight feet away and gliding.
- **Their angle tells you where they want you to go.** One cutting off the middle is steering you to the boards to seal you there; one coming from outside is steering you into the middle, usually toward a waiting teammate. Go where they *don't* want you.

**2. Your outlet options.** An **outlet** is the pass that gets the puck away from pressure and up the ice. You want three things, in this order:

- **The first option** — the play you would most like to make.
- **The support option** — a teammate close enough to take the puck under pressure, usually behind or beside you.
- **The safe out** — a rim, a puck lifted off the glass and out, or icing it when you are shorthanded and your rules allow it. **Off the glass, never over it.** A **rim** sends the puck *onward* around the boards with the flow; a **reverse** sends it *back against* the flow — mix them up and it is a turnover. Knowing your safe out before the puck arrives is what stops panic: you are never trapped, only choosing.

**3. The danger you are responsible for.** Everything above is about you. This is about the goal. In your own end, the question is: *if the puck goes to the net right now, who scores?* Usually that is a body at the net front — the immediate area in the goalmouth — or a player sneaking into the **high slot** (the area from the faceoff dots up to the top of the circles).

Where your eyes go depends on your system. **The house default is a low zone collapse — the skaters sag toward the net and take whoever comes into their area.** Under a collapse, alternate between the puck and the most dangerous body near you. Under **strict man-to-man**, your assignment gets far more of your looks. Under a **hybrid** — man-on-man low, zone up high — it depends where you stand. **Find out which your team plays.** See [Defensive Zone Coverage](../systems/defensive_zone_coverage.md).

![](diagram:scan-the-danger-in-your-own-end)

**4. The space you could move into.** Last because it is the one you can afford to be wrong about, not because it is unimportant. Where is the empty ice now, and where will it be in two seconds? This is what makes a competent player dangerous — see [Time and Space](time_and_space.md) and [Playing Without the Puck](playing_without_the_puck.md).

**What the safe out costs.** From your own zone, a puck that flies straight out over the glass is a delay-of-game minor in the NHL, the IIHF and the PWHL even when you did not mean it (Rule 63.2(iii); PWHL 65.2(iii)), and one put out on purpose is a minor in every book. Shorthanded icing is not always allowed: USA Hockey grants the exemption only at Youth 15-Only and above, Girls 16U and above, High School and Adult (Rule 624(b)(1)). Under NHL Rule 81.6 a penalty-killing team loses it the moment a *major* **expires** with nobody on the penalty bench to come out, until the next stoppage, when they may put a player in. **The trigger is the expiry, not the kill** — an empty box earlier in the major does not do it; the exemption goes because they are now a player short *without* being shorthanded by reason of a penalty (Rule 20.3). [Special Teams](../systems/special_teams.md) covers all three limits.

### The pre-scan: deciding before the puck arrives

```facts
Key: The puck's arrival should trigger an action, not begin a decision
Action: Use look, decide, receive, act — not receive, look, decide, act
Technique: Name your first option in your head in one or two words — "up the wall", "reverse" — because naming is what commits you
Technique: Name your bail-out too, so a plan A that evaporates leaves you on plan B rather than starting from zero
Action: Let the last look overrule you — cancelling a stale plan is the whole reason that look exists
Mindset: Accept being wrong sometimes; a second-best play made instantly beats the perfect play made a second late
Action: Use your ears as well — a teammate shouting "time" or "man on" is a scan taken on your behalf
```

This is the habit that makes players look fast. **The puck's arrival should trigger an action, not begin a decision.**

Most players go *receive → look → decide → act.* That takes well over a second — long enough for a forechecker to close the gap you thought you had. Good players go *look → decide → receive → act.* Same actions, different order, and the reordering is free.

**How to do it:**

- **While the puck travels, name your first option in your head.** One or two words: *"up the wall."* *"D-to-D."* *"reverse."* *"off the glass."* Naming commits you; a vague intention is not a decision.
- **Name your bail-out too.** If the first option vanishes in the last half-second — and it often will — you are already on plan B. Panic is what happens when you run out of options mid-play.
- **Let the last look overrule you.** Cancelling a stale plan is the whole reason that look exists.
- **Accept being wrong sometimes.** A second-best play made instantly beats the perfect play made a second late almost every time. That hockey below the professional level rewards speed of decision over quality is **coaching consensus, not a measured finding** — but it is widely held, and it matches what happens to a slow decision under a forecheck.

**Use your ears too.** A teammate shouting "time" or "man on" has scanned for you. That is why talking teams look faster. See [On-Ice Communication](../foundation/on_ice_communication.md) for the call list.

## Part 2 — Anticipation: reading the play

Scanning tells you where things *are*. Anticipation tells you where they are *going*, and almost all of it comes down to reading bodies.

### Reading a puck carrier: watch the chest and hips

```facts
Read: Put your eyes on the numbers on the carrier's chest, or their belt — you will see the puck perfectly well peripherally
Read: Feet and hips together are a reliable second cue — hips rotated and outside foot planted means they are going that way
Never: Bite on a head fake, a shoulder dip or a puck fake — those are free to produce and aimed precisely at you
Key: The centre of the body is where to weight your attention, not a single magic cue you can read in isolation
Risk: The best carriers can lie with their hips too — when one genuinely beats you, re-establish position instead of chasing
Action: When you have the puck, deceive with your head and hands — they cost you nothing
```

**Look at the middle of their body, not at the puck, the stick or the head.**

- **Put your eyes on the numbers on their chest, or their belt.** You will see the puck well enough in your peripheral vision. Head, shoulders, hands and stick can all be thrown one way without the player going anywhere; shifting the body's weight sideways is part of how a change of direction actually happens. It is a bias in your favour, not a law.
- **Do not bite on a head fake, a shoulder dip or a puck fake.** They are free for the attacker to produce and aimed precisely at you.
- **Watch their feet as a second cue.** Hips rotated and outside foot planted means they are going that way.
- **The very best carriers can lie with their hips too.** A whole-body change of direction, sold and then reversed, will beat you. When it does, you were simply beaten — re-establish position rather than chasing.

When you have the puck, flip it: **your head and hands are your deception tools, and they cost you nothing** — a look-off, a shoulder drop, a puck fake. See [Puck Handling](../technique/puck_handling.md).

**The evidence is one line of research, from rugby, not hockey.** Brault, Bideau, Kulpa and Craig (*PLoS ONE*, 2012) studied deceptive side-steps and called the **side-to-side movement of the centre of mass** an **"honest signal"**: it is tied to where the runner can actually go, so an attacker has to keep it small to stay unpredictable. Experts waited longer before committing (268 ms against 193 ms for novices) and were right far more often; novices were pulled around by trunk rotation and foot placement. Lynch, Olivier, Bideau and Kulpa (*PLoS One*, 2019) then complicated it: shown *only* a dot for the centre of gravity, experts and novices both fell to about chance, and adding the dot to a full-body display did not help. Limb motion and trunk orientation matter too. So the middle of the body is *where to weight your attention*, not one magic cue.

### Reading a passer

```facts
Read: Most passes go roughly where the front shoulder points — an unsquared passer cannot pass hard across their body
Read: An open blade lifts the puck into a saucer or a chip; a closed blade pointed at a lane means that lane is about to be used
Read: At most levels players look at their target before passing to it, often a full second before
Countered by: The look-off — a player staring at one option and passing to another has already scanned, so read the shoulders and blade instead
Action: Pressure a passer who received without scanning — they have to look before they pass and will telegraph it
```

A pass is easier to predict than a carry, because it has to be aimed.

- **Shoulders.** Most passes go roughly where the front shoulder points. A passer who has not squared up cannot pass hard and accurately across their body.
- **The blade.** An open blade — face rolled back — lifts the puck into a saucer or a chip. A closed blade keeps it flat. A closed blade pointed at a lane means that lane is about to be used.
- **Where their eyes have been.** At most levels players look at their target before passing, often a full second before. If they looked at the far winger while receiving, the far winger is the pass.
- **The look-off.** A player staring at one option and passing to another has scanned earlier and knows where everyone is. Against them, read the shoulders and blade, not the eyes.
- **A passer who received without scanning has to look before passing.** Pressure them at once: they need an extra beat, and they will telegraph the pass.

### Reading pressure: knowing where the forecheck is coming from

```facts
Key: F1, F2 and F3 are roles set by order of arrival, not positions and not people — your left winger can be F1 one shift and F3 the next
Read: Against the house-default 2-1-2, two bodies arrive fast from two directions and the wall gets sealed early
Action: Escape a 2-1-2 behind the net with a reverse to your partner or a hard rim — not a wheel, because the second forechecker holds the far side
Options: Against a 1-2-2, an early up-the-wall pass or a regroup; against a 1-3-1 trap, skate it up or chip past the layer; against a full press, the safe out
Action: Find out what your opponent is forechecking on your first shift, and tell the bench
Rule: After your own icing you take a defensive-zone faceoff with no line change (NHL Rules 81.4, 82.1), though an injured player can still be replaced, and no timeout (87.1) — expect immediate pressure
Rule: The line-change bar after your own icing is not absolute — NHL and IIHF 81.4 and PWHL 83.4 still allow a substitution to put back a goalkeeper pulled for an extra attacker, to replace an injured player, or on a strength-changing penalty; the IIHF's 81.4 adds a broken skate; its 82.1 does not
Rule: USA Hockey blocks neither the line change nor the time-out (Rules 624, 636(f)), so there the tired line gets off
```

A **forecheck** is the other team pressuring you in your own end to force a turnover. Its shape tells you where pressure will arrive **before it arrives**, so you can pre-decide your breakout.

The roles are **F1, F2 and F3 — set by order of arrival, not positions or people.** Your left winger can be F1 one shift and F3 the next.

**The house default is a 2-1-2 forecheck** — two forwards in deep, one high, two defencemen on the offensive blue line. Against it:

- **Two bodies arrive fast, from two directions.** F1 attacks the puck; F2 attacks the obvious outlet.
- **The wall gets sealed early**, so the wall pass is the likeliest to be picked off.
- **Escape with a reverse to your partner or a hard rim** — both forecheckers are committed, and the reverse punishes their momentum. A wheel around the net is dangerous, because F2 holds the far side.

Other shapes change the read:

- **A 1-2-2.** One in deep, two across the middle, two back. You get time in the corner, but it is deliberate — they want you to carry into a wall of three. Beat it with a hard, early up-the-wall pass before the second layer sets, or a regroup back to your goal line.
- **A 1-3-1 or other neutral-zone trap.** Almost no pressure in your corner, which is bait; the wall and middle are filled. Skate it up with speed to force someone to commit, or chip it past the layer rather than passing into it.
- **An aggressive 2-1-2 or full-ice press.** Everything is pressured at once. Take the safe out — off the glass or a rim — and live to play the next shift.

**Find out what your opponent is forechecking on your first shift, and tell the bench.** Almost nobody does this job. See [Forechecking Systems](../systems/forechecking_systems.md) and [Breakouts](../systems/breakouts.md).

**After your own icing, expect immediate pressure and pre-decide the safe out.** In the NHL and IIHF a tired unit that ices the puck stays on for a defensive-zone draw against a fresh opposing unit.

**What it costs.** After your own icing, NHL and IIHF teams cannot change lines (Rules 81.4 and 82.1) and cannot take a timeout (Rule 87.1). The bar has exceptions. ⚠️ **An injured player does not have to be left on the ice for that draw** — NHL Rule 81.4 continues: *"However, a team shall be permitted to make a player substitution to replace a goalkeeper who had been substituted for an extra attacker, to replace an injured player, or when a penalty has been assessed which affects the on-ice strength of either team."* **IIHF Rule 81.4 adds *"a Player with a broken skate"*** — which the IIHF's own Rule 82.1 does not repeat, so do not count on it without the official's say-so. **PWHL Rules 83.4 and 84.1 print the NHL's three exceptions and no fourth.** A tired line is not on any list.

**USA Hockey is different** — its Rule 624 puts no substitution bar on an icing and its Rule 636(f) sets no icing condition on time-outs, so the tired line simply gets off. CARHA's icing rule has no substitution bar either. Hockey Canada bars the change only in U18 AAA and Junior, and in Senior at its Member's option — and even there lets either team take its time-out, though the team that iced it still cannot change during it (Rule 6.7(d)). [Game Management](../systems/game_management.md) carries the book-by-book comparison.

### Reading a shooter

```facts
Read: Hands wide with the bottom hand down the shaft means a slap or heavy wrist shot that takes time to load; hands close means a quick release or a pass
Read: Weight transfer decides whether — weight still sitting back means the shot is not coming yet, whatever the hands are doing
Read: Blade angle at release decides where — an open blade lifts it, a closed blade keeps it low
Never: Read the head — any shooter worth worrying about is looking somewhere other than where they intend to shoot
Action: As a defender, get your feet into the lane a line-walking shooter is creating, square to it with your head out of it, rather than lunging
Action: As a goaltender, move your head to look around a screen rather than over it
Rule: A no-contact screen outside the crease is legal and the goal stands — NHL Table 14 and IIHF 2025/26 Table 16, Situations 5C and 5E — unless you stop the goalie moving in the crease (Rule 69.1); stand off the line too, as most books count it as crease
Rule: Getting in a returning goalie's way is interference — NHL and IIHF 56.2(i), PWHL 57.2(i), Hockey Canada 8.3(i), CARHA 66(a); USA Hockey 625 Sit. 9: no goal, a minor; deliberate contact is charging (607), at least a minor plus misconduct; reckless: major and game misconduct, plan on a match
Rule: Leave a returning goalie a lane — under Hockey Canada (8.3(b), 8.3(e), 8.5(b), 8.5(e)) and CARHA (66(e), 30(a)) every interference major ejects you, and injuring someone makes the major and ejection mandatory
Rule: Never block a goalie's lane recklessly — IIHF 56.5 lets the referee add a major and game misconduct for reckless interference, no injury needed
Rule: Stay off a goaltender outside the crease: they are not "fair game" there — NHL and IIHF 69.4, PWHL 71.4, Hockey Canada 8.5, CARHA 52(b) and USA Hockey 607(d) each call for a penalty for unnecessary contact, and NHL and IIHF 69.2 and PWHL 71.2 price deliberate contact at a minor or a major
Rule: Never charge a goaltender, in or out of the crease — mandatory major and game misconduct: Hockey Canada 8.5(b), CARHA 52(b) in the crease or on any injury, USA Hockey 607(b) when reckless; CARHA 66(b) a minor or ejecting major for deliberate contact anywhere
Rule: Never try to hurt a goaltender — an attempt to injure is a mandatory match penalty under NHL and PWHL 21.1, Hockey Canada 7.1(c) and 8.5(c), CARHA 48(a) and USA Hockey 602(a)
Rule: Charging a goaltender in the NHL, IIHF or PWHL can cost a major and game misconduct (NHL and PWHL 42.1 in the crease, IIHF 42.4), and NHL and PWHL 42.5 mandate the game misconduct when a charging major injures the face or head; NHL and PWHL 42.4 also allow a match
```

Goaltenders and shot-blocking defenders read the same cues.

- **The hands are the loudest cue.** Hands wide, bottom hand well down the shaft, means a slap shot or a heavy wrist shot that takes time to load. Hands close together means a quick release, a snap shot, or a pass.
- **Weight transfer decides *whether*.** A shot needs weight moving onto the front leg. Weight still sitting back means the shot is not coming yet — they are waiting for a lane, a screen or a pass.
- **Blade angle at release decides *where*.** An open blade lifts the puck; a closed one keeps it low. For a goalie, it is the difference between a low save and getting beaten upstairs.
- **Do not read the head.** Any shooter worth worrying about is looking somewhere else.
- **For defenders: a shooter walking the line is hunting a lane, not a shot.** Walking the line means sliding along the blue line to change the angle. Get *into* the lane they are creating, feet first, square to it with your head out of it — do not lunge, because over-committing turns a point shot into a two-on-one behind you. See [Risk Management](risk_management.md).
- **For goaltenders,** coaching resources consistently list hands, blade angle, weight shift and foot position, plus moving your head to look *around* screens rather than over them. **Precise figures circulate** for how much shoulder rotation or weight shift predicts shot direction, and how many milliseconds it buys; none has a credible source, so none is given here. See [Goaltender](../positions/goaltender.md).

**Screen from in front of the crease, not in it — and as a goaltender, look around a screen rather than waiting for a whistle.** As the screener, stand in the goalie's sightline just outside the crease and off its line, without contact, and leave the goalie room to move. ⚠️ **Keep your skates and stick out of the paint.** Never step into a goalie's path back to the crease, stay off a goalie outside it altogether, and never charge one.

**What it costs:**

- **A screen outside the crease, without contact, is legal and the goal stands.** NHL Table 14 and IIHF 2025/26 Table 16 (Situations 5C and 5E) allow it, even planted on the crease line; only the in-crease rows disallow it. The PWHL's Rule 71 is the same screening rule as Rule 69. USA Hockey, Hockey Canada and CARHA have no rule that reaches a screen set from outside the crease. So a goalie has nothing to appeal. Rule 69.1 still takes the goal if your position stops the goalie moving freely within the crease, and contact you start can cost it in or out of the crease (NHL 69.1, Hockey Canada 8.5 Interpretation 1, CARHA 66(b)). Stand off the line anyway: the IIHF's own Rule 1.7, USA Hockey, Hockey Canada and CARHA count it as part of the crease.
- **In the crease it changes.** USA Hockey Rule 625(b) disallows a goal scored with an attacker in the crease unless the puck got there first, the goalie was out of the crease or a defender put you there, and its Casebook voids a goal scored with even a stick or skate in the paint while the goalie is in it (Rule 625, Situation 10). CARHA Rule 66(b) and Hockey Canada Rule 8.5 bar standing in the crease unless the puck is there: CARHA disallows a goal scored while you do, and Hockey Canada allows a teammate's goal only if you stayed passive and out of the goalie's view and movements. Two books can also stop play: USA Hockey's 625(b), with your team in possession in the attacking zone and the goalie in contact with the crease, and the IIHF's 69.1 when you do not leave at once.
- **Blocking a goalie's way back** ⚠️ **is interference.** Holding ice you already stand on is body position (NHL and IIHF Rule 56.1); moving into a goaltender's path back to the crease is interference in every book here — NHL and IIHF 56.2(i) and 69.4, PWHL 57.2(i) and 71.4, Hockey Canada 8.3(i), CARHA 66(a). Under USA Hockey, even with the crease empty, it costs the goal and an interference minor (Casebook Rule 625, Situation 9). It is usually a minor. A violent or reckless block can cost a major — under the IIHF, an ejection — and one that hurts someone can end your game; under Hockey Canada and CARHA every interference major is an ejection, and injuring someone makes one mandatory.
- **A goaltender out of the crease is not "fair game".** Every book calls for a penalty for unnecessary contact (NHL and IIHF 69.4, PWHL 71.4, Hockey Canada 8.5, CARHA 52(b), USA Hockey 607(d)), and deliberate contact is a minor or a major (NHL and IIHF 69.2, PWHL 71.2, CARHA 66(b)). Under USA Hockey even accidental contact is interference, and any deliberate body contact is charging (607(d), Note 1).
- **Charging a goaltender, in or out of the crease,** ⚠️ **can end your game in every book.** Hockey Canada 8.5(b): a major and a game misconduct. CARHA 52(b): the same in the crease or when it injures, and CARHA's Rule 30(a) ejects for any major. USA Hockey 607(a): a minor plus a misconduct or a major plus a game misconduct; a reckless one is a mandatory major and game misconduct (607(b)) — plan on a match penalty for it too (602(a)). NHL and PWHL 42.1 reach a major and a game misconduct for charging a goalkeeper in the crease; anywhere on the ice, their 42.5 makes the game misconduct mandatory when a charging major injures the face or head, as the EIHL Casebook's Rule 42 does, and their 42.4 allows a match penalty. The IIHF's 42.4 can give a major and a game misconduct anywhere on the ice. An attempt to injure requires a match penalty under NHL and PWHL 21.1, CARHA 48(a), Hockey Canada 8.5(c) and USA Hockey 602(a).

### Reading a defenceman (as an attacker on the rush)

```facts
Read: A tight gap closing hard means they intend to end this at the blue line — momentum is a commitment
Action: Against a hard closer, change pace — slow down and let them over-skate, cut underneath, or chip the puck past them and race
Never: Skate straight into a hard closer at full speed; that is exactly the collision they set up
Read: A soft gap concedes the line to protect the slot — beat it with the delay, the drop pass, or a shot for a rebound with a body arriving
Read: Skates parallel and square means uncommitted; hips opened to one side means they have committed, so attack the side they opened or abandoned
Options: Against a defender standing up at the line, chip and chase, pass laterally into another lane, or regroup rather than force it
Key: The stick is the weakest of the defender cues — a stick is easy to move, so weight it below the feet
```

Your **gap** is the distance between the defender and you. Reading a defender is mostly reading their gap and their feet.

- **A tight gap — about one to one and a half stick lengths, closing hard.** They mean to end this at the blue line, and their momentum is a commitment. Change pace: slow down and let them over-skate you, cut underneath, or chip the puck past them and race. Do not skate straight into a hard closer at full speed — that is the collision they set up.
- **A soft gap — backing off to protect the middle.** They concede the blue line to protect the **slot**, the dangerous scoring area in front of the net. You will get in with the puck but not to the slot. Beat it with the delay (curl back for the second wave), the drop pass, or a shot aimed for a rebound with a body arriving at the net.
- **Their feet.** Skates parallel and square means they can still pivot either way. Skates angled or hips opened to one side means they *have* committed. Recognise the commitment, then attack the side they opened or abandoned.
- **Standing up versus backing off at the line.** Standing up takes the entry away: chip and chase, pass laterally to a teammate entering in another lane, or regroup rather than forcing it. Backing off gives you the line and takes the middle. (**Stepping up** is challenging forward in the neutral zone or at your own line; a **pinch** is stepping *down* from the *offensive* blue line.)
- **Their stick.** An active stick in the middle lane protects the middle; a trailing or outside stick means the middle may be open. A stick is easy to move, so this is the weakest cue — weight it below the feet.

The gap figures — one to one and a half stick lengths tight, two to three ordinary through the neutral zone — are coaching convention, not measurement, and the coaching pages that print numbers disagree. [Defending the Rush](../systems/defending_the_rush.md) sets out the scale and which published pages back or contradict each figure; [Neutral Zone Systems](../systems/neutral_zone_systems.md) covers why a tight gap is a five-man job.

### Reading the play's next state

```facts
Read: A hard rim follows the curve and arrives predictably; a soft one dies in the corner, and a flip off the glass lands somewhere else entirely
Action: Learn your home rink's boards and glass deliberately, and bounce a couple off the end boards in warm-up at an away rink
Convention: The rebound heuristic — the puck kicks off the way the pad or blocker was facing — is coaching craft, not a measured finding
Action: Arrive at the net moving, just outside the crease and off the goaltender, stick on the ice, blade out of the paint, expecting the puck rather than watching for it
Read: Arriving second, off-balance, on your backhand or against an established bigger player means you will probably lose that battle
Action: Play for the next state — get where the loose puck will squirt out, or pin it to the boards with skate or stick for a moment, never the player; holding it for a whistle is a minor in every book
```

The most advanced read is not about a person. It is where the *puck* is about to be.

- **Where a loose puck will go.** A hard rim follows the boards and arrives predictably; a soft one dies in the corner; a puck flipped off the glass comes down somewhere else entirely. Rinks differ — lively glass, dead spots behind the net. **Learn your home rink deliberately**, and at an away rink bounce a couple off the end boards in warm-up.
- **Where a rebound will go.** The rule of thumb is that it kicks off the way the goalie's pad or blocker was facing — so a shot from an angle tends to go to the far side, and you should arrive at the far post. **This is coaching craft, not a measured finding**, and goalies who steer rebounds break it. The reliable part: arrive at the net moving, just outside the crease and off the goaltender, stick on the ice, expecting the puck rather than watching for it.
- **Which battles you will probably lose.** This read saves more goals than any other. Arriving second, off balance, on your backhand, or against a bigger player already established, **you will probably lose.** Stop trying to win it and play for the *next* state: get to where the loose puck will squirt out, or pin the puck — not the player — against the boards with your skate or stick for a moment, for a teammate to dig out. Never with your hands, and never hold it for a whistle. The player who concedes the battle and takes the second puck ends up with it. See [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md).

**What it costs.** Holding the puck to force a stoppage is a delay-of-game minor in every book (NHL and IIHF 63.2, PWHL 65.2, Hockey Canada 10.1, CARHA 74(b), USA Hockey 632(b)). Hockey Canada and CARHA excuse you only while you are being checked, and USA Hockey calls it after three seconds whether or not you were pressured.

## Pattern recognition: how experience turns into instinct

```facts
Key: Experience does not give you faster eyes — it gives you fewer things to think about, because details compress into recognised units
Action: Name patterns out loud after they happen, on the bench — unnamed experience mostly evaporates
Action: Predict out loud before they happen and check yourself; a wrong prediction you notice beats ten right ones you never made
Action: Work on one pattern per week — trying to notice everything means noticing nothing
Action: Chase decisions per minute, not ice time — a three-on-three in a third of the ice gives you far more reads
Convention: Off-ice cognitive tools are a supplement to playing and being watched by someone who knows what they are looking at, not a substitute
```

Experienced players do not have faster eyes. They have **fewer things to think about**, because experience compresses groups of details into single recognised units.

A new player sees ten people in ten places and has to work out what each means. An experienced player sees *"low-to-high developing, net front unmarked"* — one thing, with an answer attached. That is why instinct feels instant: nothing is computed, something is *recognised*. It takes repetitions, but you can get far more from the ones you already have.

**How to speed it up:**

- **Name patterns out loud after they happen.** On the bench: *"that was an overload — three on the strong side, and the weak-side point was wide open."* Unnamed experience mostly evaporates.
- **Predict out loud, then check.** Watching a game, say what happens next — "they'll reverse it", "he's going to walk the line and shoot for the tip". A wrong prediction you notice is worth more than ten right ones you never made, and you can do it from the stands.
- **Work on one pattern a week.** "This week I only watch what the weak-side defenceman does on the breakout." Trying to notice everything means noticing nothing.
- **Get more decisions per minute.** A three-on-three in a third of the ice gives many times the reads of a full-ice drill. Decisions build recognition, not ice time. See [Practice and Development](../off-the-ice/practice_and_development.md).

**A caution about off-ice "hockey IQ" tests.** Fortin-Guichard and colleagues (*Journal of Sports Sciences*, 2025) tested 40 elite male players aged about 15–16 on cognitive tasks, video anticipation with eye-tracking, virtual-reality hockey and small-sided games, while five scouts tracked the same players for a year. **The scouts alone beat the tests alone; combining them did best.** But "accurate" meant **agreeing with coaches' assessments**, not who was later drafted or turned professional — so it is evidence about predicting expert judgement, not careers. Off-ice tools are a supplement, not a substitute for playing and being watched by someone who knows what to look for.

## The precondition: the head-up habit

```facts
Key: Scanning is unavailable to you if you have to look at the puck to control it
Technique: Keep the puck in your peripheral vision — aware of it the way you are aware of your own hands
Technique: Hands away from your body, so the puck is out in front where your peripheral vision can see it
Technique: A soft grip — tension in the hands is what makes you look down at a puck you cannot feel
Action: Train it with a forcing function, not willpower. Stickhandle while calling out fingers or coloured cards, ten to fifteen minutes a day
```

None of the above is available if you have to look at the puck to control it. A player who must watch their stick can scan only without the puck — missing exactly the moments when scanning is worth most. So head-up puck control is the **entry requirement for hockey sense**, and why "keep your head up" is the sport's most repeated instruction.

What it takes (full treatment in [Puck Handling](../technique/puck_handling.md)):

- **Puck in your peripheral vision**, aware of it the way you are aware of your hands.
- **Hands away from your body**, so the puck is out in front where you can see it without looking, not tucked in at your feet.
- **A soft grip.** Tense hands cannot feel the puck, so you look down to check on it.
- **Train it with a forcing function, not willpower.** Stickhandle while someone holds up fingers or coloured cards for you to call out — looking down *fails the drill*. Ten to fifteen minutes a day off the ice, with a ball or a puck on a slideboard, moves this faster than almost any other off-ice work.

## Anticipation versus guessing

```facts
Key: Anticipation is acting on information; guessing is acting on hope
Read: The test is not whether you were right. Ask whether your position would still be useful if you turn out to be wrong
Action: Jump a lane only on a real cue, with support behind you, and arrive in it with your feet — reaching is the signature of a guess
Position: Jump in the neutral or offensive zone, where being wrong costs a rush against rather than a goal against
Never: Jump as the last player back, in your own zone, or again after you have already missed once this shift
Risk: A successful jump creates a chance and a failed one in your own zone concedes one — so the read has to be well over 50/50
```

They look the same when they work and the same when they fail, but they are not the same thing. **Anticipation is acting on information. Guessing is acting on hope.**

The test is not whether you were right. It is this:

> **Would my position still be useful if I turn out to be wrong?**

An anticipating player shades toward the likely pass while staying between the puck and the net — defensible either way. A guessing player commits to a spot that is worthless unless the guess lands.

![](diagram:lane-jump-anticipation)

![](diagram:lane-jump-guess)

**When jumping a passing lane is the right play:**

- You have a real cue — the passer's shoulders, a closed blade pointed at that lane, or their eyes have already been there.
- There is **support behind you**, covering the ice you leave.
- The puck is in the **neutral or offensive zone**, where being wrong costs a rush against rather than a goal against.
- You can get there **with your feet**, not by reaching with your stick. Reaching is the physical signature of a guess.
- The game state allows it. Tied in the second period, fine. Protecting a one-goal lead with two minutes left, no. See [Risk Management](risk_management.md) and [Game Management](../systems/game_management.md).

**When it is how you concede a breakaway:**

- You are **the last player back**.
- You are **in your own zone**, especially the middle of it, where a miss is a point-blank chance.
- You decided **before the passer even had the puck** — a plan looking for a reason, not a read.
- You are **reaching** rather than skating, leaving your body in the wrong place and your feet stopped.
- **You have already missed once this shift.** Chasing one gamble with another is how one mistake becomes a goal.

**The costs are not equal:** a jump that works usually creates a chance; one that fails in your own zone usually concedes one. So the read has to be well over 50/50 — and more than that in your own end.

## How to train it

```facts
Options: Shoulder-check outlet drills, number and colour calls, constrained small-area games — anything that fails if you do not look
Action: Talk in every drill; a call is a scan you did not have to take yourself
Action: Watch your own shifts and genuinely count your head turns, then count those of a teammate whose vision you rate
Action: Pause the video a second before you receive, name your three options, then unpause and see what you did
Action: At a live game, watch one player for a whole shift away from the puck — the broadcast camera crops everyone else out
Action: Off the ice, head-up stickhandling with a visual task, daily, in short blocks
Risk: Reaction and peripheral-vision tools train the eyes, not the hockey
```

**On the ice:**

- **Shoulder-check outlet drills.** You must look over your shoulder before receiving, and the coach changes something behind you, so not looking fails the drill.
- **Number or colour calls.** A partner holds up fingers or a coloured card; you call it out while handling or receiving. The drill is impossible with your head down.
- **Constrained small-area games.** Three-on-three in a zone, two-touch rules, or calling your target's name before you receive. Constraints build scanning without nagging.
- **Talk in every drill.** A call is a scan you did not have to take. See [On-Ice Communication](../foundation/on_ice_communication.md).

**With video:**

- **Watch your own shifts and count your head turns.** Genuinely count them — it turns "I should look more" into a number you can move.
- **Pause one second before you receive and name your three options.** Then unpause and see what you did. The gap is your scanning deficit.
- **Count the head turns of a teammate whose vision you rate.** Comparison motivates more than an abstract target.

**Watching live hockey:**

- **Watch one player for a whole shift, away from the puck.** The best method a spectator has. **Live** is easiest, because the broadcast camera follows the puck and crops everyone else; wide-angle tactical feeds, where a league offers them, are next best. One shift watching a good centre — where they look, when they turn their head, where they go when not involved — teaches more than a season of highlights. See [How to Watch Hockey](../off-the-ice/how_to_watch_hockey.md).
- **Watch the defenceman without the puck.** Most of the interesting anticipation happens where the camera is not pointing.
- **Predict out loud.** Being wrong, and noticing, is the mechanism.

**Off the ice:**

- **Head-up stickhandling with a visual task**, daily, in short blocks.
- **Reaction and peripheral-vision tools** (light trainers, reaction balls) train the eyes, not the hockey. As the Fortin-Guichard study shows, off-ice measures alone do not capture game sense.

## Common Mistakes

- **Watching the pass all the way onto your blade.** Look while it travels, name your play, then bring your eyes back just before it lands. Otherwise you find the forechecker only once the puck has arrived.
- **Puck-watching on the backcheck.** Look over your inside shoulder twice and count opponents, or you arrive in your zone and pick up nobody.
- **Staring at the puck carrier.** Watch the chest with your central vision and snapshot everything else. Staring gets a defender beaten by the pass and sends a teammate to where the puck *was*.
- **Only watching your own assignment.** Every time the puck moves in your end, ask who scores if it goes to the net now. Under a collapse, alternate between the puck and the most dangerous body near you; under man-to-man, keep your check in view but still take that look. Know which your team plays.
- **Not scanning at stoppages.** At every whistle, check who is out, who takes the draw, their defence pair, where your bench is, and the score and the clock.
- **The "I didn't see him" goal.** **Every time the puck goes into a corner in your own end, check the net front.** The back-door tap-in is a scanning failure, not bad luck.
- **Looking, then taking the hit with your back to the checker.** **Never turn your back to the checker, and never duck.** Come to the wall on an angle, feet apart, knees bent, **skates parallel to the wall**, **forearm and hip to the wall** — not the point of your shoulder — head up, chin off your chest. Already facing the wall? Stick and gloves up and spread on the glass, no head contact. Hitting the boards or a post head-on with your chin down is how necks get broken, even at walking speed — USA Hockey's *Heads Up Hockey* guide puts almost all on-ice spinal injuries down to it — so this outranks every tactical point here. See [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md).
- **The chin dip.** Turn your head until your chin passes over your shoulder. Glancing sideways without turning shows you almost nothing.
- **Scanning only to your strong side.** Make one look go to the side you were not planning to use — that is where the surprise is.
- **Looking but not deciding.** Name your first option and your bail-out while the puck travels. A scan that ends in no plan is just head movement.
- **Still looking away when the puck arrives.** Make the last look the one back at the puck.
- **Chasing a scan count.** Clear the bar of one look, then work on the quality of what you see. Hockey has not tested whether more looks beat some.
- **Guessing and calling it anticipation.** If your position is worthless when you are wrong, you guessed — even if it worked. Shade toward the likely pass while staying between the puck and your net.
- **Trying to win a battle you will lose.** Go to where the loose puck will squirt out, or pin the puck — not the player — against the boards with your skate or stick for a moment, for a teammate to dig out. Never with your hands, and never hold it for a whistle: that is a penalty in every book, and USA Hockey calls it after three seconds even under pressure.
- **Watching for the rebound instead of arriving for it.** Arrive at the net moving, just outside the crease and off the goaltender, blade out of the paint.

## Key Takeaways

1. **Take at least one look while every pass is travelling to you, then get your eyes back on the puck.** Looking at all is what hockey has measured; whether more looks help has not been tested, so work on what you see rather than the count.
2. **Decide before the puck arrives.** Name your first option and your bail-out while it travels, so its arrival triggers an action. **Goaltenders reverse this**: once a shot is released, track it all the way in.
3. **Use the free moments** — the puck on the far side, the instant after your own pass, and every stoppage.
4. **Turn your head, check both shoulders and keep skating**, and make your last look the one back at the puck.
5. **Keep the puck as your anchor and spend extra looks on pressure, outlets, danger and space.** In your own end, keep asking who scores if the puck goes to the net now. The anchor evidence comes from football, not hockey.
6. **Read a carrier's chest and hips, not the head, hands or stick**, and if one beats you, re-establish position instead of chasing. The evidence for this is from rugby.
7. **Read a passer's front shoulder and blade, and a shooter's hands and weight.** Pressure a passer who received without looking. Against a shooter, get your feet into the lane, head out of it, rather than lunging.
8. **Read the shape.** Against a 2-1-2 behind your net, reverse to your partner or rim it hard rather than wheeling. Against a defender closing hard, change pace. After your first shift, tell the bench what they are forechecking.
9. **Play where the puck goes next.** Arrive at the net moving, just outside the crease and off the goaltender, blade out of the paint. In a battle you will lose, go for the loose puck instead.
10. **Get your head up off the puck**, and train it with a drill that fails if you look down.
11. **Anticipate; do not guess.** Your position should still be useful if you are wrong. Jump a lane only on a real cue with support behind you — never as the last player back or in your own zone.
12. **On a wall reception, position protects you, not looking.** ⚠️ **Never turn your back to the checker, and never duck** — come in on an angle, knees bent, skates parallel to the wall, forearm and hip to it, never the point of your shoulder, chin off your chest. Already facing the wall? Stick and gloves up and spread on the glass, no head contact. See [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md).

**What is measured here, and what is not.**

- **The hockey scanning figures** come from **Berg et al. (2025)**, a peer-reviewed, open-access study of 2,545 puck receptions by 88 SHL and SDHL professionals: 1.48 scans per five seconds, at least one scan in 61% of receptions, and 78.8% against 70.1% action success on criteria formulated with the Swedish Ice Hockey Association. Three figures are arithmetic on Berg's 1.48 and 61%, not printed in the paper, and are marked where they appear: 0.30 scans a second, one scan per 3.4 seconds, and 39% of receptions with no scan. The football figures are corroboration only and labelled as football throughout.
- **No duration is quoted for a hockey scan.** Berg reports only a 0.2-second minimum for a head turn to count, and the Aksum study's 242 milliseconds is an average *fixation*, a different thing.
- **The "every one to two seconds" coaching benchmark** is contradicted by Berg's measured professional rate — with the caveat that Berg measured only the window before a reception, while the benchmark describes all off-puck play.
- **The claim that elite players anchor on the puck carrier** rests on the Aksum eye-tracking study of **five male football players aged 17 to 23, watching a ball carrier**, and is labelled as football everywhere it appears, including Key Takeaway 5. Hockey's on-ice gaze research (Martell and Vickers, 2004) measures the *timing* of gaze, not where looking time goes, so it does not settle the question.
- **That hockey below the professional level rewards speed of decision over quality** is coaching consensus, not a measured finding.
- **Precise figures for shooter tells** — degrees of shoulder rotation, centimetres of weight shift, milliseconds of goaltender advantage — circulate with no credible source, so the shooter section is qualitative. The rebound-direction rule is likewise coaching craft, not a measured finding.
