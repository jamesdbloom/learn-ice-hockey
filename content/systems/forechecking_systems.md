## Overview

```facts
Key: Forechecking is the pressure your team applies in the opponents' end when they have the puck and you don't — the opposite of backchecking
Key: It is the first thing your team does after losing the puck in the attacking end, and the biggest lever a coach has over how a game feels
Convention: There is no single correct forecheck — there is a family of named systems, each buying pressure at the price of risk
Action: Ask your coach which forecheck your team runs, and which one you switch to with a lead — most teams have at least two
Rule: Find out whether you may body check at all before you learn to arrive on one — USA Hockey 604(a) bars it at 12 & under and below, in all Girls'/Women's and non-check Adult classifications; Hockey Canada 7.3 at U13 and below, throughout female hockey and in any Member-approved division
Never: Finish into the back of a player facing the end boards — the NHL and IIHF write no minor for checking from behind at all (43.2 in both), so the floor is a major with a game misconduct
Risk: Guessing which one you are in is how odd-man rushes happen
```

**Forechecking** is the pressure your team applies at the other team's end of the rink while they have the puck and you do not — the opposite of racing back to defend your own net.

**Pressure and risk are the same dial.** Every player you send deep to hunt the puck is one fewer between the puck and your own goalie. One chaser rarely costs you anything; two win a lot of pucks and sometimes leave you outnumbered coming back; three plus a defenceman win pucks constantly and, when they fail, leave an opponent alone on your goalie. There is no single correct setting, so **ask which forecheck your team runs and which one it switches to with a lead** — most have at least two, and guessing is how you end up outnumbered.

**Angling is the whole skill: skate at the space you want to deny, not at the puck.** Pick a side before you arrive and take it away, so the only route left is the one a teammate is already standing on. Which side that is, is a coaching choice — the common default takes away the middle and forces the play towards the side wall, and some coaches invert it deliberately — so find out which way your team angles before you play, not during the game. Arrive under control as well; the test is whether you could stop and pivot right now. And the aim is to eliminate options, not to land a hit: a hit removes two players from the play, yours as well as theirs, and in most adult recreational hockey body checking is not permitted at all, which makes angling the whole toolkit rather than a beginner's version of hitting.

**When you shoot the puck into their end for a teammate to chase, understand what you have created.** A player facing the end wall with someone closing on their back is the exact situation the checking-from-behind and boarding rules exist for, and the onus is on you as the arriving player. Take the puck, take the ice, or steer them off it — never finish into the back of a player facing the wall.

## A Note on Language

```facts
Position: High means closer to the blue line, low means closer to the goal line — in the offensive zone, high is back towards the line you came in over
Key: Strong side is the side of the ice the puck is on; weak side is the other
Key: A rim sends the puck onward around the boards, a reverse sends it back against the flow — a rim beats a forechecker sealing you in, a reverse beats one who has over-committed
Never: Treat a pinch and stepping up as the same play — a pinch steps down from the offensive blue line, stepping up challenges an attacker in the neutral zone
Technique: Angling is forcing an opponent to go in a desired direction, normally towards the boards or the outside — Hockey Canada's own definition
Action: Steer by shadowing the carrier; seal by arriving second and closing the escape route the first forechecker has forced them towards
```

Terms used throughout, defined here so this document stands alone. See [Rink Map and Glossary](../foundation/rink_map_and_glossary.md) for the full set.

- **High** = closer to the blue line and further from the goal line. **Low** = closer to the goal line, nearer the net. (In the offensive zone, "high" means back towards the line you came in over.)
- **Strong side** = the side of the ice the puck is on. **Weak side** = the other side.
- **The point** = the area just inside the blue line where defencemen stand. **Half-wall** = the boards roughly level with the faceoff dot; **half-boards** is the same place under another name.
- **Breakout** = the opponents' organised attempt to carry or pass the puck out of their own zone. Your forecheck exists to break it. See [Breakouts](breakouts.md).
- **Dump-in** = deliberately putting the puck deep into the opponents' zone without possession, then chasing it.
- **Rim** = a hard puck sent *onward* around the boards. **Reverse** = a puck sent *back* against the flow. These are different plays and both are used to beat forecheckers — a rim beats a forechecker sealing you in, a reverse beats one who has over-committed past you.
- **Pinch** = a defenceman stepping down from the *offensive* blue line to keep a puck in. Not the same as **stepping up**, which is challenging an attacker in the neutral zone.
- **Angling** = Hockey Canada's own definition is "the ability to force an opponent to go in a desired direction," normally towards the boards or the outside. It is the foundation of everything below.
- **Steering** = shadowing the carrier to push them into an area where you can take away their time and space.
- **Seal** = arriving second and closing the escape route the first forechecker has forced them towards.

## What Forechecking Is For

```facts
Key: A forecheck has exactly two possible wins, and everything in every system serves one of them
Goal: Win 1 — take the puck back deep in their zone; a turnover below their hash marks is a scoring chance almost immediately, because their forwards have already released above the puck
Goal: Win 2 — force a bad exit you collect in the neutral zone; you never touch the puck in their end, and it hands you the puck moving forward with speed
Key: Passive systems are not "not forechecking" — they have decided Win 2 is the better bet, and the argument for them is that it is the more forgiving win, not the likelier one
Key: The only public tracking located for this document that measures both wins publishes each rate on a different denominator and states no ratio between them
Key: Hand-tracked by one tracker: a forecheck stops the breakout by either route on 45.7% of attempts, rising to 78.1% when the carrier is pressured at the moment of the exit
Key: That pair is one tracker's hand-tracking of roughly a quarter of the 2018-19 season plus half of each of the two before it
```

A forecheck has exactly two possible wins. Everything in every system serves one of them.

**Win 1 — take the puck back deep in their zone.** This is the jackpot. A turnover below the opposing hash marks is a scoring chance almost immediately, because by the time their defenceman has the puck down there their forwards have already released **above** it — climbing towards their blue line looking for the exit — and they cannot get back below the puck in time. You win it with the net 20 feet away and only one or two defenders between you and it. This is what aggressive forechecks are hunting.

**Win 2 — force a bad exit you collect in the neutral zone.** You never touch the puck in their end. Instead you take away every good option until they chip it out blindly, and one of your players is standing where blind chips go. This is a quieter win, and it hands you the puck moving forward with speed — which is worth more than a turnover in the corner that leaves you standing still.

**Coaches usually call Win 2 the commoner of the two, and the only public tracking located for this document that measures both is more equivocal than that.** Alex Novet's Hockey Graphs analysis of Corey Sznajder's hand-tracking splits a forecheck's success into exactly these two pieces — a *Forecheck Recovery*, forcing *"a turnover or icing in their offensive zone"* that *"prevents a zone exit entirely"*, and a *Zone Entry Prevention*, stopping the play *"in the neutral zone before their opponent can get a zone entry"* — and publishes both by team: Forecheck Recovery runs about **21% to 25.5%** of breakout attempts, Zone Entry Prevention about **40% to 45.5%** of the breakouts that did get out. ⚠️ **Those two denominators are different and the article says so in terms**, so the percentages cannot be read against each other and the post publishes no ratio between them. What the same work does state is what happens **when you actually pressure the carrier at the moment of the exit**, and it is the finding the author singles out as crucial. Pressure takes a forecheck's overall success — stopping the breakout before it reaches your end at all, by either route — from **45.7%** of breakout attempts to **78.1%**. Both pieces improve, and the deep turnover improves more: **Forecheck Recovery goes from 13.5% to 43.9%**, **Zone Entry Prevention from 37.2% to 60.9%**. The mechanism is visible in what kind of exits pressure produces — failed exits rise from **10% of exits to 41%** and carry-outs collapse from **19% to 4%**, while dumped-and-lost exits go only from **26% to 31%**. ⚠️ **A failed exit is not the whole of a Forecheck Recovery, though**: the definition is a turnover *or an icing*, and the icing share moves the other way, from 4% of exits to 3%. (One tracker, hand-tracked, sample roughly a quarter of the 2018-19 season plus half of each of the two before it. The per-team ranges are read off a scatter plot that prints no values; the pressure and exit-mix figures are labels printed on the charts themselves, and the article's own text quotes one of them back — carry-outs at 4% under pressure against 19% without it.) **So the argument for a passive system is that Win 2 is the more forgiving win, not that it is overwhelmingly the likelier one — and those are different arguments.**

Passive systems are not "not forechecking." They are systems that have decided Win 2 is the better bet.

### The fundamental trade-off

```facts
Key: Pressure and risk are the same dial — every player you send below the top of the circles is one fewer between the puck and your goalie
Options: One forechecker is easy to beat but almost never costs you anything
Risk: Two forecheckers win a lot of pucks and occasionally leave you 3-on-2 going the other way
Risk: Three forecheckers plus a pinching defenceman win pucks constantly and, when they fail, produce a breakaway
Action: Say it out loud — "I've got F1" or "I'm high" prevents two players doing the same job while nobody does the other one
```

There are five of you on the ice. Every player you send below the top of the circles to hunt the puck is one fewer player between the puck and your goalie.

- **One forechecker** is easy to beat but almost never costs you anything.
- **Two forecheckers** win a lot of pucks and occasionally leave you 3-on-2 going the other way.
- **Three forecheckers plus a pinching defenceman** win pucks constantly and, when they fail, produce a breakaway.

That is the whole subject in three lines. Pressure and risk are the same dial. A coach picks a forecheck by deciding where on that dial the game currently sits — score, time remaining, how good the other team's defencemen are with the puck, and how much gas your legs have left.

**Say it out loud on the bench and on the ice.** "I've got F1" or "I'm high" takes half a second and prevents the most expensive forechecking error there is — two players doing the same job while nobody does the other one. See [On-Ice Communication](../foundation/on_ice_communication.md).

## F1 / F2 / F3 — Roles, Not People

This is the most misunderstood idea in team hockey, so it gets its own section.

**F1, F2 and F3 are defined by order of arrival at the puck. Nothing else.**

- **F1** is whichever forward gets there first.
- **F2** is whichever forward arrives second.
- **F3** is the one who is left, and who stays high.

They are **not** positions. F1 is not "the centre." F2 is not "the strong-side winger." If your left winger is closest when the puck goes in the corner, your left winger is F1 and your centre is F2 — immediately, without discussion, without waiting for anyone to swap back.

Some coaches do assign them by position as a teaching shortcut for beginners, and the [Center](../positions/center.md) and [Winger](../positions/winger.md) documents describe the common case where the centre is F1. Treat that as the *typical* case, not the rule. At any real speed the puck will not wait for the correct person to arrive.

### The roles rotate mid-shift

```facts
Read: When the opponents move the puck defenceman-to-defenceman, your F1 has just been beaten and is now behind the play
Action: The forward closest to the new puck carrier becomes the new F1 and attacks; everyone else adjusts one slot
Never: Turn around and chase after you have been beaten. Take the vacated support role instead, usually the new F3, high in the zone
Risk: Chasing every puck you touch is doing three players' jobs — you will be dead by the second shift
Key: Accept demotion to F3 when you get beaten and you get a rest built into the system
```

The labels change during a single possession. When the opponents move the puck defenceman-to-defenceman across the ice, your F1 has just been beaten and is now behind the play. What should happen next is:

1. The forward closest to the new puck carrier becomes the new **F1** and attacks.
2. The old F1 does **not** turn around and chase. They take the vacated support role — usually dropping in as the new **F3**, high in the zone.
3. Everyone else adjusts one slot.

This is why forechecking is exhausting when done badly and sustainable when done well. If you chase every puck you touch, you are doing three players' jobs and will be dead by the second shift. If you accept demotion to F3 when you get beaten, you get a rest built into the system.

### The three jobs, in general terms

```facts
Action: F1 — arrive fast, arrive on an angle, take away one side of the ice and make the carrier's decision for them
Convention: Whether F1 also hunts the puck is a coaching choice — steering in the 1-2-2, 1-3-1 and 1-4, hunting in a 2-1-2 or press; ask which your team wants
Key: F1's first job is to make the next play predictable — nothing works if the carrier still has three good options when you arrive
Action: F2 — skate to where the puck is about to be forced, not to the puck, and stay far enough from F1 that one deke cannot remove both of you
Position: F3 — stay high, take away the middle of the ice, and be the first man back if the puck escapes
```

The *shape* of these three jobs holds across most systems; what changes is *where* F2 and F3 stand, and — importantly — **how hard F1 is told to go**.

- **F1 — take away options.** Arrive fast, arrive on an angle, take away one side of the ice, and make the carrier's decision for them.

  **Whether F1 is also trying to win the puck is a coaching choice, and it is the single biggest difference between the systems below.** In the **containment** systems (1-2-2, 1-3-1, 1-4) F1's job is explicitly *not* to win the puck — F1 steers, and somebody else collects. In an **aggressive 2-1-2 or full-ice press**, F1 goes hard at the puck carrier with the intent of taking it, and the [Center](../positions/center.md) document describes exactly that, because the house default throughout is a 2-1-2. Both are correct within their own system, and getting it backwards is expensive: F2 reads off F1, so a steering F1 with a sealing F2 wins pucks, and a hunting F1 with a sealing F2 leaves the middle wide open. **Ask which one your team wants.**

  What *is* common to both: F1's first job is to make the next play predictable. Winning the puck outright is a bonus in one system and the plan in the other, but neither works if the carrier still has three good options when you arrive.
- **F2 — read F1 and seal.** Do not skate to the puck. Skate to where the puck is about to be forced. Keep enough distance from F1 that you can still react if F1 is beaten — arriving in the same square metre as F1 means one deke removes both of you.
- **F3 — be patient and be the insurance.** Stay high, take away the middle of the ice, and be the first man back if the puck escapes. F3 is the most disciplined job on the forecheck and the least rewarding. It is also the one that decides whether a failed forecheck costs you nothing or costs you a goal.

## Angling: The First Forechecker's Job

If you only improve one thing about your forechecking, improve this. Hockey Canada places **positioning and angling as Step 1 of its four-step checking progression** — before stick checks, before body contact, before body checking — precisely because it is the skill that makes all the others unnecessary most of the time.

**The goal of angling is to eliminate options, not to make a hit.** A hit that separates the carrier from the puck is a bonus. A hit that you miss, or that you get by flying past the carrier, has removed you from the play and turned a 5-on-5 into a 4-on-5.

### Approach angle

```facts
Action: Skate at the space you want to deny, not at the puck
Convention: The common default is taking away the middle and forcing them to the boards — a coaching choice, not a law; some coaches invert it
Action: Find out which way your team angles before your first shift, not during it — F2 seals the route F1 leaves open
Technique: Curve your approach so your body is between them and the centre of the ice and your momentum runs along the wall, not across it
Never: Approach in a straight line — it commits you to a single point in space and any change of direction beats you
Never: Finish into the back of a carrier who has turned to the end boards — take the puck, take the ice, or angle them off it; the onus is on you as the arriving player
```

Do not skate at the puck. Skate at the **space you want to deny**.

Pick a side before you get there and take it away, so the carrier's only remaining route is the one your teammates are covering. The common default is **taking away the middle and forcing them towards the boards**, because the boards are a wall that helps you and the middle of the ice is where they can go anywhere.

**That default is a coaching choice, not a law.** Some coaches invert it and have F1 take away the boards to force the play into the middle, where three of their players are waiting. Either can be right; what cannot be right is F1 and F2 disagreeing about which one it is, because F2 seals the route F1 leaves open. **Find out which way your team angles before your first shift, not during it.**

Concretely: if the carrier is behind their net and about to come up the left side, you approach on a curve that puts your body between them and the centre of the ice, arriving so that your momentum carries you *along* the wall in the same direction they are travelling — not across it.

The reason this works is that a curved approach keeps you in a position to react. A straight-line approach commits you to a single point in space, and any change of direction by the carrier beats you.

⚠️ **The one thing that curve must never finish in is contact with a back.** The carrier behind their own net is about to turn away from you, and Hockey Canada's checking-from-behind rule names your exact arrival among the situations it tells referees to enforce strictly: *“[w]here a player along the boards, with their head down looking for the puck or ‘digging the puck out,’ is hit from behind and driven head-first into the boards”* (Rule 7.5). Take the puck, take the ice, or steer them off it. What that hit costs is set out across all four books under **Off a dump-in** below — in short, neither the NHL nor the IIHF writes a minor for it at all (43.2 in both), and the other two books eject on theirs.

### Stick position

```facts
Position: Stick out front, blade on the ice, in the passing lane you are taking away — not tucked beside your skates, not raised
Technique: Aim the blade at the puck as you close
Goal: A stick in the lane back to the far defenceman removes that pass without costing you any body position
Key: Even if you never make contact, the carrier has to protect the puck and has to look at you — so they are not looking for their outlet
```

**Stick out front, blade on the ice, in the passing lane you are taking away.** Not tucked beside your skates, not raised.

The stick is the cheap part of angling — it takes away a pass without costing you any body position. If your stick is in the lane back to the far defenceman, that pass simply does not get made, and you have removed an option without doing anything athletic.

Aim the blade at the puck as you close. A USA Hockey ADM drill shared by **Roger Grillo**, an ADM Regional Manager, at the 2021 USA Hockey Level 5 Coaches Symposium puts it as **"good angling and stick on puck to force the defending player to the boards"**, with the F1 key point given as *"timing, angling and body/stick positioning"*. (The write-up is a single *Drill of the Week* post from **Minnesota Hockey and CoachThem**, 4 January 2022, not a USA Hockey publication — the drill is ADM, the sheet is not.) The fuller coaching phrase you will hear on benches, **"stick on puck, stick on stick, body on body"**, comes from team playbooks rather than from USA Hockey's own material. Two things then happen even if you never make contact: the carrier has to protect the puck, which slows them down, and they have to look at you, which means they are not looking for their outlet.

### Speed control — the part everybody gets wrong

```facts
Action: Arrive under control, not at maximum speed
Technique: Get to top speed on the way in, then get your feet under you in the last two or three strides so you can change direction
Key: The test — could you stop and pivot right now? If not, you are going too fast
Risk: A forechecker who arrives flat out has one option, a straight line; one turn and you are 40 feet behind the play
Rule: Building speed into the contact is charging, and the books do not count it alike — Hockey Canada 7.4 says two or more strides, USA Hockey 607 more than two, IIHF 42.1 "multiple"; the NHL sets no count and judges distance travelled
Rule: Charging is not two minutes everywhere either — USA Hockey 607(a) writes no bare minor at all, only "a minor plus a misconduct or a major plus a game misconduct", and 607(e) adds a match penalty for reckless endangerment
```

**Arrive under control, not at maximum speed.**

Get to top speed on the way in — you want to arrive early, because early pressure is what forces the rushed decision. But in the last two or three strides, get your feet under you so you can change direction. A forechecker who arrives flat out has exactly one option: continue in a straight line. Every experienced defenceman in the world beats that with a single turn, and you are now 40 feet behind the play.

The test: **could you stop and pivot right now?** If not, you are going too fast.

⚠️ **That test is a rule as well as a tactic, because building up speed immediately before contact is how three of the four books define charging — and they do not count it alike.** **Hockey Canada Rule 7.4** lists among the acts that are charging *“[b]uilds up speed by taking two or more strides immediately prior to making contact.”* **USA Hockey Rule 607**'s Note reaches a player who *“takes more than two strides or travels an excessive distance to accelerate through a body check for the purpose of punishing the opponent.”* **IIHF Rule 42.1 — the British reader's — writes the same act without a number**: *“builds up speed by taking multiple strides immediately prior to making contact.”* ⚠️ **So the two strides that are your ceiling under USA Hockey are already the offence under Hockey Canada.** The **NHL** sets no count at all, defining a charge as the actions of a player who *“as a result of distance traveled, shall violently check an opponent in any manner”* (42.1) — and all four books open the offence with a player who *skates or jumps into* an opponent, so leaving your feet is part of it rather than a separate question. ⚠️ **Nor is charging two minutes in every book:** USA Hockey **607(a)** writes no bare minor at all — *“[a] minor plus a misconduct or a major plus a game misconduct penalty”* — with **607(e)** adding *“[a] match penalty for reckless endangerment.”* [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md) holds the four-book comparison in full.

### Why "eliminate options" beats "make a hit"

```facts
Goal: Angle a player into a spot where they have one bad pass available — your F2 is already standing on it
Risk: A hit takes two players out of the play, you and them; containment takes out only them
Key: At rec and beer-league level body checking is usually not permitted at all, so angling is the whole of your forechecking toolkit
Never: Treat angling as the beginner's version of hitting — it is the actual skill
Rule: Both North American books name angling legal in non-check play and stop it at contact — USA Hockey's 604(c) Note allows closing an established angle, then penalises "deliberate physical contact with an opponent, with no effort to legally play the puck"
Rule: Hockey Canada draws the same line by body part — 7.3 counts extending an arm, shoulder or hip after angling as body-checking, while its Interpretation 1 permits steering a player to the boards "without actually touching" them
```

- A hit you land does not reliably win you the puck — it just as often ends with the puck loose and both of you out of the play. Angling a player into a spot where they have one bad pass available wins the puck far more often, because your F2 is already standing on that pass. *(Neither claim is a measured figure; there is no public tracking of hits-versus-angling outcomes. Treat it as coaching consensus, not statistics.)*
- A hit takes two players out of the play — you and them. Containment takes out only them.
- At rec and beer-league level, body checking is usually not permitted at all, and angling is the *whole* of your forechecking toolkit there. Do not treat it as the beginner's version of hitting; it is the actual skill.

  **Both North American books say so in writing — and both stop it at the same place.** USA Hockey's **Rule 604(c) Note** is the one that bars the check in Competitive Contact play, and it is also the one that permits the angle: *“Competitive contact also occurs when a player has established an angle (Angling – see Glossary) on the opponent and closes the gap to create an opening that is too small for the puck carrier to advance,”* and *“[l]egal competitive contact shall not be penalized under this rule.”* ⚠️ **The very next sentence is the boundary:** *“However, deliberate physical contact with an opponent, with no effort to legally play the puck, shall be penalized.”* Its Glossary defines angling itself with no contact in it at all — *“[a] legal defensive skill used to direct/control the puck carrier to an area that closes the gap and/or creates an opening that is too small for the puck carrier to advance.”* **Hockey Canada draws the identical line by naming the body part.** Its **Interpretation 1 to Rule 7.3(a)** gives the permission: *“A player may steer or direct an opposing player into the boards, without actually touching or body-checking them. This is acceptable,”* and *“[t]his is legal, as long as either player does not use their body to intentionally body-check, bump, push, or shove an opponent.”* And Rule **7.3**'s own preamble counts *“when a player extends an arm, shoulder, or hip after angling an opponent”* inside its definition of body-checking — so in a division where 7.3 applies, that extension is the 7.3(a) minor, with **7.3(b)** a major plus a game misconduct *“based on the degree of violence of the impact”* and mandatory where it injures, and **7.3(c)** a match penalty where a player *“deliberately attempts to or deliberately injures an opponent by body-checking.”* **So: angle with your feet and your stick, and let the boards do the rest.**

See [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md) for the mechanics of angling, pinning and stick lifts, and [Time and Space](../hockey-iq/time_and_space.md) for the general principle of taking space away.

## The Named Systems

Forechecks are named by counting players from the goal line outwards: a **2-1-2** has two deep, one in the middle, two high. The numbers always add to five, and most of them name three groups — but not all of them do: the **1-4** and the **2-3** below each name two.

**Where the naming convention breaks.** That count only works while the shape actually sits inside the opponents' end. Three of the systems below — the passive 1-3-1, the 1-4, and the retreated version of the 1-2-2 — are usually played as **neutral-zone** structures, with the back layers at or near *your own* blue line. For those, the same digits are counted from the opponents' end outwards across the whole sheet, not from their goal line, and a "1-4" describes four skaters standing about 114 feet from the net they are supposedly forechecking. They appear here because coaches call them forechecks and because a team's offensive-zone forecheck collapses into them, but the shape they describe lives in the middle third. **[Neutral Zone Systems](neutral_zone_systems.md) is the authority on all of them.**

> **The same names live in two places, so check which one you want.** Here they mean the **offensive-zone version** of each system — the shape while the opponents still have the puck in their own end, and the jobs of the forwards hunting it. In **[Neutral Zone Systems](neutral_zone_systems.md)** the same names mean the **neutral-zone version**: the wall your five players hold across the middle third, plus gap control, regroups and how to break a trap. Where a system exists in both places, each half is written up where it belongs.

**The house default throughout is a 2-1-2 forecheck** (paired with a low zone collapse in the defensive zone). It is the default here because it is the easiest to teach and because the position documents here are written around it.

> **On "the most common forecheck".** You will see that phrase attached to both the 2-1-2 and the 1-2-2, including in this document, and there is **no published count of what teams actually run**. The public tracking that exists measures forechecking by *pressure* and by *outcome* — how often a forecheck stops a zone exit, how often it prevents the entry that follows — not by which formation was on the ice; an analytics column on the Seattle Kraken's own NHL.com site put the reason plainly in 2021, that forechecking *"isn't officially tracked by the league"*. The honest version, and the one used from here on: the **2-1-2** is the one most commonly *taught first* in North American youth and rec hockey, which is why it is the default here; the **1-2-2** is the one most commonly described in coaching material as a team's base structure, largely because the same shape covers both the offensive zone and the neutral zone. Both statements are coaching-material consensus, **not verified data**, and they are not in conflict — they are about different things.

**A system is not only a shape — it is a set of answers.** Each system below describes where five players stand. What decides whether it works is what each of them does once the breakout actually starts, and that breakout will be one of the named options [Breakouts](breakouts.md) sets out — **up, wheel, reverse, D-to-D, rim, over, quick-up, the stretch pass and off the glass**, which is the full list [Breakouts](breakouts.md) sets out, not a shorter one. A well-drilled forecheck has a worked-out response to each of them — who takes the puck, who jumps the outlet, who covers the far wall, who holds — and a badly drilled one produces five players improvising five different answers to the same play. [Breakouts](breakouts.md) sets those options out from the other side of the same moment, including how each is chosen to beat the forecheck you are running here; read the two together and you have both halves of it. **If you learn only the shape, you will be in position and still lose the puck.** The question worth asking your coach is not "what forecheck do we play?" but "what do we do when they wheel it?"

### 2-1-2 — the aggressive standard

```facts
Position: Two forwards deep, one forward high at roughly the top of the circles, both defencemen at the offensive blue line
Convention: F1 goes hard at the puck carrier, angling them to one side — usually towards the boards, but ask, because F2 reads off it
Rule: Never finish into the back of a carrier who has turned to the end boards — no minor exists for checking from behind under the NHL or IIHF (43.2 in both), Hockey Canada 7.5(e) ejects on its own minor, and USA Hockey's Casebook puts it in the major or match tier (608, Situation 1)
Rule: Find out whether you may body check at all — USA Hockey 604(a) bars it at 12 & under and below, in all Girls'/Women's and non-check Adult classifications, and Hockey Canada 7.3 at U13 and below, in female hockey and any Member-approved division. Where it is barred, F1's pressure is feet and stick
Convention: F2 attacks the first passing option, not the puck
Convention: F3 stays high in the middle — weak-side by default here, strong-side in the stacked variant, so find out which your team runs
Rule: After their own icing the offending team may not change lines or take a time-out under NHL and IIHF rules (81.4, 87.1) — under USA Hockey they may do both
Read: Pick it when trailing, off an offensive-zone faceoff, against a defence pair that is slow or panics, and any time they have just iced the puck
Risk: A successful breakout against it is frequently a 3-on-2 or worse, and it is the most physically expensive system to run
Countered by: A quick first pass before F2 arrives, a reverse into the space F1 has vacated, and above all a rim past the pinching defenceman
```

**Shape:** Two forwards deep in the zone, one forward high (roughly the top of the circles), two defencemen at or just inside the **offensive** blue line — the opponents' line, the one you are pinching down from.

![](diagram:forecheck-212)

> **Two positions, two phases — both are correct.** The blue line the defencemen stand on changes as the sequence does, and this is the commonest source of confusion between this document and [Neutral Zone Systems](neutral_zone_systems.md). **While the forecheck is live and the puck is still in their end**, your defencemen are at the *offensive* blue line, holding it and pinching selectively — that is the shape described here. **Once the opponents get the puck out and the play turns into a neutral-zone sequence**, those same two defencemen have retreated and are holding a deliberately tight gap in the middle third, back toward *your own* blue line, with the strong-side D ready to step up. That second phase is the subject of [Neutral Zone Systems](neutral_zone_systems.md)' "Pressure" section, which sets it out in full. Same two players, same system, the length of the neutral zone apart, two different moments.

**The jobs:**
- **F1** goes hard at the puck carrier, angling them to one side. Usually towards the boards, though some coaches teach F1 to take away the boards and force the play into the middle where three of your players are waiting — ask which your team does, because F2 reads off it and getting it backwards means F2 seals an empty lane.

  ⚠️ **"Hard" describes the speed of your arrival, not what you do when you get there.** The carrier is behind their own net and the next thing they do is turn up the wall with their back to you, so the onus is on you as the arriving player: take the puck, take the ice, or angle them off it, and never finish into their back. Neither the NHL nor the IIHF writes a minor for checking from behind at all (43.2 in both), and the other two books eject on theirs — Hockey Canada 7.5(e), and USA Hockey's Casebook at Rule 608, Situation 1, which puts a forceful check on an opponent *"standing along the boards (back toward the middle of the ice)"* in the major or match tier. **And find out first whether you may body check at all**: USA Hockey 604(a) bars it *"in the 12 & under youth age classifications and below, all Girls'/Women's age classifications and all non-check Adult classifications"*, and Hockey Canada 7.3 *"applies only in divisions of U13 and below, in female hockey, and any other divisions approved by a Member of Hockey Canada"*. Where it is barred, F1 still goes — with feet and stick. The checker's obligations are in [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md), §5, and all four books are set out in full under **Off a dump-in** below.
- **F2** attacks the first passing option. Not the puck — the *option*. If F1 has forced the carrier towards the strong-side wall, F2 takes the strong-side winger or the wall itself. The intent is that the puck is under pressure from two players until you get it back.
- **F3** stays high, covering the middle of the ice and ready to be the first man back. **Which side F3 sits on is a genuine coaching split, so find out which your team runs:** the common default — and the one the position documents here assume — is that F3 is the **weak-side** forward, high and away from the puck, covering the cross-ice breakout option and the middle lane. In the **strong-side (stacked) variant** below, F3 shades to the puck side instead, so that all three forwards overload one corner and the far-side breakout is conceded deliberately. Weak-side F3 protects against the cross-ice escape; strong-side F3 maximises pressure on one wall. See [Center](../positions/center.md) and [Winger](../positions/winger.md), which describe the weak-side default.
- **Defencemen** hold the **offensive** blue line and pinch selectively on rims and loose pucks. With two forwards already deep, their pinches must be *right*, not brave. Once the puck is out, they become the tight-gap pair described in [Neutral Zone Systems](neutral_zone_systems.md) — see the phase note above.

**What it's good at:** Sustained offensive-zone time, forcing turnovers close to the net, physically wearing down a defence pair over three periods, and pinning a tired line in their own end so they cannot change. This is why teams forecheck aggressively immediately after the opposition ices the puck — under NHL and IIHF rules the offending team **cannot change lines and cannot use a timeout** after their own icing, so you have a guaranteed mismatch to attack. (Under USA Hockey they can do both — see the rule-set note further down.)

**What it concedes:** Everything behind it. Two forwards deep plus a pinch means a successful breakout is frequently a 3-on-2 or worse. It is also the most physically expensive system to run.

**When a coach picks it:** When trailing; on the first shift after an offensive-zone faceoff; against a defence pair that is slow or panics under pressure; after a dump-in when your forwards have a head start on the retrieval; and any time the opposition has just iced the puck.

**How it is beaten:** A quick first pass before F2 arrives; a **reverse** behind the net into the space F1 has just vacated; and above all a **rim** past the pinching defenceman — the counter that hurts a 2-1-2 most, because a pinch that misses turns two deep forecheckers into two players who are irrelevant. That ranking is coaching emphasis, not a counted one. A defence pair that can make one clean pass under pressure destroys a 2-1-2.

**Variants worth knowing:** the **strong-side (stacked) 2-1-2**, where F1 and F2 both come from the same side to overload one corner, and the **spread 2-1-2**, where they come from opposite sides to cover both. Edmonton's 1980s dynasty is the classic historical example of the aggressive 2-1-2 generally. *(The spread version is attributed to John Tortorella's New York Rangers teams, and that attribution does hold up in contemporaneous writing: Blue Seat Blogs' systems breakdown, published while he was coaching them, says the Rangers "generate most of their 5-on-5 offense from an aggressive 2-1-2 spread forecheck **and transitional offense, meaning offense generated from the rush**" — the second half matters, because it means the forecheck was never being claimed as the sole source of that offence — and explicitly contrasts it with the variant "where F1 and F2 approach from the same lane, rather than spread out" ([The Suit, 21 February 2012](https://blueseatblogs.com/2012/02/21/overview-of-john-tortorellas-system/)). **That is a fan-analytics site, not a coaching manual or a team source** — well attested, not official.)*

![](diagram:forecheck-212-stacked)

### 1-2-2 — the containment default

```facts
Position: One forward deep, two forwards across the middle of the zone, two defencemen high
Convention: F1 (the "dog") pressures but does not commit to winning the puck. F1's job is to cut the ice in half, then stay inside so it cannot come back across
Convention: F2 (the "fox") sits on the strong-side wall and creates the turnover by jumping the outlet F1 has made predictable
Convention: F3 (the "hawk") roams the high middle, committed to neither side; on a puck across, F3 becomes the new fox and everyone shifts one role
Goal: Never being out of position — the 1-2-2 concedes the retrieval and builds a wall they have to pass through
Read: Pick it protecting a lead, against a fast transition team, when your forwards are tired, or as the forgiving rec-league default
Countered by: D-to-D to shift the wall then a quick-up to a winger coming underneath the fox, or a stretch pass past the defencemen — they are the deepest layer it has
```

**Shape:** One forward deep, two forwards across the middle of the zone, two defencemen high.

![](diagram:forecheck-122)

Widely described in coaching material as the most common *base* structure in hockey — see the caveat on "most common" above — and the natural choice when you want to be organised rather than chaotic.

**The jobs:** Some coaches teach these with animal names — F1 the "dog," F2 the "fox," F3 the "hawk" — which is a useful memory hook.
- **F1 (dog)** pressures the carrier but **does not commit to winning the puck**. F1's whole purpose is to cut the ice in half: force the play to one side, then stay on the inside so it cannot come back across.
- **F2 (fox)** sits on the strong-side wall, taking away the pass to their winger. F2 is the one who actually creates the turnover in this system, by jumping an outlet pass that F1 has made predictable.
- **F3 (hawk)** roams the high middle, protecting the centre lane. Not committed to either side.
- **Defencemen** hold the line; the strong-side D pinches on rims, the weak-side D stays home in the middle as the safety.

**The rotation is the clever part.** When the puck goes back across to the far side, F3 slides over and becomes the new fox on that wall, F2 becomes the hawk, and F1 covers the middle. The shape survives; the people inside it change. This is the clearest illustration of why F1/F2/F3 are roles and not people.

**What it's good at:** Never being out of position. The 1-2-2 concedes the initial retrieval and instead builds a wall the opponent has to pass through. It is excellent against teams that want to carry the puck out with speed, because there is no space to skate into.

**What it concedes:** Time. You are letting their defencemen pick the puck up unmolested and choose a play. Against a defence pair that moves the puck well, you will spend a lot of the night defending clean zone entries.

**When a coach picks it:** Protecting a lead; against a fast transition team; when your forwards are tired; when your team is undisciplined and keeps getting caught deep; as the standard rec-league answer, because it is forgiving.

**How it is beaten:** Patience and puck movement. D-to-D passes to shift the wall, then a quick-up to a winger who has come *underneath* the fox rather than standing on the wall in front of them. Also the simple stretch pass or chip past all four players above F1, because **the two defencemen are the deepest layer the 1-2-2 has** — the weak-side D is the safety *inside* the shape, covering the middle behind the forwards, not a fifth man stationed behind the pair. Get a puck past the D line and there is nobody at all in behind it.

#### The 1-2-2 as a neutral-zone structure

```facts
Key: The same three numbers describe two things — all three layers inside their end, or the same layers held back across the middle third
Never: Assume a neutral-zone 1-2-2 is "the trap" — the trap is one particular setting of it, funnelling the carrier into a kill zone at the red line
Key: The neutral-zone shape is what the forecheck collapses into, so nobody has to sprint anywhere — the main practical argument for it as a base system
Options: The 1-1-3 is the conservative cousin — the third forward drops in line with the defencemen to make a three-man wall
```

The same three numbers describe two different things, which confuses almost everyone the first time.

- As an **offensive-zone forecheck** — the version set out here — the numbers count outward from the opponents' goal line, and all three layers sit inside their end.
- As a **neutral-zone forecheck**, the same layers are held further back: one forward pressuring near their blue line, two forwards across the middle third, two defencemen at your own blue line.

**A neutral-zone 1-2-2 is not automatically "the trap."** The trap is one particular setting of it — the layers dropped back further still and, more importantly, the *intent* changed from contesting the exit to funnelling the carrier into a kill zone at the red line. A team can play a neutral-zone 1-2-2 that genuinely contests the puck and is not trapping at all. [Neutral Zone Systems](neutral_zone_systems.md) draws that distinction properly and is the authority on both.

The two versions connect because **the neutral-zone shape is what the forecheck collapses into**. When your 1-2-2 forecheck fails and the opposition exits their zone, your five players are already spaced correctly — F1 retreats to become the lone pressure, F2 and F3 form the middle band, the defencemen back off. Nobody has to sprint anywhere. That seamlessness is the main practical argument for the 1-2-2 as a team's base system.

The New Jersey Devils' 1990s and 2000s teams are the standard reference for building a whole identity around this. See [Neutral Zone Systems](neutral_zone_systems.md) for the trap proper and how to break one.

**A closely related variant is the 1-1-3**, where the third forward drops all the way in line with the defencemen to make a three-man wall. Treat it as the 1-2-2's more conservative cousin.

### 1-3-1 — the modern trap-adjacent forecheck

```facts
Position: One forward pressuring, three players spread in a line across the width of the ice, one player behind them as the last man
Convention: The single forechecker does not chase. They steer, take away the middle and shepherd the breakout to one side
Convention: The line of three is F2, F3 and one defenceman, the defenceman in the middle as the read-maker; it shifts across as a unit with the puck
Convention: The single high player is the safety, responsible for anything that gets behind the line of three
Goal: Take away lanes rather than players — against a team exiting on three short passes there is nowhere to put the puck
Read: Pick it against short controlled breakouts, when you cannot get pressure on in time such as against a line change, and to protect a lead
Risk: You concede the initial puck entirely, and a great deal of ice if the line of three is beaten in one pass — this is not a beginner system
Countered by: Chipping past the flat line and beating it with speed, banking a pass off the boards, and moving the puck side to side before committing
```

**Shape:** One forward pressuring, three players spread across the width of the ice in a line, one player behind them as the last man.

![](diagram:forecheck-131)

Popularised in the NHL by Guy Boucher's Tampa Bay teams around 2010–11 — famously so, because it produced games where the opposing team simply stood still with the puck rather than skate into it.

**The jobs:**
- **The single forechecker** (usually the centre, but again — whoever gets there) does not chase. They **steer**. They take away the middle and shepherd the breakout to one side, then keep angling so the puck cannot come back to the defence partner or through the middle.
- **The line of three** — conventionally **F2, F3 and one defenceman**, with the defenceman in the middle as the read-maker and the two forwards on the walls — spreads across the ice and erases the passing lanes. (It cannot be three forwards: F1 is already a forward, and there are only three on the ice.) As the puck commits to one side, the whole line shifts with it, so the puck-side player becomes an immediate second attacker while the others cover the retreat.
- **The single high player** is the safety, responsible for anything that gets behind the line of three.

**What it's good at:** Taking away *lanes* rather than *players*. Against a team that wants to make three short passes to exit, the 1-3-1 is miserable to play against because there is nowhere to put the puck. It is also flexible: it can be pushed up aggressively into the offensive zone — the version described here — or dropped back and run passively as a neutral-zone shape, which is how coaching material usually describes it — consensus rather than a counted usage, on the same footing as the note on "the most common forecheck" above — and is covered in [Neutral Zone Systems](neutral_zone_systems.md). Note that dropped back it is no longer a forecheck in any literal sense; only the name survives.

**What it concedes:** The initial puck, entirely, and a great deal of ice in behind if the line of three is beaten in one pass. It also demands very good lateral skating and reading from three players simultaneously — this is not a beginner system.

**When a coach picks it:** Against a team that breaks out with short controlled passes; when you cannot get pressure on the puck in time — for example against a line change, where the 1-3-1 lets you set up rather than chase; and as a lead-protecting shape.

**How it is beaten:** Three well-documented counters. **Chip it past the line of three and beat it with speed** — the line is flat, so anything behind it is behind all three at once. **Bank a pass off the boards** to a teammate who has already got behind the line. And **move the puck side to side** before committing, because the line has to shift as a unit and the shift is where the seams open.

### 1-4 — the full retreat

```facts
Position: One forward applying token pressure, the other four flat across the ice at or near their own blue line
Key: This is a neutral-zone structure, not an offensive-zone forecheck — Neutral Zone Systems is the authority on it
Convention: The lone forechecker steers and slows the exit rather than pressuring; nobody pinches and nobody gambles
Goal: Preventing odd-man rushes and high-danger chances, full stop
Risk: It concedes any realistic chance of getting the puck back in their end, and therefore any offence
Read: A situational tool only — a one-goal lead in the last two minutes, the end of a period, no legs left, or a far better opponent
Countered by: A hard chip past the flat line and a race for it — not a stretch pass, which is offside against a wall sitting on its own blue line
```

**Shape:** One forward applying token pressure; the other four in a flat line across the ice at or near **their own** blue line.

**This is a neutral-zone structure, not an offensive-zone forecheck**, and [Neutral Zone Systems](neutral_zone_systems.md) is the authority on it. It is listed here only because coaches call it a forecheck and because it is the shape a failed forecheck retreats into. The short version:

- **The jobs:** the lone forechecker steers and slows the exit rather than pressuring. The four behind form a wall whose only purpose is that nothing gets through it with speed. Nobody pinches. Nobody gambles.
- **What it's good at:** preventing odd-man rushes and high-danger chances, full stop.
- **What it concedes:** any realistic chance of getting the puck back in their end, and therefore any offence.
- **When it's correct:** as a *situational* tool only — protecting a one-goal lead in the last two minutes, killing the final seconds of a period, surviving the tail end of a long shift with no legs, or against a team so much better than you that opening the game up loses 6–1. No serious team runs it for 60 minutes.

**How it is beaten:** patience and a willingness to dump the puck in and forecheck it. A 1-4 dares you to enter the zone with control; the counter is to stop trying, put the puck behind them, and turn the game into a series of retrievals. Concretely, **a hard chip past the flat line and a race for it** — because the line is flat, nobody is deeper than anybody else, so anything put behind them is a genuine footrace with only the goalie beyond. Note that a *stretch pass* is the wrong tool here: a 1-4 sits on its own blue line, so a receiver already behind the wall would be offside. Chip it past and skate onto it. See [Zone Entries](zone_entries.md).

### 2-3 and the left-wing lock

```facts
Position: Two forwards forechecking aggressively, three players across the ice behind them
Convention: In the left-wing lock the centre and right winger forecheck as F1 and F2, and the left winger drops back to the half-boards
Convention: If the puck is not won, the left winger slides into line with the two defencemen — effectively a third defenceman going the other way
Goal: Two-man pressure, nearly as good as a 2-1-2, with three players always between the puck and your net
Read: Pick it when you want pressure but cannot afford to bleed rush chances, or to nurse a lead without going fully passive
Risk: It demands a left winger with total positional discipline and the skating to back-check, and gives up the fifth attacker so you cycle less
Never: Read "forecheck hard" as a licence to finish into the back of a carrier who has turned to the boards — same onus as the 2-1-2, and the same prior question of whether you may body check at all
```

**Shape:** Two forwards forechecking aggressively, three players across the ice behind them.

The **left-wing lock** is the famous version. The centre and right winger forecheck hard as F1 and F2. ⚠️ **"Hard" means the same thing here as it does in the 2-1-2, and carries the same two conditions**: their defenceman is retrieving with their back to you, so take the puck, take the ice, or angle them off it and never finish into their back — and find out first whether you may body check at all, which USA Hockey 604(a) and Hockey Canada 7.3 bar outright across a long list of classifications. Both are set out in full under **2-1-2** above and **Off a dump-in** below. The **left winger "locks"** — instead of joining the attack, they drop back to the half-boards and, if the puck is not won, slide into line with the two defencemen to form a three-man wall across the ice. The left winger is effectively a third defenceman whenever the puck is going the other way.

**What it's good at:** Genuinely aggressive forechecking with a genuinely solid back end. You get two-man pressure — nearly as good as a 2-1-2 — while never being outnumbered on the counter-attack, because there are always three players between the puck and your net.

**What it concedes:** Less than most systems, which is why it was so successful. The real costs are structural rather than tactical: it demands a left winger with total positional discipline and the skating to back-check, and it puts less offensive weight on that player. It also gives up the fifth attacker in the offensive zone, so you cycle less.

**When a coach picks it:** When you want pressure but cannot afford to bleed rush chances — a strong two-way team's default, or a team trying to nurse a lead without going fully passive.

**Where the left-wing lock actually came from.** The two origin stories usually told about the lock are **complementary, not competing**. It was developed in **Czechoslovak hockey in the 1970s** as a way to cope with the dominant Soviet teams, and was used in various forms across Swedish and Finnish hockey before it reached North America. It was then **adapted for the narrower North American rink by Barry Smith**, an assistant on the Detroit Red Wings' staff, where Scotty Bowman adopted it; Detroit's teams from the mid-1990s onward made it famous. Invention in Europe and adaptation in Detroit are separate events roughly twenty years apart, and there is no conflict between them — Detroit did not invent it, and Barry Smith never claimed to. The lock is much rarer in the modern NHL than it was in 1997, largely because contemporary systems ask wingers to be more involved offensively — but it remains a common structure at junior, college and European club level.

**Where the lock lives once possession changes** — the three-man wall across the neutral zone, and the modern "lock whichever winger is highest" variant — is covered in [Neutral Zone Systems](neutral_zone_systems.md).

### The full-ice press — maximum pressure

```facts
Position: Three forwards deep or nearly deep, both defencemen pinching at the offensive blue line, effectively no safety at all
Convention: All three forwards hunt the puck and both defencemen pinch on anything, on the understanding that a puck past them is a breakaway
Action: Call it a full-ice press or an all-out forecheck — "2-2-1" is standard basketball terminology and is not what coaching material standardly calls this in hockey
Read: Almost exclusively late and trailing — down a goal in the last two or three minutes, or with the goaltender pulled for the extra attacker
Rule: On a delayed penalty play stops only when the offending team gains control, not when it merely touches the puck (NHL Rule 15.1). Play until the whistle
Risk: A single clean exit is a breakaway or an empty-net goal against
Never: Take "pinch on anything" as a licence to seal a winger against the wall — whether you may seal at all depends on your book, and When to pinch below sets out all four
Countered by: One pass — you are betting they cannot make it in the time you allow them
```

**Shape:** Three forwards deep or nearly deep, both defencemen at the offensive blue line and pinching aggressively, effectively no safety at all.

> **A note on the name.** The label "2-2-1" is sometimes used for this in casual discussion, and it is standard terminology in basketball — but it is not what coaching material standardly calls this in hockey. Coaching sources describe it as a **full-ice press**, an **all-out forecheck**, or simply "an aggressive 2-1-2 with both D pinching." Use one of those names with your team so everyone means the same thing.

**The jobs:** All three forwards hunt the puck. Both defencemen hold the line and pinch on anything, on the understanding that a puck getting past them is a breakaway. ⚠️ **"On anything" is about the puck, not about the man.** Whether a pinch may end in a seal against the boards at all depends on which book you are under, and **When to pinch** below sets out all four; where it may not, a pinch is a play on the puck and nothing else. The goaltender, in a delayed-penalty or empty-net situation, may be out of the equation entirely.

**What it's good at:** Not letting the opposition breathe. It is not really a system so much as a decision to accept unlimited risk in exchange for keeping the puck in the zone.

**What it concedes:** Literally everything. A single clean exit is a breakaway or an empty-net goal against.

**When a coach picks it:** Almost exclusively late and trailing.
- Down a goal in the last two or three minutes.
- With the goaltender pulled for the extra attacker, where you are already six skaters and giving up the empty net anyway.
- During a **delayed penalty**, when the whistle blows as soon as the offending team **gains control** of the puck — not merely touches it (Rule 15.1: *"When the team to be penalized gains control of the puck, the Referee will blow his whistle to stop play"*). The distinction matters on the ice: a deflection, a blocked pass or a puck that glances off a defender does **not** stop play, so a team that eases off after any contact concedes a live puck while its own goalie may be on the bench. Keep playing until the whistle actually goes. This is still the one situation where an all-out press is nearly free, and the classic error is failing to press hard enough during it.

**How it is beaten:** One pass. That is the point of it — you are betting that they cannot make that pass in the time you allow them.

## The Defencemen on the Forecheck

Forwards get most of the attention here, but the defencemen decide whether a forecheck is worth running. If they hold the zone, a failed forecheck becomes a second forecheck. If they get it wrong, a failed forecheck becomes a 2-on-1.

### Holding the line

```facts
Position: At or just inside the offensive blue line, feet moving, on your toes
Never: Stand still — stationary, you can neither reach a rim coming around nor retreat
Technique: Small forward-and-back adjustments keep you able to do both
Rule: You are offside only once both skates have completely crossed the blue line before the puck completely crosses it (NHL Rule 83) — one skate on or behind keeps you onside
Rule: A skate raised over the neutral zone is still onside under NHL and IIHF rules; USA Hockey 630(a) and Hockey Canada 6.11 both require skate contact, so in two books of the four it is offside
```

Your default position as a defenceman on the forecheck is **at or just inside the offensive blue line**, feet moving, on your toes.

Standing still is the mistake. If you are stationary when a rim comes around, you cannot get to it, and you cannot retreat either. Small forward-and-back adjustments keep you able to do both.

Note the offside rule while you are up there: **both of your skates must completely cross the blue line before the puck completely crosses it** for you to be offside. One skate on or behind the line keeps you onside, and a skate in the air over the neutral zone still counts as onside — **under NHL and IIHF rules only; USA Hockey 630(a) and Hockey Canada 6.11 require skate *contact*, so a raised skate is offside there.** This is why a defenceman can keep one heel touching the line and still reach a long way into the zone.

### When to pinch

```facts
Read: Pinch when you can arrive on contact — "if you can't arrive on contact, don't go" — so you can play the puck or, where your book allows a seal at all, legally seal the winger
Read: On a pure race for a loose puck or a dying rim with nobody to seal, you need to arrive clearly first, not "probably"
Read: Pinch when the puck is on the wall and slowing down, not zipping past you
Read: Pinch only when F3 is high and in position to rotate back and cover the point you are vacating
Rule: None of the checking permissions below exist where body checking is barred — USA Hockey 604(a) at 12U and below, in all girls'/women's and all non-check adult classifications; Hockey Canada 7.3 at U13 and below, throughout female hockey and in any Member-approved division
Rule: The IIHF book and the In-House Rules name no body-checking age — ask your league. IIHF 101.1 allows a women's-hockey bodycheck with clear intention of playing or gaining possession of the puck, subject to the exception it sets out; an illegal hit is a minor, or a major plus a game misconduct
Rule: Under the NHL book, a player who has lost the puck may still be checked if the check is "rendered immediately following his loss of possession" (56.1) — a beat later is interference (56.2)
Rule: The IIHF sets two conditions where the NHL sets one — the check must follow the loss of control immediately AND you must still be inside an "objectively reasonable window of opportunity" and, separately, "in the immediate vicinity of the skater with the puck" (IIHF 56.1)
Rule: USA Hockey's rulebook writes no window — 640(b): a player who has released a shot or pass "are not eligible to be body checked", though that minor excepts Adult Male Classifications, where 640 Note 2 makes it roughing. Its Casebook allows a finish only if the check was in motion as the puck left
Rule: Hockey Canada writes that window as a distance — Rule 8.3: immediate contact is "contact occurring within a maximum distance of an arm and stick length between the puck carrier and the player delivering the check, at the instant the puck has been released"
Rule: No book of the four caps a late hit at a minor — NHL 56.4 a discretionary major, its 56.5 game misconduct needing an injury; IIHF 56.5 and Hockey Canada 8.3(b) a major plus a game misconduct with nobody hurt, and 8.3(c) a match; USA Hockey 640(g) the same, 640(h) a match, under 640(b–f)
```

A **pinch** is stepping down from the blue line along the wall to kill a rim or a chipped clear before it escapes.

![](diagram:forecheck-pinch)

**Pinch when:**
- **You can arrive on contact — and on an uncontested puck, clearly first.** ⚠️ **First, though: none of this applies if you may not body check at all.** Body checking is barred under USA Hockey 604(a) at 12U and below, in all girls'/women's and all non-check adult classifications, and under Hockey Canada 7.3 at U13 and below, throughout female hockey and in any Member-approved division; neither the IIHF book nor the In-House Rules name an age, so ask your league. ⚠️ **Do not read that silence as "nothing is restricted", though — the IIHF restricts by *category* where the other two restrict by age.** Rule 101.1 says that in women's hockey *"'bodychecking' is allowed when there is a clear intention of playing the puck or attempting to 'gain possession' of the puck with the exception from the situation described in this rule"* — a purpose test, not a free hit, and subject to the exception the rule itself then sets out — and a player who makes an illegal hit under it *"will be assessed one of a: (I) Minor penalty (2') (II) Major penalty (5') and automatic game misconduct penalty"*. **That is the British reader's book**, and the In-House Rules go further still: under their Section 11 any major in women's hockey carries an additional game misconduct and ejection. ⚠️ **And Rule 101.1 has a second limb that takes the seal away even where the hit itself would be legal.** Two sentences after the purpose test it reads that if two or more players are competing for possession of the puck, *"they are not allowed to use the boards to make contact with an opponent to eliminate her from the play, push her into the boards, or pin her along the boards."* **That is the sealing option below, named and forbidden, in the one situation a pinch is by definition** — a contest for the puck on the wall — so under the IIHF book a women's-hockey pinch is a play on the puck whatever you conclude about body checking, and the British reader is under that book. Where checking is barred outright, the same conclusion arrives by the other road: a pinch is a play on the **puck** and the sealing described below is not available to you. The standard coaching test, used identically in [Offensive Zone Play](offensive_zone_play.md), is *"if you can't arrive on contact, don't go"*: you and the puck reach the wall at the same moment, so you can either play the puck or — in a book that still allows it, which as above is not the IIHF book in women's hockey and not a non-checking classification at all — legally seal the opposing winger against the boards. You do not have to beat the puck there — you must not be *late*, because there is only a narrow window once the puck is gone — and how narrow depends entirely on which book you are under. **The NHL** allows the player deemed in possession to be *"checked legally, provided the check is rendered immediately following his loss of possession"* (56.1); a beat later and you may not hit the player who no longer has it (56.2). **The IIHF sets two conditions where the NHL sets one** — the check must be *"rendered immediately"* following the loss of control *and* you must still be inside an *"objectively reasonable window of opportunity"*, with the checker *"in the immediate vicinity of the skater with the puck"* (56.1) — and that same rule calls a late hit *"reckless endangerment"* in terms. **Under USA Hockey the rulebook writes no window**: 640(b) says a player who has released a shot or pass *"are not eligible to be body checked"*, and the test there is whether you could have avoided the contact, not how late you were. That named minor is written *"except Adult Male Classifications"* — but it is not a free hit there either: 640 Note 2 directs a roughing penalty for *"an avoidable body check to an opponent … who is no longer in control of the puck"* with no classification exception. ⚠️ **The Casebook does write one, though, and it is narrower than it first looks.** *Standard of Play*, Situation 10 says a check *"can be finished as long as the defending player first makes an effort to gain possession of the puck and is an arm's length plus stick length away from the opponent when the puck was dumped and the check that is delivered is unavoidable – meaning it was in the process of being delivered when control of the puck was relinquished"*, and the checker is *"expected to minimize the force used"* and not to *"take advantage of a vulnerable or defenseless opponent"*. **Read as four cumulative conditions rather than four alternatives** — ⚠️ **that is this document's reading of the passage's grammar rather than something it states**, three of the four sitting inside one *"as long as"* clause while the minimise-force limb is a separate sentence phrased as an expectation, and it is the cautious reading — and the same answer shuts the door on the obvious abuse: *"It is not acceptable to skate one or two more strides after the puck is gone and then decide to deliver the check."* **Hockey Canada writes that same window as a distance rather than as a moment, and writes it into the rulebook rather than a casebook.** Rule 8.3 defines a late hit as *"a hit where the opponent has released the puck and the offending player does not make immediate contact with the opposing player"*, and then says what immediate means: *"Immediate contact is best described as contact occurring within a maximum distance of an arm and stick length between the puck carrier and the player delivering the check, at the instant the puck has been released."* Under that book you can check the gap rather than the clock — if the puck was more than an arm and a stick away from you when it left, you were late. **And it is not capped at a minor**: IIHF 56.5 reaches a major plus a game misconduct with nobody hurt — on two conditions, both of them the referee's judgement, that the player *"recklessly endangers their opponent by an 'interference or a late-hit'"* **and** *"would not be sufficiently sanctioned by imposing a major penalty"* — and Hockey Canada 8.3(b) reaches the same tier on *"the degree of violence of the impact"* with nobody hurt, and makes it mandatory where it injures, with 8.3(c) a match penalty for a player who *"attempts to injure or deliberately injures an opponent by interference."* ⚠️ **Nor under USA Hockey, which is your book if you play in the United States — it just gets there by a different road.** Its *Standard of Play and Rule Emphasis – Body Checking* section lists the late hit — *"Delivering a late check to a player who has already relinquished control of the puck by a pass or shot. This is oftentimes referred to as 'finishing' the check"* — under the heading **ROUGHING**, and roughing is Rule 640. **640(g)** then assesses *"a major plus a game misconduct penalty ... to any player who recklessly endangers an opponent as a result of any actions falling under Rule 640(b, c, d, e or f)"*, and **640(h)** adds *"a match penalty for reckless endangerment"* under the same sub-sections. ⚠️ **Note which sub-sections that reaches.** 640(b) — the late hit itself — is the one written *"(except Adult Male Classifications)"*, while (d) (a check *"with no effort to gain possession of the puck"* with the stick blade above the knees) and (e) (a check on an opponent already physically engaged for the puck) carry no such limit. **Whether 640(g) reaches a late hit in adult men's hockey, where (b) is not assessed, the book does not say, and this document does not know.** **Rule 602(a) does not depend on the answer**: a match penalty for any player who *"recklessly endangers or attempts to injure any opposing player or team official"*, tied to no named foul at all. You have also turned an even rush into an odd-man one. Where the bar is higher is a **pure race for a loose puck or a dying rim with nobody contesting it**: there you need to arrive clearly first, not "probably", because there is no man to seal and second place just means you have turned your back on the play.
- The puck is on the wall and **slowing down**, not zipping past you.
- Your **F3 is high and in position** to rotate back and cover the point you are vacating. This is the exchange that makes pinching safe: you go down, F3 comes back. If F3 is in the corner, the exchange is not available.
- The score and clock allow it. See below.

### When not to pinch

```facts
Never: Pinch when you are reaching for it, or when you would have to turn your back to the play
Never: Seal a winger against the boards in IIHF women's hockey, the British reader's book — Rule 101.1 bars players competing for the puck from using the boards to eliminate, push or pin an opponent
Never: Pinch when the opposition already has speed coming through the middle
Never: Pinch when you are protecting a lead late
Never: Pinch when you have already been beaten once on the same shift — the second gamble is how one mistake becomes two
```

**Do not pinch when:**
- You are reaching for it, or you would have to turn your back to the play.
- The opposition has speed coming through the middle already.
- You are protecting a lead late.
- You have already been beaten once on the same shift — the second gamble is how one mistake becomes two.

### The weak-side defenceman is the safety

```facts
Convention: At 5-on-5 in every forecheck above, only one defenceman pinches at a time — very widely taught, but a coaching choice rather than a law
Position: The weak-side defenceman holds the middle and does not join in — the reason a failed pinch is a 2-on-1 rather than a breakaway
Read: Both defencemen go only on a called situation — a full-ice press, a delayed penalty, or 6-on-5 with your goalie pulled
Convention: Some teams run a five-man rotation or a standing green light for both defencemen instead, with the highest forward covering the line — ask which yours is
Action: Look for your partner before you commit — if they are already down the wall, you are the safety
Convention: When the strong-side D pinches, the common default is F3 recovering to the point; some teams slide the weak-side D across instead
```

**The default, at 5-on-5 in every forecheck described above: only one defenceman pinches at a time.** This is a very widely taught coaching choice rather than a law of hockey, but it is close to universal, and if nobody has told you otherwise it is the assumption to play on.

The **weak-side defenceman** — the one away from the puck — holds the middle of the ice and does not join in. They are the last line of defence and the reason a failed pinch is a 2-on-1 rather than a breakaway. Both defencemen pinching simultaneously is described in coaching material as a last resort, because it leads directly to breakaways.

**The situations where even a one-at-a-time team sends both defencemen on purpose:**
- **The full-ice press** described above — the whole point of it is that the safety has been given up deliberately.
- **A delayed penalty**, when play cannot continue past the moment the offending team gains control, so there is no counter-attack to be exposed to.
- **6-on-5 with your goalie pulled**, where the empty net is already conceded and the only currency left is time in the zone.

Every one of those is a decision to accept a breakaway as the price. None of them is a thing you do because the puck looked gettable.

**And the list above is situational, not exhaustive** — some teams simply run a different default. A team playing a **five-man rotation** or giving both defencemen a standing green light in the offensive zone has decided to carry that risk as a matter of routine rather than in named moments, and whichever forward is highest covers the line. That is a real system, not a mistake; it just is not the one this document assumes. [Offensive Zone Play](offensive_zone_play.md) sets out the same choice from the attacking side. Ask which yours is before you decide your partner is out of position.

The practical version, for you personally: **look for your partner before you commit.** If they are already down the wall, you are the safety unless the bench has called one of the situations above. That decision does not depend on how tempting the puck looks.

When the strong-side defenceman does pinch, somebody has to fill the point they left. **The common default is that F3 recovers towards the middle and replaces them at the blue line** — and when nobody does, a pinch that gets beaten becomes a rush the other way with your defenceman below the puck and the line unguarded. It is not the only arrangement: some coaches instead have the **weak-side defenceman slide across** to cover both points and accept a hole on the far side, and some teams pinch only when F3 has already called that they are high and ready, so no rotation is needed at all. Ask which of the three your team runs. See [Defender](../positions/defender.md) and [Risk Management](../hockey-iq/risk_management.md).

## Off a Dump-In, a Turnover, or a Control Setup

These are three completely different situations and most players forecheck them identically. They should not. The first two are the common pair — you put the puck in, or you lost it. The third is the one nobody practises: the opposition already has the puck, stopped behind their own net, with everybody looking up.

### Off a dump-in — you control the clock

```facts
Key: You chose when the puck went in and you know where it is going — you arrive at full speed with a head start
Action: Leave before the puck does — F1 should be accelerating as the dump is released, not after it
Targets: Aim the dump at a person, not a zone. Put it where their defenceman must retrieve it facing their own end boards
Never: Finish into the back of a player facing the wall. Take the puck, take the ice, or angle them off it instead; the onus is on you as the arriving player
Rule: The NHL and IIHF write no minor for checking from behind at all (43.2 in both), so the floor is a major — NHL 43.3 where the opponent cannot protect or defend himself, IIHF 43.3 where the check recklessly endangers them, each with an automatic game misconduct (NHL 43.5, IIHF 43.3)
Rule: USA Hockey and Hockey Canada do write a minor for checking from behind, but it never travels alone — 608(a) pairs it with a misconduct and Hockey Canada 7.5(a) with a game misconduct; 608(b) and 7.5(b) reach a major, and both books a match penalty
Rule: That minor is not this hit — USA Hockey's Casebook, Rule 608 Situation 1, puts a forceful check on an opponent standing along the boards with their back to the middle of the ice in the major-plus-game-misconduct or match tier "in every instance"
Rule: The boarding onus is weighed, not absolute — NHL 41.1, IIHF 41.1 and Hockey Canada 7.2 let the referee weigh whether the opponent put themself in a vulnerable position or the check was unavoidable; USA Hockey 603 attaches no such balance. Never rely on it: arrive as though it were absolute
Rule: A deliberate turn is not the same carve-out in every book — NHL 43.1, "no penalty shall be assessed"; IIHF 43.1 waives only the checking-from-behind call and adds "other penalties may still be assessed"; Hockey Canada's 7.5 interpretations penalise the checker anyway. Never a free hit
Convention: F1 takes the retrieval and F2 commits early to the passing lane rather than following F1 into the corner
Options: Three kinds of dump — a hard rim, a soft chip behind the defenceman, and a cross-corner dump; your line should agree in advance
```

When you dump the puck in, **you chose when it went in and you know where it is going**. You get to arrive at full speed with a head start, and their defenceman has to turn their back on the play to retrieve it.

What that means for you:

- **Leave before the puck does.** The forward who is going to be F1 should be accelerating as the dump is released, not after it. The whole advantage of a dump-in is the head start, and players routinely throw it away by watching the puck first.
- **Aim the dump at a person, not at a zone.** Put it where their defenceman has to retrieve it facing their own end boards, with their back to you. **Then understand what you have just created.** A player facing the end boards with a forechecker closing on their back is the exact scenario the checking-from-behind and boarding rules exist for, and the onus is on you as the arriving player. Take the puck, take the ice, or angle them off it — do not finish into their back, and do not drive a player who is facing the wall. The checker's obligations are in [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md), §5.

  **And under two of the four books there is no minor for that one at all** — NHL Rule 43.2 reads *"There is no provision for a minor penalty for checking from behind"*, and IIHF Rule 43.2 that *"This infraction is considered serious and dangerous, therefore there is no option to award a minor penalty for 'Checking from Behind'."* ⚠️ **What each book then requires is the part worth knowing, because it tells you which contact is the ejection rather than merely which is illegal.** **NHL Rule 43.3** makes it a major where a player *"cross-checks, pushes or charges an opponent from behind who is unable to protect or defend himself"*, and **43.5** makes a game misconduct automatic *"anytime a major penalty is applied for checking from behind."* **IIHF Rule 43.3** assesses *"a major penalty and an automatic game misconduct penalty to a Player guilty of 'checking from behind' an opponent, and who recklessly endangers the fouled Player."* A player who is aware of you, square, and able to brace is not that opponent; a player facing the wall with their head down is. **That is a reason to arrive under control, not a gap to aim for** — 41.1's onus above still sits on you either way, and the two rules read together are why the same arrival is two minutes on one player and a five-and-a-game on another.

  **The other two books do write a minor — and it is still not this hit.** USA Hockey Rule 608(a): *"A minor plus a misconduct penalty, or a major plus a game misconduct penalty, shall be assessed for checking from behind."* Hockey Canada Rule 7.5(a): *"A Minor penalty and a Game Misconduct penalty will be assessed to any player who intentionally pushes, body-checks, cross-checks while in motion, or otherwise hits an opposing player from behind, anywhere on the ice"* — with 7.5(e) making that game misconduct mandatory *"any time a Minor or Major penalty is assessed for Checking From Behind"*, and 7.5(d) forbidding a plain misconduct for it. ⚠️ **But USA Hockey's minor is not the hit this section is about.** Its Casebook answers the question at Rule 608, Situation 1: the minor plus misconduct is for an open-ice push on an opponent *"not near the boards"*, or *"minimal body contact"* in a pinch against the wall. The major plus game misconduct, or the match, *"must be called"* in three listed instances, the first of which is *"In every instance where a player forcefully checks an opponent who is standing along the boards (back toward the middle of the ice)"* — precisely the player your own dump-in just created. The same passage names the *"danger zone"* as *"the area approximately ten feet out from the boards."* **So read across all four books, the floor for this particular hit is an ejection**: the NHL (43.5), the IIHF (43.3) and Hockey Canada (7.5(e)) attach a game misconduct to every checking-from-behind penalty they assess, and USA Hockey attaches one to the major and the match, which is where its Casebook puts this one.

  **The boarding onus is real but it is not strict liability, and neither fact should change how you arrive.** NHL Rule 41.1 states the onus and then adds that "the circumstances of the check, including whether the opponent put himself in a vulnerable position immediately prior to or simultaneously with the check or whether the check was unavoidable can be considered," and that "this balance must be considered by the Referees when applying this rule." ⚠️ **That balance belongs to a boarding rule, not to the checking-from-behind rules above, and it is not in all four books.** **IIHF Rule 41.1** — the British reader's boarding rule — writes the same passage, ending with the same *"This balance must be considered by the Referees when applying this rule."* **Hockey Canada Rule 7.2** writes the however-clause too, ending *"may be considered"* rather than "can be considered" and without that closing sentence. **USA Hockey's Rule 603 attaches no such balance**: its Note states the onus flat — *"The onus is on the player delivering the check to avoid placing a vulnerable or defenseless opponent in danger"* — with no however-clause. ⚠️ **Its Casebook is not silent, though, and what it writes is a different weighing:** Rule 603, Situation 2 asks *"was the check reckless and/or what was the vulnerability of the player being checked?"* and works it both ways — a boarding penalty may be assessed where a player *"was not necessarily hit that hard, but was considered to be in a vulnerable or defenseless position when unnecessarily hit with a degree of force that caused them to be thrown into the boards excessively"*, and *"a penalty may not be warranted when a player is hit much harder, but their preparedness for the hit causes them to go only mildly into the boards."* ⚠️ **Read what that leniency turns on — the opponent's *preparedness*, which is exactly what the player your own dump-in created does not have**, facing the end boards with their back to you. **And the same Situation frames both limbs from the checker's side.** It opens *"For USA Hockey youth games, the onus is on the player delivering the check to do so in a manner that does not place the opponent in danger"*, and immediately after the leniency it closes: *"USA Hockey is not eliminating a good, hard and clean check where the focus is on gaining possession of the puck. However, players must be aware of the situation when delivering a check and then held accountable for checking a vulnerable or defenseless opponent."* **The Casebook's test is force weighed against the opponent's preparedness — not the vulnerability-and-unavoidability balance the other three books write**, so it is not the excuse those books offer, and it is not in the playing rule. So the leniency you might be counting on may not be written in your rulebook at all. The four-book comparison is in [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md), §5. Going the other way, a defender who turns their back on purpose to draw the call does not get a checking-from-behind penalty out of it under the NHL's book or the IIHF's — but **the four books do not all stop in the same place.** NHL Rule 43.1 reads *"When a player intentionally turns his body to create contact with his back, no penalty shall be assessed."* IIHF Rule 43.1 waives that same call — *"no penalty for 'checking from behind' shall be assessed"* — and then adds a sentence the NHL's does not have: ***"However, other penalties may still be assessed."*** That rider names neither the penalty nor the player it falls on, and the IIHF book is the British reader's, so do not read a deliberate turn as a free hit there. **And two of the four books do not write the carve-out at all.** Hockey Canada answers this exact case in its Rule 7.5 interpretations — asked what penalty applies *"when a player, who is about to be checked, turns and, as a result, creates contact with the back"*, the answer is that *"The appropriate penalty for Checking from Behind will be assessed to the player delivering the check"*, because *"The responsibility is always on the player delivering the check to avoid making contact with the back."* USA Hockey's Rule 608 states its onus — *"The onus is on the player delivering the check to not hit from behind"* — and attaches no however to it; ⚠️ **no deliberate-turn provision was found in either its rulebook or its Casebook, searched flattened.** Under those two books the turn buys the checker nothing. You cannot forecheck on a prediction of how a referee will weigh that balance, and the injury is not undone by winning the argument. Arrive as though the onus were absolute.
- **F1 takes the retrieval, F2 takes the outlet.** Because you know where the puck is going, F2 can commit early to the passing lane rather than following F1 into the corner.
- **F3 and the D can be aggressive**, because your team is organised and coming forward as a unit.

There are broadly three kinds of dump — a **hard rim** around the boards, a **soft chip** into the corner behind the defenceman, and a **cross-corner** dump that changes the side of retrieval. Which you use is a [Zone Entries](zone_entries.md) topic, but the forecheck timing differs for each and your line should agree in advance.

### Off a turnover — you are behind the play

```facts
Mindset: You are recovering, not hunting — you are disorganised and they are not
Action: The closest player pressures immediately, whoever they are — two seconds of pressure buys the rest of your team time to sort themselves out
Priority: Everyone else's first job is to find their layer, not the puck — a second and third chaser is how a lost puck becomes a goal against
Never: Pinch off a turnover unless the puck is genuinely dying on the wall — you do not have the structure behind you that a dump-in gives you
Goal: Accept the lower ambition — slow them down and collect it in the neutral zone
```

When you lose the puck in the offensive zone, **you are disorganised and they are not**. Somebody is out of position, at least one of your players is skating the wrong way, and the opposition has a numbers advantage for about two seconds.

What that means for you:

- **The closest player pressures immediately, whoever they are.** This is the purest F1-is-a-role situation there is. Two seconds of pressure buys the rest of your team the time to sort themselves out.
- **Everyone else's first job is to find their layer, not the puck.** The instinct after a turnover is to chase and win it back. The correct instinct is to look at where your teammates are and take the empty role. A second and third chaser after a turnover is how a lost puck becomes a goal against.
- **Do not pinch off a turnover** unless the puck is genuinely dying on the wall. You do not have the structure behind you that a dump-in gives you.
- **Accept a lower ambition.** Off a turnover, the realistic win is Win 2 — slow them down and collect it in the neutral zone. Trying for Win 1 with three players out of position is how odd-man rushes are made.

### Off a control setup — when they already have it

```facts
Key: The third situation — they are stopped behind their own net with full control, off a line change or because they beat you to the puck
Options: Deep trap — all five back off and meet them at the blue line, conceding the build-up and taking the speed away at the line
Options: Swing with speed — go in and skate with the players swinging deep, so the speed never gets built in the first place
Options: Quick flush — strike before they are organised, F1 forcing the carrier out one side and F2 arriving to double it
Read: A team that has just stopped behind its own net has not yet settled who is swinging where — that hesitation is what the flush aims at
Risk: Swinging with them concedes the long pass — the stretch forward is your defencemen's the whole way — and flushing commits two forecheckers before the puck is contested
Never: Finish into the back of a defenceman facing their own end boards on a flush. Take the puck, take the ice, or angle them off it instead
Rule: No minor for checking from behind under the NHL or IIHF (43.2 in both); USA Hockey 608(a) and Hockey Canada 7.5(a) do write one, but never alone. And USA Hockey's Casebook puts a forceful check on a player standing along the boards in the major tier "in every instance" (Rule 608 Situation 1)
Rule: USA Hockey 604(a) bans body checking at 12 & under and below, in all Girls'/Women's and non-check Adult classifications; Hockey Canada 7.3(a) bans INTENTIONAL checks, bumps, shoves and pushes at U13 and below, in female hockey and in any Member-approved division — incidental contact is not a penalty
Convention: Which of the three you run is a coaching choice that usually follows the appetite of the rest of your system, so ask rather than picking one mid-shift — nothing published counts that
```

Neither of the two situations above describes this one. Nobody dumped anything and nobody turned it over — their defenceman simply has the puck behind their own net, stopped, head up, with their forwards starting to move. It happens constantly off line changes and whenever they win a race you thought you had. **A forecheck that only knows how to hunt a dump-in has nothing to say here**, which is why the puck so often comes out untouched.

**Deep trap.** Give it up. All five of you back off and meet the attack at the blue line, exactly as you would in a neutral-zone forecheck. F1 takes a shallow angle and steers the carrier to one side; the forward on that side stands up and denies the red line; the third forward either locks across or stays wide. You are conceding the whole build-up — they will get time, space and speed — and taking it all away in one place instead. The turnovers come at the line.

**Swing with speed.** A team breaking out with control will usually **swing** one or two players behind the net or through the corner to build speed, with a forward stretching high. So go in and swing *with* them: F1 stays in to deter the pass while F2 and F3 skate with and lock onto whoever is swinging deep. If their defenceman keeps it and comes out from behind the net, F1 angles in and forces them to one side. Your defencemen have to account for the stretch forward the entire time, because the long pass is exactly what this concedes. It kills their speed at the price of opening ice elsewhere.

**Quick flush.** The opposite instinct: attack them before they are organised. A team that has just stopped behind its own net has not yet settled who is going where, and that is the moment you are attacking. F1 forces the carrier out to one side — ideally onto their backhand — and once F1 has committed, F2 comes in on an angle and doubles it. **Both of you are arriving on a player facing their own end boards, so this is angling, not hitting:** take the puck, take the ice, or steer them off it, and never finish into their back. The NHL and the IIHF write no minor for checking from behind at all (43.2 in both), and what makes a hit the major is exactly the position a flush puts them in — NHL 43.3 reaches an opponent *"who is unable to protect or defend himself"*, IIHF 43.3 a hit that *"recklessly endangers the fouled Player"*. USA Hockey and Hockey Canada do write a minor for it, and it does not help you here either: 608(a) pairs it with a misconduct and 7.5(a) with a game misconduct, and USA Hockey's Casebook (Rule 608, Situation 1) puts a forceful check on an opponent *"standing along the boards (back toward the middle of the ice)"* in the major or match tier. F3 and both defencemen drop off to take away the longer passes out. Of the three it commits the most players before the puck is contested — and it is the only one of them aimed at winning the puck outright rather than at meeting the attack later.

**Which of the three is right is a coaching choice like every other one in this document**, and it tends to follow the appetite the rest of the system already shows — the coach who runs a 1-2-2 is the likelier one to trap it, the coach who runs a 2-1-2 the likelier one to flush it, though nothing published counts that. Ask which yours does, because all three start with F1 doing something different and F1 is whoever gets there first.

The distinction in one line: **off a dump-in you are hunting; off a turnover you are recovering; off a control setup you are choosing.**

## The Trapezoid — Why Dump-and-Chase Works At All

This is the rule that makes the entire forechecking game viable, and most players do not know it applies to them.

**The rule, in two halves — and you have to cite each half from the right place.**

**NHL Rule 1.8 (the markings painted on the ice) gives the dimensions.** The trapezoid's lines begin **eight feet from each goal post** at the goal line and widen to **eleven feet from each post** at the end boards — which, across a six-foot net, works out at **28 feet apart**. (Rule 1.8 gives the per-post figures; the 28-foot total is arithmetic from them.)

**NHL Rule 27.8 (Restricted Area) gives the offence and the penalty.** The goalkeeper may not play the puck behind the goal line **outside** that trapezoid.

*(Cite them that way round deliberately, because the two rules give different near dimensions. Rule 27.8 still reads six feet from either goal post — wording left over from before the trapezoid was widened by two feet each side in 2014. It is **stale, not self-contradictory**: six feet a side gives 18 feet at the goal line, which diverges coherently to the same 28 feet at the boards. (**27.8 is the only one of the two that prints "28 feet" as a number**; in 1.8 that total is arithmetic from its eleven-feet-from-each-post figure. Both rules agree on the far end and differ only at the goal line.) It is simply the old shape. **Never take dimensions from 27.8.** See [Rules Primer](../foundation/rules_primer.md) and [Rink Map and Glossary](../foundation/rink_map_and_glossary.md).)* Playing the puck outside the trapezoid is a **two-minute minor for delay of game** (Rules 27.8, 63.2(viii)). The determining factor is the position of the puck. **The sole exception is a goalkeeper playing the puck while maintaining skate contact with their crease** — there is no teammate-based exception and no "they were about to get hit" exception.

**Where it applies:** the **NHL** (Rules 1.8 and 27.8) and **IIHF play** (Rules 1.8 and 27.7) — the IIHF integrated the trapezoid into its unified rulebook for the 2021-22 season, in its own announcement's words *"to create more offensive play"*. That announcement is also the only support held here for the **KHL**, which it names only in passing — the trapezoid *"familiar to NHL and KHL"* — so treat the KHL as unverified: no KHL rulebook was available to check it against. **The NHL's commonly given 2005-06 date is in no rulebook held here.** **Neither North American governing body's book has one** — the words *"trapezoid"* and *"restricted area"* appear nowhere in USA Hockey's playing rules, its Casebook or Hockey Canada's playing rules, and neither does *"designated area"* in this sense — Hockey Canada's single use of that phrase, at Rule 5.4(b), is the spot a Goal Judge stands in. How much rec, beer-league and youth hockey that adds up to is a question about leagues rather than about books, and this document has no count for it. Check your league's rink and look behind the net for painted lines — but treat an unmarked sheet as a hint rather than a ruling, because IIHF 27.7 turns on *"the position of the puck"*, not on the markings.

**🇬🇧 In England and Wales, that check is not optional — the status is genuinely unsettled there.** The IIHF book carries the rule, numbered **27.7** rather than the NHL's 27.8, and neither the current 2026-27 In-House Rules nor the 2025-26 edition before them amend it. But England Ice Hockey's own *Rules & Regulations* **22.3** say the Goalkeepers' Restricted Area is *"not currently enforced to allow ice rinks the necessary time to make the changes to implement this rule"* — and that edition is 2024-25, so the published documents do not settle whether the suspension still stands. **The evidence runs both ways, and the other side of it is worth stating**: the In-House Rules' own Introduction says *"unless otherwise stated, all rules will be enforced in line with the IIHF Rule Book"* — so their silence on Rule 27 reads as leaving it live, which points against the suspension rather than for it. **For a forechecking team the safe assumption is that their goalie may retrieve the puck**, which is the case in which dump-and-chase loses most of its value. Look behind the net for painted lines, and ask your league before you build a forecheck on it. [UK and England Rules](../foundation/uk_rules.md) records the same uncertainty.

### Why this matters so much to a forechecker

```facts
Risk: Without the trapezoid a puck-handling goaltender is a third defenceman you may chase but not hit — take the outlet away, because finishing a check on a goalkeeper is penalised under all four books
Rule: A goaltender is not "fair game" outside the crease — the NHL and the IIHF permit only incidental contact, only while they are in the act of playing the puck out there, and only if you made a reasonable effort to avoid it (Rule 69.4 in both)
Rule: Hockey Canada is narrower than the NHL and the IIHF in the force of its language, not in how far from the net it reaches — 8.5 requires an effort to avoid contact "in all circumstances", and where NHL and IIHF 69.4 say a penalty "should be assessed", 8.5 says they "must be penalized"
Rule: Charging a goaltender is a mandatory major plus game misconduct under Hockey Canada 8.5(b), wherever it happens — NHL and IIHF 42.1 confine their charging clause to the goal crease, but their 69.4 still penalises unnecessary contact outside it, so a goaltender out there is never fair game
Rule: USA Hockey 607(c) prices body checking or charging a goalkeeper inside the crease or privileged area at "a minor plus a misconduct or a major plus a game misconduct", and 607(e) adds that "a match penalty for reckless endangerment may also be assessed for charging"
Rule: That area takes in the ice behind the net but not the corners (USA Hockey 607(d) Note 2; Casebook 607 Situation 6), and 607(d) Note 1 routes "any accidental or unavoidable contact" to interference, a 625(a)(8) minor, and deliberate contact to charging
Rule: The one permission for the corner is USA Hockey Casebook 607 Situation 5 — a goalkeeper "can be legally checked when outside the privileged area", but only "when the goalkeeper has possession of the puck", any check "to intimidate or punish" is charging, and taking the outlet away is safer
Rule: The restricted area is IIHF Rule 27.7, not the NHL's 27.8; England Ice Hockey's Rules & Regulations 22.3 say it is "not currently enforced" in England and Wales, but the In-House Rules Introduction enforces IIHF rules "unless otherwise stated" — so plan as though their goalie may come
Key: With it, the corners belong to you: their defenceman has to go and get it, turning their back to a forechecker arriving at speed
Goal: That exchange is the whole engine of modern forechecking
```

Without the trapezoid, a puck-handling goaltender is a third defenceman. They stand in the corner, collect the dump-in and hand it to a defenceman who is already facing up ice. **You may chase them down and take the outlet away — what you may not do is finish a check on them.** A goaltender is not *"fair game"* just because they are outside the crease, and the permission the NHL and the IIHF give you is narrow: *"incidental contact will be permitted when the goalkeeper is in the act of playing the puck outside his goal crease provided the attacking player has made a reasonable effort to avoid such unnecessary contact"* (Rule 69.4, in the NHL's wording; the IIHF writes the same condition at the same number). ⚠️ **Hockey Canada is narrower still — but not in reach, and that distinction matters more than the narrowness does.** All three books protect a goaltender well beyond the crease, in near-identical words: Hockey Canada says a penalty *"will be called where an opposing player makes unnecessary contact with the goaltender anywhere on the ice"*, and the NHL and the IIHF say at 69.4 that *"[a] goalkeeper is not 'fair game' just because he is outside the goal crease. The appropriate penalty should be assessed in every case where an attacking player makes unnecessary contact with the goalkeeper"* (the NHL's wording; the IIHF writes the same at the same number). **Nothing in any of the four books makes a goaltender outside the crease fair game, and if you take one thing from this section, take that.** What Hockey Canada does differently is the *force* of the language and the conditions attached. **Rule 8.5 attaches no act-of-playing-the-puck condition at all**, allowing only that *"While incidental contact with the goaltender may occur, attacking players must make an effort to avoid contact in all circumstances"* — and where the NHL and the IIHF leave the referee some discretion with *"should be assessed"*, the sentence straight after that one leaves none: *"The onus is always on the attacking player and players who do not make an effort to avoid the goaltender must be penalized."* **Charging a goaltender is a mandatory major and game misconduct under 8.5(b) wherever on the ice it happens**, the rule attaching no location to it; the NHL and the IIHF write their charging clause at 42.1 only for a player who charges a goalkeeper *"while the goalkeeper is within his goal crease"* — and the NHL grades it, *"[a] minor, major or a major and a game misconduct."* ⚠️ **Read that as one tier fewer outside the crease, never as a permission**: 69.4 is still there, and it is the rule you will be penalised under. [Goaltender](../positions/goaltender.md) holds the four-book position on contact with a goalkeeper.

⚠️ **USA Hockey diverges in both directions, and a forechecker in a league with no trapezoid is the reader most likely to be under it.** Its **Note 1 to Rule 607(d)** routes *"any accidental or unavoidable contact that occurs with the goalkeeper"* to the interference rule — a **625(a)(8)** minor — while *"[a]ny deliberate body contact or check that is delivered to the goalkeeper shall be penalized as charging."* ⚠️ **Charging one is never merely two minutes:** **607(c)** assesses *"[a] minor plus a misconduct or a major plus a game misconduct"* for body checking or charging a goalkeeper *"while the goalkeeper is within the goal crease or privileged area"*, and **607(e)** adds that *"[a] match penalty for reckless endangerment may also be assessed for charging."* But that area is bounded by lines run back from the end-zone faceoff spots to the end boards (607(d) Note 2, with *Casebook* Rule 607, Situation 6), **so it takes in the ice behind the net and not the corners** — and the corner is exactly where a goalie without a trapezoid comes to get the puck.

⚠️ **On that ice USA Hockey writes the only permission any of the four books gives you to engage a goalkeeper deliberately, and it is a permission to battle for a puck rather than to hit.** The NHL and the IIHF permit something much narrower on the same ice — the *incidental* contact quoted above at Rule 69.4, tolerated while the goalkeeper is playing the puck and only where you made a reasonable effort to avoid it. That is a tolerance for contact you could not avoid, not a licence to go and make some. Its *Casebook* at **Rule 607, Situation 5** says a goalkeeper *"can be legally checked when outside the privileged area"* but *"are not considered to be 'fair game.'"* You may *"physically engage with the goalkeeper in an effort to gain possession of the puck"* only *"[w]hen the goalkeeper has possession of the puck"*; *"any check delivered with the intent to intimidate or punish the goalkeeper should be penalized as charging"*; and once they are *"no longer in control of the puck, any avoidable contact… must also be penalized under this rule."* ⚠️ **Those are four conditions, and a forechecker arriving at speed at a goaltender whose back is turned is in no position to weigh them.** Take the outlet away instead — **keeping off the goaltender altogether is the safer coaching line rather than a flat prohibition all four books write**, and the difference matters because a rule you were told wrongly is one you stop believing. [Rules Primer](../foundation/rules_primer.md) and [Playing Without the Puck](../hockey-iq/playing_without_the_puck.md) set out the same Situation.

So the puck battle you would have won against a defenceman either never happens or happens on much narrower terms, and dump-and-chase loses most of its value.

With the trapezoid, **the corners belong to you**. Their defenceman has to go and get it, turning their back to a forechecker arriving at speed. That is the whole engine of modern forechecking.

### How to aim your dump-ins to exploit it

```facts
Targets: Dump into the corners — the "soft" areas the goalie cannot legally touch
Never: Dump straight down the middle behind the net — that is inside the trapezoid, exactly where the goalie is allowed to play it and will be waiting
Technique: A rim that dies in the corner is the most valuable dump against a trapezoid — the goalie may not touch it, so a defenceman must skate over for it, under pressure, facing the boards
Options: A cross-corner dump forces the retrieval on the far side from where their defencemen are set up, adding a second or two of travel
Read: Watch the goalie's stick side — many are far better on their forehand, so dumping to the backhand buys time even inside the trapezoid
Action: In a league with no trapezoid, carry it in, dump where the goalie must leave the net entirely, or forecheck the outlet instead of the retrieval
Rule: No trapezoid is not no restriction — USA Hockey 614(c)(1) is a minor if their goalkeeper falls on the puck "behind the goal line and their body is entirely outside of the goal crease", but its Casebook (Situations 8 and 11) excuses the freeze where your pressure left no chance to use the stick
Rule: Hockey Canada 10.2 is a minor for a goaltender who gathers the puck in "while outside their crease", but Interpretation 3 to Rule 10.1(a), clause ii lets them come out "to cut down the angle and catch the shot or, after stopping the shot, cover the puck"
Risk: A cover you think was illegal is still a stoppage — skate at a goalkeeper who has legally frozen the puck outside their crease and you arrive into the one player you may not finish a check on
```

- **Dump into the corners, not straight behind the net.** Directly behind the net is inside the trapezoid — that is exactly where the goalie is allowed to play it and where they will be waiting. The corners are the "soft" areas the goalie cannot legally touch.
- **A rim that dies in the corner is unretrievable by their goalie.** A defenceman has to skate all the way over for it, under pressure, facing the boards. Against a trapezoid that makes it the most valuable dump available — coaching emphasis, not a counted ranking.
- **Cross-corner dumps** force the retrieval to happen on the far side from where their defencemen are set up, which adds a second or two of travel — often the difference between them making a pass and you sealing them in.
- **A dump straight down the middle behind the net is a gift.** The goalie stops it, sets it for their defenceman, and your forecheck has achieved nothing. If you must go there, at least make it hard enough to come off the end boards and out to a corner.

Watch the goalie's stick side as well: many goalies are far better at handling and setting the puck on their forehand, so dumping to their backhand side buys extra time even inside the trapezoid.

**In a league with no trapezoid, change the plan.** Dump-and-chase loses most of its value against a puck-handling goalie. The alternatives are to carry the puck in with control, to dump it to areas the goalie has to leave the net entirely to reach, or to forecheck the *outlet* rather than the retrieval — position F1 on the defenceman who is about to receive the goalie's pass instead of racing to the corner. ⚠️ **It does not lose all of it, though, because no trapezoid is not no restriction.** USA Hockey **Rule 614(c)** makes it a delay-of-game minor for a goalkeeper who falls on or gathers the puck and causes a stoppage where *"(1) The puck is behind the goal line and their body is entirely outside of the goal crease"*, and Hockey Canada writes it in rule text as well as in interpretation: **Rule 10.2** assesses a minor to a goaltender who *"deliberately falls on or gathers the puck into their body or holds or places the puck against the goal, while outside their crease, thus delaying the game"*, and **Interpretation 3 to Rule 10.1(a)**, clause v, adds *"When a goaltender leaves their crease, they will not be allowed to freeze the puck. If they do, they will be assessed a Minor penalty. No warning will be issued."* So under those two books their goalie may fetch your dump-in but may not sit on it: the puck still has to be moved, under whatever pressure you bring. Forecheck it as a puck-handler, not as a whistle.

⚠️ **But do not forecheck it as a penalty either, because both books publish carve-outs and one of them is triggered by your own pressure.** Read USA Hockey's test exactly as it is written: 614(c)(1) needs the puck behind the goal line **and** the goalkeeper's body *entirely* outside the crease, so a goalie with a toe still touching it is not caught by that clause at all — the *Casebook* at Rule 614, Situation 7 says as much, that *"[a]s long as some part of the goalkeeper's body is in contact with the goal crease when initial contact with the puck is made, this is considered to be in the 'act of playing goal'"*, and no penalty follows. **Situation 8** puts the goalie who skates out to cut the angle and catches the shot in the air in the same place — no penalty, unless they *"had sufficient time to drop the puck and play it with no attacking players in their immediate vicinity."* ⚠️ **Situation 11 is the one that should change how you forecheck**: a goalkeeper who dives on a loose rebound outside the crease gets no penalty *"if the attacking team is in a position to pressure the goalkeeper"*, and one *"[y]es, if the goalkeeper is not being pressured and has an opportunity to play the puck with their stick."* **So the harder you close, the more legal their freeze becomes.** Hockey Canada's clause v has the matching carve-out immediately above it at clause ii, which lets a goaltender *"come out of their crease to cut down the angle and catch the shot or, after stopping the shot, cover the puck."* ⚠️ **The consequence is a safety one rather than a tactical one: a cover you believe was illegal is still a stoppage.** Play on into a goaltender you have decided should have been penalised and the only person who gets penalised is you — and 614(c) cuts the other way as well, at **(c)(2)** for failing *"to play the puck with their stick when provided the opportunity to do so prior to being pressured"* and **(c)(3)** for the puck being *"outside the boundaries of the 'goalkeeper's privileged' area"*, neither of which mentions the crease at all. Note also that a goaltender out there is not someone to finish a check on, under any of the four books; see [Goaltender](../positions/goaltender.md) and [Rules Primer](../foundation/rules_primer.md).

## Choosing a Forecheck by Game State

This is the coach's decision, but you should understand the logic, because you will be expected to switch mid-game on a bench call — often just a shout of "back off" or "we're on the 1-2-2 now."

| Situation | Typical choice | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Leading late** (last 5 minutes) | 1-2-2, 1-3-1 or 1-4 | The value of one more goal is small; the cost of conceding one is enormous. Take zero risk. |
| **Trailing late** | 2-1-2, then full-ice press | The cost of conceding is now near zero. Maximise the chance of a turnover in their end. |
| **Tied, early** | Your team's base system | No reason to distort. |
| **Against a strong puck-moving defence** | **Genuine split — see below** | Coaches go both ways on this one, and the two answers solve different halves of the problem. |
| **Against a defence that panics** | 2-1-2 | Two-man pressure produces turnovers immediately. |
| **After their icing** | 2-1-2 or press | Under NHL and IIHF rules they cannot change lines or call a timeout, so you are attacking tired players. **Under USA Hockey there is no mismatch to attack.** |
| **Your line is tired / long shift** | 1-2-2 or 1-4 | A passive shape is far cheaper to skate. Survive to a whistle or a change. |
| **Immediately after a long defensive shift** | Passive, then change | Do not chase. Get the puck deep, get off the ice. |
| **Killing a penalty** | Separate structures entirely | Penalty-kill forechecks are their own topic. See [Special Teams](special_teams.md). |

> **⚠️ The "after their icing" row does not travel to every book.** The mismatch that row is built on comes from two rules: **Rule 81.4** stops the offending team substituting and **Rule 87.1** says *"No time-out shall be granted to the defensive team following an icing…"*
>
> - **IIHF — the whole thing applies, and it is the book that matters for British league hockey.** Rule 81.4 bars the same substitution — a team in violation *"shall not be permitted to make any Player substitutions prior to the ensuing 'face-off'"* — but **it is not the NHL's rule word for word**: it lists **four** exceptions where the NHL lists three, adding *"a Player with a broken skate"* to the pulled goalkeeper, the injured player and the strength-changing penalty. ⚠️ **That fourth one is not consistent inside the IIHF book**: its Rule 82.1, which states the identical post-icing ban, carries only the same three as the NHL, in the 2025/26 and 2026/27 editions alike, and neither book says which copy governs. Nothing about that changes the shape of your forecheck — a broken skate is one extra way a single opponent might get off, not a line change — and if the officials refuse the change, that is their problem rather than yours: IIHF **82.2(V)** sends the players who attempted it back to the bench with a warning to the team through its Coach, and a bench that has already had one takes a bench minor for delaying the game, which is your power play. **IIHF Rule 87.1 does carry the icing restriction in the NHL's own words**: *"No 'time-out' shall be granted to the defensive team following an icing, following the goalkeeper causing a stoppage of play from a shoot-in from beyond the center red line, or when a defending Player accidentally dislodges the net causing a stoppage of play."* So against an IIHF-rules opponent — English, Welsh and Scottish league hockey included — press after their icing exactly as you would in the NHL.
> - **USA Hockey — neither half applies.** Rule 624 contains no line-change restriction at all, and Rule 204(a) gives both teams their normal five-second substitution windows after *any* stoppage. Rule 636(f) puts no icing condition on time-outs either. So a team that ices the puck under USA Hockey rules changes its tired line off and may still call a time-out. ⚠️ **Both of those windows shut on the same thing, though, and it is not an icing rule — it is the line change procedure.** The visiting team gets five seconds to substitute and then the home team five, after which *"no additional player substitutions shall be permitted until the face-off has been completed legally and play has resumed, except when a penalty or penalties are imposed that affect the on-ice strength of either or both teams"*, and Rule 636(f) requires that *"[t]ime-outs must be requested during a stoppage of play prior to the conclusion of the line change procedure."* **Ask inside that window and the sixty seconds are yours — what is barred is asking late, and that is true after any stoppage rather than anything to do with icing.** ⚠️ **A late change is not free the way a late ask is**: *"any undue delay by either team in placing their lineup on the ice or changing players"* is a warning and then a minor for delay of game, while a late time-out request simply does not get the time-out.
>
> If you play North American rec or youth hockey, there is no mismatch to attack here. Press after their icing because you like the shape, not because they are stuck out there.

#### The one row coaches genuinely disagree on: facing an elite puck-mover

```facts
Read: Pressure (2-1-2) if your forwards are fast and fit, or you are chasing the game — deny the time and the skill never gets used
Read: Contain (1-2-2 or 1-3-1) if you cannot be confident of arriving first, your forwards are tired, or you are protecting a lead
Risk: Pressure gives an elite puck-mover exactly what they want — a committed forechecker and space behind them
Key: The tiebreaker is not the opponent, it is you — pressure only beats a skilled passer if you actually arrive before the pass
Action: Ask which your bench wants, because the two produce opposite F1 routes
```

This is worth spelling out, because you will be told both things by different coaches and both are defensible. **[Neutral Zone Systems](neutral_zone_systems.md) carries the same split, worded the same way.**

- **The case for pressuring (2-1-2).** A defenceman who can pass is dangerous because of the *time* they get. Deny the time and the skill never gets used — you force the puck out early, before their forwards have read the play. This is the answer if your forwards are fast and fit, and it is the usual choice when you are chasing the game.
- **The case for containing (1-2-2 or 1-3-1).** Pressure gives a genuinely elite puck-mover exactly what they want: a forechecker who has committed, and space behind them. One pass past your F1 and your structure is worse off than if you had never gone. This is the answer if you cannot be confident of arriving first, if your forwards are tired, or if you are protecting a lead.

**The tiebreaker is not the opponent — it is you.** Pressure only beats a skilled passer if you actually arrive before the pass. If you cannot, containment is not the cowardly option; it is the only one that works. Ask which your bench wants, because the two produce opposite F1 routes.

### At rec level: simpler is better

```facts
Key: Run the forecheck everybody actually remembers at 10:45pm on a Tuesday
Convention: A 1-2-2 is usually the right default for your league — a recommendation for rec hockey, not a claim about what most teams play
Convention: The house default throughout is a 2-1-2 because the position documents are written around it; run it if your team can sustain it
Action: Agree only that "one goes, one supports, one stays high" and you have most of the benefit of a real system
Never: Send both defencemen unless the bench has explicitly called an all-out press — coaching material puts most rec-league odd-man rushes there, and nothing counts them
Mindset: An aggressive forecheck is a conditioning decision as much as a tactical one
```

If you play beer league or adult rec, the honest answer to "which forecheck should we run?" is **the one everybody actually remembers at 10:45pm on a Tuesday.**

- **A 1-2-2 is usually the right default** — note that this is a recommendation for *your* league, not a claim about what most teams play. It is forgiving, it does not require anyone to sprint, and its failure mode is "we didn't get the puck back," not "breakaway." The house default throughout is a 2-1-2 because that is what the position documents are written around; if your team can sustain it, run it.
- **Two players on one puck is the practical version of every aggressive system.** If your line agrees only that "one goes, one supports, one stays high," you have most of the benefit of a real system.
- **The habit that matters most is not a shape — it is that only one defenceman goes**, unless the bench has explicitly called an all-out press. Coaching material puts most rec-league odd-man rushes down to both defencemen deciding to be heroes at the same moment — **consensus rather than a tracked figure**, because nobody counts rec-league rushes at all, let alone classifies them by the play that created them. Take it as the habit worth drilling rather than as a measurement.
- Be realistic about fitness. An aggressive forecheck is a conditioning decision as much as a tactical one. See [Conditioning and Recovery](../off-the-ice/conditioning_and_recovery.md).

## Reading the Opposition's Forecheck

You will spend as much time breaking a forecheck as running one. Recognising which one you are facing takes about a second and changes what you should do with the puck.

**Look at three things the moment your defenceman touches the puck behind your net.**

**1. Count the forwards below the top of the circles.**
- **Two or more deep** → aggressive: a 2-1-2 or a 2-3/lock. Expect immediate contact.
- **One deep** → containment: 1-2-2, 1-3-1 or 1-4. Expect space now and a wall later.

**2. Watch F1's route.**
- **Straight at the puck, at speed** → they want the puck. They are committed and therefore beatable by a reverse or a quick D-to-D.
- **Curving to one side and slowing down** → they are steering, not attacking. They want you to go one way. Going that way is walking into the trap; the counter is to change the side of the ice before you commit.

**3. Watch their far-side defenceman.**
- **Standing inside their offensive blue line, feet moving** → they intend to pinch. A rim past them is your best play, and there is a great deal of ice behind them.
- **Backing towards the red line** → they are conceding the zone. Do not force a rim into a defenceman who is retreating; you have time to make a controlled play instead.

**One more tell, one pass later: what happens after a D-to-D pass?** If a new forechecker immediately attacks the receiving defenceman, they are running a rotating aggressive system and D-to-D will not save you. If the whole shape simply shifts across and holds its distance, you are facing containment and D-to-D is a free reset you can use repeatedly.

### What to do about each

```facts
Action: Against an aggressive forecheck, move the puck early — before pressure arrives, not after
Options: Reverse against an over-committed F1, rim against a sealing one
Targets: Your winger should come underneath the pressure to receive, not stand still on the wall
Action: Against containment, use D-to-D and controlled regroups to make the wall shift, then chip past the line and race for it
Options: Bank passes off the boards to teammates who have already got behind the line
Never: Skate into the middle of three players — the one thing that never works
```

- **Against an aggressive forecheck:** move the puck early — before pressure arrives, not after. Use the reverse against an over-committed F1 and the rim against a sealing one. Attack the space behind their pinching defencemen; a rim or a chip that gets past the point is worth more than a pretty pass. Your winger should be coming *underneath* the pressure to receive, not standing still on the wall.
- **Against a containment forecheck or trap:** stop trying to force it. Use D-to-D and controlled regroups to make the wall shift, chip the puck past the line of players and race for it, and use bank passes off the boards to teammates who have already got behind the line. The one thing that never works is skating into the middle of three players.

Full detail on the counters lives in [Breakouts](breakouts.md) and [Neutral Zone Systems](neutral_zone_systems.md). The skill of reading the shape before the puck arrives is covered in [Scanning and Anticipation](../hockey-iq/scanning_and_anticipation.md).

## Common Mistakes

- **Treating F1/F2/F3 as fixed people.** Waiting for the centre to arrive so they can "be F1" hands the opposition two free seconds. Whoever is closest goes. Now.
- **F2 following F1 into the same corner when the system did not ask for it.** Two players in one square metre are beaten by one turn, and in most shapes F2's job is the escape route, not the puck. The exception is deliberate and called from the bench: a **stacked 2-1-2** sends both at the same corner on purpose. Know which you are running before you decide someone is out of position.
- **Arriving at full speed with no ability to stop.** You get one straight line and they get everything else. Sprint in, then get your feet under you for the last two strides. ⚠️ **And it is not only a tactical error, because building up speed immediately before contact is the definition of charging in three of the four books — counted differently in each.** Hockey Canada Rule 7.4 lists *“[b]uilds up speed by taking two or more strides immediately prior to making contact”*; USA Hockey Rule 607's Note reaches a player who *“takes more than two strides or travels an excessive distance to accelerate through a body check for the purpose of punishing the opponent”*; IIHF Rule 42.1 writes *“builds up speed by taking multiple strides immediately prior to making contact”* with no number attached; the NHL's 42.1 sets no count and judges *“distance traveled”* instead. **So the two strides that are your ceiling under USA Hockey are already the offence under Hockey Canada** — and USA Hockey's 607(a) has no bare minor in it, only *“[a] minor plus a misconduct or a major plus a game misconduct penalty”*, with 607(e) a match penalty available.
- **Learning to finish a check before finding out whether you may throw one.** Body checking is barred at USA Hockey 12 & under and below, in all Girls'/Women's and non-check Adult classifications (604(a)), and at Hockey Canada U13 and below, throughout female hockey and in any Member-approved division (7.3) — and both books let a local governing body go further. **The IIHF names no age but does restrict by category**: in women's hockey a bodycheck is legal only *"when there is a clear intention of playing the puck or attempting to 'gain possession' of the puck"* (101.1) — the second limb is part of the permission, so a hit thrown to win the puck is not outside it — and an illegal one there is a minor, or a major plus an automatic game misconduct. ⚠️ **The same rule then bars the seal separately**: players competing for possession *"are not allowed to use the boards to make contact with an opponent to eliminate her from the play, push her into the boards, or pin her along the boards"* — so in IIHF women's hockey a wall battle is a play on the puck even where a bodycheck would be legal. Where that is your classification, everything in the bullet below is a penalty, and the forecheck is your feet and your stick. ⚠️ **And know where your angle has to stop, because both North American books put that boundary inside the same rule that bars the check.** Hockey Canada's **Interpretation 1 to Rule 7.3(a)** gives the permission — *“A player may steer or direct an opposing player into the boards, without actually touching or body-checking them. This is acceptable”* — and then closes it: *“[t]his is legal, as long as either player does not use their body to intentionally body-check, bump, push, or shove an opponent.”* Rule 7.3's own preamble counts *“when a player extends an arm, shoulder, or hip after angling an opponent”* as body-checking. USA Hockey's **604(c) Note** says the same from the other side — competitive contact *“also occurs when a player has established an angle … and closes the gap”*, and *“[l]egal competitive contact shall not be penalized under this rule. However, deliberate physical contact with an opponent, with no effort to legally play the puck, shall be penalized.”* **Angle with your feet and your stick; the boards do the rest.**
- **Arriving a beat after the puck has gone, and thinking it costs two minutes.** NHL 56.1 lets you finish a check *"immediately following his loss of possession"* and 56.2 penalises anything later. The IIHF writes that window with two further conditions on top, both in Rule 56.1: a checked player *"may be checked legally, provided the check is rendered immediately following them 'loss of control'"* **and** their opponent is still inside an *"objectively reasonable window of opportunity"* to deliver it; and separately, a player *"in the process of 'abandoning' or 'losing control or possession of the puck' is subject to a bodycheck **so long as the aggressor is in the immediate vicinity of the skater with the puck**."* **That last one is a proximity requirement rather than a timing one — but the IIHF attaches no distance to it, so it tells you to be close without telling you how close.** ⚠️ **And do not carry any of it into a USA Hockey game.** Rule 640(b) says a player who has released a shot or pass *"are not eligible to be body checked"*, and the Declaration of Player Safety calls *"finishing the check"* an *"unacceptable action"*. That named minor is written *"except Adult Male Classifications"* — but it is not a free hit there either: 640 Note 2 directs a roughing penalty for *"an avoidable body check to an opponent … who is no longer in control of the puck"* with no classification exception. **There the test is whether you could have avoided it, not how late you were.** ⚠️ **The USA Hockey rulebook leaves no window, but its Casebook does — a narrow one, and it is four conditions rather than one.** Casebook *Standard of Play*, Situation 10 answers the dumped-puck case: a check *"can be finished as long as the defending player first makes an effort to gain possession of the puck and is an arm's length plus stick length away from the opponent when the puck was dumped and the check that is delivered is unavoidable – meaning it was in the process of being delivered when control of the puck was relinquished"*, with the checker *"expected to minimize the force used"* and not to *"take advantage of a vulnerable or defenseless opponent"*. ⚠️ **Read that as four conditions only with the grammar in front of you, because the passage does not join them as one.** Three of them sit inside the single *"as long as"* clause — the effort to gain possession, the arm-and-stick distance, and the check being unavoidable — and those three do stand or fall together. **The fourth is a separate sentence, and it is phrased as an expectation rather than a condition**: the checker *"would also be expected to"* minimise force and not take advantage. **Treating all four as one test is this document's reading, and it is the cautious one** — the safe assumption for a player is that failing any of them costs you the hit. The same passage closes the escape: *"It is not acceptable to skate one or two more strides after the puck is gone and then decide to deliver the check."* **Hockey Canada writes that window as a distance in the rulebook itself**: Rule 8.3 says *"Immediate contact is best described as contact occurring within a maximum distance of an arm and stick length between the puck carrier and the player delivering the check, at the instant the puck has been released."* ⚠️ **So the arm-and-stick measure is not one book's idiosyncrasy** — it is Hockey Canada's rule and USA Hockey's Casebook rule of thumb; the NHL's and the IIHF's 56.1 give no measure at all. **And in none of the four books is two minutes the ceiling — the NHL's included, where 56.4 gives the Referee a discretionary major for interference *"based on the degree of violence"*. What separates the other three is a major plus a game misconduct with nobody hurt**, because the NHL's game misconduct arrives only *"for an infraction resulting in an injury of an opponent"* (56.5). **IIHF 56.5 reaches a major plus a game misconduct with nobody hurt**, which is the British reader's book — it asks two things of the Referee rather than one, that the player *"recklessly endangers their opponent by an 'interference or a late-hit'"* **and** *"would not be sufficiently sanctioned by imposing a major penalty"* — and Hockey Canada 8.3(b) reaches the same tier on *"the degree of violence of the impact"* with nobody hurt, and makes it mandatory where a late hit injures, with 8.3(c) a match penalty for a player who *"attempts to injure or deliberately injures an opponent by interference."* ⚠️ **Under USA Hockey the tier is written into the roughing rule.** The *Standard of Play* lists *"finishing"* the check under the heading **ROUGHING**, which is Rule 640; **640(g)** is *"a major plus a game misconduct penalty ... to any player who recklessly endangers an opponent as a result of any actions falling under Rule 640(b, c, d, e or f)"*, **640(h)** a match under the same sub-sections, and **602(a)** a match penalty for any player who *"recklessly endangers or attempts to injure any opposing player"*, tied to no named foul at all. 640(b) is the sub-section written *"(except Adult Male Classifications)"*; the pinching section above sets out what that does and does not settle. If you cannot arrive on contact, do not go.
- **Forechecking to hit rather than to take away options.** A missed hit removes you from the play. A completed hit that doesn't win the puck removes you too. Angling removes only them.
- **Skating at the puck instead of at the space you want to deny.** Chasing the puck is the beginner's version of forechecking and every defenceman in the league beats it.
- **Chasing after you have been beaten.** When the puck goes D-to-D past you, your job is to take the high role, not to turn and sprint. Chasing burns your legs and leaves the middle of the ice empty.
- **Both defencemen pinching when nobody called for it.** With nobody holding the middle, a failed pinch is a breakaway rather than a 2-on-1 — that is the whole reason the weak-side defenceman stays home. There are situations where both *should* go — a full-ice press, a delayed penalty, 6-on-5 — but they are called from the bench, not decided individually. Look for your partner before you commit.
- **F3 drifting low.** F3 is the insurance policy. When F3 gets bored and joins the cycle, a failed forecheck becomes a rush against.
- **Forechecking a dump-in, a turnover and a control setup the same way.** Off a dump-in you have a head start and can pressure hard. Off a turnover you are behind the play and should be recovering shape, not hunting. And when they already have it, stopped behind their own net with their heads up, you are choosing between trapping it, swinging with their speed, and flushing them before they are organised — a forecheck that only knows how to hunt a dump-in has nothing to say there. **What "hard" never means:** whether you dumped it or they already had it, their defender's back is to you against the end boards, so take the puck, take the ice, or angle them off it. Finishing into the back of a player facing the wall is a major with an automatic game misconduct under the NHL and IIHF books, which write no minor for it at all (43.2 in both). **USA Hockey and Hockey Canada do write a minor — and it is still not this hit.** USA Hockey 608(a) offers *"a minor plus a misconduct penalty, or a major plus a game misconduct penalty"*, but its Casebook (Rule 608, Situation 1) reserves the major or the match for *"every instance where a player forcefully checks an opponent who is standing along the boards (back toward the middle of the ice)"*; Hockey Canada 7.5(a) pairs its minor with a **game** misconduct and 7.5(e) makes that mandatory. Treat the onus as yours, whatever your book says about it.
- **Dumping the puck straight behind the net in a trapezoid league.** That is precisely the area their goalie is allowed to play. Aim for the corners. In England and Wales, check first whether you are in one — the restricted area's status is unsettled there.
- **Running the goaltender in the corner because there is no trapezoid.** Chasing them down is the point; hitting them is not. Under the NHL and the IIHF only incidental contact is permitted, and only while they are playing the puck (Rule 69.4); Hockey Canada asks you to avoid contact *"anywhere on the ice"* (Rule 8.5). USA Hockey alone lets you *"physically engage"* a goalkeeper outside the privileged area, and only *"[w]hen the goalkeeper has possession of the puck"* (Casebook, Rule 607, Situation 5) — behind the net is **inside** that area, where a body check or a charge is a minor plus a misconduct or a major plus a game misconduct (607(c)), with a match penalty available (607(e)).
- **Not knowing which forecheck your team runs, or which one you switch to with a lead.** If F1 is angling the carrier to the boards and F2 is sealing the middle, both players are doing their job correctly and neither is doing anything useful.
- **Running an aggressive forecheck you are not fit enough to sustain.** A 2-1-2 for the first ten minutes and nothing after that is worse than a 1-2-2 for sixty.

⚠️ **Before any of these: find out whether you may body check at all.** It is barred at USA Hockey 12 & under and below, in all Girls'/Women's and non-check Adult classifications (604(a)), and at Hockey Canada U13 and below, throughout female hockey, and in any Member-approved division (7.3) — and both books let a local governing body go further. **The IIHF book names no age, but in women's hockey it allows a bodycheck only *"when there is a clear intention of playing the puck or attempting to 'gain possession' of the puck"* (101.1)** — both limbs, not the first alone — an illegal hit costing a minor, or a major plus an automatic game misconduct. **And that same rule separately bars players competing for the puck from using the boards *"to eliminate her from the play, push her into the boards, or pin her along the boards"***, so in IIHF women's hockey the seal against the wall is gone even where a bodycheck would be legal. Where it is barred, the forechecking here is still yours; the contact is not.

## Key Takeaways

1. **F1, F2 and F3 are roles set by order of arrival — not positions and not people.** Some coaches assign them by position to teach beginners, so treat that as the typical case rather than the rule. The labels rotate the moment the puck moves, and the old F1 does not turn and chase: they drop in as the new F3.
2. **Angling is the whole skill: skate at the space you want to deny, not at the puck**, and arrive under control — the test is whether you could stop and pivot right now. **Which side you take away is a coaching choice**: the common default takes away the middle and forces the play towards the side wall, and some coaches invert it deliberately, so find out which way your team angles before your first shift. In most adult recreational hockey there is no body checking at all, which makes angling the entire toolkit rather than a beginner's version of hitting. **Both North American books name angling legal there in writing, and both stop it at the same place** — Hockey Canada's Interpretation 1 to Rule 7.3(a) permits steering a player to the boards *“without actually touching or body-checking them”* while Rule 7.3 counts extending an arm, shoulder or hip after angling as body-checking, and USA Hockey's 604(c) Note allows closing an established angle but penalises *“deliberate physical contact with an opponent, with no effort to legally play the puck.”* Angle with your feet and your stick. **And whatever your book, never finish into the back of a player who has turned to the end boards** — Takeaway 8 sets out what that one costs.
3. **Pressure and risk are the same dial.** Every player you send below the top of the circles wins you more pucks and costs you more when it fails, and the game state decides where the dial should be set.
4. **A forecheck has two ways to win:** take the puck back deep in their zone, or force a rushed exit you intercept in the neutral zone. Passive systems are not lazy; they have chosen the second one. **Which of the two happens more often is not the settled thing coaches make it.** The one public analysis found that separates them — Alex Novet's, at Hockey Graphs, on Corey Sznajder's hand-tracking — publishes the two rates on different denominators and states no ratio between them. What it does state is that pressuring the carrier at the moment of the exit takes a forecheck's overall success from 45.7% of breakout attempts to 78.1%, and that it lifts the deep-turnover rate further than the neutral-zone one. Choose the passive system because it is more forgiving, not because the other win is rare.
5. **F1 makes the play predictable; F2 takes the next play.** Whether F1 also hunts the puck is the single biggest difference between the systems — hunting in a 2-1-2 or a full-ice press, steering in a 1-2-2, 1-3-1 or 1-4 — so ask which your team wants. If F2 is chasing the puck F1 is already on, the forecheck has failed.
6. **At 5-on-5 the default is that only one defenceman pinches at a time.** That is a very widely taught coaching choice rather than a law of hockey, and it is the assumption to play on if nobody has told you otherwise. The weak-side D is the safety, and the reason a failed pinch is a 2-on-1 rather than a breakaway. Ask who covers the point the pincher left: usually F3, sometimes the weak-side D sliding across to cover both points, and in some teams nobody, because the pinch only goes when F3 has called that they are high.
7. **Where the trapezoid applies the goalie may play the puck behind the goal line only inside it** — the NHL (Rule 27.8) and IIHF play (Rule 27.7), and the KHL reportedly too though that one is unverified here — so dump into the corners rather than straight behind the net. The sole exception is a goalie keeping skate contact with the crease. **Neither USA Hockey nor Hockey Canada writes a trapezoid at all**, and how much rec, beer-league and youth hockey that adds up to is a question about leagues rather than about books — this document has no count for it. Where there is none, dump-and-chase loses most of its value but not all of it: USA Hockey 614(c)(1) penalises a goalkeeper who falls on the puck behind the goal line with their body *entirely* outside the crease, and Hockey Canada 10.2 one who gathers it in *"while outside their crease"*, so the puck still has to be moved. ⚠️ **Both books carve out the goalie who came out to make the save, though** (USA Hockey *Casebook* Rule 614, Situations 8 and 10; Hockey Canada Interpretation 3 to Rule 10.1(a), clause ii), **and Situation 11 carves out one your own pressure has denied the chance to play it with the stick — so treat a freeze out there as a stoppage, not as a penalty you are owed.** **Where their goalie is handling the puck they are a third puck-handler, not a third defenceman to hit:** a goaltender is not *"fair game"* just because they are outside the crease, and all four books protect them — the NHL's and the IIHF's Rule 69.4 permit only incidental contact and only while the goalkeeper is playing the puck, Hockey Canada 8.5 attaches no such condition and requires an effort to avoid contact *"in all circumstances"*, and USA Hockey sends accidental or unavoidable contact to interference (607(d) Note 1) while pricing a body check or a charge inside the crease or the privileged area at a minor plus a misconduct or a major plus a game misconduct, with a match penalty available (607(c), 607(e)). ⚠️ **USA Hockey alone permits a check outside that area** — its Casebook at Rule 607, Situation 5, says a goalkeeper *"can be legally checked when outside the privileged area"* — but only while they have possession of the puck, only as an effort to gain it, and never *"to intimidate or punish"*, and the area itself takes in the ice behind the net. Chase the puck and take the outlet away instead. **🇬🇧 In England and Wales treat the status as unsettled:** England Ice Hockey's *Rules & Regulations* 22.3 say the restricted area is "not currently enforced", but that edition is 2024-25 — and the In-House Rules do not amend Rule 27 while their Introduction says *"unless otherwise stated, all rules will be enforced in line with the IIHF Rule Book"*, which points the other way. As the attacking team, plan as though their goalie may retrieve the puck, and ask your league. Treat an unmarked sheet as a hint rather than a ruling — IIHF 27.7 turns on the position of the puck, not on the markings.
8. **Off a dump-in you are hunting; off a turnover you are recovering; off a control setup you are choosing.** Leave before the puck does on a dump-in; after a turnover the closest player pressures and everyone else finds a layer rather than chasing. When they already have it behind their own net and nobody is pressuring them, your team picks one of three — back off into a deep trap, swing with the players building speed, or flush them before they are organised — and which one is a coaching choice, so ask. And know what you are arriving into, whether you dumped it or they already had it: a player facing the end boards with you closing on their back. Take the puck, take the ice, or steer them off it — never finish into the back of a player facing the wall. The onus is on you as the arriving player. The NHL and IIHF write no minor for checking from behind at all (43.2 in both), so it is a major with an automatic game misconduct; USA Hockey 608(a) and Hockey Canada 7.5(a) do write one, but Hockey Canada attaches a game misconduct to its minor (7.5(e)) and USA Hockey's Casebook puts a forceful check on an opponent standing along the boards in the major or match tier — so in all four books this particular hit ends your game.
9. **Read the forecheck you are facing in one second:** count the forwards below the top of the circles — two or more means an aggressive system, one means containment — then watch whether F1 attacks or steers, and whether their far-side defenceman is pinching or retreating.
10. **No forecheck is a law of hockey.** Find out which one your team runs and which one it switches to with a lead; most teams have at least two. Aggressive forechecks are beaten by moving the puck early, traps by chipping past the line and skating onto it. Never try to stickhandle through the middle of three players.
