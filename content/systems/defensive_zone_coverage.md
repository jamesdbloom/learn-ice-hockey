## Key focus

**Find out which system your team plays.** Coverage is the most system-dependent part of hockey. The fastest question: when the puck is in your corner, does the winger stay at the blue line or come down to help?

**Never move the puck across the front of your own goal — not with a pass, not by skating it.** Go behind your net, where an interception costs little. Some teams exchange high above the circles instead, so ask which route yours wants.

**Defend the middle before the outside.** Take away the pass into the slot before the shot from distance. A point shot is a save your goalie expects; a pass into the slot is not.

**Be goal side — between your man, or your area, and your own net.** Angle an attacker toward the boards rather than reaching across your body: reaching turns you, and a turned defender is beaten half a second later.

**Somebody must always have the doorstep — the edge of the blue paint — and both defencemen must know who.** The classic breakdown is both of you on the same puck. Say it: "I've got them, you've got the net."

**Take away the next pass, not the puck.** Hold your area, or your man in a man system, with your stick flat in the lane. In a scramble the most dangerous player is almost never the one with the puck.

**Talk early — say what you are about to do, not what you can see.** "I've got the corner, you have the net" settles who has whom. The moment you do not know who is yours, go to the middle.

**Own the net front with position, never with a push.** Get there first, inside shoulder ahead. As the puck arrives, lift their stick low on the shaft, up not sideways, then let go — never on into their head. Never walk a screener off their spot or shove anyone into your goalie.

**Decide where the puck is going before it is yours, then leave as a group.** Scan while the battle is on: winger, centre or glass. Puck out before your third forward, unless your breakout sends a forward early.

## Overview

```facts
Key: Coverage is the agreement your five skaters have about who protects what when the other team has the puck in your end
Convention: The most system-dependent part of hockey — two good teams can play completely different systems and both be right
Risk: The same instruction — "get to the corner", "stay high" — can be correct on one bench and a firing offence on the next
Action: Work out which system your team plays, so you stop guessing
Never: Move the puck across the front of your own goal, with a pass or with your feet — go behind your own net instead, where an interception costs you almost nothing
Position: Be goal side — between your man, or your area, and your own net. An opponent between you and your goalie means you are defending nothing
```

Coverage is the agreement your five skaters have about who protects what while the other team has the puck in your end. **The same instruction can be right on one bench and a firing offence on the next**, which is why knowing your system comes first. Whether the winger stays high or comes down when the puck is in your corner separates the two main families.

**The most dangerous chances come from a small area in front of your net** — roughly from the posts out to the faceoff dots and up to the tops of the circles. A shot from inside it is worth several times one from outside ([Shooting](../technique/shooting.md) covers the values, [Rink Map](../foundation/rink_map.md) the shape). Every system keeps bodies between that area and the puck, which is why you take away the pass into it before the shot from distance.

**The player who scores from close in is standing on the doorstep**, so somebody has to be there. Who it is changes with the system; that it is somebody never does.

**In a collapse, the weak-side winger decides whether the system works.** When the centre goes down into the strong-side corner, the high slot empties, and the seam, the back-door pass and the low-to-high shot all run through it. So the weak-side winger drops into it and climbs back the moment the puck leaves the corner. Only that winger: if both sag, one point-to-point pass gives the other team a free walk-in. Under man-on-man the wingers follow their checks down instead.

**Once you win the puck, your five are turned to leave.** An interception in the slot puts an opponent on the most dangerous ice, in front of a goalie who has just moved across; picked off behind your net, the opponent has no angle. That is why nothing goes across the front of your goal, and why you choose the target before the puck arrives.

**Most defensive-zone breakdowns are not effort failures.** Usually both players worked hard, on different assumptions about who had whom. That is coaching consensus rather than a measured fact — public tracking counts the play the offence made, not the defensive breakdown ([Neutral Zone Systems](neutral_zone_systems.md) covers what it counts). Talking early is the cure.

## The Problem Every System Solves

**In short:** Protect the house first and choose which chances to give up, while keeping someone ready for the outlet.

```facts
Key: The most dangerous chances come from the house, or home plate — the two goalposts widening to the faceoff dots and up to the top of the circles
Convention: The house is an analytics convention rather than an official marking — Shooting covers what a shot from inside it is worth, Rink Map covers the shape
Goal: Allocate five bodies so the house is never open, while still being able to take the puck off them and leave
Priority: Choose which chances you are willing to give up — coverage is not about eliminating them
Risk: With all five packed onto the goal line nobody is available for the outlet — you win the puck and give it straight back
Key: Twenty point shots through no traffic and zero back-door tap-ins is a good night
```

Five of you. Five of them. One net.

But the ice is not equally dangerous. The most dangerous chances come from a small area in front of your net, known as **the house** or **home plate** — the region bounded by the two goalposts, widening out to the two faceoff dots, and up to the top of the two faceoff circles. Draw those lines and you get a shape like a baseball home plate. That's the area that matters.

![](diagram:dz-the-house)

Two honest qualifications. **The boundaries are an analytics convention, not an official marking** — Natural Stat Trick, Evolving Hockey and MoneyPuck each draw them slightly differently. And **how much of the scoring it accounts for depends on whose figure you take**; [Shooting](../technique/shooting.md) covers the values and hedges every one.

The counted figures are more modest than dressing-room versions: **26.8%** of NHL goals came within two stick-lengths of the net and **51.6%** within twenty feet, from the 2,498 goals with coordinates in 400 games of the 2024-25 NHL play-by-play feed ([Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md) covers both; those patches are smaller than the house). What every source agrees on is the ratio: a shot from inside this area is worth several times the same shot from outside it. That is enough to build a system on.

Some vocabulary, used consistently throughout this document and the rest of this guide (full definitions in [Rink Map](../foundation/rink_map.md)):

- **The slot** — the lane in front of the net between the two faceoff circles.
- **The high slot** — the upper part of it, from the faceoff dots up to the top of the circles.
- **The goalmouth** — the immediate front of the net, the doorstep. Distinct from the slot; this is where tap-ins and deflections happen.
- **The crease** — the blue paint the goalie works in. Under NHL rules it is eight feet wide (one foot outside each post) and six feet deep at its deepest point.
- **The point** — the area just inside the blue line where the opposing defencemen stand. An area, not the line itself.
- **The half-wall** — the boards roughly level with the faceoff dot, between the corner and the point.
- **Strong side** — the side of the ice the puck is on. **Weak side** — the other side.
- **Low** — nearer your own goal line. **High** — nearer the blue line.

So the actual problem is: **how do you allocate five bodies so that the house is never open, while still being able to take the puck off them and leave?**

Every system is a different answer, and every answer costs something. There are only five of you, and if you put four in the house then somebody at the point is unmarked. Defensive zone coverage is not about eliminating chances — it is about **choosing which chances you're willing to give up**. A team that concedes twenty point shots through no traffic and zero back-door tap-ins has had a good night.

There is one more constraint people forget. You are not only defending; you are trying to leave. A shape that packs all five players onto the goal line protects the house beautifully and has nobody available to receive an outlet pass, so you win the puck and immediately give it back. Every system trades net-front safety against **breakout readiness**. Keep that tension in mind — it explains most of the design decisions below.

## What Stays True Across Every Coverage System

**In short:** Whatever the system: defend the middle first, stay goal side, and make sure somebody always has the goalmouth.

```facts
Priority: Defend the middle before the outside — it tells you which chance to concede, not which square metre to stand in
Position: Stay on the defensive side of the puck — between your man, or your area, and your own net
Key: Somebody must always have the goalmouth — the doorstep, not the slot; the person changes, the requirement never does. Never both defencemen on the puck without knowing who has taken it
Technique: Sticks flat in the passing lanes — when you cannot reach the puck you can almost always reach the lane
Action: Box out at the net front, inside shoulder ahead. As the puck arrives, lift their stick low on the shaft, well below the bottom hand, then let go. Never sideways or into their head; never cross-check
Rule: Do not count on 69.1 against a screen — its positioning limb sits under a "should be disallowed only if" ceiling, and NHL, PWHL and IIHF 2026/27 Table 14 answer "Goal is allowed" at 5C and 5E for an outside-the-crease screen impairing your goalie. Beat it by position, never by moving them
Rule: Under USA Hockey and Hockey Canada beat a screen outside your crease by position, never by moving them, which can be called interference. Hockey Canada's goalkeeper-interference rule does not reach it; USA Hockey 617(c)(4)'s "interfering with the goalkeeper" is undefined
Rule: Inside the paint the two books split — USA Hockey 625(b) does not ask who scored but lifts if the puck preceded them in or the goalie is out of the crease area; Hockey Canada 8.5 lifts if the puck is already in it, or the crease player stays passive and clear of the goalie's view and movements
Rule: Shoving an attacker into your crease does not disallow the goal — USA Hockey 625(b) allows any legal goal where a defending player's physical interference put them there; Hockey Canada Interpretation 2 to 8.5(a) also penalises the defender who committed the initial foul, under the appropriate rule
Never: Shove an attacker into your own goalie — it can be called interference in every book, and the attacker arrives backwards onto a goalie who is down, square to the shot and unable to see them coming
Action: Talk, and talk early — "I've got the corner", "you have the net", "switch", "back door"
```

These hold no matter what system your coach runs. If you remember nothing else, remember these.

**1. Defend the middle before the outside.**
A shot from the point through clean ice is a save your goalie expects to make. A pass into the slot is not. Given the choice, take away the pass. You are steering the puck to where it is least dangerous, not trying to touch it everywhere.

**This principle does *not* dictate where your feet go** — the system does that. Under **low zone collapse** you sag toward the middle, and the middle stays full because everybody stands in it. Under **man-on-man** you follow your check to the boards, and the middle stays protected because the man who would take the seam pass has someone on them. Under a **strong-side overload** you abandon the weak side and squeeze the puck side, betting that no cross-ice pass gets through.

All three defend the middle first; only the first does it by standing there. So apply the principle by asking "which is the more dangerous option to leave open?", not by drifting to the slot in a system that told you to go with your man.

**2. Stay on the defensive side of the puck.**
Be between your man (or your area) and your own net. Any time an opponent is between you and your goalie, you are defending nothing — you're just skating behind a play. This is why you angle attackers toward the boards rather than reaching across your body at the puck: reaching turns you, and a turned defender is on the wrong side a half-second later.

**3. Never both defencemen on the puck without knowing who has the goalmouth.**
One defenceman engages, the other stays home. If both of you get sucked into the same corner battle, the goalmouth is empty and one pass beats your entire team. Coaches at every level name this as one of the commonest causes of goals against — coaching consensus, not a statistic — and talking prevents it.

**What is invariant is that somebody is home — not that it is your partner.** Under low zone collapse, the default this document assumes, it is the weak-side defenceman. Under **man-on-man** it is whoever's check went there; under a **collapsing box** both defencemen are inside the house; under **Box+1** it is the two low players in the box; under the **hybrid**, whichever of the low three is matched to the net-front forward.

Under a **strong-side overload** some coaches put **both** defencemen low along the boards and hand the middle to a forward. That is one coaching account rather than anything counted, and what it gives the forward is the slot, not the goalmouth — a reason to ask, not to assume. So the rule is **never both of you without knowing who has taken the goalmouth.** [Risk Management](../hockey-iq/risk_management.md) states the same principle.

**4. Somebody must always have the goalmouth.**
Not "the slot" — the doorstep. The player who is going to score is the one standing at the edge of the blue paint with their stick on the ice. Under low zone collapse that is the weak-side defenceman; under man-on-man it's whoever's check went there. The person changes. The requirement never does.

**5. Sticks in passing lanes, on the ice.**
An active stick lying flat in a lane takes away a pass without committing your body — which means you can still get to the next thing. A stick held off the ice or waving at hip height does nothing. When you cannot reach the puck, you can almost always reach the *lane*.

**6. Box out at the net front.**
Get there first: your inside shoulder ahead of theirs, your body between them and the puck. As the puck arrives, lift their stick — ⚠️ **never cross-check their back.** **Take it up rather than sideways, low on the shaft, well below the bottom hand, then let go — stopping at their stick rather than driving on into their head.** **Keep their skates on the ice** — a player whose edges are gone cannot brace, or choose what meets the post first. Full technique in [Defender](../positions/defender.md) and [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md).

**Screening your own goalie is your problem, not the referee's.** An attacker standing outside the crease in your goalie's sightline, without touching them, is normally doing something legal, and the referee will not move them for you. Take the sightline back with your body. ⚠️ **Never walk the screener off their spot** — that can be called interference wherever you play, and where checking is barred an illegal check as well.

**Don't shove an attacker into your goalie hoping for a disallowed goal.** An attacker pushed in by a defender has not caused that contact, so the goal generally stands, and the shove can be called on you. ⚠️ **Hold your position, never push** — the attacker arrives backwards onto a goalie who is usually down, square to the shooter and unable to see the attacker coming, with the post beside them. That injury risk is coaching caution: no source here counts injuries from it. [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md) has the same collision for the player being shoved, and [Goaltender](../positions/goaltender.md) has it from the goalie's side.

**7. Talk, and talk early.**
"I've got the corner." "You have the net." "Switch." "Back door." Coverage is a shared model of who has whom; the only way to keep the model shared is out loud. See [On-Ice Communication](../foundation/on_ice_communication.md).

**What it costs.**

- **The stick lift.** At or near the hands, three of the six books call a lift hooking. The IIHF does so on the location alone (55.1); its next sentence exempts stick-to-stick contact and the book leaves the tension unresolved, so low is still the answer. USA Hockey (623) and Hockey Canada (Interpretation 1 to 8.2(a)) call it hooking where it stops the opponent playing the puck. The NHL reaches a chop there as slashing (61.1). Above the hands, Hockey Canada escalates a lift whose accidental contact injures the head or neck: a double minor in Junior and Senior, and a mandatory major and game misconduct in minor and female hockey (7.6(b)). It makes a *"pitchfork action in lifting the stick"* that injures the head or neck a major and game misconduct.
- **The cross-check in the back.** ⚠️ **Lift the stick; never cross-check their back.** No book here caps it at two minutes, a major ends your game in all six, and Hockey Canada and CARHA must give the major and game misconduct where it injures (9.2(b), 54(c)). Under USA Hockey a reckless one is a mandatory major and game misconduct (609(b)), so plan on a match and a likely multi-game suspension (602(a)).
- **The screen outside your crease.** NHL and IIHF 69.1 and PWHL 71.1 do name positioning: a goal *"should be disallowed **only if**"* an attacker *"either by his positioning or by contact, impairs the goalkeeper's ability to move freely within his crease or defend his goal"* (NHL wording; the PWHL's says "her", the IIHF's "their" and "relevant contact"). But that "only if" is a ceiling, not a direction. All three then answer the outside-the-crease screen that impairs your goalie, Situations 5C and 5E, *"Goal is allowed"* (NHL Reference Table 14; IIHF Appendix IV Table 14, Table 16 in 2025/26 with identical rows; PWHL Table 14). The call is the referee's on-ice judgement, so treat that as what the text supports, not a prediction. Britain plays the IIHF's 69.1, which adds that the Referee *"has the possibility to stop the game"* if an attacker *"is in the goal crease and does not leave immediately"* — a possibility, and only inside the paint.
- **The screen outside your crease, under Hockey Canada and USA Hockey.** Hockey Canada writes no provision about screening a goalie and makes the penalty turn on contact. USA Hockey's no-goal rule (617(c)(4)) says only *"interfering with the goalkeeper"*, undefined, so don't count on it either way; its *"protective screen"* (625(a)(1)) is set on a checker.
- **The screener inside your crease.** **USA Hockey 625(b) is flat** — no goal with an attacker in the crease unless the puck preceded the attacker or the goalie is out of it. **Hockey Canada 8.5** allows the goal if the puck is already in the crease, or if the player in it does not play the puck, interfere with the play or obstruct the goalie's view or movements — and a screener standing in your paint is normally obstructing the view. Those negatives come from the two playing-rule books and USA Hockey's Casebook; **no Hockey Canada casebook is held here**. [Rules Primer](../foundation/rules_primer.md) owns the full comparison.
- **The shove into your goalie.** Under NHL and IIHF 69.1 and PWHL 71.1 an attacker pushed in by a defender has not initiated the contact, *"provided the attacking player has made a reasonable effort to avoid such contact"*. USA Hockey gives the attacker more relief still: *"play shall not be stopped and any legal goal scored shall be allowed"*, no proviso. ⚠️ **And the shove can be a penalty on you in every book** — interference (NHL and IIHF 56.2(i), PWHL 57.2(i), USA Hockey 625, Hockey Canada 8.3(a), CARHA 66(a)), or cross-checking with the stick in both hands. Hockey Canada's Interpretation 2 to 8.5(a) covers exactly this play: *"The referee should also penalize the defending player who committed the initial foul under the appropriate rule."*
- **A forward who runs your goalie** is the referee's problem, not yours to answer. Hockey Canada 8.5(b) can reach a major and game misconduct on the violence of the contact with nobody hurt, mandatory for charging the goalie or for an injury, and 8.5(c) a match.

## Low Zone Collapse (Zone Coverage) — the standard coverage

**This is the system assumed throughout**, and the one coaching material most often describes as taught first in North American minor, junior and rec hockey — *again, consensus from coaching material, not a survey; no count of what teams run has been found.* It is also frequently called **sagging zone**, **zone coverage**, or simply "the collapse". If your coach has never named the system, this is probably what they mean.

The idea: you defend **space**, not people. Each of the five of you owns an area. Whoever skates into your area becomes yours until they leave it. **Three of you — both defencemen and the centre — live below the top of the circles**, which matches their three forwards down there, while the two wingers hold the points so the opposing defencemen never get a clean seam pass into the middle. When the centre goes low to help, the weak-side winger sags into the high slot and you are briefly four down there. Only under maximum pressure — the collapsing box described later — do all five drop below the circles.

### The base shape — puck in the strong-side corner

**In short:** Under low zone collapse one defenceman pressures the puck, the other owns the goalmouth, the centre adds a second body, and the wingers stay high.

```facts
Convention: Under low zone collapse, the strong-side defenceman is on the puck in the corner or behind the net — pressure and pin the puck, or angle them away from the net. Pin the puck, not the player
Convention: The weak-side defenceman owns the goalmouth and boxes out — do not get drawn to the puck
Convention: The centre is the second body into the strong-side corner battle — two on one wins it
Convention: The strong-side winger stays high on the strong-side point, between that defenceman and the net
Convention: The weak-side winger covers the weak-side point and collapses into the high slot when the centre goes low
Rule: In IIHF women's hockey, and so British women's hockey, pin the puck, not the player — Rule 101.1 bars using the boards to eliminate an opponent from the play, push her into the boards or pin her along them
Rule: Pin the puck only until help arrives, never to get play stopped — under USA Hockey, holding it against the boards for three seconds is a minor, help coming or not, pressured or not (Rule 632(b); Casebook Rule 632, Situation 6)
Rule: Outside USA Hockey a deliberate freeze is a minor — NHL 63.2(i), IIHF 63.2(I), PWHL 65.2(i); Hockey Canada 10.1(i) and CARHA 74(b) exempt a hold while "being checked by an opponent", but Hockey Canada 6.12(b) still penalises a freeze for a stoppage and CARHA 55(a) makes stalling a bench minor
Never: Arrive into the back of a player facing your end boards. Take the puck, take the ice, or angle them off it instead
Rule: Pull up on anyone digging at the wall. Hockey Canada 7.5: minor and game misconduct, major on violence, a must if hurt; a match if meant to injure, or if defenceless — into boards or goal, or on the head (head contact, 7.6; 7.7); head first into boards or goal, the harsher of 7.5 and head contact
Rule: Never hit a turned back. On the head: Hockey Canada head contact, a match if defenceless or meant to injure; major and game misconduct a must on a high cross-check at any level, injury (7.6, minor and female), high violence or head injury off glass, ice or boards (7.7, Junior and Senior)
Rule: Pull up on a turned back — the NHL, PWHL and IIHF write no minor for a check from behind (43.2); the NHL and PWHL give a major and game misconduct (43.3, 43.5), and under the IIHF plan on its major and automatic game misconduct (43.3); on the head, the harsher of Rules 43 and 48
Rule: Never put a defenceless player into the boards — no book caps boarding at a minor (NHL, IIHF, PWHL 41; Hockey Canada 7.2; CARHA 49), and USA Hockey's floor adds a misconduct to its minor (603(a))
Rule: Automatic next-game bans: USA Hockey 404(b) for any game misconduct, two or more after a second major; CARHA 32(d) for one with a major, bar an accidental high stick, and 32(c) for abuse or unsportsmanlike conduct late; Hockey Canada 4.8(c) for any in the last 10 minutes, overtime or after the game
```

| Player | Where | Job |
|---|---|---|
| **Strong-side defenceman** | On the puck — corner or behind the net | Pressure and pin the *puck*, or angle them away from the net (see the note that follows) |
| **Weak-side defenceman** | Goalmouth | Own the doorstep. Box out. Do *not* get drawn to the puck |
| **Centre** | Low support, strong side | Second body into the corner battle — two on one wins it; the high slot is theirs whenever they are not down here |
| **Strong-side winger** | High, on the strong-side point | Between that defenceman and the net; block the shot with your head up and out of the lane |
| **Weak-side winger** | High, on the weak-side point — collapsing into the high slot when the centre goes low | Cover the point *and* the middle, in that order of movement |

**"Pin the puck, not the player."** Pinning the *puck* against the wall with your skate or stick is how you stop it going the wrong way; pinning the *opponent* is something else, and [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md) covers the difference. **Hold the pin only until the second body arrives to dig it out — never to get play stopped, and never by falling on it or gathering it into your body. Under USA Hockey, three seconds is already a minor.**

**The second half of "pin the puck, not the player" is how you arrive.** If you are the second body into a corner battle, arrive on the puck or on the ice your opponent wants — never on their back. They are facing your end boards with their head over the puck and cannot see you coming, so ⚠️ **if you can see their numbers, pull up, put your stick on the puck and angle them off it.** That is the whole instruction: take the puck, take the ice, or angle them off it — never finish into a back.

Note that the two forwards' jobs are asymmetric. The strong-side winger holds their point; the weak-side winger is the flex player who covers two areas depending on what the centre is doing. That is the defining feature of the system, and the reason it is sometimes called a *sagging* zone.

**What it costs.**

- **Pinning the player.** **In this system, pin the puck, not the opponent.** In women's hockey it is also a rule: **IIHF Rule 101.1 prohibits using the boards to eliminate an opponent from the play, push her into the boards, or pin her along the boards.**
- **Freezing the puck.** **Pin it only until help arrives, never to get play stopped.** Falling on it or gathering it into your body is a minor in every book, and a penalty shot when the puck is in your own crease — under Hockey Canada, whenever you are. **Under USA Hockey the clock is the penalty, not your reason:** a hold that stops play is a minor, three seconds at the latest, and *"The fact that the player may or may not have been pressured by their opponent has no bearing on the call"* (632(b); Casebook Rule 632, Situation 6). Hockey Canada 10.1(i) and CARHA 74(b) exempt a player *"being checked by an opponent"*, but Hockey Canada 6.12(b) still penalises making the puck unplayable *"in order to gain a stoppage of play"*. CARHA 55(a) is a bench minor for *"deliberately delaying the game in any manner"*, its Note 2 naming *"freezing the puck"*. [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md#when-to-just-pin-it) sets out every book.
- **The hit from behind, under Hockey Canada.** ⚠️ **Pull up — Rule 7.5 is written for this picture**: a player *"with their head down looking for the puck or 'digging the puck out,'"* hit from behind and driven head first into the boards, with the onus on the player delivering the check. Even the floor ejects you: a minor *and* a game misconduct, anywhere on the ice (7.5(a), (e)). Above it: a major and game misconduct on the violence, mandatory if they are hurt (7.5(b)), and a **match** where they go into the boards or goal frame *"unable to protect or defend themselves"*, or where you meant to injure them (7.5(c)).
- **Once it reaches the head, under Hockey Canada.** ⚠️ **Keep the hit off the head — there it is head contact**, because Rule 7.6 *"supersedes any other rules"* except fighting. In minor and female hockey (7.6) it runs from a minor — a third in one game ejects — up to a **major and game misconduct on the violence alone, mandatory if they are hurt or it was a cross-check above the shoulders**. It is a **match** if they could not protect themselves or you meant to injure them. In Junior and Senior (7.7) it is a major and game misconduct at a high degree of violence, for a cross-check above the shoulders, or where their head is driven into the glass, ice or boards and injured, and a match on the same two grounds. **Driven head first into the boards or goal frame, plan on the harsher of 7.5 and the head-contact rule.**
- **The hit from behind, elsewhere.** **Pull up — the NHL, the PWHL and the IIHF write no minor for it as checking from behind** (43.2). The NHL and PWHL give a major and game misconduct (43.3, 43.5); under the IIHF, plan on the same (43.3), because its Glossary counts a check on a skater without the puck who cannot see it coming as reckless. On the head, all three charge the harsher of Rules 43 and 48: still no minor in the NHL and PWHL, and under the IIHF plan on the major and game misconduct (43.3, 48.3). **CARHA** ejects at every tier — a minor or a major, each with a game misconduct, the major a must if they are hurt — and a hit into the boards or goal net on a player unable to protect themselves is a match (53(a), 53(b)). In every book but the IIHF, which has no match penalty, a hit meant to injure is a mandatory match anywhere on the ice (Hockey Canada 7.5(c), CARHA 48(a), NHL and PWHL 21.1, USA Hockey 602(a), with a likely multi-game suspension there). [Body Contact and Battles → Checking from behind](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md#checking-from-behind-and-what-the-onus-does-and-does-not-mean) has every tier.
- **The hit from behind, under USA Hockey.** ⚠️ **Pull up here too — the softer floor does not reach this play.** 608(a) starts at a minor plus a misconduct, which is not an ejection (404(a)), but its Casebook, Rule 608, Situation 1, requires the major plus a game misconduct, or the match penalty, *"[i]n every instance where a player forcefully checks an opponent who is standing along the boards (back toward the middle of the ice)"*. It requires the same where they are *"thrust head first into the boards or goal frame"*, and where the check recklessly endangers them. For a reckless one, plan on a match and a likely multi-game suspension (602(a)), and **608(c)** says a match *"shall be assessed"* where the check comes *"with excessive force while the opponent is in a vulnerable or defenseless position"*. The same entry names the ice it worries about, the *"“danger zone,” which is the area approximately ten feet out from the boards"* — the corner, the half-wall and the wall side of the net front.
- **The next game.** ⚠️ **An ejection can cost you the next game too.** Under **USA Hockey 404(b)** any game misconduct also suspends you for your team's next game — at least two where it came from a second major (403(b)). CARHA suspends automatically for at least one game where the game misconduct comes with a major, unless it was for an accidental high stick (32(d)), and for an abuse-of-officials or unsportsmanlike game misconduct in the last 10 minutes or overtime (32(c)). Hockey Canada suspends automatically for any game misconduct in the last 10 minutes, overtime or after the game (4.8(c)); earlier ones go to your league for further action. The NHL and PWHL suspend automatically on repeat game misconducts, counted by category (23.3–23.6) — for checking from behind or boarding, the second (23.6). The IIHF writes no count of its own. [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md) and [Rules Primer](../foundation/rules_primer.md) own the full comparison; **no Hockey Canada casebook is held here to compare**.
- **The next game, at a club in England or Wales.** ⚠️ **Outside the Elite League, a game misconduct for checking from behind or a check to the head, or a major for boarding, is at least a one-game ban, in every league or age group you are registered in;** in the Elite League, its Department of Player Safety decides (Casebook Rule 28). [UK and England Rules](../foundation/uk_rules.md) has the full tariff.
- **Boarding.** ⚠️ **Pin the puck, never the player, against the boards.** Checking a defenceless opponent into them is boarding, and no book here caps it at a minor. USA Hockey never gives the minor alone (603(a)) and makes a reckless one a mandatory major and game misconduct (603(b)), so plan on a match and a likely multi-game suspension (602(a)). [Body Contact and Battles → Boarding](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md#boarding-and-the-player-who-cannot-see-you-coming) has every tier.

### The rotations

```facts
Convention: In the collapse, coverage is a set of positions that swap as the puck travels, not jobs bolted to people
Convention: Corner to behind the net — the defencemen exchange roles at the midline; call it, "I've got them, you've got the net"
Convention: Corner to up the strong-side wall — the strong-side winger comes down the wall to between the hash marks and the goal line and takes it; that is your breakout starting, not a corner battle
Convention: Low to high — everyone climbs a level: wingers close on their points, the centre steps up into the high slot, the defencemen recover to the net front and low slot
Convention: Point to point — both wingers slide across and the shape shifts as a unit; nobody chases across the middle at speed
Convention: Puck to the far corner — the defencemen exchange again, the centre follows it low, and the wingers swap flex roles
```

Coverage is a set of positions that swap as the puck travels, not a set of jobs bolted to people.

- **Puck moves corner → behind the net.** The strong-side defenceman follows it to the post and hands it off at the midline. The other defenceman is now the strong-side defenceman and takes the carrier as they emerge; the first one slides back to the goalmouth. You have exchanged roles without either of you ever leaving the net unattended. Call it: "I've got them, you've got the net."
- **Puck moves corner → up the strong-side wall.** The strong-side winger engages it. That is not a corner battle — that is the start of your breakout, and it is the one time in this system a winger comes off the point voluntarily. **They come down the wall to between the hash marks and the goal line** and take the puck there; the point coverage they are leaving is finished, because the other team no longer has the puck. Take it. See [Breakouts](breakouts.md).
- **Puck goes low → high, to the point.** Everyone climbs a level. The wingers close on their points, the centre steps up out of the low battle into the high slot, the defencemen recover to the net front and the low slot, and shooting lanes get filled.
- **Puck moves point → point across the top.** Both wingers slide across with it and the whole shape shifts a few feet toward the new strong side. Nobody chases across the middle at speed; you shift as a unit.
- **Puck moves to the far corner.** The defencemen exchange again, the centre follows the puck low, and the wingers swap flex roles — the new weak-side winger becomes the one who collapses.

### The weak-side winger's collapse — the moment that decides the system

```facts
Convention: When the centre goes down to help in the corner, the weak-side winger drops off their point into the high slot to fill it
Action: Climb straight back up to the point as soon as the puck comes out of the corner
Action: Anyone with a clear view calls it — the centre, the weak-side defenceman, or the winger; don't wait to be told
Never: Both wingers sag at once — the point-to-point pass across the top then gives their defenceman a free walk-in
Read: If your weak-side winger has already followed the opposing defenceman down off the point, the collapse is not available — the defencemen or the centre must cover the high slot instead
```

When the puck goes into the strong-side corner and **the centre goes down to help**, the high slot is momentarily empty, and it cannot stay empty. So the **weak-side winger drops off their point into the high slot** to fill it. As soon as the puck comes out of the corner, that winger climbs straight back up to their point.

**The high slot is not the most dangerous ice on the rink** — that is the inner slot, nearer the crease. [Shooting](../technique/shooting.md) has close to half of NHL goals coming from the inner slot against just over a quarter from the outer slot, which is the band called the high slot here — a three-year, five-on-five Sportlogiq sample that is not public, so nobody outside Sportlogiq can check it. What makes this the moment the system turns on is that the high slot empties by design and the seam runs through it: the back-door pass crosses it and a low-to-high shot can come from it.

![](diagram:dz-collapse-corner)

![](diagram:dz-collapse-high-slot)

Two things to get right:

- **Anyone with a clear view calls it** — the centre going down, the weak-side defenceman who can see the whole picture, or the winger themselves. Don't wait to be told; the rotation is late by the time somebody realises it's missing.
- **Only the weak-side winger does this.** The strong-side winger holds their point. If both wingers sag, the point-to-point pass across the top gives their defenceman a free walk-in.

There is one situation where the collapse isn't available: if the **weak-side opposing defenceman has already come down** off the point and your weak-side winger has followed them, that winger is committed. Then the high slot has to be covered by the two defencemen being more vigilant about arrivals, or by the centre choosing not to commit fully to the corner. Read it and call it.

### What low zone collapse is good at

```facts
Goal: Protect the middle by construction — you don't have to make good reads for the house to be full
Key: The easiest system to learn, because the areas are fixed even when the play is chaotic
Action: Let them cycle — grinding the wall spends their energy in the least valuable ice while you sit in the most valuable
Mindset: Area coverage doesn't ask a tired player or a mismatched winger to win a footrace
Position: Both wingers are already high — two outlets the moment you win the puck
```

- **It protects the middle by construction.** You don't have to make good reads for the house to be full; standing in the right place does most of the work.
- **It is the easiest system to learn.** Areas are fixed, so you always know roughly where to be even when the play is chaotic.
- **It is very hard to beat by cycling.** Teams that grind the puck along the wall and behind the net are working in the least valuable part of the ice while you sit in the most valuable part. Let them cycle. They're spending energy on the perimeter.
- **Area coverage handles fatigue and mismatches.** When you're tired, or a fourth-line winger is on with their top centre, it doesn't ask you to win a footrace.
- **The wingers are already positioned to break out.** Two forwards high means two outlets the moment you win the puck.

### Low Zone Collapse: What it concedes

```facts
Key: The walk-down, not the point shot, is the system's actual weakness — every collapse team needs an explicit answer to it
Countered by: The opposing defenceman skating down into the top of the circle, roughly halving their distance to the net while a winger guards an area rather than a man
Countered by: The seam between areas — skilled attackers stand where each defender assumes the other has them
Risk: Point shots, conceded on purpose — the bargain gets worse against a defenceman with a genuine shot
Risk: Extended possession — you are waiting for a mistake, and against a patient team that means tired legs
```

- **Point shots.** Unapologetically. The bargain is that a shot from 55 feet with a stick in the lane is one your goalie should have — and that you'd rather concede it than concede a seam pass. The bargain gets worse as the shooting gets better; at higher levels, and against a defenceman with a genuine shot, "let them shoot" is a real cost.
- **The walk-down from the point.** This is the system's actual weakness, not the point shot. If the opposing defenceman doesn't shoot from the blue line but skates *down* into the top of the circle, a zone winger who is guarding an area rather than a man will often let them go. **The top of the circle is about 35 feet from the goal line** (the end-zone faceoff spots sit 20 feet out, and the circles have a 15-foot radius). So they have roughly halved their distance to the net without anybody touching them, and from there they can shoot or feed the slot. Every collapse team needs an explicit answer to this (see [Defending Specific Threats](#defending-specific-threats)).
- **Extended possession.** You will spend long shifts in your own end. You are not generating turnovers; you're waiting for a mistake or a loose puck. Against a patient team this means tired legs and eventually a breakdown.
- **The seam between zones.** Skilled attackers deliberately stand *between* two defenders' areas, where each assumes the other has them. Zone coverage's blind spot is the player nobody feels responsible for.

## Man-on-Man

Every player is assigned an opponent and covers that opponent everywhere in the zone. Your winger's opposing defenceman goes to the corner — you go to the corner. Their centre goes behind the net — your centre goes behind the net. Nobody is ever unmarked, because "unmarked" is not a state that exists in the system.

### Assignments

```facts
Convention: Under man-on-man, both defencemen normally take the two most dangerous forwards — typically whoever is low and around the net
Convention: The centre takes the third forward, often the one supporting on the wall or in the slot
Convention: The two wingers take the two opposing defencemen and follow them, including down into the corner — the sharpest practical difference from low zone collapse
Action: Switch when two attackers cross, it is clean, and both of you call it out loud; stay when it isn't
```

- **Both defencemen** normally take the two most dangerous forwards, typically whoever is low and around the net.
- **The centre** takes the third forward, often the one supporting on the wall or in the slot.
- **The two wingers** take the two opposing defencemen — and follow them, including down into the corner if they walk down. This is the sharpest practical difference from low zone collapse and the one that causes most confusion between teammates.
- **Switching** is legal and necessary. When two attackers cross, the two defenders trade checks and say so out loud. The rule is usually: switch when it's clean and both of you call it; stay when it isn't.

### What man-on-man is good at

```facts
Key: Nobody is ever unmarked — the seam problem zone coverage has simply doesn't exist
Goal: Generate turnovers — pressure everywhere means bad passes everywhere; zone coverage waits, man-on-man takes
Countered by: Nothing a cycle can do — if you are glued to a man, moving the puck to someone who beat an area creates nothing
Options: Match up — your best defensive forward can be put on their best player, all shift
Mindset: "They're yours" is the easiest instruction in hockey to understand, though not to execute
```

- **Nobody is ever unmarked.** The seam problem that zone coverage has simply doesn't exist. Every attacker has someone within a stick length.
- **It is excellent against skilled cyclers and set plays.** A cycle works by moving the puck to a teammate who has beaten the *area* — if you're glued to a man, the movement doesn't create anything.
- **It generates turnovers.** Pressure everywhere means bad passes everywhere. Zone coverage waits; man-on-man takes.
- **It lets you match up.** Your best defensive forward can be put on their best player, all shift.
- **Conceptually simple.** "They're yours" is the easiest instruction in hockey to understand — though not to execute.

### Man-on-Man: What it concedes

```facts
Countered by: Picks and screens — one collision the referee lets go and your man is gone with a two-stride head start
Risk: Mismatches — a winger in a footrace with a mobile defenceman has no help behind them
Risk: One beaten player leaves an attacker with the puck and nobody within twenty feet; every breakdown is immediately a high-danger chance
Risk: It is exhausting — a full shift at your opponent's chosen pace, in your own zone
Never: Follow your check into harmless ice while their teammate walks into the slot you vacated
Risk: Poor breakout readiness — when you win the puck, your five are wherever their five decided to stand
```

- **Picks and screens.** Attacking teams beat man coverage by running players into your defenders on purpose — often interference, but not always called. One collision the referee lets go and your man is gone with a two-stride head start.
- **Mismatches.** Your winger is now defending a mobile defenceman in a footrace. If they lose that individual battle, there is no help behind them, because everyone else is occupied.
- **Total collapse from one error.** In zone coverage a beaten player leaves a hole; in man-on-man a beaten player leaves an attacker with the puck and nobody within twenty feet. Every breakdown is immediately a high-danger chance.
- **It is exhausting.** You are skating a full shift — 30 to 80 seconds on the measured range, nearer 30–45 when it is well managed; [Conditioning and Recovery](../off-the-ice/conditioning_and_recovery.md) covers both figures — at your opponent's chosen pace, in your own zone.
- **It pulls you out of the house.** The classic failure is chasing a man into the corner who never had any intention of getting the puck, while their teammate walks into the slot you vacated. Good man-on-man teams have a rule about *not* following a check into harmless ice.
- **Poor breakout readiness.** When you win the puck, your five players are wherever their five players decided to stand, which is rarely a breakout shape.

Coaching material has man-on-man much less common as a pure system than it used to be — coaching consensus rather than a count. Coaching resources are consistent that the removal of clutch-and-grab defending after 2004-05 made pure man coverage very hard to execute against modern speed and skill. What survives is the hybrid below.

## Hybrid — man-on-man below the dots, zone above

**In short:** Below the dots take a body and stay with it, above them cover an area, and agree exactly where the line is.

```facts
Convention: Man-on-man below the dots, zone above them — some teams say the hash marks or the top of the circles, so learn your team's line
Convention: The two defencemen and the low forward, usually the centre, go man-on-man on the three attacking forwards: one on the carrier, one on the net front, one on the low support
Convention: The wingers play the points by area — but when your point man crosses the line and comes down, they become yours and you go with them
Convention: Below the goal line the low three stay locked on their men; when the puck comes back to the point, everyone releases and reverts to areas
Goal: Remove the walk-down and the unmarked net front while keeping the wingers high enough to break out
Risk: Two players on different sides of "are they below the dots yet?" is exactly the disagreement that produces a goal against
Options: "Man on the puck, zone off the puck" is the same family — man on the carrier anywhere, area coverage away from it
```

It is a deliberate compromise: man coverage where being unmarked is fatal, zone coverage where it isn't. Coaching material routinely calls it the most common arrangement in professional hockey, and increasingly common below it. **No count of what teams run in this zone has been found**, so treat that as coaching consensus. (The one team-level measure on record, KHL pass-clustering data described in [Breakouts](breakouts.md), is about a different phase of play.)

**The rule of thumb:** **man-on-man below the dots, zone above them.** Some teams say "below the hash marks" or "below the top of the circles" — the line varies by a few feet, so learn your team's version. Above that line, you cover an area and pass players off. Below it, you take a body and you stay with it.

How it plays out:

- **The two defencemen and the low forward** (usually the centre) are effectively man-on-man on the three attacking forwards down low. One takes the puck carrier, one takes the net front, one takes the low support.
- **The two wingers play zone up high** on the points — but with a man-on-man trigger: *if your point man crosses the line and comes down, they become yours and you go with them.* This is the direct fix for low zone collapse's biggest weakness.
- **When the puck goes below the goal line**, the low three stay locked on their men rather than rotating by area, which kills cycles far more effectively than a pure zone.
- **When the puck comes back up to the point**, everyone releases and reverts to areas, and the shape re-forms.

**What it's good at:** it removes the walk-down problem and the unmarked-net-front problem while keeping the wingers high enough to break out. It also degrades gracefully — if the low man coverage falls apart in a scramble, you can revert to "protect the house" without changing anything up high.

**What it concedes:** it's the hardest of the three to learn, because it requires everyone to agree on where the line is and to switch mode at the same instant. Two players on different sides of "are they below the dots yet?" is exactly the kind of disagreement that produces a goal against.

There are inverted versions — some teams play man on the puck carrier anywhere and zone away from the puck, which is arguably the true modern default. If your coach says "man on the puck, zone off the puck", that's this family.

## Box+1 and the Collapsing Box

### Box+1

```facts
Convention: Four players form a box around the slot — two low near the posts, two higher near the tops of the circles
Convention: The "+1" is the only player who chases the puck; when the puck moves, the role passes to whoever is nearest and the box rotates one position to fill the space
Convention: Coaching material has it the standard introductory structure for young players and beginner adults — passive, forgiving of mistakes, and a simple picture of where to stand; nobody publishes a count of what teams run
Key: Not a penalty-kill box — that is four players, two high and two low; Box+1 is a five-player even-strength shape
Countered by: A good puck-moving team — one chaser cannot pressure them, so they hold the puck and pick their moment
```

Four players form a box around the slot — two low near the posts, two higher near the tops of the circles — and the fifth player, the "+1", is the only one who chases the puck. When the puck moves, the +1 role passes to whoever is now nearest, and the box rotates one position to fill the space they left.

![](diagram:dz-box-plus-one)

**Where it actually gets used:**

- **As a teaching system.** Coaching material describes it as the standard introductory defensive-zone structure for young players and beginner adults, because it is passive, forgiving of individual mistakes, and gives everyone a simple picture of where to stand. Coaching resources present it explicitly as the stepping stone to sagging zone or hybrid coverage later. **Nobody publishes a count of what teams actually run**, at any level, so take both of those as coaching consensus rather than a measured fact.
- **In rec and beer league**, where nobody has practised rotations and the priority is that five people are not all chasing the same puck.
- **It is not a penalty-kill box.** A penalty-kill box is four players with two high and two low (see [Special Teams](special_teams.md)). Box+1 is a five-player even-strength shape. Same word, different thing.

**What it concedes:** almost everything on the perimeter. One chaser cannot pressure a good puck-moving team, so they will hold the puck as long as they like and pick their moment. It buys structure at the cost of ever getting the puck back.

### The collapsing box (and the strong-side overload)

```facts
Convention: The collapsing box is a state, not a full system — the box shrinks toward the crease as the puck goes below the goal line, all five inside the house with sticks in lanes
Read: Use it protecting a lead late, defending 6-on-5 with their goalie pulled, when structure has already gone, or against a team dangerous below the circles but not from its defencemen
Risk: You concede the entire outside by design
Options: The strong-side overload is the opposite adjustment — all five shift to the puck side, cutting the ice in half and squeezing the carrier
Countered by: One hard, accurate cross-ice pass — the overload leaves the whole weak side unoccupied by design
```

The **collapsing box** is a state rather than a full system: the box shrinks toward the crease as the puck goes below the goal line or into the corner, so all five of you are inside the house, sticks in lanes, conceding the entire outside. It's what low zone collapse turns into under maximum pressure.

![](diagram:dz-collapsing-box)

Where it gets used:

- **Protecting a lead late**, when a shot from the point costs you nothing and a seam pass costs you the game. See [Game Management](game_management.md).
- **Defending 6-on-5** with the opposing goalie pulled.
- **When you are outnumbered or scrambled** and structure has already gone (see [When the System Breaks](#when-the-system-breaks)).
- **Against a team that generates nothing from its defencemen** but is dangerous below the circles.

The **strong-side overload** is the opposite adjustment: instead of collapsing toward the net, all five players shift to the puck side, cutting the ice in half and squeezing the carrier for time and space. It is aggressive, generates turnovers, and dies to one hard accurate cross-ice pass — the whole weak side is unoccupied by design. Some teams use it as their base system; others use it as a situational squeeze when they need the puck back. **Nobody publishes a count of which use is commoner.**

## How to Tell Which System Your Team Plays

Ask the coach. That is genuinely the first answer, and one specific question gets you most of the way: **"When the puck is in our corner, does the winger stay on the point or go help?"** The answer separates zone from man immediately.

If you can't ask, watch one shift with a defensive-zone faceoff and look for these tells:

- **Watch the weak-side winger when the puck goes into the corner.** Do they stay at the blue line and then sag into the high slot? That's low zone collapse. Do they leave with the opposing defenceman? That's man or hybrid.
- **Watch what happens when an opposing defenceman walks down from the point.** Nobody follows past a few feet — zone. Somebody tracks them all the way — man or hybrid.
- **Count your players below the tops of the circles when the puck is behind your net — but count the *wingers*, not the heads.** Both a collapse and a hybrid put three low (two D and the centre), so a raw headcount tells you nothing. What separates them is **where the two wingers are and why**. In a collapse both wingers start on the points and only the weak-side one drops, into the high slot, and only while the centre is low — and it climbs straight back. In man or hybrid a winger leaves the point because a *body* left the point, and goes as far as that body goes, including into the corner. **Five low is neither: that is a collapsing box, which is a situational state rather than a system.**
- **Listen to the talk.** "I've got the net", "you take the corner", "shift over" is zone language. Names and "they're yours", "switch", "stay with them" is man language.
- **Watch the second defenceman.** If they never leave the goalmouth regardless of who is standing there, you're in a zone. If they follow a specific body out to the half-wall, you're in man.

### Most defensive-zone breakdowns are not effort problems — coaching consensus, not a counted figure

```facts
Convention: Almost always both players worked hard, on different assumptions about who had whom, and skated hard in the wrong directions — coaching consensus rather than a measured fact, because public tracking counts the play the offence made and not the breakdown that let it in
Mindset: Knowing your system is a skill, not admin — a player who knows the structure and skates at 80% out-defends one who doesn't and skates at 100%
Action: Announce your intentions, not your observations — "I'm going to the corner, you've got my slot" resolves the ambiguity instead of describing it
Priority: When in doubt, default to the house — get inside, stick in a lane, cover the most dangerous unmarked player
```

When a goal goes in from the slot, the instinct on the bench is to say someone didn't work hard enough. Almost always, both players worked hard. What happened is that **two players were operating on different assumptions about who had whom** — the centre thought the weak-side winger was collapsing, the winger thought the centre was staying high; the defenceman thought their partner had the net, the partner thought the puck battle needed two. Both skated hard, in the wrong direction, at the same time. *(Coaching consensus rather than a measured fact: public tracking counts the play the **offence** made, not the breakdown that let them in.)*

Three consequences follow:

1. **Knowing your system is a skill, not admin.** A player who knows the structure and skates at 80% will out-defend a player who doesn't and skates at 100%.
2. **Announce your intentions, not your observations.** "They're coming down your side" is useful. "I'm going to the corner, you've got my slot" is far better, because it resolves the ambiguity rather than describing it.
3. **When in doubt, default to the house.** If you genuinely don't know whether someone is yours, get inside, get your stick in a lane, and cover the most dangerous unmarked player. You will almost never be badly wrong.

## Defending Specific Threats

Systems tell you where to stand. These are the recurring plays you'll actually face, and what to do about each.

### The cycle

**In short:** Don't chase it round the boards: defend the next pass and the exit points, and send two to win one battle.

```facts
Key: Attackers rotate the puck between the corner, the half-wall and behind the net, passing into the space they just left, until somebody chases out of position
Never: Chase the puck around the boards. Let it come to you instead and defend the exit points, not the puck's current location
Read: Take away the next pass, not the current one — the man arriving is the threat
Position: Cut it at the goal line — meet a carrier coming out from below the goal line at the post, not in the corner
Convention: Under a collapse, be patient — the house is full and they aren't threatening you; under man or hybrid, stay on bodies, goal side — never hold or hook them, puck or no puck, nor bump one without it, or at all where checking is barred
Action: Get one hard win — cycles end when somebody wins a puck battle, so send two, which in the collapse is the strong-side defenceman and the centre
Never: Send the second body into a back — the player digging along your boards cannot see you, and no book prices a hit to the back that stays off the head at two minutes alone as checking from behind
Rule: At a club in England or Wales, outside the Elite League, a game misconduct for checking from behind or a head check, or a boarding major, is at least a one-game ban (IHUK DOPS C.1)
Rule: A hit from behind into the boards or goal frame on a player unable to protect themselves is a mandatory match in Hockey Canada 7.5(c) and CARHA 53(b), and one meant to injure in every book but the IIHF, which has no match penalty; CARHA 53(a) ejects at every tier, a major a must on injury
Rule: USA Hockey alone lets you play on at its floor (608(a)'s minor plus misconduct) — but its Casebook (608 Sit. 1) requires a major and game misconduct or a match "[i]n every instance where a player forcefully checks an opponent who is standing along the boards"; a match: likely multi-game ban
Rule: USA Hockey 608(c) is a mandatory match — "shall be assessed" — for a check from behind that recklessly endangers an opponent "with excessive force while the opponent is in a vulnerable or defenseless position"; for any reckless check from behind, plan on a match and a likely multi-game ban (602(a))
```

**What it is:** attackers rotate the puck between the corner, the half-wall and behind the net, passing to a teammate who is arriving into the space they just left. It's a possession tactic designed to make you chase until someone is out of position.

**Defending it:**
- **Don't chase the puck around the boards.** The cycle wins when your five players are strung out following it. Let it come to you and defend the exit points, not the puck's current location.
- **Take away the *next* pass, not the current one.** The cycle is a sequence; the man arriving is the threat.
- **Cut it at the goal line.** The most valuable place to break a cycle is stopping a carrier who is trying to come out from below the goal line to the front of the net. Meet them at the post, not in the corner.
- **Under a collapse, be patient.** The house is full and they aren't threatening you. Time is on your side; they'll eventually force something.
- **Under man or hybrid, stay on bodies.** The cycle is specifically designed to beat area coverage, and man coverage low is the strongest answer to it. **Staying on a body means goal side of them and within a stick length — never holding or hooking them, puck or no puck, and no bump while they are without it — or at all where checking is barred.** Each draws a penalty wherever you play.
- **Get one hard win.** Cycles end when somebody wins a puck battle. Two of you on one carrier — strong-side defenceman and centre in the collapse system — is how you get that. ⚠️ **The second body arrives late and unseen, so arrive on the puck or on the ice they want, never on their back.** A cycling forward on your boards is digging the puck out with their head down.

**What it costs.** ⚠️ **Arrive on the puck, never on a back: no book here prices a hit to the back that stays off the head at two minutes alone as checking from behind, and in most it ends your game.** Into the boards or goal frame on a player who cannot protect themselves, Hockey Canada and CARHA must make it a match, and a hit meant to injure is a match in every book but the IIHF. At a club in England or Wales, outside the Elite League, the game misconduct also costs at least one game.

**USA Hockey's softer floor does not reach this play:** ⚠️ **its Casebook requires a major plus a game misconduct, or the match penalty,** *"[i]n every instance where a player forcefully checks an opponent who is standing along the boards (back toward the middle of the ice)"* (Rule 608, Situation 1) — the cycling forward exactly — and for a reckless one, plan on a match and a likely multi-game suspension (602(a)). [Body Contact and Battles → Checking from behind](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md#checking-from-behind-and-what-the-onus-does-and-does-not-mean) has every tier, book by book.

### The low-to-high play

**In short:** When the puck goes low, wingers close on their points early and everyone else boxes out the bodies in front.

```facts
Key: The puck goes low to drag your coverage down, then back up to the point or the high slot for a shot with your backs turned and traffic in front — it exists to punish low zone collapse
Never: Get sucked low as the strong-side winger. Stay high instead, in the lane, between that defenceman and the net
Read: When the puck goes behind the net, expect the low-to-high next — close on your point as it disappears, not when it comes out
Action: Get out to the shot, don't sit under it — a winger at the hash marks blocks nothing; one closing hard at the top of the circle, head up and out of the shot lane, does
Never: Lunge at the shot, lift a leg, turn sideways or turn your back on it — close with your feet, square, shin pads to the puck, and stay on your feet until it is released
Action: As the puck goes high, the low players watch it and box out the man — hold the inside, no lean, no push, under CARHA no body contact; never walk them off it — that can be called interference anywhere
```

**What it is:** the puck goes down to the corner or behind the net, drags your coverage low, and then gets passed back *up* to the point or the high slot for a shot with your defenders' backs turned and traffic in front. Coaching material routinely calls it one of the most common ways modern teams score, and it exists precisely to punish low zone collapse. *(Public tracking splits five-on-five offence into rush and forecheck-and-cycle, and does not break out the low-to-high on its own — [Neutral Zone Systems](neutral_zone_systems.md) covers what it counts — so "one of the most common" is coaching consensus rather than a measured figure. Shot **location** is public and plentiful; [Shooting](../technique/shooting.md) covers it.)*

**Defending it:**
- **The strong-side winger's discipline is the whole answer.** They stay high, in the lane, between that defenceman and the net. If they got sucked low, the low-to-high is free.
- **Recognise the trigger.** When the puck goes behind the net, expect the low-to-high next. Wingers should already be closing on their points as the puck disappears behind the goal line, not reacting when it comes out.
- **Get out to the shot, don't sit under it.** Close the distance to the shooter. A winger standing at the hash marks isn't blocking anything; a winger closing hard at the top of the circle, head up and out of the shot lane, is. Close with your feet rather than lunging, square with your shin pads to the puck, hands behind your body with the backs of the gloves out, stick down beside you — never lift a leg, turn sideways or turn your back on it — and stay on your feet until it is released.
- **Everyone else boxes out — eyes on the puck, body beside the man.** The shot is only dangerous because of the tip and the rebound, and you cannot beat anyone to a rebound you did not see leave the stick. As the puck goes high, get to the inside of the player in front of you and hold that spot — no lean, no push, and in a CARHA game hold it without putting a body on them at all. **Never walk someone off a spot they already hold** — that can be called interference wherever you play.

**What it costs.** If your game bars checking, the walk-off is an illegal check as well ([Dealing with the screen](#dealing-with-the-screen) has the citations). Even in a checking game, NHL and IIHF 56.1 and PWHL 57.1 bar moving laterally into a player without established position, and USA Hockey 625(a)(4) bars changing your lane or foot speed to play the body of an opponent no longer in control of the puck. Under the IIHF's women's rule, 101.1, the inside position is yours only if you reached it first: a player already standing there is entitled to that ice, and stepping or gliding into her is at least a minor.

### The point shot with a screen

**What it is:** they shoot from the blue line with a forward parked in front of your goalie. The shot doesn't need to be good; the goalie can't see it, and the rebound comes off a moving body.

**Defending it:** two jobs, and different rules govern each.

#### Dealing with the screen

```facts
Key: Screening from outside the crease, without contact, is normally legal — the referee will not help you in real time
Action: Deal with the screen, don't watch it. Get your body between them and your goalie's sightline
Technique: Never walk a screener out — it can be called interference wherever you play, and where checking is barred an illegal check too. Take the position and hold it, no lean, no push
Rule: A checking game does not make the net-front walk-out legal — a body check is delivered on a puck carrier and the screener is not one, so interference governs: NHL and IIHF 56.1 and 56.2(i), PWHL 57.1 and 57.2(i) — at least a minor for impeding a player "not in possession of the puck"
Rule: USA Hockey 625(a)(4) names the walk-out, a minor for changing "skating lane or foot speed in an effort to play the body" of a player without the puck, in checking games too; a check can be roughing (640); if reckless, major and game misconduct — plan on a match and a likely multi-game ban (602(a))
Rule: Walking an opponent off an established position is a push, and USA Hockey does not cap it at a minor where checking is barred — 604(c) a minor; if the push recklessly endangers them, 604(d) a major plus a game misconduct, and plan on a match and a likely multi-game ban (602(a), 604(e))
Rule: Hockey Canada prices the push that walks a screener off their position at U13 and below, in female hockey and in any division a Member has approved — 7.3(a) a minor, 7.3(b) a major plus a game misconduct on the degree of violence with nobody hurt, mandatory on injury, 7.3(c) a match penalty
Rule: Under the IIHF's women's rule it is an illegal hit (101.1) — "[a]ny move by a Player to step or glide into an opposing Player" is at least a minor, with a major and an automatic game misconduct above it. Hold only ice you reached first
Rule: CARHA lets defending players "stand their ground" (66(a) Note 2), but 49(a) prices pushing, shoving or not averting body contact at a minor or major, mandatory if intentional contact injures, and a major ejects (30(a)). Hold the spot; never lean, push or step in
Rule: USA Hockey's Competitive Contact list is a floor, not a ceiling — 604(a) lets a local governing body prohibit body checking in any classification, and 604(b) prohibits it whenever a Competitive Contact team plays a Body Checking team. Check the game, not the age group
```

**Deal with the screen, don't watch it.** Screening from outside the crease, without contact, is normally legal, and the referee will not help you in real time. Get your body between the screen and your goalie's sightline. ⚠️ **Do not walk them out — that can be called interference wherever you play, and where checking is barred an illegal check as well.**

**Stop at the box-out and the stick lift.** Holding a position you already have is legal in most books; driving someone off theirs is not. **In a Hockey Canada game, hold only ice you already have** — a box-out that stops a forward getting where they are going can be called. **Under CARHA, hold the spot with no lean and no push**, never step into their path to take the contact, and avoid any contact you can. ⚠️ **Under IIHF women's rules, the ice you hold is ice you reached first** — a player already standing there has it, and you go around her. Some women's leagues go further: Britain's WNIHL sets its senior and U16 games as *"Full ice, non-checking"*.

**Check the game, not the age group.** Under USA Hockey a checking-age team can be in a no-checking game for the night purely because of who it drew, and a league can take checking out of a classification the list says permits it. [Body Contact and Battles → 1. Which Contact Rules Are You Actually Playing Under?](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md#1-which-contact-rules-are-you-actually-playing-under) sets out which rules are yours.

**What it costs.**

- **Interference, wherever you play.** A body check is delivered on a player in control of the puck, and the screener is not one, so what reaches you is interference. NHL and IIHF **56.2(i)** and PWHL **57.2(i)** make it a minor for a player who *"interferes with or impedes the progress of an opponent who is not in possession of the puck"*. USA Hockey **625(a)(4)** names this play: *"[a] defending player who changes their skating lane or foot speed in an effort to play the body of an opponent who is no longer in control of the puck."* Rule 625 has no Competitive Contact scope, so it reaches you even where checking is legal.
- **Above the minor.** ⚠️ **Only USA Hockey's interference rule stops at a minor.** The NHL (56.4) and PWHL (57.4) allow a major on the violence, with the game misconduct compulsory once it is for an injury (56.5, 57.5). The IIHF allows a major, or a major and game misconduct, where it recklessly endangers (56.4, 56.5). Hockey Canada allows a major and game misconduct with nobody hurt, compulsory if they are injured (8.3(b)). CARHA allows a major and game misconduct (66(a)), a major compulsory on injury (66(e)), and a CARHA major ejects you (30(a)). **A push meant to injure the screener is a match penalty in every book here but the IIHF**, which tops out at a major and game misconduct (56.5) — NHL and PWHL 21.1, Hockey Canada 8.3(c), CARHA 48(a), USA Hockey 602(a), with a likely multi-game suspension there.
- **USA Hockey, beyond interference.** A walk-out that becomes a body check can be roughing under **640**, where **640(g)** is a major plus a game misconduct for reckless endangerment, with a match and a likely multi-game suspension to plan on (602(a)). Done two-handed with the arms extended it is a cross-check under **609**, with the same major and the same match; the Casebook draws that line at the net front itself (Rule 609, Situation 2).
- **Where checking is barred, under USA Hockey.** Driving an opponent off their spot is a minor under **604(c)** in every Competitive Contact classification — 12U and below, all girls'/women's, all non-check adult — because 604's Note 1 counts *"physically forcing the opponent off the puck and with no effort to legally play the puck"*. ⚠️ **That minor is not the ceiling**, and at the net front it matters most, because the player is stationary with your goalie and the post behind them. **604(d)**: *"A major plus a game misconduct penalty shall be assessed to any player who recklessly endangers an opponent with a body check in a Competitive Contact category"*. **604(e)** adds that a match *"may also be assessed"*. Plan on one anyway, and a likely multi-game suspension, because **602(a)** says a match *"shall be assessed"* to any player who *"recklessly endangers or attempts to injure"* an opponent.
- **Where checking is barred, under Hockey Canada.** **7.3(a)** is a minor for a player who *"intentionally body-checks, bumps, shoves, or pushes any opposing player"*, and it applies *"only in divisions of U13 and below, in female hockey, and any other divisions approved by a Member of Hockey Canada"*. ⚠️ **7.3(b) allows a major and game misconduct on the violence with nobody hurt, and makes that pair mandatory** — *"If a player is injured, as the result of a body-checking infraction that would otherwise call for a Minor penalty, a Major penalty and Game Misconduct penalty must be assessed."* A screener shoved into your own goalie or the post is how that arrives. **7.3(c)** is a match for deliberately injuring or attempting to.
- **USA Hockey's list is a floor.** **604(a)** adds that *"A local governing body may prohibit body checking in any classification"*, and **604(b)** that *"[a]ny time a team from a Competitive Contact category… plays a team from a Body Checking category, body checking shall be prohibited under this rule."*
- **What the books say about holding position.** The NHL at **56.1** — *"[a] player is allowed the ice he is standing on (body position) and is not required to move in order to let an opponent proceed"* — and the IIHF (56.1) and PWHL (57.1) say the same with "they" and "she". USA Hockey's Declaration of Player Safety says a skater *"is entitled to stand their ground"*. The Note to **604(c)** is narrower: it covers players *"focused on gaining possession of the puck"* **and** *"simply maintaining established body position"*, and protects them only *"under this rule"*. So it is no answer to the interference rule, whose Note leaves alone a player who maintains *"an established skating lane"*. **Hockey Canada writes no equivalent** — its only mention of body position, **8.1**, assumes it rather than granting it — and its **8.3** calls impeding *"the progress of an opponent, who is not in possession of the puck"* interference.
- **CARHA.** Note 2 to **66(a)** says *"the defending players are entitled to 'stand their ground' or 'shadow' the attacking players"*. But **49(a)** penalises a player who *"intentionally bodies, pushes, shoves"* or *"does not avert body contact with an opponent"*: a minor or a major, the major mandatory *"[w]hen injury results from intentional body contact"*, and a CARHA major ejects you (**30(a)**).
- **IIHF women's hockey.** **Rule 101.1** allows contact only for the puck: *"'bodychecking' is allowed when there is a clear intention of playing the puck"*, and two players chasing it may push and lean *"provided that 'possession of the puck' remains the sole object"*. Moving a screener is not playing the puck. A stationary player *"is entitled to that area of the ice"*, and *"[i]t is up to the opponent to avoid body contact with such a Player"*. And *"[a]ny move by a Player to step or glide into an opposing Player will be assessed at least a minor penalty (2') for an 'illegal hit'"*, with a major (5') plus an automatic game misconduct above it. The WNIHL is non-checking at U16 and senior level.

#### Lifting the screener's stick

```facts
Technique: Lift the stick up, not sideways, low on the shaft, well below the bottom hand, as the puck arrives, then let go — never on into their head. A screen with a free stick is a deflection waiting to happen
Rule: IIHF 55.1 is the flattest — a stick at or near the hands is hooking, though its next sentence exempts stick-to-stick contact and the book does not reconcile them. Low is still the answer. USA Hockey 623 and Hockey Canada's Interpretation 1 to 8.2(a) reach a lift only where it stops a pass or shot
Rule: A chop at the hands is slashing in all six books — NHL 61.1, IIHF 61.1 and PWHL 63.1 in the same words bar the pronoun, USA Hockey 634, Hockey Canada 9.3, CARHA 80. A clean lift for the puck is not a chop and sits outside all six, but USA Hockey's note writes no attempt-to-play-the-puck let-out
Rule: A chop reaches a major plus a game misconduct with nobody injured in all six books — NHL 61.3 and 61.5, IIHF 61.3, USA Hockey 634(b), Hockey Canada 9.3(b), PWHL 63.3 and 63.5, CARHA 80(a) with 30(a)'s game misconduct. A hooking major needs an injury everywhere but IIHF 55.3 and USA Hockey 623(b)
Rule: Hockey Canada 9.5's list names no division of its own: an accidental lift injuring the head or neck is a double minor, a "pitchfork action in lifting the stick" doing the same a major and game misconduct; in minor and female hockey 7.6 governs: "no type of legal contact to the head, face, or neck"
Rule: Never trade the lift for a cross-check — above the shoulders it is a major and game misconduct with nobody hurt under Hockey Canada (7.6(b); 7.7(b)) and CARHA (54(b)); in minor and female hockey a lift reaching the head gets there on violence, and must on injury (7.6(b))
```

**Lift the stick as the puck arrives — up rather than sideways, low on the shaft, well below the bottom hand — then let go.** ⚠️ **Lift under the shaft, not through it, and stop at their stick** — the stick you lift ends near their face. **Low is the answer**: below the bottom hand the lift stays off their hands, still takes the stick off the ice, and keeps your blade further from their head.

**Never chop at the hands instead** — a chop is slashing in all six books, and it only moves you up the ladder. And never trade the lift for a cross-check: under Hockey Canada and CARHA a cross-check above the shoulders is a major and game misconduct whether or not anyone is hurt. Under Hockey Canada the lift gets there only on high violence or an injury, and CARHA makes any stick to the head a major (62(b)). A screen with a free stick is a deflection waiting to happen; a screen with a lifted stick is just a person, and this is the highest-value thing a net-front defender does. [Body Contact and Battles → The stick lift](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md#3-stick-checks--legal-effective-and-under-used) carries the full comparison.

**What it costs.**

- **A lift at the hands.** Three of the six books call it hooking. **IIHF 55.1** does so on the location alone: *"[i]f the stick goes against the opponent's hands / or near the opponent's hands, it shall be penalized as 'hooking'"*. USA Hockey 623 and Hockey Canada's Interpretation 1 to 8.2(a) do so only where it stops them passing or shooting. The IIHF's next sentence exempts *"only stick-to-stick contact"*, and no IIHF interpretation reconciling the two is held here; reading the hands sentence as operative is the cautious choice, and may be stricter than the rule. The NHL, PWHL and CARHA leave a clean lift alone. A hooking major needs an injury under the NHL (55.3), Hockey Canada (8.2(b)), the PWHL (56.3) and CARHA (64(b)), but not under the IIHF (55.3) or USA Hockey (623(b)).
- **A chop at the hands.** NHL **61.1** makes a *"forceful or powerful chop"* on or near the hands that *"is not an attempt to play the puck"* slashing. A chop is slashing in all six books (IIHF 61.1, PWHL 63.1, USA Hockey 634, Hockey Canada 9.3, CARHA 80), and in every one it reaches a **major plus a game misconduct with nobody injured**. ⚠️ **In CARHA a major ejects you:** it puts a game misconduct on every major except an accidental high stick that injures nobody (30(a), 62(b)).
- **A lift that reaches the head, Hockey Canada Junior and Senior.** It writes two escalations, both triggered by injury, in the interpretations under Rule 9.5. An *"attempted lifting of the opponent's stick, which results in accidental contact to the head or neck area"* that *"causes injury"* is a **double Minor**. A *"pitchfork action in lifting the stick"* that *"results in contact to the neck or head area, causing injury"* is a **Major penalty and Game Misconduct**. That list states no division of its own; Junior/Senior is inferred from Rule 9.5, *High-Sticking – Junior/Senior*, which it sits inside.
- **A lift that reaches the head, Hockey Canada minor and female hockey.** ⚠️ **Rule 7.6 governs, and it is no relief:** *"There is no type of legal contact to the head, face, or neck"*. Even accidental head contact is a minor (7.6(a)), three in one game is an ejection (4.8(b)(ii)), a major and game misconduct is discretionary with nobody hurt and mandatory if they are injured (7.6(b)), and 7.6(c) is a match if they cannot protect themselves or you meant to injure them.
- **The high cross-check, every Hockey Canada division.** ⚠️ **It is a major and game misconduct whether or not anyone is hurt:** *"Any player who strikes an opponent above the normal height of their shoulders with a cross-check will be penalized with a Major penalty and a Game Misconduct penalty, whether or not injury results"*. That is 7.6(b), printed word for word in Junior/Senior's 7.7(b), and CARHA 54(b) is the same. In minor and female hockey it is aimed at a deliberate cross-check, not minimal accidental stick contact (Interpretation 2 to 7.6(b)). Three stick-infraction minors in one game, cross-checking and high-sticking among them, also end your night (4.8(b)(i)).

### When the shot is already away

```facts
Never: Block a shot from a screening position — you change the direction of a puck your goalie never saw
Technique: Block from close if you block at all — ten feet from the shooter, square, shin pads to the puck, head up and out of the shot lane, hands behind you, backs of the gloves out, stick down beside you
Never: Lift a leg, turn your back or go sideways, or lunge. Close with your feet instead, and stay on your feet until the puck is actually released
Action: Talk to your goalie — "screen left", "I've got the tip"; they are trying to find the puck through legs
```

**What it is:** the screen is still standing, the shot is on its way, and your goalie has not seen it leave. Winning the position back is no longer one of your options.

**Defending it:**
- **Don't block the shot from a screening position.** Half-blocking a shot your goalie cannot see is worse than not blocking it, because you've changed the direction of an already invisible puck. If you're in the sightline, get out of it or take the inside on your man without pushing them; let the puck through.
- **Block from close if you block at all.** Ten feet from the shooter, square, shin pads to the puck, **head up and out of the shot lane**, hands behind your body with the backs of the gloves out, stick down beside you. Never lift a leg, never turn your back or go sideways, and never lunge — close with your feet, and stay on your feet until the puck is actually released. See [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md) for the rest of what to protect, and for when not to block at all.
- **Talk to your goalie.** "Screen left", "I've got the tip" — they are trying to find the puck through legs.

### The back door / weak-side seam

```facts
Key: While everyone watches the strong-side battle, an attacker drifts to the far post — one cross-ice pass and it is an open net
Priority: The chance every coverage system is most afraid of, and worth conceding almost anything else to prevent
Never: Lose the man at the back post while you watch the puck — as the weak-side defenceman your eyes track the puck and your body stays between them and the net
Technique: Lie your stick flat in the seam across the slot — it makes the play impossible even when the passer is open
Convention: Weak-side winger sags into the middle rather than standing pretty at the point — a body in the high slot is worth more than perfect point coverage
Action: Call the arrival, not the goal. "Back door!" the moment they appear, not when the pass is on the way
```

**What it is:** while everyone watches the strong-side battle, an attacker drifts to the far post. One cross-ice pass and it's an open net. This is the chance every coverage system is most afraid of, and the one worth conceding almost anything else to prevent.

**Defending it:**
- **The weak-side defenceman never watches the puck.** Your eyes track the puck, your body stays between the man at the back post and the net — **position, never a hold or a push**, either of which can draw a penalty. If you find yourself facing the corner, you've already lost them.
- **Stick in the seam.** The pass has to cross the slot. A stick lying flat in that lane makes the play impossible even when the passer is open.
- **Weak-side winger sags into the middle** rather than standing pretty at the point. The seam runs through the high slot; a body there is worth more than perfect point coverage.
- **Call the arrival, not the goal.** "Back door!" the moment they appear, not when the pass is on the way.

### The walk-down from the point

```facts
Key: Instead of shooting, the opposing defenceman skates down toward the top of the circle — in a pure zone they cross out of the winger's area and into nobody's
Action: Find out your team's version before you need it — this one is a coaching choice, not a rule of hockey
Convention: Under low zone collapse, follow a few feet, stay connected and call the switch loudly, but do not chase into the corner
Convention: Under man-on-man, you go with them all the way, wherever they go
Convention: Under hybrid, release from zone and pick them up man-on-man as soon as they cross the dots
Priority: When a fourth attacker arrives low, somebody gives up their area and it must not be the goalmouth — usually the centre absorbs them and the weak-side winger takes the high slot
```

**What it is:** instead of shooting, the opposing defenceman skates down from the blue line toward the top of the circle — about **35 feet** from the goal line — or all the way to the hash marks. In a pure zone system, they cross out of the winger's area and into nobody's, and get a clean look from there or an easy feed into the slot.

![](diagram:dz-walk-down-zone)

![](diagram:dz-walk-down-man)

**Defending it — and this is a coaching choice, so know your team's version:**
- **Under low zone collapse:** the standard instruction is that the winger **follows them down a few feet and stays connected, but does not chase into the corner**, and calls the switch loudly so the centre and defencemen know a fourth attacker is arriving low. This is a genuine compromise, and it is the system's weak point.
- **Under man-on-man:** you go with them, all the way, wherever they go. Simple, and the reason the walk-down doesn't trouble man teams.
- **Under hybrid:** you release from zone and pick them up man-on-man as soon as they cross the dots. This is the cleanest answer of the three, and the main reason hybrids exist.
- **Everyone else:** when a fourth attacker arrives low, you are outnumbered down there. Somebody has to give up their area and it must not be the goalmouth. Usually the centre absorbs the extra man and the weak-side winger takes over the high slot entirely.

### Pinned in your own end on a long shift

**What it is:** forty seconds and more in your own end, legs going, and the other team working the puck around the wall. This is when the shape breaks, because tired players start chasing.

**Defending it:**
- **Shrink, don't chase.** Hold your area or your man, keep your stick in the lane and let them work the wall. Chasing costs the most energy and opens the most ice, and under the collapse the house stays full while they cycle.
- **Take the first clean chance to get it out, and get it out where it stays out.** Off the glass and out, or chipped into the neutral zone — not fired the length of the ice. An icing brings the faceoff straight back to your end. Under the NHL, IIHF and PWHL books, and Hockey Canada's in U18AAA, Junior and (where the Member opts in) Senior, your tired line cannot change for it, though an injured player can still be replaced. [Clearing the Zone](#clearing-the-zone) sets out which books do what.
- **Change only once the puck is out and heading the other way.** A change with the puck still loose in your end is one of the commonest ways a team hands the other an odd-man rush. The common approach is that whoever is nearest the bench goes first as the puck leaves; some coaches want it dumped deep before anyone moves, so ask your bench which yours wants.
- **Say it.** "Out — change!" as the puck leaves tells your linemates and the bench at the same moment.

### The net-front scramble

**In short:** Take the most dangerous stick first, stay on your feet, and move the puck with your stick, never a closed hand.

```facts
Priority: Body first, puck second — as the puck arrives, lift the most dangerous stick low on the shaft, well below the bottom hand, or press its blade down; stop at their stick, then let go
Never: Grab their stick, or keep a pin on it after the shot — a grab is at least a holding minor in every book, in CARHA possibly a major that ejects and bans you, and a pin you keep can be called holding
Rule: Holding an opponent's stick so that it injures them is a major plus a game misconduct under Hockey Canada 8.1(b), and a major under CARHA 63(b) that ejects you (30(a))
Never: Lunge at the puck through your goalie — you will knock the puck loose or knock them off their post
Rule: Your stick is legal for moving a loose puck — jab it out low rather than swinging, which NHL 60.1 strips of the accidental-contact defence and 60.3 makes at least a double minor where it injures
Rule: Never freeze the puck under your blade for a whistle — a minor under the NHL (63.2(i)), IIHF (63.2(I)), PWHL (65.2(i)) and Hockey Canada (6.12(b), 10.1(a)); USA Hockey 632(b) and CARHA 74(b) write theirs for a puck held against the boards or goal; CARHA 55(a) adds a bench minor for stalling
Never: Close your hand on the puck, cover it or smother it under your chest — a minor, and a penalty shot in your crease. An open-hand knock-down is no penalty anywhere
Rule: Outside your own crease, dropping to block a shot is no penalty where the puck is shot under you or becomes lodged in your clothing or equipment — what is penalised is using your hands to make it unplayable (NHL 63.2(v) NOTE, IIHF 63.2(V), USA Hockey 614(a))
Rule: Hockey Canada prints no shot-blocker Note of its own but reaches the case further, at Interpretation 6 to 10.1(a): a body lying in the crease with the puck frozen under it is "No Penalty Shot or Minor penalty, provided that the player has made no deliberate attempt to cover the puck"
Rule: You may hand-pass in your own zone (NHL Rule 79.2), and the test is where the puck is, not where the skates are
Never: Hand-pass it out to a breaking winger — once the puck itself has left your zone the whistle goes, wherever your skates are; it is a stoppage and a faceoff, not a penalty
Technique: Stay on your feet as long as possible — a defender on the ice is out of the play for two seconds, which is the whole scramble
Risk: Going down hands-and-head-first at the goalmouth puts them under four sets of sticks swinging at the same puck. Prefer the stick — a coaching caution, not a rule
Rule: NHL and IIHF 61.1 and PWHL 63.1 punish a chop "on or near the opponent's hands" only where it is "not an attempt to play the puck" — USA Hockey 634 and CARHA 80 print no such let-out, and Hockey Canada 9.3 exempts only a tap on the puck-carrier's stick, so under those three a chop there is a slash
```

**What it is:** a rebound, a loose puck at the top of the crease, four bodies, two seconds. Coaches will tell you more goals come from here than from any highlight play. **The rebound half of that is countable, and it is substantial:** **11.4%** of NHL goals come within three seconds of a previous shot on goal by the same team, and **19.7%** within ten. Those figures are derived from the NHL's public play-by-play feed over 400 games of 2024-25 rather than published statistics ([Offensive Zone Play](offensive_zone_play.md) section 10 carries the source). No public tracking breaks out the scramble as its own play, so "more than any highlight play" stays a coaching comparison.

**Defending it:**
- **Body first, puck second.** Take the most dangerous stick away with your own before you look for the puck: as the puck arrives, lift it up, low on the shaft, well below the bottom hand, stop at their stick and let it go. Or press its blade to the ice as the shot comes, and let go once the shot is through. **Never hook it, keep it pinned or grab it with your hand.** A player with a free stick at the goalmouth will score before you get there. **Whatever contact happens, keep their skates on the ice** — a player lifted off their edges cannot choose what meets the post first.
- **Don't lunge at the puck through your goalie.** You'll knock the puck loose or knock them off their post. Trust them to smother it and protect them while they do.
- **Whack it out with your stick — a low jab, not a swing.** Your stick is legal for *moving* the puck; a wild swing at a bouncing puck is a high-sticking risk with no accidental-contact defence.
- **Your hand is a much narrower tool and your chest is not one at all.** Batting a loose puck down or pushing it along the ice with your hand is no penalty anywhere. Closing your hand on it, covering it or smothering it under your body is the offence.
- **Blocking a shot and having the puck die under you is not the offence.** ⚠️ **Reaching for it with your hands to hold it there is** — in your own crease, a penalty shot. The variable is deliberateness, not where you are lying.
- **You may hand-pass in your own zone** — shoving a bouncing puck to a teammate with your hand is legal in your own end. **The test is where the *puck* is, not where the skates are**: a skate over the blue line does not matter while the puck is still in the zone, and once the puck has left it the whistle goes, wherever you stand. That is a stoppage and a faceoff, not a penalty — but don't hand-pass it out to a breaking winger.
- **Stay on your feet as long as possible.** A defender on the ice is out of a scramble that lasts two seconds, and ⚠️ **going down hands-and-head-first at the goalmouth puts your hands and head under four sets of sticks and skates.** Staying up is a coaching caution rather than a rule, and it is the reason to prefer the stick.

**What it costs.**

- **The stick on a stick.** Holding an opponent's stick is at least a holding minor in every book, and in CARHA possibly a major, which ejects you; if it injures, Hockey Canada 8.1(b) and CARHA 63(b) make it a major plus a game misconduct. A pin you keep can be called holding. The IIHF's interference rule, 56.1, also names stopping a player without the puck from receiving a pass, and no IIHF ruling says whether a lift as the pass arrives counts — one more reason to let go at once.
- **The swing.** NHL 60.1, IIHF 60.1 and PWHL 61.1 remove the accidental-contact defence from *"[a] wild swing at a bouncing puck"*, and 60.3 makes it a double minor where injury results. **That is not the top of it.** NHL 60.4 and PWHL 61.4 make it a match where the swing was meant to hurt, and IIHF 60.4 allows a major and automatic game misconduct where it recklessly endangers. USA Hockey calls a stick to the head outside a check high-sticking: a minor that becomes a mandatory major and game misconduct where it recklessly endangers (621(a) and (b); Casebook Rule 621, Situation 1). CARHA 62(b) makes any stick above the shoulders a major, accidental or not, with a game misconduct if deliberate or anyone is hurt. Hockey Canada sends a violent one, and in minor and female hockey any that injures, to a major and game misconduct (7.6(b) via 6.9(c); Junior and Senior 7.7(b) via 9.5(b)). USA Hockey 621(c), CARHA 62(c) and Hockey Canada 7.6(c) and 7.7(c) reach a match — and under USA Hockey plan on one, and a likely multi-game suspension, for a reckless or deliberate swing (602(a)).
- **Freezing it.** Freezing the puck under the blade to force a whistle is its own minor under the NHL (63.2(i)), the IIHF (63.2(I)), the PWHL (65.2(i)) and Hockey Canada (6.12(b), 10.1(a)); USA Hockey (632(b)) and CARHA (74(b)) write theirs for a puck held against the boards or the goal, though CARHA 55(a) also makes deliberate stalling a bench minor. Batting it down by hand is free: NHL and IIHF **79.1** and PWHL **81.1** permit it with no zone limit, and USA Hockey **618(b)** and Hockey Canada **10.2(a)(i)** restrict the hand *pass* rather than the bat. Closing your hand on it is priced in the next section.
- **The shot-blocker's carve-out.** Outside the crease, **deliberately** falling on the puck is a minor (NHL 63.2(v)). The rule prints the exception: *"Any player who drops to his knees to block a shot should not be penalized if the puck is shot under him or becomes lodged in his clothing or equipment but any use of the hands to make the puck unplayable should be penalized promptly."* IIHF 63.2(V) and USA Hockey 614(a) print the same Note. ⚠️ **Hockey Canada reaches the crease version under Delay of Game:** Interpretation 6 to Rule 10.1(a) has the goalie pulled, a skater lying in the crease and the puck shot under them and frozen. It rules *"No Penalty Shot or Minor penalty, provided that the player has made no deliberate attempt to cover the puck."* USA Hockey's Casebook rules the same (Rule 614, Situation 2): *"No penalty shall be assessed"* where *"the puck simply ends up beneath them"*, but *"if the player makes any action to cover the puck by gathering it into their body, the penalty shot or awarded goal situation would apply."*
- **The hand pass.** NHL **79.2**: *"The location of the puck when contacted by either the player making the hand pass or the player receiving the hand pass shall determine the zone it is in."* Hockey Canada's **10.2(a)** says in terms that *"Play will be stopped but a Minor penalty will NOT be assessed for a player who commits a 'hand pass' violation"*, and USA Hockey **618(b)** that *"a stoppage of play will occur and a face-off will take place according to last play face-off rules"*.
- **A chop on your hands when you are down.** Under the NHL, IIHF and PWHL little protects you. Their 61.1 (the PWHL's 63.1) penalises a chop *"on or near the opponent's hands"* only where it *"in the judgment of the Referee, is not an attempt to play the puck"*, and a stick coming down on a loose puck usually is one. ⚠️ **Do not carry that across to the other three books, in either direction.** USA Hockey 634, Hockey Canada 9.3 and CARHA 80(a) print no such let-out (Hockey Canada's spares only a tap on the puck-carrier's stick, not a hand), so there a forceful chop that lands on a hand in a scramble is slashing whether or not it was going for the puck. It is not capped at a minor. USA Hockey 634(b) is a major plus a game misconduct for reckless endangerment, with a match and a likely multi-game suspension to plan on (602(a)). Hockey Canada 9.3(b) reaches the same pair on the violence alone, and CARHA 80(a) allows a major, which ejects you there (30(a)). That is some protection for your hands and a real penalty risk for your own stick.

### Handling a loose puck in your own crease

**In short:** Knock it down and play it with your stick; only your goalie may smother a puck in your crease.

```facts
Rule: The NHL and IIHF (67.2), PWHL (69.2), Hockey Canada (10.2(a)(iii)) and CARHA (61(a)) print the permission, the last two only where you gain no advantage. USA Hockey's playing rules print none; 618(a) leaves the catch-and-drop unpenalised outside the crease, and its Casebook says so in terms
Never: Hold a puck you have caught, carry it, turn with it or throw it — that is the act the books reach: a minor in five books, a USA Hockey stoppage outside the crease, the penalty shot in your own crease
Rule: What buys the penalty is what you do after the catch, not how high it was — holding it, taking a stride, turning to avoid a check, or throwing it (NHL and IIHF 67.2(I), PWHL 69.2(i); Hockey Canada Interpretation 4 to 10.2(a)(iii))
Rule: Under USA Hockey a caught puck not dropped at once outside the crease is a stoppage, not a penalty (618(a)) — but covering a loose puck with your hand there is a minor (614(a) Note; Casebook 614 Sit. 1), and in the crease the penalty shot or optional minor (614(b))
Rule: USA Hockey's minor under 618(a) is a different act — picking the puck up off the ice with the hand while play is in progress
Rule: A skater other than the goalie who covers a puck that is itself inside their own crease gives up a penalty shot — an awarded goal if your own net is empty (NHL 67.4 and 67.5; the IIHF's 67.4 is a bare pointer, so its penalty shot is 63.6 and its awarded goal 67.5)
Rule: Never cover the puck in your own crease with your goalie pulled — an awarded goal on the bare fact under NHL and IIHF 67.5, PWHL 69.5, Hockey Canada 10.2(a)(vi) and CARHA 58(c) Note 1; USA Hockey 614(b) and 618(a), and CARHA's own 36(b), add an obvious-and-imminent-goal test
Rule: Every book read here awards the crease penalty shot when the puck is in the crease — NHL and IIHF 63.6, PWHL 65.6, USA Hockey 614(b) and 618(a), CARHA 58(c), Hockey Canada 4.11(a)(v), which its 10.2(a)(v) cross-refers to; USA Hockey alone lets the other side — not you — elect a minor (406(a))
Rule: Puck outside the paint, you inside: a minor, not the shot, under NHL and IIHF 63.6, PWHL 65.6, USA Hockey's Casebook (614 Sit. 1) and CARHA 58(a)/(c); Hockey Canada 10.2(a)(v)-(vi) also go by where you stand: price the shot, or the goal with your net empty
Rule: Catching the puck is free; holding it is not. Height only sets the price — the crease runs four feet up to the top of the goal frame (NHL 1.7, 2.1), so above the crossbar is a minor and knee high in the paint the penalty shot (IIHF Situations 63.27 v 63.15, 67.4, worked applications, not rule text)
Never: Reach higher to hold a puck above the crossbar — once you are holding it, height only selects which penalty you pay, not whether you pay one; knocking it straight down is free at any height
```

**In a scramble, a puck in the air at your own crease gets knocked straight down and played with your stick.** Never close your hand on it, skate with it, turn away with it or fall on it — smothering it is your goalie's job. The catch itself is free; what comes after it is not, and with the puck inside your own crease the price is a penalty shot.

**What costs you is what you do next, not how high the puck was.** Holding it, taking a stride with it, turning to avoid a check or throwing it is what the books reach. Height only changes which penalty you pay — never whether you pay one — and reaching higher in a scramble puts your hands four feet up with sticks coming down. Knock it down and play it.

**Moving with it does not cure it.** The permission is to put the puck down immediately, not to carry it somewhere better first. Your goalie may smother it in the crease; you may not — and even the goalie can take a delay-of-game minor for freezing a puck they did not need to (see [Goaltender](../positions/goaltender.md)).

**What it costs.**

- **The permission to catch.** NHL **67.2**: *"A player shall be permitted to catch the puck out of the air but must immediately place it or knock it down to the ice."* The IIHF's 67.2 and the PWHL's 69.2 carry the same sentence, and Hockey Canada's **10.2(a)(iii)** and **CARHA 61(a)** exclude the honest catch from their hand-closing minors — a player who closes a hand on the puck and *"immediately drops it to the ice"* without gaining an advantage. **USA Hockey's playing rules print no such sentence; its Casebook does** (Rule 618, Situation 1): *"[t]he motion of catching, then dropping the puck, must be virtually one continuous motion"*. In your own crease (Rule 614, Situation 3): *"if the puck is caught and dropped to the ice immediately, no penalty shall be assessed"*. So under USA Hockey the permission is a casebook interpretation rather than rule text.
- **What comes after the catch.** NHL and IIHF 67.2(I) and PWHL 69.2(i) reach the player who *"catches it and skates with it, either to avoid a check or to gain a territorial advantage"*. Hockey Canada's Interpretation 4 to 10.2(a)(iii) lists three acts, none of them a height: throwing the puck after closing your hand on it, trying *"to maneuver around another player while holding the puck"*, and *"tak[ing] a stride while holding the puck in their hand."*
- **Outside the crease, under USA Hockey.** A caught puck not dropped at once is a stoppage, not a penalty — *"Play shall be stopped and a last play face-off shall occur"* (618(a)), whose minor is for a player who *"picks the puck up from the ice with their hand(s) while play is in progress."* ⚠️ **Covering a loose puck on the ice with your hand out there is a minor**: 614(a)'s Note says *"the use of hands to make the puck unplayable should be penalized promptly"*. Casebook Rule 614, Situation 1 gives a minor to a player who *"slides and covers the puck with their hand"*. In the crease, a hand that closes and does not open is holding the puck — the penalty shot or optional minor under 618(a)'s third paragraph and 614(b).
- **Covering it in your own crease.** If a skater other than the goalie *"falls on the puck, holds the puck, picks up the puck, or gathers the puck into his body or hands from the ice in the goal crease area"*, the other team gets a **penalty shot** (NHL **67.4**). ⚠️ **With your own goalie pulled, the same act is an awarded goal** (NHL 67.5), on the bare fact in five of the six books: the PWHL's **69.5**, the IIHF's **67.5**, Hockey Canada's **10.2(a)(vi)** and CARHA's **58(c)** Note 1 — *"If the goalkeeper has been removed from the ice, then a goal is awarded"*. USA Hockey conditions it on the act having *"prevented an obvious and imminent goal"* (614(b); 618(a) says the same of the hand). CARHA's own 36(b), which Note 1 points to, carries that test too, so under CARHA play to 58(c): with your net empty, any cover in your crease is the goal.
- **Puck in the crease or you in the crease.** NHL 63.6: *"If the puck is in the crease, penalty shot. If the puck is outside the crease and gathered into the body of a player (other than the goalkeeper) who is inside the crease, minor penalty."* **Every book read here gives the shot when the puck is in the crease** — Hockey Canada in 4.11(a)(v). The converse minor is rule text in the NHL, the IIHF (63.6, near-identical wording) and the PWHL (65.6, word for word). USA Hockey reaches the same outcome in its Casebook (Rule 614, Situation 1), on close but not identical facts, and CARHA conditions its shot on the puck being *"within their goal crease"* (58(c)), with the plain minor at 58(a). USA Hockey 614(b) judges *"at the moment the infraction occurs"*, and only USA Hockey lets the other side elect a minor instead of the shot (406(a)) — **their election, not yours**, so never a discount.
- **Under Hockey Canada, price the shot whenever you are in the paint.** Its **10.2(a)(v)** gives the shot *"when the infraction occurs inside the offending team's goal crease"*, words that could mean you or the puck, and 10.2(a)(vi) uses the same words for the goal. Its list at **4.11(a)(v)** says *"while the puck is in the goal crease"*, but a list does not cancel the rule's own wording, and **Interpretation 5** has the player outside the crease as well as the puck, so it does not settle you in the paint with the puck outside. **No Hockey Canada casebook is held here**, so price the shot, or the goal with your net empty. USA Hockey's Casebook answers its own version of the question your way — *"[t]he location of the puck at the instant it is covered or held is the determining factor"* (614, Situation 1), and batting a puck out of the crease into your body is *"only a minor penalty"* (Situation 4). But its player covers outside and slides in, so the exact NHL permutation is still unwritten there.
- **Height.** All six books define the crease upward to the top of the goal frame — NHL 1.7, *"extending vertically four feet (4') to the level of the top of the goal frame"*, and IIHF 1.7, Hockey Canada 1.5(c), the Note to USA Hockey 618(a), PWHL 1.7 and CARHA 5(c) alike. The IIHF Situation Handbook rules both ways: in **Situation 63.27** a puck caught *"(above the crossbar)"* and thrown *"was not considered to be in the crease"*, a minor; in **Situations 63.15 and 67.4** a puck caught **knee high**, dropped and shot out is a penalty shot. In both, the player turned to avoid opponents — the conduct 67.2(I) reaches — so height changed only whether the minor became the shot. The Handbook ranks itself below the Rulebook, so read these as worked applications, not rule text.
- **Moving it.** In IIHF Situation 67.6 a defender with their own net empty grabs a puck *"out of mid-air (around the height of the waist)"* and drops it **outside** the crease; the ruling is *"an Awarded Goal to Team B."*
- **Your goalie.** The goalie's exemption is from the penalty shot only: in the crease only an opponent actually checking them excuses freezing it, and outside it an attacker bearing down does not (Rules 63.2(vii), 67.3(ii); IIHF Situation 63.1). [Goaltender](../positions/goaltender.md) states plainly that how those two interact is a reading, because the NHL publishes no interpretation of it.

## When the System Breaks

**In short:** When structure goes, get bodies to the net front and sticks in lanes, find the dangerous unmarked man, then count.

```facts
Priority: Bodies to the net front immediately — not to the puck
Technique: If you can't reach anyone, lie your stick flat in the passing lane through the slot
Read: Find the most dangerous unmarked player — scan the house, not the puck; in a scramble it is almost never the person holding it
Action: Count — outnumbered low, somebody high comes down and says so; even, match up loudly: "I've got 19", "you take the net"
Action: Get the puck out any way you can that keeps it in the rink — off the glass, never over it; a clear that concedes possession has still bought your team a change
Action: Reset before you attack — the commonest second goal is the one conceded straight after surviving the first
Key: The moment you realise you don't know your assignment, go to the middle — lost there, you cost your team less than lost anywhere else
```

Every system document describes an orderly world. A large share of your defensive-zone time will not look like that — often enough that it deserves its own plan: a broken play, a bad change, a puck ricocheting off shin pads, three of you below the goal line and nobody where the diagram says. *(How large a share is not something anybody has published; the "roughly a third" figure you sometimes hear is not sourced and is not used here.)* Systems don't cover this state, so players freeze, and freezing is what turns a scramble into a goal.

Here is the priority order when structure is gone. Work down it and don't skip.

**1. Bodies to the net front, immediately.**
Not to the puck — to the front of the net. The most dangerous thing on the ice during a scramble is an unmarked player at the goalmouth, and the second most dangerous is nobody standing there when the puck squirts out. If you are the closest player and unsure what to do, go and stand between your goalie and the nearest opponent.

**2. Sticks in lanes.**
If you can't reach anyone, lie your stick flat in the passing lane through the slot. Broken plays are decided by passes that shouldn't have got through. A stick on the ice is defence you can perform while out of position.

**3. Find the most dangerous unmarked player.**
Scan the house, not the puck. Ask "who could score in the next two seconds?" and go and stand on them. In a scramble the answer is almost never the person with the puck — it's the person who's about to receive it.

**4. Count.**
Are you outnumbered low? Then somebody high has to come down, and the person who does should say so. Are you even? Then match up, loudly: "I've got 19", "you take the net". A scramble ends the instant five people have re-agreed who has whom.

**5. Get the puck out any way you can that keeps it in the rink.**
Once you touch it, get it out. Off the glass but never over it — from your own zone that is two minutes under the NHL, IIHF and PWHL books even by accident — around the boards, into the neutral zone, off a shin pad. Nobody is grading you. A clear that concedes possession has still bought your team a change and reset the coverage — which is the whole point.

**6. Reset before you attack.**
When it's over, take the extra second to re-form. The most common second goal is the one conceded immediately after surviving the first, because everyone relaxed while three attackers were still in the zone.

**The habit to build:** the moment you realise you don't know your assignment, **go to the middle** — lost there, you cost your team less than lost anywhere else.

## Clearing the Zone

Winning the puck is only half the job. The handoff from coverage into a breakout is where good defensive shifts become good shifts. Full detail in [Breakouts](breakouts.md); this is the transition itself.

### Getting the puck out

**In short:** Decide before the puck reaches you, and never move it across the front of your own goal.

```facts
Action: Decide before you touch it — scan while the battle is still going, so you already know whether it's the winger, the centre or the glass
Never: Move the puck across the front of your own goal on the way out, with a pass or with your feet. Behind your own net is the route across, where an interception costs you almost nothing
Never: Turn your back to anyone coming at you, or duck, going back for a puck in your corner — keep your head up and your chin off your chest
Priority: Controlled exit whenever it is genuinely available, then a rim or a chip off the glass, then anything that ends the shift
Convention: Under low zone collapse the pass goes to the winger coming down the wall to between the hash marks and the goal line — not to the blue line
Never: Cheat up the ice — the puck should leave the zone before your third forward does, unless your team's breakout asks a forward to leave early — a centre fly, a slash or a stretch
```

**Decide before you touch it.** A defenceman receiving the puck and *then* starting to look is how a zone exit gets turned over, and coaches put it at the head of that list — coaching consensus rather than a counted ranking. Scan while the battle is still going — see [Scanning and Anticipation](../hockey-iq/scanning_and_anticipation.md) — so that when the puck is yours you already know whether it's the winger, the centre, or the glass.

**And one route is closed whatever you decide: not across the front of your own goal.** Neither a pass nor your own feet go through the slot with the puck. If the play has to change sides, it goes behind your own net, where an interception costs you almost nothing — through the slot, the same interception is a point-blank chance against. Some teams exchange above the tops of the circles rather than behind the net, so ask which route yours wants; the bar on the slot route is the fixed half of it. [Breakouts](breakouts.md) owns the cross-ice "over" and why it is the riskiest of the nine breakout options, and [Defender](../positions/defender.md) states the same instruction for the pairing.

**And on this play you are the one at the wall with your head over the puck, so the receiving posture is yours — in every league, checking or not.** Unexpected contact happens in non-check hockey too, and the boards are just as hard. ⚠️ **Never turn your back to anyone coming at you, and never duck.** Get your skates parallel to the wall instead, forearm and hip to it, head up and chin off your chest — **never the tip of your shoulder**, and no part of the shoulder if you can help it. If you are already square to the wall and cannot turn in time, get your stick and gloves up and spread on the glass.

**The turned back and the tucked chin are two prohibitions against two different injuries.** A turned back converts the contact into a hit from behind, which you can neither see nor brace for; USA Hockey warns the player delivering one that *"Even a light hit from behind could inflict a severe head or neck injury."* A chin tucked to the chest straightens the cervical spine, and USA Hockey records that the injury from a head-down, head-first collision with the boards or a goal post can occur at walking speed. Its *Introduction To Body Contact* gives the shoulder rule — *"Never hit the boards or glass with the tip of your shoulder – it can cause injury"* — and, for a player checked while skating parallel to the boards, the broader *"[a]void hitting the boards with the shoulder"*. [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md), §6, covers the full three-case rule, and [Breakouts](breakouts.md) carries it for the reverse and the rim.

**The three outcomes, in descending order of value:**

1. **Controlled exit** — a pass to a winger on the wall or the centre swinging low, and out with possession. Under low zone collapse your wingers are high on the points *while the other team has the puck*, and **they come down the wall to between the hash marks and the goal line as you win it** — that is where the pass goes, not the blue line. Being high is what makes the trip short; it is not the receiving position. Take this whenever it's genuinely available. See [Breakouts](breakouts.md).
2. **Uncontrolled but safe exit** — **rim** it around the boards, chip it off the glass, or bank it out. A *rim* is a hard puck sent **onward** around the boards, following the curve of the rink to the next teammate along; it is not a **reverse**, which sends the puck **back** against the flow into the space a forechecker has just vacated. Either way there is no possession, no chance against, and your team gets to change. This is a good play, not a failure.
3. **Anything that ends the shift.** When it's bad, it's bad. Get it out.

**Structure first, speed second.** It is tempting to leave the zone the instant the puck turns over, and forwards cheating up the ice is how a cleared zone becomes an odd-man rush the other way. Exit as a group. The puck should leave the zone before your third forward does — **unless your team's breakout asks a forward to leave early — a centre fly, a slash or a stretch**, so find out which your team plays before you treat an early exit as an error.

### Rules that constrain the clear

**In short:** Clear it off the glass, never straight over it, or short enough to die in the neutral zone.


**Use the glass and keep the puck in the rink.** Off the glass and out is good, and so is a puck that dies in the neutral zone. Never flip one out deliberately, and from your own zone never put it straight over the glass. Aim clears wide and high off the glass, or short enough to die in the neutral zone — a long flat clear risks an icing, and under some books your tired line then cannot change. Shorthanded, where your league allows it, ice it freely while you are still below their strength. And know whether your goalie may fetch a puck in the corner, because that decides who retrieves it.

**What it costs.**

- **Over the glass, under the NHL.** Under NHL 63.2(iii), shooting or batting the puck **directly out of the playing surface from your own defending zone**, with no deflection, is a two-minute minor for delay of game, whether or not you meant it. It has three narrow carve-outs: *"except where there is no glass"*, and the NOTE's *"[w]hen the puck is shot into the players' bench, the penalty will not apply"* and *"[w]hen the puck goes out of the playing area directly off a face-off, no penalty shall be assessed"*. Over the glass **behind** the bench is still a penalty. Clause **63.2(ii)** is wider: a **deliberate** one *"from anywhere on the ice surface"* takes the same minor, so from the neutral zone you are safe only if it was an accident. Straight off a draw is unsettled — Rule 85.1 says no penalty, but no book reconciles that with 63.2(ii) — so never aim it out.
- **Over the glass, under the IIHF and PWHL.** ⚠️ **The IIHF writes the defending-zone rule too, at 63.2(III), which makes it a British player's rule**, with the same carve-outs; so does the PWHL, at 65.2(iii). The IIHF's runs against you: the NHL penalises a puck *"shot over the glass 'behind' the players' bench"*, the IIHF one over the glass *"surrounding"* it — a wider word, which appears to reach the sides of the bench as well. And the bench is not a target: IIHF Situation Handbook **63.24** still calls the minor on a clear that goes over the glass and lands in the bench, because *"the determining factor… is where the puck leaves the playing surface"*. The Handbook ranks itself below the rulebook, so treat that as how it is officiated rather than as the rule.
- **Over the glass, under USA Hockey, Hockey Canada and CARHA.** They write no defending-zone limb. Their minors — USA Hockey 610(c), Hockey Canada 10.1(ii), CARHA 75(b) — are for a *deliberate* one, and USA Hockey's 631(d) makes the accidental one a last-play faceoff in your zone; Hockey Canada's 10.1(v) and CARHA's 55(a) drop the deliberateness word only for the goalie. So find out which book your league runs before you decide how safe a high clear is.
- **Icing at even strength.** A hard flat clear that reaches their goal line untouched brings the puck straight back for a defensive-zone faceoff, and **under NHL, IIHF and PWHL rules your line cannot change** (PWHL 83.4) and cannot take a timeout. The bar has narrow exceptions — NHL 81.4 permits a change *"to replace a goalkeeper who had been substituted for an extra attacker, to replace an injured player, or when a penalty has been assessed which affects the on-ice strength of either team"*. IIHF 81.4 adds *"a Player with a broken skate"*, but ⚠️ **do not plan a change around it:** the IIHF's own 82.1 lists only the NHL's three, neither says which governs, and an attempted change that is not allowed earns a warning and then a bench minor (IIHF 82.2(V), NHL 82.2(v)).
- **Icing and an injured teammate.** **Injury is in every list, in all three books: you never have to leave a hurt teammate on the ice to serve your own icing.** IIHF Situation 81.7 reads the bar more narrowly than either rule, though the Handbook ranks below the rulebook — so make the change for the injured player rather than negotiating. ⚠️ **If they are down and not moving, leave them where they lie and their helmet on** — don't move them except to keep their airway clear — and get the first aider or medical cover to them; [Team Play and Culture](../off-the-ice/team_play_and_culture.md) has the rest.
- **Icing under Hockey Canada.** ⚠️ **The no-change rule applies only in U18AAA, Junior and, at the Member's option, Senior (6.7(d))** — below that, icing costs you the faceoff and nothing else. Where it does apply, a change is allowed *"[w]hen a penalty or penalties have been assessed to either team"*, wider than the NHL's limb. You may take your timeout but not change during it (6.7(d)(iii)), where NHL and IIHF 87.1 and PWHL 89.1 all refuse it: the NHL's *"No time-out shall be granted to the defensive team following an icing"*.
- **Shorthanded icing.** A penalty-killing team may ice the puck with no whistle (Rule 81.6), subject to three limits, all owned by [Special Teams](special_teams.md). **Two vary by book.** USA Hockey gives the exemption only at Youth 15-Only and above, Girls 16U and above, High School and Adult (624(b)(1)). The NHL and the PWHL (83.6) withdraw it once a major **expires** with nobody in the penalty box to come out: the team plays a player short until the next stoppage, *"not officially considered short-handed"* (20.3). ⚠️ **The NHL and IIHF (68.2) and the PWHL (70.2) also make that empty box a bench minor** — and the IIHF requires the substitute *"immediately"* (68.2, and 8.1 for an injured player), so the two minutes can land on a kill already down a skater and make it a 5-on-3.
- **The head count.** **The third limit is in all six books:** you are exempt while you are below their on-ice strength. A lone minor expiring puts you level and the exemption goes with it; on a 5-on-3 the first expiry leaves you 4-on-5, still exempt. Shoot while you are still below them and it is not icing, however late the puck lands. What the books do *not* share is the instant they judge, and [Special Teams](special_teams.md) sets out which times it to what.
- **The trapezoid, under the NHL and the IIHF.** Under NHL 27.8 and 63.2(viii) and IIHF 27.7 and 63.2(VIII), your goalie may play the puck behind the goal line only inside the **trapezoid**, on a two-minute minor for delay of game, unless a skate stays in contact with the crease. Retrieval outside it is on the defencemen. The KHL is commonly said to have one too; that is unverified here, with no KHL rulebook to check. The IIHF's own 2021 announcement dates its adoption to the 2021-22 season; the NHL's commonly given 2005-06 date is in no rulebook held here.
- **No trapezoid elsewhere.** USA Hockey, Hockey Canada and CARHA write none — *"trapezoid"*, *"restricted area"* and *"designated area"* appear in none of them, nor in USA Hockey's Casebook, save one Hockey Canada use about where the Goal Judge stands (5.4(b)) — so there your goalie is a genuine third puck-handler behind the net. **A PWHL goalie should play as if it binds:** its 1.8 and 27.8 switch the trapezoid off, but its 65.2(viii) still writes the minor for a goalie *"who plays the puck outside of the designated area behind the net"*. How much rec, beer-league and youth hockey that covers is a question about leagues, and this document has no count for it.
- **No trapezoid is not no restriction.** ⚠️ **A goalie who freezes the puck out there takes a minor**, so behind the net the goalie plays it rather than sits on it. USA Hockey **614(c)(1)** and CARHA **58(b)** price a freeze behind the goal line with the goalie's body out of the crease. Hockey Canada's clause v of Interpretation 3 to 10.1(a) is blunter — *"When a goaltender leaves their crease, they will not be allowed to freeze the puck. If they do, they will be assessed a Minor penalty. No warning will be issued."* — and its Rule 10.2 makes falling on the puck outside the crease a minor too. Clause ii of the same Interpretation lets the goalie come out to cut down the angle and, *"after stopping the shot, cover the puck"*; Hockey Canada never says which governs, and no Hockey Canada casebook is held here. But a cut-down-the-angle save is made out in front, not behind the net. For the skaters it is the same either way: a loose puck out there has to be moved, not smothered. See [Goaltender](../positions/goaltender.md).
- **🇬🇧 Under the IIHF book, play as though it binds.** The IIHF book carries it at 27.7, and nothing in the Elite League's Casebook switches it off. England Ice Hockey's *Rules & Regulations* 22.3 say it is *"not currently enforced"* (its scope and standing are unsettled), and the IHUK In-House Rules do not mention it, though their Introduction says *"[u]nless otherwise stated, all rules will be enforced in line with the IIHF Rule Book"*. Assume it binds, ask your league, and remember that IIHF 27.7 turns on *"the position of the puck"*, not on whether the lines are painted.

## Common Mistakes

- **Not knowing which system you're playing.** The root cause of most of what follows. Ask on day one.
- **Both defencemen drawn to the same puck with neither on the goalmouth.** One pass beats five players. Somebody must have taken the goalmouth before you both go — even under a strong-side overload that sends both of you low on purpose.
- **Moving the puck across the front of your own goal** — D-to-D through the slot, or skating it across yourself. Go behind your own net, or above the circles if that is your team's route — never through the slot.
- **Receiving the puck and only then starting to look.** Scan while the battle is on, so that when it is yours you already know: winger, centre or glass.
- **Finishing a corner pressure into a back.** **Take the puck, take the ice, or angle them off it.** If you can see their numbers, pull up — the second body in most of all, because it arrives late and unseen. ⚠️ **No book here prices a hit to the back that stays off the head at two minutes alone as checking from behind, and in most of them it ends your game.** Into the boards or goal frame when they cannot protect themselves, Hockey Canada and CARHA must make it a match. Under USA Hockey a forceful one on a player at the boards is a major and game misconduct or a match, and if it is reckless, plan on the match and a likely multi-game suspension (602(a)). [Body Contact and Battles → Checking from behind](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md#checking-from-behind-and-what-the-onus-does-and-does-not-mean) has every tier.
- **Wingers drifting into the corner in a collapse system.** Stay up on your point and let the corner be a two-player battle. Go down and you abandon the point *and* your best breakout outlet.
- **The weak-side winger failing to collapse** when the centre goes low — or both wingers sagging at once. Under the collapse only the weak-side winger sags, and climbs back as the puck leaves the corner; under man-on-man the wingers follow their checks instead.
- **Chasing the puck instead of covering the pass.** Hold your area — or your man, in a man system — with your stick flat in the passing lane.
- **Losing the man at the net front while watching the puck.** Eyes on the puck, body goal side of the man. Turn square to the puck and they score from six feet.
- **Walking the screener out of the lane.** **Take the position and hold it, and as the puck arrives lift their stick low on the shaft, then let go.** ⚠️ **Driving them off their spot is interference wherever you play, and where checking is barred an illegal check as well — above two minutes once it endangers them, and a mandatory major and game misconduct under Hockey Canada if they are hurt.** Legal checking does not make the walk-out legal. Under IIHF women's rules hold only ice you reached first, and under USA Hockey check the game, not the age group. The ladders are under [Dealing with the screen](#dealing-with-the-screen).
- **Shoving a net-front attacker into your own goalie to get the goal disallowed.** **Hold your position, never push** — the goal generally still counts, and the shove can be called on you. ⚠️ **The attacker lands backwards on a goalie who is down and cannot see it coming, with the post beside them** — coaching caution, since no source here counts injuries from it.
- **Cross-checking the net-front player.** **Lift their stick instead — up rather than sideways, low on the shaft, well below the bottom hand, then let go, stopping at their stick rather than carrying on into their head.** ⚠️ **The cross-check has no two-minute ceiling:** in the NHL a major on severity alone brings an automatic game misconduct, and under Hockey Canada and CARHA one above the shoulders is a major and game misconduct whether or not anyone is hurt. The lift is not risk-free at the hands, but taken low it is much the cheaper.
- **Blocking a shot from a screening position**, deflecting a puck your goalie never saw. Get out of the sightline, or take the inside on your man without pushing them, and let the puck through.
- **Blocking a point shot with your head in the shot lane.** Shin pads to the puck, head up and out of it — never lift a leg, turn your back or sideways, or lunge. Stay on your feet until the puck is released.
- **Following your check into harmless ice** in a man system while their teammate walks into the slot you left. Pick up the slot threat instead — and learn your team's rule for releasing a check.
- **Freezing when the structure breaks** instead of defaulting to the house. Get a body to the net front and a stick in a lane, and say out loud who you have.
- **Holding a loose puck you have caught.** Knock it straight down and play it with your stick. ⚠️ **Hold it while the puck is inside your own crease and it is a penalty shot — with your own net empty, in most books an awarded goal.** Under Hockey Canada standing in the paint can cost you the same. Smothering it is your goalie's job alone, and height only changes which penalty you pay, so never reach higher for it. The detail is in [Handling a loose puck in your own crease](#handling-a-loose-puck-in-your-own-crease).
- **Taking the contact at the wall with your back to whoever is coming at you — or ducking as it arrives.** **Keep your head up and your chin off your chest.** ⚠️ **A turned back turns the contact into a hit from behind; a tucked chin straightens the cervical spine**, and USA Hockey records that a head-first collision with the boards or a goal post can injure it at walking speed. [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md), §6, owns the rule.
- **Cheating up ice before the puck is out.** Leave when the puck leaves — unless your team's breakout sends a forward early (a centre fly, a slash or a stretch), so find out which yours plays.
- **Silent defending.** Name your man out loud, and early — what you are about to do, not what you can see.

## Key Takeaways

1. **Find out which system your team plays before you need it.** Ask: when the puck is in our corner, does the winger stay on the point or come down? Under the collapse the wingers stay high; under man-on-man they follow their check down; a hybrid marks man-on-man below the dots and looks like man coverage from the stands.
2. **Under the collapse, with the puck in your strong-side corner:** the strong-side defenceman goes to the puck, the weak-side defenceman owns the goalmouth, the centre comes down as the second body, and both wingers hold their points. When the centre goes low, only the weak-side winger drops into the high slot, and climbs back as the puck leaves the corner.
3. **Whatever the system, defend the middle, the goal side and the next pass.** Take away the pass into the slot before the shot from distance, stay between your man or area and your own net, make sure somebody has the goalmouth, and lay your stick flat in the next lane rather than chasing the puck.
4. **Pressuring a player digging at your boards, play the puck or the ice — never their back.** See their numbers? Pull up and angle them off it, above all as the second body in. ⚠️ **No book here prices a hit to the back that stays off the head at two minutes alone as checking from behind, and in most it ends your game.**
5. **At the net front, get there first and take their stick, never their back.** Inside shoulder ahead, no lean, no push. As the puck arrives, lift their stick low on the shaft, up not sideways, then let go — never on into their head; never hook, pin or grab it. ⚠️ **No book here caps a cross-check at two minutes.**
6. **Beat a screen by position — the referee will not move the screener.** ⚠️ **Never walk them off their spot:** that can be interference anywhere, and an illegal check where checking is barred. **Never shove them into your goalie:** the goal usually stands, you can be penalised, and they land on a goalie who cannot see them (coaching caution).
7. **When their defenceman walks down from the point, play your team's answer — this is the collapse's real weakness.** Under the collapse, follow a few feet, stay connected and call it; under man-on-man, go with them all the way; under a hybrid, pick them up as they cross the dots. Never give up the goalmouth to cover them.
8. **Under man-on-man, switch on a crossing pair only when the trade is clean and called out loud; otherwise stay with your man.** Do not follow them into harmless ice while their teammate walks into the slot you left. When to switch or release is your team's rule, not a law of hockey, so ask.
9. **When structure is gone, go to the net front, not the puck.** Bodies to the goalmouth, sticks in lanes, find the most dangerous unmarked player — usually the one about to receive it — and say out loud who you have. Get the puck out any way that keeps it in the rink, then reset. Lost? Go to the middle.
10. **When you win the puck, never move it across the front of your own goal; decide where it goes before it is yours.** ⚠️ **Going back at the wall, never turn your back to anyone coming at you and never duck** — head up, chin off your chest.
11. **When you clear it, use the glass and keep it in the rink.** Never flip one out deliberately — that is a minor in every book. ⚠️ **Under the NHL, IIHF and PWHL books, a puck shot directly out of play from your own zone is two minutes even by accident** — and British rulebooks leave the IIHF's unchanged.
12. **Knock a loose puck at your crease down and jab it clear low with your stick; never swing.** Smothering it is your goalie's job. ⚠️ **Hold it with the puck inside your crease and it is a penalty shot — with your net empty, in most books an awarded goal.** Under Hockey Canada, standing in the paint costs the same.
