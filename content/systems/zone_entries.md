## Key focus

**Carry the puck in when you can.** In tracked NHL games a carry was worth about twice a dump-and-chase in shots, and a failed carry cost far less than it feels. Dumping and changing was the worst play of all.

**Attack the space between two defenders, not one of them — head up, because both can stand you up there.** If you get there alone, turn away rather than force it: protect the puck, look back up the ice and come again with a teammate — curling in open ice, not against the wall.

**Carry if you have the line or the support; chip it past a defender who is committing; delay if support is a second away.** Dump only to a place a teammate is already skating to.

**Be the last of your three forwards to the blue line.** Your wingers get there first, at speed and onside, so the defence has to respect three threats. **Be slower thirty feet out so you are fastest as the puck crosses.**

**Use the onside rule.** Under the NHL, the IIHF and the PWHL you stay onside until both skates have completely crossed the line at the instant the puck does, so your body can already be over it. Keep the trailing blade touching the line.

**Never forecheck during a delayed offside — hold at the line, tag up together, and wait for the arm to drop.** The defenceman retrieving has their back to you, so hitting them is checking from behind, which gets you thrown out in most books.

**Chase a dump on an angle, not straight at the puck.** Take away the retriever's easiest escape — usually up the boards or a reverse behind the net — and arrive on the puck, not the player: never on their back, never on anyone who cannot see you coming, never on their goalie. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call, and if you can see their numbers, pull up.

**Without the puck, give the carrier a threat and an option.** One forward drives the net as the puck crosses, to back the defence off, and stops at the edge of the paint, head up; one stays level for a pass; nobody stands still at the line.

## Overview

A **zone entry** is your team bringing the puck across the opponent's blue line into their end. Coaches often say attacks are won or lost there. That is a commonplace, but the entry itself has also been measured, and the measurement is why this document leans the way it does.

**Carrying wins because control produces shots.** Across 330 hand-tracked NHL games, a carry-in produced roughly twice the shot attempts of a dump-in, and other trackers, eras and leagues show the same two-to-one shape. The numbers are over a decade old and from the NHL, so take the direction, not the decimals.

**Trying is cheaper than it feels.** A failed carry costs a little going the other way, but less than dumping and changing, so the break-even sits near one in three. That is a probability, not a licence, but it is why the study's authors concluded the NHL players they tracked gave the puck up at the line too easily.

**The seam and the timing are how you make carrying work.** One defender facing you has an easy job; two sharing a gap have to decide who takes you — and both can stand you up, so go through it head up. Wingers arriving first, with the carrier a beat behind, make the defence respect three threats instead of collapsing on one — a coaching convention rather than a rule. Turning away when you are alone feels like retreating, but it turns a doomed entry into a fresh one with support.

**The onside rule is a tool, not a trap.** It is judged on the skates at one instant, not on the body, so a forward who knows it arrives at full speed with body and stick already in the zone. The books differ on whether the trailing skate may be in the air, so find out which one your league plays.

**A dump is only as good as its chaser.** A dump nobody is skating to is a pass to their defenceman. Pick the dump to the picture, and chase on an angle: the first forward takes the easy escape away, the second reads where the puck goes next.

## 1. Why entries decide games

### The finding

```facts
Key: At 5-on-5 a carry-in was worth 0.53 to 0.62 unblocked shot attempts per entry against 0.22 to 0.28 for a dump-in, across Tulsky's full 330-game NHL sample from 2011-12
Key: Goals per entry ran the same way — 0.027 to 0.039 carried in against 0.010 to 0.018 dumped in, at 5-on-5 across that same 330-game sample
Key: In Tulsky's tables "shots" means unblocked shot attempts, shots on goal plus misses, so do not set 0.53 to 0.62 against a shots-on-goal rate
Key: The ranges span the nine rows of Tulsky's Table 1, and the 0.62 top end is its rest-of-league row rather than any single team's
Key: Every row of that table had carrying in more than twice as productive as dumping in, on cells of at least 3,000 entries each
```

The reference work is Eric Tulsky, Geoffrey Detweiler, Robert Spencer and Corey Sznajder, *Using Zone Entry Data To Separate Offensive, Neutral, And Defensive Zone Performance*, presented at the 2013 MIT Sloan Sports Analytics Conference. They hand-tracked 330 NHL games from the 2011-12 season, recording every time the puck went into the attacking zone and how.

Their headline result, in their own words: *"carrying the puck across the blue line generates more than twice as many shots, scoring chances, and goals as dumping the puck in."*

The 5-on-5 numbers, per entry, **from the full 330-game sample** (each cell in their Table 1 is based on at least 3,000 entries):

| | Shots per entry | Goals per entry |
|---|---|---|
| Puck **carried** in | 0.53 – 0.62 | 0.027 – 0.039 |
| Puck **dumped** in | 0.22 – 0.28 | 0.010 – 0.018 |

**What "shots" means here.** Not shots on goal. Tulsky's group extracted from the play-by-play *"the number of shots (including those that miss the net) and goals produced in each offensive zone possession"* — so these are **unblocked shot attempts** (shots on goal plus misses, i.e. Fenwick events). Do not compare 0.53–0.62 shots per carry-in against a shots-on-goal rate.

The ranges are across the nine rows of their Table 1 — eight team data sets (Philadelphia, Minnesota, Washington and Buffalo, entries for and against) plus a "rest of league" row. The 0.62 upper bound for shots per carry-in comes from that rest-of-league row, not from any individual team's data set. Every single row showed carrying in as more than twice as productive as dumping in.

### The part everyone gets wrong

**In short:** In the tracked NHL games a failed carry-in cost far less than it feels, so the bar for trying one is lower than instinct says.

```facts
Key: Every figure in Tulsky's net-value table comes from the Washington games alone, not from all 330 — those were the only games in which failed attempts and dump-and-change plays were tracked
Key: A failed carry-in cost about eight hundredths of a shot in net value in Tulsky's Washington games, and dumping the puck in and changing lines cost more than that
Key: All of that cost is defensive — a failed carry produces no shots of its own, and it gives up 0.40 shots on the opponent's next entry, the highest shots-against figure in Tulsky's table
Key: Averaged over every attempt in those Washington games a carry was worth +0.42 net shots against +0.12 for dump-and-chase, and dump-and-change was the only play worse than a failed carry
Key: Tulsky's group put the break-even at 34% confidence between equal teams, 26% for a stronger line against a weaker pair, and 44% the other way round
Key: The break-even thresholds come off one decision curve with two values Tulsky's group could not measure — how good a well-defended carry is if completed, and how likely it is to be completed
Key: Only 14% of the carry attempts Tulsky's group tracked ended in a turnover, and they said in terms that the rate "would be higher in the marginal plays in question"
Key: Tulsky's group concluded about the NHL players they watched that players "give up the puck far too easily at the blue line" — the study observed no other level of hockey
```

The standard objection: *yes, but a failed carry-in is a turnover at the blue line, and a turnover at the blue line is a rush the other way.* Tulsky's group tested exactly that in their Washington games, following what happened on the **next** entry either team got. Their Table 3, in net expected shots — **every figure from the Washington games alone, not all 330**, because only there were failed attempts and dump-and-change plays tracked:

| Entry type | Shots for on this entry | Net value overall |
|---|---|---|
| Successful carry | 0.55 | **+0.50** |
| **Failed** carry | 0.00 | **−0.08** |
| Carry attempt (all attempts averaged) | 0.48 | **+0.42** |
| Dump-and-chase | 0.24 | **+0.12** |
| Dump-and-change | 0.02 | **−0.10** |

A blown carry-in cost about **eight hundredths of a shot** on net — not free, but nowhere near the disaster the folklore says, and *cheaper* than dumping and changing. Only **14% of carry attempts** ended in a turnover at all.

**All of that cost is defensive.** A failed carry produces no shots of its own — the first column is 0.00 — so the net figure is what happens next. Tulsky's Table 3 breaks it down further: after a failed carry the attacking team gets **0.31 shots for** on the next entry and gives up **0.40 against**, the highest shots-against figure in the table, above a dump-and-chase (0.39), a successful carry (0.37) and a dump-and-change (0.32). So a turnover at the line is not harmless going the other way; you win the puck back often enough to pay most of it off. That is measured in **shots**, which weights every shot the same; Chatel, below, prices it in expected goals.

**Read that 14% with the caveat the authors attached to it**: *"this is still not sufficient data to say conclusively that teams should be trying harder to carry the puck in at every opportunity… only 14% of all carry-in attempts resulted in a turnover, but surely the turnover frequency would be higher in the marginal plays in question."* The 14% covers every attempt, most of them not close calls. The ones you actually deliberate over, into a set defence, turn over more often, and their tracking does not say how much more.

Because the downside is small, the break-even is low: with equal teams you need only be **34% confident** of completing the carry for it to beat dumping in. If your line is better — they modelled a team expecting to shoot 9% while allowing 7% — it falls to **26%**; if you are the weaker team it rises to **44%**, the data-backed version of "just put it in deep and go to work."

All three come off one decision curve, which assumes a carry completed against a well-defended play is still worth the **0.57 shots** an average successful carry-in produced. The authors list that, and how likely such a carry is to succeed, as the **unknown parameters** in their calculation — so it is a decision curve with two unmeasured values, not a measured threshold.

Their conclusion, about the NHL players they watched: *"It is likely that players give up the puck far too easily at the blue line,"* and *"in general NHL teams probably could benefit from being more aggressive at the blue line."* The study observed no other level of hockey and makes no claim about one.

### Corroboration

**In short:** Other trackers, leagues and eras found the same answer: getting in with control beats putting the puck in without it.

The finding has held up across other data sets. None of these is a peer-reviewed paper, none of the underlying tracking is published, and they are not all reached first-hand — where a figure comes through a restater rather than from its own author, the bullet says so. Treat all of it as indicative:

- **Corey Sznajder** — roughly **0.66 shots per carry-in** against **0.29 per dump-in**. **This is the one still reached at second hand**, and its two restaters date it differently: Hockey's Arsenal gives the tracking as 2014-16, while Chatel credits the same pair of figures to *"Corey Sznajder presentation at RITSAC 2018"*. No publication by Sznajder himself was located for this document.
- **Thibaud Chatel**, April 2022, reported **47%** of controlled attempts led to a shot against **18%** of dump-ins, *"excluding dump and change"*. **Note the population, because it is neither the NHL nor your league:** over **56,700 five-on-five zone entries**, *"built from manually tracking games in Switzerland National League since 2019"* — European professional hockey. Chatel himself treats the agreement with the North American numbers as the finding, calling the two-for-one gap *"pretty much universal, small ice or big ice, and no matter the stat you look at."*
- **PWHPA data (via Sportlogiq)** — from a July 2022 article describing "this last year's PWHPA Secret Dream Gap Tour", so **probably the 2021-22 season, though the source does not say so** — showed the same shape in women's professional hockey: carry-ins **0.73** shots per entry, pass-ins **0.54**, chip-ins **0.21**, dump-ins **0.14** at even strength.

Different trackers, leagues and eras, same answer — **for the comparison Table 1 makes: getting in with control against putting it in without.** None of them reports the shots-for value of a *failed* attempt, so the attempt-inclusive 0.48 rests on Tulsky's Washington games alone. Chatel does measure the other half — what giving the puck up costs going the other way.

**Chatel tested the same objection** — *yes, but a turnover at the blue line is a rush the other way.* He priced the **next sequence** after the attacking team lost the puck in [expected goals](../off-the-ice/how_to_watch_hockey.md#expected-goals-xg), using **his own model, not a public one** — *"[w]e are using our own xG model here, based on shots manually tracked in Switzerland since 2019"*. He says it accounts for rush shots and shots through the royal road, the slot line down the middle defined in [Language and Glossary](../foundation/language_and_glossary.md).

The scenarios barely separated: *"all scenarios (dump or controlled entry, successful or failed) give up on around 0.06 or 0.07 xG to the opponent"*. **The next sentence does not go entirely his way:** counting the chance of losing the puck at once, *"a failed controlled entry is still the biggest threat, with a potential 0.015 xG against you, but very close to a failed dump that has a potential 0.013 xG against you."* So the failed carry **is** the worst — by two thousandths of an expected goal.

His explanation of why the gap feels larger is the part to carry onto the ice: *"we might remember goals allowed on ugly turnovers at the blue line, but nobody think about the dozens of possessions just handed over to the other team when you are dumping the puck."* **This is a different league, measure and set of hands from Tulsky's tables**, so it does not extend them — it is a separate test of the same objection, and it came out the same way.

### What this actually means for you

- **Possession in is worth roughly double a dump-in in shot terms.** Not marginally better. Double.
- **The cost of trying and failing is smaller than it feels**, because it feels dramatic when it goes wrong and invisible when it works.
- **Dumping and changing is the worst play on the board** — in the Washington games it was the only entry type with a worse net value than a failed carry. If you are tired, that is a reason to have changed twenty seconds ago, not a reason to fire the puck away.
- **This is a probability argument, not a licence.** One-third confidence means you should also be putting it in deep a lot of the time. The point is that the bar is much lower than the NHL players in this tracking behaved as if it was. **They are the only players the study watched** — whether your own league gives the puck up too readily, or not readily enough, is not something these numbers measured.

See [Risk Management](../hockey-iq/risk_management.md) for how this thinking generalises.

## 2. The offside rule as a tactical tool

Most players know offside as something whistled against them. Learn the exact wording and it becomes something you use. **[Rules Primer](../foundation/rules_primer.md) covers Rule 83** in full; this document adds one thing — the **trail-skate drag** — and the four clauses it rests on.

**If you play in Britain, read the IIHF flags below as your own.** Every level here — the Elite League, the NIHL, the recreational and junior game, university hockey — runs the IIHF Rule Book. One local amendment touches this rule: because **on-ice video review is not available in EIH or SIHA competition**, a **Coach's Challenge is not permitted** there either (In-House Rules, Rules 37 and 38, amending IIHF Rule 38 and therefore Rule 83.5). In those competitions a missed offside on the ice is the end of the matter. See [UK and England Rules](../foundation/uk_rules.md).

### The four clauses the technique rests on

**In short:** Offside is judged by your skates, not your body: one skate touching the line keeps you onside until the puck is fully across.

```facts
Rule: You are offside only when both skates are completely over the leading edge of the blue line (NHL Rule 83; IIHF Rule 83.1 word-for-word)
Rule: One skate in contact with the line, or on your own side of it, keeps you onside at the instant the puck completely crosses (NHL Rule 83; IIHF Rule 83.1)
Rule: The whole width of the painted blue line counts as "the line" — a skate touching any of it is onside (NHL Rule 1.5)
Rule: Your own side of the line is a vertical plane — a skate lifted and held back over the neutral zone is onside (NHL Rule 83; IIHF Rule 83.1 and PWHL Rule 85.1, in the same words), but not under USA Hockey or Hockey Canada
Rule: A "skate" is the blade only — NHL Rule 83.1 since 2025-26, PWHL Rule 85.1, and the IIHF's 2026/27 book; the 2025/26 IIHF book carries no such definition. USA Hockey defines it more broadly: its Casebook (Rule 630, Situation 8) says "skate" means "the blade or the boot"
Rule: An airborne trail skate is offside under USA Hockey 630(a), whose Casebook Situation 7 answers this exact case, and Hockey Canada Rule 6.11 with its Interpretation 3 — the blade must have contact. CARHA 72(c) borrows "own side" without defining it, so it settles nothing either way
Rule: British hockey runs the IIHF book at every level, so the IIHF wording is the rule you play under — an Elite League game runs the 2026/27 edition, which its Casebook makes the ruling for every rule it does not mention, and it mentions no Rule 83; below that the edition is unsettled, so ask
Rule: Under the IIHF's 2026/27 Rule 83.1 a carrier crossing ahead of the puck is not offside only where they have "possession and control" of it "with their stick" before "both skates completely" cross the leading edge
Rule: The IIHF's 2025/26 Rule 83.1 asks only for possession and control "prior to their skates crossing" — no stick, and not "both skates", so the 2026/27 book is stricter on one limb and looser on the other, not uniformly stricter
```

Quoted from **NHL Rule 83** in the *NHL Official Rules 2025-2026*:

- **"A player is off-side when both skates are completely over the leading edge of the blue line involved in the play."** Both. Not one. Conversely, *"[a] player is on-side when either of his skates are in contact with the blue line, or on his own side of the line, at the instant the puck completely crosses the leading edge of the blue line."*
- The **leading edge** is the near edge of the blue line as you approach it. The line is painted **twelve inches wide** (Rule 1.5), and that whole foot of it counts as "the line" — a skate touching any of it is onside.
- **Your own side of the line is a vertical plane** rising from the leading edge. *"If a player's skate has yet to break the plane prior to the puck completely crossing the leading edge, he is deemed to be on-side for the purpose of the off-side rule."* A skate lifted in the air, held back over the neutral zone, is onside.
- From 2025-26 the NHL says **"a 'skate' is to be considered the blade of the skate only."** The IIHF's **2026/27** Rule 83.1 and the PWHL's Rule 85.1 say the same; the **2025/26** IIHF book carries no such definition. It cuts both ways: your boot overhanging the line is irrelevant, and a **blade** across the plane is offside however far back the boot still is. **USA Hockey defines the word more broadly, and in your favour** — its *Casebook* at Rule 630, Situation 8 rules that *"[t]he word 'skate' in the Rule refers to the blade or the boot of the skate"*, so a boot still touching the neutral zone keeps you onside there.

**The IIHF's Rule 83.1 uses the same words for the first three clauses.** Its 2026/27 book adds two things to the 2025/26 one: the blade-only sentence above, and a rewritten carve-out for the puck carrier. A player crossing ahead of the puck is not offside *"provided they have 'possession and control' of the puck **with their stick** prior to **both skates completely** crossing the leading edge"*; 2025/26 asked only for possession and control *"prior to their skates crossing the leading edge"*. **⚠️ Those two changes run opposite ways, so do not read the new edition as uniformly stricter.** Requiring the stick is stricter — push the puck over the line with a skate and the carve-out is gone. *"Both skates completely"* is looser. Both bring the IIHF's wording onto the NHL's. The IIHF's *Situation Handbook* covers the airborne case directly: a player jumping at the line must have their skates *"on its own side of the leading edge of the blue line and have yet to break the 'plane of the leading edge' prior to the puck crossing the leading edge"* (Situation 83.29, word-identical in both editions).

**In the Elite League the 2026/27 book applies** — the EIHL Casebook makes that edition the ruling for every rule it does not mention, and it mentions no Rule 83. **Below the Elite League, which edition applies is unresolved** — the In-House Rules point both ways, and [UK and England Rules](../foundation/uk_rules.md) sets out the contradiction. **Nothing in the trail-skate drag below turns on it**: the contact and plane tests it rests on are the same in both editions (2026/27 adds only that a "skate" means the blade).

> **The third clause is NHL, IIHF and PWHL, and both North American governing bodies reverse it.** Under **USA Hockey Rule 630(a)** you are offside without *"skate contact with any part of the Neutral Zone or the blue line when the puck crosses the determining edge of the blue line"*, and its *Casebook* rules a player whose *"trailing skate is directly above the ice in the Neutral Zone (off the ice)"* offside (Rule 630, Situation 7). **Hockey Canada Rule 6.11** counts *"[o]nly the player's skate(s) that are in physical contact with the ice surface"*, and its Interpretation 3 to Rule 6.11(a) rules one skate above the line, not touching the ice, and one over it *"OFF-SIDE."* **CARHA's Rule 72(c) borrows the phrase *"own side"* without defining it, so it settles nothing.** So an airborne trail skate is **onside under the NHL, the IIHF and the PWHL (Rule 85.1), and therefore in Britain, and offside under USA Hockey and Hockey Canada**. How much North American rec and youth hockey runs on those two books is a question about leagues, and this document has no count for it, so ⚠️ **find out which book your league plays under.**

### The trail-skate drag

**In short:** Glide your trail blade on the line with body and stick already in the zone, head up, and push once the puck is across.

```facts
Position: Far-side winger flying at the line, a stride ahead of a carrier about to put the puck over
Technique: Attack the line at full speed with your inside foot leading
Technique: Stop pushing with the trail leg and let that blade glide so it stays touching the painted blue line
Action: Keep your body, hands and stick already in the zone — only the trail blade is holding you onside
Action: The instant the puck completely crosses, put that blade down and push
Key: The judgement is made at one instant — time your glide to it, don't glide for two seconds
Never: Drag the trail skate with your head down — your body, hands and stick are in the zone before your skate is, and that is where a defender standing up at the line will be
Rule: Lifting the trail skate is legal under the NHL, the IIHF and the PWHL, so it is legal in Britain (IIHF Rule 83.1; PWHL Rule 85.1) — USA Hockey Rule 630(a) and Hockey Canada Rule 6.11 require skate contact, so keep the blade down there
```

This is the single most useful piece of applied rules knowledge for a forward, and it comes straight out of the wording above.

You are the far-side winger. You are flying. The puck carrier is a stride behind you and about to put the puck over the line. If you simply skate, both blades cross, the puck crosses second, and the linesperson's arm goes up.

Instead:

1. Attack the line at full speed with your **inside foot leading**.
2. As you reach it, **stop pushing with the trail leg** and let that blade glide so it stays touching the painted line — or, **under NHL, IIHF and PWHL rules, and so in Britain**, lift it and hold it back over the neutral zone without breaking the plane. **Under USA Hockey and Hockey Canada the blade must stay in contact** (Rule 630(a); Rule 6.11), so keep it on the line.
3. Keep your **body, hands and stick already in the zone**. Only the trail blade is holding you onside.
4. The instant the puck completely crosses, **put that blade down and push**.

You have arrived at the line at top speed, you have your stick in the zone ready to receive, and you are legal. A player who does not know the rule has to slow down and check their feet. You do not.

![](diagram:entry-trail-skate-drag)

Three details that separate people who *sort of* know this from people who use it:

- The judgement is made at **one instant** — the moment the puck fully crosses. Time your glide to that instant; do not glide for two seconds.
- Because the **skates** decide and not the body — and under the NHL, the PWHL and the IIHF's 2026/27 book, only the **blade** — you can lean most of your body over the line. Aggressive body position is free. **Your head is not.** You arrive at top speed with your body, hands and stick across the line, which is where a defenceman standing up at their own blue line will be — [§11](#standing-up-at-the-line) is the same play from their side. **A head up is the condition of the technique, not an extra**: as [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md) puts it, you cannot brace for a hit you cannot see.
- **Which rule book you are under changes step 2.** Gliding the blade along the line is legal everywhere; lifting it is not. Find out which book your league plays under — and under USA Hockey's or Hockey Canada's, keep that trail skate on the ice.

### Delayed offside and tagging up — three consequences for the entry

**In short:** Once the arm goes up, leave the puck alone, get everyone back to touch the line together, and come again.

```facts
Rule: On a delayed offside the linesperson raises a non-whistle arm and play continues (NHL Rule 83.3; IIHF Rule 83.3 in the same terms)
Rule: Under USA Hockey Rule 630(d) delayed offside and tag-up exist only at Youth 15-Only and above, Girls 16U and above, High School and Adult — below that the whistle is immediate. That restriction is USA Hockey's alone; Hockey Canada 6.11(b), the NHL, IIHF and PWHL set no classification limit
Rule: Tagging up needs actual skate contact with the line; the vertical plane does not apply — "'3D' judgement is not used in this situation" (IIHF Rule 83.3(I), the wording British hockey plays under; NHL 83.3(i) says "skate contact with the blue line" without the gloss)
Rule: The zone must be empty before anyone re-enters — NHL 83.3(i), IIHF 83.3(I) and USA Hockey 630(d)(2) word it "at the same instant"; Hockey Canada 6.11(b)(ii) as a result, "completely clear of attacking players". Both cite the tag-up clause. Same requirement, not a permission
Rule: Two more books write the same tag-up duty — PWHL 85.3(i) in the NHL's "at the same instant" wording, and CARHA 72(e)(2) asking every attacker to make "skate contact with the blue line", its Note stating the result as Hockey Canada does: "completely clear of attacking players"
Action: Get everybody out at once — one straggler keeps the arm up in every book here, so shout it
```

**What to do is the same everywhere: stop, get back to the line, put a skate on it together, and come again.** The detail below is why that is the whole answer, and what it costs to try anything else.

A **delayed offside** is when attackers precede the puck but the defending team can bring it out without interference: the linesperson raises a non-whistle arm and play continues (Rule 83.3). **The IIHF's 83.3 is the same rule in the same order**, so this describes the British game as written. Its arm comes down when *"All Players of the offending team clear the zone at the same instant (skate contact with the blue line, '3D' judgement is not used in this situation)"* or when *"the defending team passes or carries the puck into the neutral zone"*. [Rules Primer](../foundation/rules_primer.md) has the full procedure. Three things change what you do at the line:

- **First, check that you have a delayed offside at all.** Under **USA Hockey Rule 630(d)** the delayed offside and the tag-up exist only *"(For all Youth 15-Only levels and above, all Girls 16U levels and above, High School and Adult classifications **only**)"*. Below those levels the whistle goes the moment you precede the puck in, so the entry has to be right first time. ⚠️ **Check your own classification.** How much minor hockey sits below that line is a question about leagues rather than books, and this document has no count for it. **This restriction is USA Hockey's alone** — Hockey Canada's Rule 6.11(b), the NHL's 83.3 and the IIHF's carry no classification limit.
- **Tagging up needs actual skate contact with the line.** The vertical plane does *not* apply: the IIHF says *"'3D' judgement is not used in this situation"* (Rule 83.3(I)), and NHL 83.3(i) says *"(skate contact with the blue line)"*. So the airborne trail skate that keeps you onside on the entry does **not** clear you here. Touch the painted line.
- **Everybody has to be out before anyone re-enters — in every book here.** One straggler keeps the arm up. The NHL and IIHF (83.3) and USA Hockey (630(d)(2)) say *"at the same instant"*; Hockey Canada's 6.11(b) words it as a result — *"so that the attacking zone is completely clear of attacking players"* — and its Interpretation 11 resolves that to *"the instant the zone is completely free of attacking players"*. IIHF Situation 83.34 adds that *"[s]hould another Team A player enter the attacking zone while A31 is airborne, the delayed off-side shall remain in effect."* The PWHL (85.3(i)) and CARHA (72(e)(2), with a Note in Hockey Canada's words) write the same duty. **Different wording, same requirement: play it as though the instant matters and you are legal under all of them.** ⚠️ **If you look up USA Hockey's 630(d)(2), read both lists:** Rule 630(d) carries two numbered lists, so "630(d)(2)" names both the tag-up clause and the stop-play pressure clause, and the *Casebook* uses the same reference for both; Hockey Canada's 6.11(b) has the same shape. Nothing on the ice turns on it. Shout the tag-up — see [On-Ice Communication](../foundation/on_ice_communication.md).

#### Forechecking during a delayed offside

```facts
Never: Forecheck during a delayed offside. Hold at the line instead and wait for the arm to drop, whichever book you are under
Rule: Every book here whistles a delayed offside for touching the puck, and for going for a loose one — NHL and IIHF 83.3, PWHL 85.3, Hockey Canada 6.11(b)(ii) and CARHA 72(e)(2) ("while the puck is still in the attacking zone"). USA Hockey 630(d) is wider: any attempt on the puck, loose or not
Rule: NHL and IIHF 83.3 add two further delayed-offside whistle triggers — forcing the defending puck carrier deeper, and being about to make contact with them. PWHL 85.3 writes both too; USA Hockey reaches the first through 630(d)'s stop-play pressure limb and Casebook Situation 38
Rule: USA Hockey 630(d), Hockey Canada 6.11(b)(ii) and CARHA 72(e)(2) write no about-to-make-contact trigger: nothing you can rely on stops play before you arrive, so hold at the line. Hockey Canada 6.11(d)(i) can whistle a knowing play, or even an attempt, on the defending carrier
Rule: USA Hockey 630(d) does whistle an attacker who "continues to apply pressure to the defending puck carrier" — wider than the NHL's forcing-them-deeper limb, but a linesperson's judgement of pressure, not of an imminent hit, so hold at the line, never close on them
Rule: Hitting a defenceman turned to retrieve is checking from behind — under USA Hockey a forceful check at the boards is a major plus game misconduct or a match (Casebook 608 Situation 1); if reckless, plan on a match (602(a)), and it is mandatory with excessive force if they are vulnerable (608(c))
Rule: USA Hockey's Casebook names its minor plus misconduct for a push "not near the boards" or a minimal-contact "pinch"; a check from behind that "recklessly endangers the opponent", board contact or none, is a major plus a game misconduct — plan on a match (608 Situation 1; 602(a))
Rule: Any USA Hockey game misconduct also suspends the player for their team's next game (404(b)), and for at least two when it follows a second major in the same game (403(b))
Rule: Hockey Canada 7.5 is a minor and game misconduct for an intentional hit from behind "anywhere on the ice", off the head or head first into boards, and a match if they are defenceless into boards or goal frame; on their head, head contact (7.6; 7.7 Junior/Senior), up to a match, a must if defenceless
Rule: The checking-from-behind ejection does not wait for an injury — Hockey Canada 7.5(b) reaches a major plus a game misconduct on the violence of the impact alone and makes both mandatory on injury; USA Hockey 608(b) requires the pair if reckless, and plan on a match (602(a))
Rule: A hit from behind meant to injure is a mandatory match wherever the player lands — Hockey Canada 7.5(c)'s second paragraph (7.6(c) or 7.7(c) on the head), NHL and PWHL 21.1, USA Hockey 602(a) and CARHA 48(a); the IIHF has no match, so plan on its major and game misconduct (43.3)
Rule: CARHA 53(a) ends your night — a Minor or Major, each plus a Game Misconduct, for an intentional hit from behind "anywhere on the ice", and a Match "could also be assessed"; 53(b) a certain Match into the boards or net if the player hit is defenceless, no intent needed, no lesser penalty in its place
Action: Retreat to the line, tag up, come again
```

**In every book here the instruction is identical: hold at the line, retreat, tag up, come again.** Forechecking during a delayed offside brings a whistle, but which acts bring it differs, and ⚠️ **the one that matters most for your safety is written in only three books.**

**Do not count on the whistle to arrive before you do.** The **NHL** stops play if an offside attacker *"touches the puck, attempts to gain possession of a loose puck, forces the defending puck carrier further back into his own zone, or who is about to make physical contact with the defending puck carrier"*. The **IIHF** lists the same four, so that is the British rule too. **⚠️ The fourth trigger — the whistle for a hit that has not landed yet — is written only by the NHL, the IIHF and the PWHL.**

**USA Hockey** stops play when an attacker *"touches the puck"* or *"attempts to gain possession of the puck or continues to apply pressure to the defending puck carrier"*, and its *Casebook* has the linesperson stop play once the carrier is forced deeper *"or as soon as contact, however slight, is made with the puck or puck carrier."* That is a judgement of pressure, not a promise to stop an imminent hit. **Hockey Canada and CARHA stop play only for the puck** — no pressure trigger, no contact trigger. Hockey Canada can call an intentional offside on a deliberate play against the carrier, but only once it is made, and its price is a faceoff in your own end.

So under USA Hockey, Hockey Canada and CARHA, ⚠️ **never close on the defending carrier — hold at the line, because you cannot count on the whistle coming first.** The arm being up is not a stoppage, the carrier is still playing the puck, and is not braced for a hit.

And name ⚠️ **the hit you are lining up.** A defenceman turned to retrieve a puck in their own end has their back to you, so hitting them is **checking from behind**. Hockey Canada's rulebook prints your exact picture among the hits it tells referees to punish strictly, *"aware of the tragic consequences of spinal injuries"*: *"Where a player along the boards, with their head down looking for the puck or 'digging the puck out,' is hit from behind and driven head-first into the boards."* USA Hockey's *Casebook* marks a *"danger zone"* *"approximately ten feet out from the boards"*, where players checked from behind *"may be at high risk of receiving a serious and possibly life altering injury."* ⚠️ **If you can see their numbers, pull up and get back to the line.**

**What it costs.** ⚠️ **Expect to be thrown out of the game, and in several books it is not the referee's choice.** The three books that will not stop play before you arrive are the three that will take you off the ice after you do.

- **USA Hockey (Rule 608):** a minor plus a misconduct, or a major plus a game misconduct, for a check *"directly from behind, or diagonally from behind"*, in open ice or into the boards. ⚠️ **Do not price it at the minor.** The *Casebook* (608, Situation 1) keeps the minor for a push *"not near the boards"* or a minimal-contact *"pinch"*. It says *"[t]he major plus a game misconduct penalty, or match penalty, must be called"* where a player *"forcefully checks an opponent who is standing along the boards (back toward the middle of the ice)"* or where one is *"thrust head first into the boards or goal frame"*. The same call is required wherever a check from behind *"recklessly endangers the opponent ... regardless of whether or not board contact is made"*. 608(b) makes the major plus game misconduct mandatory for a reckless one; 608(c) makes a match mandatory with excessive force on a vulnerable player, as a turned defenceman is; and plan on a match for any reckless check, because Rule 602(a) says one *"shall be assessed"* for reckless endangerment.
- **Hockey Canada (Rule 7.5):** a minor **plus a game misconduct** for an intentional hit from behind *"anywhere on the ice"*. A major plus a game misconduct at the referee's discretion on the violence of the impact alone, and mandatory if a hit that would otherwise be a minor injures them — ⚠️ **the ejection does not wait for an injury**. A **match penalty** for a hit *"into the boards or goal frame, in such a way that the player is unable to protect or defend themselves"* — a defenceman turned to the wall, described — and for any deliberate attempt to injure.
- **Hockey Canada, on the head:** any hit that reaches their head is head contact, because Rule 7.6 *"supersedes any other rules, with the exception of Rule 7.10 – Fighting"*. In minor and female hockey (7.6) that runs from a minor to a major plus a game misconduct — mandatory if they are hurt or it was a cross-check to the head. In Junior and Senior (7.7) it runs from a minor plus a ten-minute misconduct to a major plus a game misconduct, mandatory at a high degree of violence, for a cross-check to the head, or where their head hits the glass, ice or boards and they are injured. Under both, a **match penalty** if they could not protect or defend themselves or you meant to injure them. ⚠️ **Where you drive them head first into the boards and the hit reaches their head, the harsher of 7.5 and 7.6 applies.**
- **CARHA (Rule 53):** a minor or a major, each plus a game misconduct, for an intentional hit from behind *"anywhere on the ice"* — so ⚠️ **even the lighter option ends your night.** A major plus a game misconduct *"must be assessed"* if they are injured, and *"A Match penalty could also be assessed under this rule"*, boards or not. Any major here also brings at least a one-game suspension (32(d)), as any USA Hockey game misconduct does (404(b)). **53(b) asks nothing about intent:** a **match penalty** where a player is *"pushed, hit or propelled in any manner from behind into the boards or goal net, in such a way that the player is unable to protect or defend themselves"*. Its Note says *"Referees are instructed not to substitute other penalties when a player is checked from behind in any manner."*
- **NHL, IIHF and PWHL:** there is no minor for checking from behind (Rule 43.2 in each), and a major carries a game misconduct.
- **Five books:** a hit from behind meant to injure is a mandatory match penalty — NHL and PWHL 21.1, USA Hockey 602(a), Hockey Canada 7.5(c) and CARHA 48(a).

[Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md) covers how to arrive on a puck-retriever without becoming that. Hockey Canada's offside rule answers the player who hits anyway only with a faceoff; **the protection is Rules 7.5 and 7.6, not the offside rule.**

### The territorial asymmetry — why the safe play isn't

**In short:** Under pressure, a failed carry costs you less than a failed dump, and a shot that creates a delayed offside cannot score for you.

```facts
Rule: A botched carry-in draws only to the neutral-zone spot outside the blue line (NHL Rule 83.6; IIHF Rule 83.6(I), the same)
Rule: A botched dump-in or stretch pass draws back at the spot the shot or pass came from, which can be your own end (NHL Rule 83.6; IIHF Rule 83.6(II), the same)
Rule: A shot that creates a delayed offside and goes in is disallowed, whether or not you cleared the zone first — NHL and IIHF 83.4, PWHL 85.4, and Hockey Canada Rule 6.11(b) with its Note 1, to the same effect
Rule: The one way a goal still stands is the defending team putting it in themselves *"without action or contact by the offending team"* — NHL and IIHF 83.4, PWHL 85.4. Hockey Canada is silent; USA Hockey's Casebook (Rule 630, Situation 41) rules the own goal out. Clear and tag up rather than chase
Rule: Read USA Hockey's Casebook Situation 41 to its end — it bars the goal while the delayed offside is live and on the offending shot, but allows any goal legally scored once you have cleared the zone with no shot away (Rule 630, Situation 41)
Rule: USA Hockey has no delayed offside below Youth 15-Only, Girls 16U, High School and Adult (Rule 630(d)) — below those classifications the whistle is immediate, so there is no zone to clear and nothing to tag up for. Hockey Canada 6.11(b) sets no such limit
Key: Under pressure at the line, failing to carry costs you less than failing to dump
```

**Under pressure at the line, failing to carry costs you less than failing to dump.** That runs the same direction as the shot-rate evidence in section 1, and it is worth remembering the next time the safe play looks safe.

The reason is where the faceoff goes. A botched **carry-in** draws only to the neutral-zone spot outside the blue line. A botched **dump-in or stretch pass** draws back **at the spot the shot or pass came from**, which can be your own end (Rule 83.6). The IIHF gives the same four faceoff locations. [Rules Primer](../foundation/rules_primer.md) sets out the rule in full.

**A shot that creates a delayed offside cannot score for you**, even if you clear the zone before it goes in (Rule 83.4; the IIHF word for word). **So do not chase it — clear the zone and tag up.** One goal can still stand under the NHL, the IIHF and the PWHL: the defending team putting it in themselves *"without action or contact by the offending team"*. Any touch or any chase stops play first, so tagging up is the only way to leave that door open. In Britain that exception is yours.

**The books differ on that own goal**, though none of it changes what you do — only what you expect at the other end.

- **Hockey Canada** writes the disallowed goal and not the exception. Rule 6.11(b) disallows the goal *"as the original shot was off-side"*, its Note 1 extends that to a shot that goes in after the zone is cleared, and nothing in Rule 6.11 or its interpretations allows an own goal.
- **USA Hockey** writes the exception out. Its *Playing Rules* do not settle the case, but its *Casebook* (Rule 630, Situation 41) does: *"Even if the defending team shoots, passes or deflects the puck into its own goal, a goal may not be allowed. This is also true in cases where the attacking team has nullified the delayed offside by clearing the zone prior to the puck entering the goal."* **It is bounded, though:** where the puck was *not* shot directly on goal and you have cleared the zone, play continues and any goal legally scored stands. *(That last sentence is printed with a word dropped from it, so it is paraphrased here.)* So the closed door is the offending puck, not the rest of the shift.
- **CARHA** leaves it unsettled: its Rule 72(e) disallows the offside shot and writes no exception, while its Rule 60(b) counts a puck *"put into the goal in any manner by a player of the defending team"*, and nothing reconciles the two.

## 3. Carry-in entries

A **carry-in** (also called a controlled entry) is any entry where your team has the puck on a stick as it crosses. A **pass-in** — a completed pass across the line to a teammate — counts as controlled too, but it is not equivalent. In the PWHPA data cited above, pass-ins produced **0.54** shots per entry at even strength against **0.73** for carry-ins: still far better than any uncontrolled entry, but about a quarter less productive than carrying it in yourself. Pass-ins only draw level with carries **on the power play** (0.96 against 0.91).

### Attack the outside shoulder

```facts
Read: The defender's weakest moment is the pivot — turning from backwards to forwards, or opening their hips to one side
Action: Aim at the outside shoulder, the one nearest the boards on your side, with pace and your eyes up
Read: The moment the defender's hips open outward the inside lane is available — cut back into the middle, slip the puck between their feet, or pass to the middle driver
Countered by: A defender who refuses to open and stays square — then you have the outside lane for real, so take it and get to the goal line
Key: You are not trying to beat them, you are trying to make them commit
```

The defender's job is to stay between you and the middle of the ice. Their weakest moment is the **pivot** — the instant they turn from skating backwards to skating forwards, or open their hips to one side.

Aim at the **outside shoulder** of the defender: the shoulder nearest the boards on your side. Do it with pace and with your eyes up. You are threatening the outside lane, which is the lane they are least worried about but most physically awkward to defend, because covering it means opening their hips toward the boards.

![](diagram:entry-outside-shoulder)

The moment their hips open outward, the **inside lane is available** — that is when you cut back into the middle, or slip the puck between their feet, or hit the middle driver. If they refuse to open and stay square, you have the outside lane for real: take it and get to the goal line.

The principle underneath: **you are not trying to beat them, you are trying to make them commit.** A defender who has committed to one lane cannot defend the other.

### Attack the space between two defenders, not one of them

```facts
Action: Skate at the seam between two defenders, head up — the two defencemen, or a defenceman and a backchecking forward
Never: Skate directly at a defender — you have handed them an easy job, because they only have to deal with you
Action: On a 3-on-2, the middle attacker drives the space between the two defencemen, not either one — head up, because both can stand you up
Action: On a 2-on-1 wide, angle your route so the single defender is forced to choose between you and your partner
Read: The moment two defenders both think the other one has it — a fraction of a second long, and where entries are won
```

Skate directly at a defender and you have handed them an easy job: they only have to deal with you. Skate at the **seam between two defenders** — between the two defencemen, or between a defenceman and a backchecking forward — and you have created a problem neither of them owns. Both have to decide whether to step or hold. Whichever one steps opens the space they were covering. Go through it head up: it is also where both of them can stand you up.

Practically: on a 3-on-2, the middle attacker should drive at the **space between the two defencemen**, not at either one. On a 2-on-1 wide, angle your route so the single defender is forced to choose between you and your partner rather than being able to face both.

You are looking for the moment two defenders both think the other one has it. That moment is a fraction of a second long and it is where entries are won.

**On a rush you will also hear this as "isolate a defender" — the same instruction, not a rival one.** Going at the seam is *how* you isolate one: whichever defender steps to take you has left their partner alone with somebody else. (At 3-on-3, [Special Teams](special_teams.md) uses the phrase for going straight at a lone defender in space — the opposite of this.) **Entering with numbers** below sets out the patterns.

### The wide entry

**In short:** Go wide to the goal line only with a teammate driving the net, stopping short of the paint, its red line and the goalie.

```facts
Action: Take the puck down the boards outside the defenceman and get to the goal line or below it
Goal: Make the defender turn and skate — every stride toward the boards is a stride away from the front of the net
Targets: A support player driving the net, plus a third player high for the back-door or point option
Action: Drive the net to the edge of the blue paint and off the red line around it, not through either — a skate inside the paint, or on that line, risks the goal
Never: Meet a goal post head first, and never duck — head up, chin off your chest. A head-down, head-first hit on a post is the same injury as one on the boards, and can happen at walking speed
Rule: Keep off the red crease line at the paint's edge too — the NHL's, IIHF's and PWHL's reference tables all allow a goal on the line, but USA Hockey's 625(b) Note and Hockey Canada's Glossary count the crease lines as crease, and IIHF Rule 1.7 says the marked line belongs to it, so keep off it
Rule: The drive is not licence to hit the goalie — contact you start with a goalie in the crease can void the goal even when incidental, and deliberate contact anywhere, or unnecessary contact outside the crease, is penalised (NHL and IIHF 69.2–69.4; PWHL 71.2–71.4)
Rule: Hockey Canada 8.5 penalises unnecessary contact with the goalie anywhere, CARHA 66(b) any contact that impedes the goalie and its 52(b) Note unnecessary contact, and USA Hockey 607(d) Note 1 sends even accidental contact to interference, deliberate contact to charging, which is never a bare minor
Rule: USA Hockey 607(c) is not scoped to the crease — a check or charge on a goalie "within the goal crease or privileged area" draws "[a] minor plus a misconduct or a major plus a game misconduct", and 607(b) makes a reckless charge a mandatory major plus a game misconduct; plan on a match (602(a))
Rule: Hockey Canada 8.5(b) makes a major plus a game misconduct automatic for any charge on the goalie, reckless or not, wherever they are standing, and for injuring them by interference that would otherwise draw a minor
Rule: CARHA 52(b) makes a major plus a game misconduct mandatory for charging a goalie in the crease or for any charge that injures, and 54(c) for cross-checking one there; NHL and PWHL 42.5 and the EIHL Casebook's Rule 42 add a mandatory game misconduct to a charging major that injures their face or head
Rule: NHL and IIHF 69.4 (PWHL 71.4) also reaches the goalie once they have left the crease to play the puck — an attacker whose "deliberate actions" prevent the goalie's return may be penalised for interference; it turns on intent, not contact
Never: Turn your back to anyone coming at you going down the wall — if contact is coming, take it on your forearm and hip, never the point of your shoulder, head up and chin off your chest
Risk: Going wide with nobody behind you is not an attack — it is a slow dump-in with extra steps
```

Take the puck down the boards outside the defenceman and get to the goal line or below it.

![](diagram:entry-wide)

- **Why it works:** the defender must turn and skate, and every stride they take toward the boards is a stride away from the front of the net.
- **What you need:** a support player driving the net so that when you reach the goal line you have somewhere to put it, and a third player high for the back-door or point option.
- **When it fails:** when you go wide with nobody behind you. Then you are alone below the goal line with three defenders between you and the net, which is not an attack, it is a slow dump-in with extra steps.

**Where the net drive stops: at the edge of the blue paint, off the red line around it, short of the goalie.** Screen from there. Screening from *outside* the crease without contact is normally legal and worth doing — but it is not a guaranteed goal. Under the NHL, IIHF and PWHL a goal can still be disallowed if your positioning stops the goalie moving freely in the crease.

Arrive ⚠️ **head up — never head first into a post, and never duck.** A head-first hit on a goal post is the same neck and spine injury as one on the boards. USA Hockey's *Heads Up Hockey Program Guide* names *"the boards or goal post"* together and says it *"can occur at walking speed"*, and its *Introduction To Body Contact* says to *"[h]it the boards or goal posts with an arm, a leg, or anything but your head first."* That holds at every level and under every book, checking or not.

**Why stay off the red line too.** The books disagree on whether it is part of the crease. IIHF Rule 1.7 says *"[t]he marked line belongs to the Goal Crease"*; USA Hockey counts the crease lines in its Note to Rule 625(b) and its *Casebook*; Hockey Canada's Glossary says *"The lines of the crease are considered part of the crease."* The NHL's rule text is silent, and the NHL, IIHF and PWHL reference tables all allow a goal for an attacker who *"plants himself on the crease line"* (NHL wording; the IIHF and PWHL say "themself" and "herself"). Staying clear is the reading that never has to be argued.

**What it costs.** ⚠️ **Contact with the goalie costs the goal, and often a penalty on top.**

- **The goal.** Contact you start with a goalie **in** the crease voids the goal, incidental or not (NHL and IIHF 69.3, PWHL 71.3); outside it, more than incidental contact voids it (69.4). The NHL, IIHF and PWHL allow incidental contact *"[i]n a rebound situation, or where a goalkeeper and attacking player(s) are simultaneously attempting to play a loose puck, whether inside or outside the crease"* (69.7; PWHL 71.7). ⚠️ **Do not plan around that exception in the Elite League, or under USA Hockey, Hockey Canada or CARHA.** The EIHL *Casebook* writes *"Contact in the blue paint area calls for disallowing a goal"*. USA Hockey bars a goal scored while an attacker is interfering with the goalie (617(c)(4)) and sends *"any accidental or unavoidable contact that occurs with the goalkeeper"* to interference (607(d) Note 1). Hockey Canada disallows any goal where an attacker starts contact with the goalie, in or out of the crease, unless the attacker was fouled into it (Interpretation 1 to 8.5(a)). CARHA 66(b) disallows the goal wherever an attacker has physically interfered with the goalie.
- **The penalty runs separately from the goal** — ⚠️ **deliberate contact is penalised in every book.** NHL and IIHF 69.2 penalise *"intentional or deliberate contact with a goalkeeper, whether or not the goalkeeper is inside or outside the goal crease, and whether or not a goal is scored"*, and 69.4 wants a penalty for any *"unnecessary contact"* outside the crease (PWHL 71.2 and 71.4). Short of a charge, Hockey Canada 8.5(a) and USA Hockey 625(a)(8) make contact that interferes with the goalie at least a minor; CARHA 66(b) makes impeding the goalie a minor, and deliberate contact, in or out of the crease, a minor or a major that ejects you and bans you a game or more (30(a), 32(d)).
- **USA Hockey:** a body check or charge on a goalie in the crease or privileged area is *"[a] minor plus a misconduct or a major plus a game misconduct penalty"* (607(c)), and 607(b) makes a reckless charge a mandatory major plus a game misconduct. Plan on a match penalty too, since Rule 602(a) says one *"shall be assessed"* for reckless endangerment, though 607(e) says only *"may also be assessed"*.
- **Hockey Canada:** a major and game misconduct is automatic for *any* charge on the goalie, wherever they are standing (8.5(b)), and a charge includes building speed with *"two or more strides immediately prior to making contact"* (Rule 7.4). Injuring the goalie with contact that would otherwise be a minor brings the same ejection.
- **CARHA:** a major and game misconduct is mandatory for a charge on a goalie in the crease, or for any charge that injures (52(b)), and for cross-checking them in the crease (54(c)). Each ejects you (30(a)), with at least a one-game suspension (32(d)).
- **NHL, PWHL and the Elite League:** a game misconduct is mandatory when a charging major injures the goalie's face or head (NHL and PWHL 42.5; EIHL Casebook Rule 42).

[Offensive Zone Play](offensive_zone_play.md) compares the books on the attacker in the crease.

### The middle drive

```facts
Position: A teammate without the puck, skating hard through the seam between the defencemen, at the net — to the edge of the blue paint, not into it
Goal: Freeze the defencemen so they cannot step up on the carrier — one route, at speed, buys the carrier several extra feet
Key: The middle driver usually does not get the puck, and that is not the point
Never: Drive half-heartedly — that is a player standing in the slot, which frees the defence rather than freezing it
Never: Meet the post head first, or duck into it — head up, chin off your chest. A head-down, head-first hit on a goal post is the same injury as one on the boards, at walking speed
Rule: Committed stops at the paint's edge and off the red line around it — IIHF Rule 1.7, USA Hockey's Note to 625(b) and Hockey Canada's Glossary count the crease lines as crease, while the NHL's, IIHF's and PWHL's tables allow a goal on the line; a skate inside the paint risks the goal
Rule: Arriving hard is no licence to hit the goalie — contact you start with a goalie in the crease can void the goal even when incidental, and deliberate contact anywhere, or unnecessary contact outside the crease, is penalised (NHL and IIHF 69.2–69.4; PWHL 71.2–71.4)
Rule: A charge on the goalie ejects you automatically under Hockey Canada 8.5(b) wherever they are, under CARHA 52(b) in the crease or if it injures, and under USA Hockey 607(b) if reckless — plan on a match there too (602(a))
Rule: Interference short of a charge still ejects you if it injures the goalie — Hockey Canada 8.5(b), and CARHA 66(e), whose major is an ejection (30(a))
```

A **middle drive** is a teammate without the puck skating hard through the middle of the ice, through the seam between the defencemen, at the net.

The middle driver usually does not get the puck. That is not the point. The point is that a defenceman who ignores a player skating at their net will get scored on, so they cannot ignore them — which means they cannot step up on the puck carrier either. One player, running one route, at speed, buys the carrier several extra feet of space.

![](diagram:entry-middle-drive)

Middle drives only work if they are **committed** — a half-hearted one is a player standing in the slot, which frees the defence rather than freezing it. **Committed still stops at the edge of the blue paint and off the red line around it**, because a skate inside the paint or on that line risks the goal. ⚠️ **And committed still means head up**: meeting a post head first is the same neck and spine injury as meeting the boards that way, so hit either with *"anything but your head first"*, and never duck.

**What it costs.** ⚠️ **A committed drive is not licence to hit the goalie:** contact you start with a goalie in the crease can void the goal even when incidental. Whether it is also a penalty is a separate question in every book, and a charge on the goalie can get you thrown out — automatically under Hockey Canada, under CARHA in the crease or if it injures them, and under USA Hockey if reckless. [The wide entry](#the-wide-entry) sets out each book's price.

### Using a teammate as a screen

```facts
Action: Cross with a teammate near the line so the defender tracking the carrier has to go around them
Key: Skate your own route to the net and let the defender's chosen path be the one that gets tangled
Never: Stop, stand, or step into a defender to make the pick — deliberately body-blocking a non-carrier is interference
Read: Officials at every level judge it on whether you were going somewhere
```

Two attackers crossing near the line can be used so that the second one runs interference — the defender tracking the carrier ends up having to go around your teammate.

**Be careful with this one.** Deliberately body-blocking a defender who is not the puck carrier is **interference**. What is legal is skating your own route to the net and letting the defender's chosen path be the one that gets tangled. What is not legal is stopping, standing, or stepping into them to make the pick. The distinction is whether you are playing hockey or setting a screen; officials at every level judge it on whether you were going somewhere. See [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md).

### Entering with numbers — 2-on-2 and 3-on-2

**In short:** Use the extra attacker to make one defender responsible for two of you, and keep your speed so someone has to commit.

```facts
Goal: Arriving with an extra attacker is worth nothing unless you use it to make one defender responsible for two of you
Key: Making one defender responsible for two attackers is what "isolate a defender" means in a rush
Never: Delay into the wall on a drive and delay. Curl in open ice instead, and never turn your back to anyone coming at you near the wall
Options: 2-on-2 — crisscross, midlane drive, or double drive, where both attackers drive and the carrier can shoot with the second going to the net
Options: 3-on-2 — high triangle with two driving wide and the third trailing, midlane drive with the middle attacker going hard at the net, triple drive with all three deep, or drive and delay
Technique: On a drive and delay, sell the drive first and turn away from the defender, level with them or as they pivot — the delay only buys time if the drive was believed
Read: What tells the carrier what is open is whether a defender goes with the wide driver — that single movement is the cue
Convention: Which of these your team runs is a coaching choice, so ask instead of assuming the pattern you know is the one being played
Rule: Every drive here stops at the paint's edge, off its red line, and none licenses hitting the goalie: your contact in the crease can void the goal even when incidental, and deliberate contact anywhere, or unnecessary contact outside the crease, is penalised (NHL and IIHF 69.2–69.4; PWHL 71.2–71.4)
Rule: A charge on the goalie ejects you automatically under Hockey Canada 8.5(b) wherever they are, under CARHA 52(b) in the crease or if it injures, and under USA Hockey 607(b) if reckless — plan on a match there too (602(a))
```

This is about the three of you together: **an extra attacker is worth nothing unless you use it to make one defender responsible for two people.** **"Attack the space between two defenders" and "isolate a defender" are the same instruction seen from two ends.** Go at the seam so one of them has to take you; the moment they do, a teammate has become somebody's second man, and that is your 2-on-1 inside the larger rush.

**Two-on-two.** Three patterns are named in coaching material. **Crisscross** — the two of you exchange lanes near the line so each defender has to decide whether to switch or follow. **Midlane drive** — the second attacker drives between the two defenders, which makes the one covering the carrier hesitate for a beat, and that hesitation is the room the carrier uses to **cross in behind them** and shoot or make a play. **Double drive** — both of you drive, and the carrier can drop the puck into the space behind a defender for the second attacker arriving on their inside shoulder, or shoot with the second going hard for the rebound.

**Three-on-two.** Four options, and they differ in where the third player goes:

- **High triangle** — the carrier drives wide with the puck, a second attacker drives wide *without* it, and the third trails into the high slot. The carrier now has a shot, a pass across, and a drop behind. **The read is whether a defender goes with the wide driver**; that single movement tells the carrier what has opened.
- **Midlane drive** — get the puck to the outside first, then the middle attacker drives hard at the net on the inside shoulder of the far defender. That defender now has to leave the prime scoring area, which turns the rest of it into a 2-on-1, and the shot can come from the middle with a screen already in place.
- **Triple drive** — a variation where all three go deep. The carrier takes it wide with speed and then cuts hard inside, using the two driving teammates to create the room to do it.
- **Drive and delay** — the carrier sells a hard drive to the net and then pivots away from the defender, back toward the blue line. The defender has already given ground for fear of the drive and needs a beat to react, and that beat is the time and space the entry was for. **The timing is the whole play:** turn up when you are level with the defender or just as they pivot toward you, and turn to the outside, away from them. Sell the drive first — a delay off a drive nobody believed is just slowing down. **It is the same turn as [§5](#5-the-delay--curl-back), at higher speed, and the same safety override applies.**

![](diagram:entry-high-triangle)

**Which of these your team uses is a coaching choice**, so ask which ones you run rather than assuming. What is not optional under any of them is speed: every one of these works by making a defender commit, and nobody commits to an attacker who is coasting.

**Every drive in every one of them ends in the same place, though** — at the edge of the blue paint, off the red line around it, and short of the goalie. A triple drive puts three of you into that ice at once, which is exactly when the boundary gets forgotten. **Contact you start with a goalie in the crease can void the goal even when incidental.** Whether it is also a penalty is a separate question in every book, and a charge on the goalie can get you thrown out — automatically under Hockey Canada, under CARHA in the crease or if it injures them, and under USA Hockey if reckless. **The wide entry** above sets out where the books put the crease line and what each does to a player who hits the goalie.

#### Three things that do not bend, whichever pattern you run

```facts
Never: Drive the middle with your head down — speed is the point and a head up is the condition of it, not an extra
Rule: IIHF 48.1 opens "There is no clean check to the head or neck" and the checker must still avoid your head — the one thing it declines to penalise is a square check with no upward motion on a puck carrier skating head down toward the checker, and how far that reaches is unresolved in its own text
Rule: Head up, and IIHF 48.1 is on your side — of a player skating head up, "whether they are in possession of the puck and may reasonably be expecting impending contact, an opponent does not have the right to hit them to the head or neck"; the NHL bars an avoidable head hit but writes neither half
Rule: The head-down carve-out is the IIHF's alone — USA Hockey's Casebook answers that crossing the ice at full speed to punish a head-down opponent rather than to play the puck is charging (Rule 607, Situation 2), and Hockey Canada's nearest rule turns on the ducking player rather than the checker
Never: Delay into the wall — a drive-and-delay is an open-ice turn, and the safety override — never turn your back to anyone coming at you near the wall — outranks the play
Never: Step into a defender on a crisscross. Skate your own route instead, because deliberately body-blocking a player who does not have the puck is interference
Technique: If you are tight to the wall and cannot get off it, take it on your forearm and hip, never the point of your shoulder, head up and chin off your chest, rather than turning away into the boards
```

The patterns above all ask you to arrive fast into a crowded piece of ice. Three things hold whichever one you are running, and none of them is a trade you get to make.

**Head up through the seam.** A middle drive is the one route where two defencemen can both stand you up in open ice, and ⚠️ **looking down as you arrive is what turns that into an injury.** Putting your head down also gives away protection the rulebook gives you. [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md) covers how to arrive.

**Under the IIHF book — the one a British reader plays under — head up keeps your protection.** Rule 48.1 opens *"There is no clean check to the head or neck. The Player delivering the hit must avoid hitting the opponent's head or neck."* ⚠️ **Then it writes a sentence for you:** *"When a Player is skating with their head up, whether they are in possession of the puck and may reasonably be expecting impending contact, an opponent does not have the right to hit them to the head or neck."* The next sentence spares the checker who *"delivers a bodycheck to an opponent who is skating with the puck with their head down in the direction of the Player and does not use an upward motion or drive their body up into the opponent"*. So head up, the checker has no right to your head; head down, a square hit with no upward motion is not penalised as one. [Rules Primer](../foundation/rules_primer.md) records that the IIHF's text does not say how far that carve-out reaches, so treat it as unresolved.

**Do not assume your own book has the same carve-out.** None was found in USA Hockey's *Playing Rules*, and its *Casebook* leans the other way: Rule 607, Situation 2 calls it charging when a player who *"sees an unsuspecting opponent skating up the ice with their head down"* crosses the ice at full speed to hit them, for the purpose of *"punishing a vulnerable or defenseless opponent"*. Hockey Canada's nearest rule is about *ducking to avoid a check*, which can put the **ducking player** in the box — the checker's amnesty is junior and senior only, while Rule 8.7's ban on crouching applies in every division. [Rules Primer](../foundation/rules_primer.md) covers that comparison.

**Delay in open ice, never against the wall.** The drive-and-delay is [§5](#5-the-delay--curl-back)'s turn, deeper and faster, and **§5's safety override applies unchanged**. If you cannot make the turn in open ice, do not make it. If you are already tight to the wall, take the contact on your forearm and hip rather than turning into the boards, head up and chin off your chest. Turn your back to anyone coming at you at the wall and the contact becomes a hit from behind — and if you turned into it on purpose, the NHL and PWHL give the hitter no penalty at all (43.1), the IIHF waives only the checking-from-behind call (43.1), and Hockey Canada still penalises the hitter.

**Skate your route; do not set a pick.** The crisscross works because two attackers exchanging lanes force two defenders to decide whether to switch. It stops working, and becomes a penalty, the moment you stop, stand, or step into a defender who does not have the puck — that is interference, and [Using a teammate as a screen](#using-a-teammate-as-a-screen) above covers the distinction.

### One thing that is not a rule

```facts
Convention: Crossing patterns, who takes the wide lane, whether the centre or a winger carries — all coaching choice, and it varies enormously
Options: Some teams demand strict lane discipline (left wing stays left); some are built entirely on crossing and swinging
Options: Some want the best puck carrier on the ice touching every entry
Action: Ask your coach what your team wants before you decide you know
```

All of this is **coaching choice**, and it varies enormously. Ask your coach what your team wants before you decide you know.

## 4. Speed and timing

### The puck carrier should be the last to the line, with the supports waiting on it onside — a coaching convention, not a rule

```facts
Position: As the carrier, reach the line half a stride to a full stride behind your wingers
Position: As a winger, be at the line, at speed, onside, before the puck gets there
Goal: Make the defence respect three threats at once, so the carrier arrives into a picture already pulled apart
Risk: If the carrier arrives first the supports are behind the play, and the defence collapses on one player
```

This is the timing principle that makes entries work. If the carrier arrives first, the supports are behind the play and the defence can collapse on one player. If the supports arrive first and the carrier arrives a beat later, the defence has to respect three threats at once and the carrier arrives into a picture that has already been pulled apart.

Concretely: as the carrier, you want to reach the line **half a stride to a full stride behind** your wingers. As a winger, you want to be **at the line, at speed, onside, before the puck gets there** — which is exactly what the trail-skate drag above is for.

### Arriving with speed is not the same as arriving early

```facts
Goal: Have your acceleration peak as the puck crosses the line
Action: Be slower thirty feet out so you can be faster at the line
Never: Arrive early and wait — standing still is easy to cover, and curling away loses your route
Key: Self-check — slowed down after crossing means you were early; crossing while accelerating into open ice means you were on time
```

Arriving early means you get to the line and then have to wait. Waiting at the line means either standing still — useless, because a stationary player is easy to cover — or curling away and losing your route.

Arriving with speed means your acceleration peaks **as** the puck crosses. To do that you often need to be *slower* thirty feet out so you can be faster at the line. This is counter-intuitive and it is the difference between a forward who looks fast and a forward who is dangerous. See [Skating](../technique/skating.md) for how to build the change of pace, and [Time and Space](../hockey-iq/time_and_space.md) for the general principle.

A useful self-check: if you crossed the line and then had to slow down to stay with the play, you were early. If you crossed the line accelerating into open ice, you were on time.

### Timing all three forwards to hit the line together

```facts
Goal: Three attackers crossing within about a second of each other, in three different lanes — one wide each side, one middle
Action: The middle lane player sets the clock — shortest route, so they adjust, usually by slowing or curling
Action: Wide players run their route full out and use the trail-skate drag to hold at the line if they beat the puck there
Read: Go anyway with two supports on time and one not. Delay when none of them are on time
Convention: On the power play coaches often want four across the line, two wide and two inside-dot — a common structure, not a universal one
```

The picture you want is **three attackers crossing the line within about a second of each other, in three different lanes** — one wide on each side, one in the middle.

How to get there:

- **The middle lane player sets the clock.** They are on the shortest route, so they must be the one who adjusts, usually by slowing or curling.
- **The wide players run their route full out** and use the trail-skate drag to hold at the line if they beat the puck there.
- **The carrier reads the picture and decides the moment.** If two supports are on time and one is not, go anyway; if none of them are, delay (see below).

![](diagram:entry-three-lanes)

On the power play, coaches often push this further and want **four across the line** — two wide lanes and two inside-dot lanes filled — so that the carrier always has an option. That is a common structure, not a universal one.

## 5. The delay / curl-back

**In short:** Arriving at the line alone, curl away in open ice, never against the wall, and come again once support catches up.

Turning away from the blue line, keeping the puck, and coming back at the line once your support has caught up.

**It runs against instinct, because turning away from the offensive zone feels like retreating.** At rec and youth level a wasted entry is typically a player who arrived at the line alone and did *something* — forced a carry into two defenders, threw a hopeful pass, fired a dump-in with nobody chasing — instead of turning away and waiting one second. That is a coaching observation: this document has no figure for what share of wasted entries it accounts for. [Time and Space](../hockey-iq/time_and_space.md) teaches the same turn as a general skill.

**The IIHF's coaching manual names the move.** Its *Coach Development Program, Level I (Tactical)* lists **delaying** among the ways to beat a defender one-on-one: *"When an attacker is cut off from moving to the net with the puck, a delay movement can create time and space for the attacker"*. The delay comes from *"a tight turn toward the boards to create space between the puck carrier and defender"*, from fading to the outside, or from *"a quick stop, facing the centre of the ice surface"* (§19.1.2). **It does not tell you when your team wants it**, and the manual is a **2002** file, so treat it as a governing body's vocabulary rather than current instruction. The re-attack with support in step 5 is this guide's, not the IIHF's; the nearest the manual says is that a player under tight checking *"may attempt to hold off the defender until a teammate moves in to provide support."*

**The IIHF's quick stop and step 1's *do not stop* are two different moments, not a disagreement.** Step 1 is for approaching the line **alone or outnumbered**; the IIHF's stop is for an attacker already cut off from the net by one defender. The manual limits it: it works *"[i]f the defender is not able to stop as quickly"*, and *"[a]gainst a skilled defender, it will be necessary for the puck carrier to move laterally with the puck immediately after the quick stop in order to gain the necessary space."*

### How to do it

```facts
Never: Stop when approaching the line alone or outnumbered — stopping there ends the entry
Convention: A quick stop is on the IIHF's list of delay movements — but for an attacker already cut off one-on-one, and only where the defender cannot stop as quickly
Action: Turn away from the pressure and curl back toward the neutral zone, body between defender and puck
Never: Curl against the wall, where turning puts your back to anyone coming at you. Curl in open ice
Action: Look back up ice as you curl — eyes to where support is coming from, not to the defender
Action: Slow the play deliberately for one or two seconds, which is how long late support takes to arrive at speed
Action: Attack the line again with the puck on whoever has the best angle and the most speed — often not you
```

1. Approaching the line alone or outnumbered, **do not stop**. Stopping there ends the entry.
2. **Turn away from the pressure** and curl away from the line, back toward the neutral zone, protecting the puck with your body between defender and puck.

   > **One safety override, and it outranks the play.** Curl in open ice, not against the wall. ⚠️ **Never turn your back to anyone coming at you near the wall** — it makes the contact a hit from behind, and USA Hockey's warning is flat: *"Even a light hit from behind could inflict a severe head or neck injury."* Protecting the puck never justifies it.

   **If you are caught at the wall, take the contact properly:** skates parallel to the boards, the hit on your **forearm and hip**, head up and chin off your chest. ⚠️ **Never the point of your shoulder** — USA Hockey's *Introduction To Body Contact* says *"Never hit the boards or glass with the tip of your shoulder – it can cause injury"* — and keep the rest of it off the boards if you can. A tucked chin straightens the neck, and USA Hockey warns that the injury from a head-first hit taken that way *"can occur at walking speed."* [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md) has the full treatment.
3. **Look back up ice** while you curl. Your eyes go to where support is coming from, not to the defender.
4. **Slow the play** deliberately for one or two seconds, which is how long it takes your late support to arrive at speed.
5. **Attack the line again** with the puck now in the hands of whoever has the best angle and the most speed — often not you.

![](diagram:entry-delay-curl)

### Why it works

You have converted a bad entry into a **fresh entry with support**. In the numbers from Section 1, you have moved yourself off the failed-carry row and back onto the successful-carry row. You also drag defenders toward you as you curl, which stretches the space behind them for the teammate arriving late.

### Why people don't do it

Because turning away from the offensive zone feels like retreating, and because a delay puts the puck on your stick for an extra second with a defender on your back, which feels dangerous. It is not — **in open ice**, which is exactly why the override above tells you to curl there and not against the wall. Losing the puck at the top of the circles on a forced entry is far worse than losing it in the neutral zone with everyone in front of you.

### When *not* to delay

```facts
Read: Numbers now that will be gone in a second — take the odd-man rush instead
Never: Delay with nobody behind you — a delay only works if support is genuinely coming
Action: If your line is caught out and the bench is calling, get it in deep and change
Never: Delay back over the blue line while a teammate is deep in the zone — you will create a delayed offside and freeze that teammate where they stand
Never: Curl against the wall — curl in open ice. Caught on the boards: forearm and hip, never the point of your shoulder, head up and chin off your chest, and never turn your back to anyone coming at you
```

- When you have numbers now and they will be gone in a second — take the odd-man rush.
- When you have nobody behind you at all. A delay only works if support is genuinely coming. If your line is caught out and the bench is calling, get it in deep and change.
- When a delay would drag you back over the blue line while a teammate is deep in the zone — you will create a delayed offside and freeze them.
- **When you are tight to the wall with anyone closing on you.** This limits *how* you curl, not whether: curl into open ice, and if you cannot get off the boards, take the contact rather than turning away into the wall.

## 6. Drop passes

A **drop pass** is a pass backwards, usually left behind you as you cross paths with a trailing teammate, so that the trailer picks it up moving forward at speed while the defence's attention is still on you.

### How it works

```facts
Action: Attack the line to draw the defenders' eyes and weight, then leave the puck in the space the trailer is skating into
Technique: Drop to a space, not to a stick — you leave the puck, the receiver skates onto it
Action: Keep going hard into the zone as though you still have it, so you take a defender with you
Key: There must be a speed differential — the trailer moving faster than the defenders can adjust
Options: On the power play, attack straight at the first penalty killer to freeze them, then drop underneath into space
```

The carrier attacks the line, drawing the defenders' eyes and weight toward the puck. A teammate arrives from behind and slightly to one side. The carrier leaves the puck stationary or gently rolling into the space the trailer is skating into, then keeps going — hard — into the zone as though they still have it.

![](diagram:entry-drop-pass)

Three things make it work:

1. **The drop is to a space, not to a stick.** You leave the puck; the receiver skates onto it.
2. **The carrier's route after the drop is the whole point.** If you drop the puck and coast, the defence simply switches to the new carrier and nothing has changed. If you drive the net, you take a defender with you.
3. **There is a speed differential.** The trailer must be moving faster than the defenders can adjust. On the power play this is the classic use — the puck carrier attacks straight at the first penalty killer to freeze them, then drops underneath into space.

### When it's right

```facts
Read: A passive neutral zone that backs off and gives you the red line for free — the trailer gets a running start at a stationary wall
Read: Breakouts and power-play entries, where you can choreograph the timing in advance
Read: A defence that has over-committed to the carrier's side
```

- Against a passive neutral zone that backs off and gives you the red line for free — the trailer gets a running start at a stationary wall.
- On breakouts and power-play entries where you can choreograph the timing in advance.
- When the defence has over-committed to the carrier's side.

### Why it fails so often

```facts
Risk: The drop goes backwards into nobody — the worst turnover in hockey, collected by a defender facing your net
Never: Let both players slow down — a drop pass at low speed is a gift
Risk: A receiver with no lane, no support and no speed is the lone attacker the drop was supposed to avoid creating
Never: Use it as a reflex — it answers one picture, a trailing teammate with speed and space behind you
Key: See the trailer before you drop it; if you are dropping it blind and hoping, throw it away instead
```

- **The drop goes backwards into nobody.** The trailer is not there yet, or is on the wrong side, and the puck sits in the neutral zone for a defender to collect facing your net. This is the worst turnover in hockey.
- **Both players slow down.** A drop pass at low speed is a gift.
- **The receiver has no plan.** They pick it up with no lane, no support and no speed and are now the lone attacker the drop was supposed to avoid creating.
- **It is used as a reflex, not a read.** A drop pass is a specific solution to a specific picture — a trailing teammate with speed and space behind you. Used because you didn't know what else to do, it is just a turnover facing the wrong way.

Rule of thumb: **you should see the trailer before you drop it.** If you are dropping it blind and hoping, throw it away instead.

## 7. Dump-ins: five types and the read that calls for each

> **Strong side and weak side.** The **strong side** is the half of the ice the puck is on; the **weak side** is the other half. Both flip the instant the puck crosses the middle, so they describe where the puck is, not fixed positions.

Putting the puck in without possession is not one play. It is at least five, and choosing the wrong one is most of why dump-ins get a bad name. The unifying rule is the same in all cases: **the puck goes to a place your teammate is already going.** A dump-in without a chaser is a change of possession you chose.

### 1. The hard rim

```facts
Action: Fire the puck low and hard around the boards, following the curve of the corner, onward in the direction of play; in CARHA leagues keep the backswing under fifteen inches or it is a slap-shot penalty
Read: Pressure on you from the inside, and a teammate coming down the far wall or the far corner
Goal: Beat the defenceman stepping up on you and arrive at the far side before their partner can rotate
Key: A rim is not a reverse — a reverse goes back against the flow, behind you, for a different purpose
Risk: Too soft and it dies behind the net; too hard and it comes off the far boards to the weak-side defenceman
```

Fire the puck low and hard around the boards, following the curve of the corner, so it travels **onward around the boards** in the direction of play. In a CARHA league keep the backswing short: a wind-up more than fifteen inches behind the puck is a slap-shot minor, and if it injures an opponent, a major that ends your game and sits you out at least the next one.

![](diagram:dump-hard-rim)

- **The read:** pressure is on you from the inside, and you have a teammate coming down the far wall or the far corner.
- **Why:** the rim beats the defenceman who is stepping up on you, and it arrives at the far side before their partner can rotate.
- **Careful:** a rim is not a **reverse**. A reverse goes *back against the flow* of the play, behind you, and it is a different play with a different purpose. Be precise about which one you are calling for.
- **Risk:** a rim that is too soft dies behind the net; a rim that is too hard comes off the far boards straight to the weak-side defenceman.

### 2. The soft area dump

```facts
Action: Place the puck gently into a specific area, usually a corner, so it dies there rather than rebounding
Read: A forechecker arriving in roughly two seconds, and a defence that has to turn and retrieve
Goal: Give your chaser a stationary puck and a body arriving on it, and give the retriever no help from the puck's momentum
Never: Use it when your chaser is four seconds away — you have just given the puck to their defenceman with time
```

Place the puck gently into a specific area — usually a corner — so that it dies there rather than rebounding.

![](diagram:dump-soft-area)

- **The read:** you have a forechecker arriving in roughly two seconds, and the defence has to turn and retrieve.
- **Why:** the value is entirely in the race. A soft dump gives your chaser a stationary puck and a body arriving on it, and gives the retrieving defenceman no help from the puck's momentum.
- **When it's wrong:** when your chaser is four seconds away. Then you have just given the puck to their defenceman with time.

### 3. The chip past a defenceman stepping up

```facts
Read: A defender committing hard and early, with space behind them on the wall
Action: Lift or push the puck past them down the wall and skate around the other side to collect it yourself
Technique: Chip early, off the boards if you need to lift it over a stick, and change your line before you release so you are already accelerating around them
Key: A chip past a defenceman stepping up is a possession play, not a surrender — you are using their own momentum against them
Mindset: Giving up the puck for a second at full speed feels wrong; it is one of the highest-value plays against an aggressive gap
```

The defenceman steps up to meet you at or before the line. You lift or push the puck **past them, down the wall**, and skate around the other side to collect it yourself.

**A chip past a defenceman stepping up is a possession play, not a surrender.** You use the defender's own momentum: they move toward you, the puck goes behind them, you go around them. If your feet are moving, you get it back behind their defence with speed.

![](diagram:dump-chip-past)

- **The read:** a defender committing hard and early, and space behind them on the wall.
- **The technique:** chip it early, off the boards if you need to lift it over a stick, and change your line **before** you release so you are already accelerating around them.
- **Why it's under-used:** giving up the puck for a second at full speed feels wrong.

Note the terminology: a **pinch** is a defenceman stepping down from the *offensive* blue line. A defenceman challenging you in the neutral zone or at their own line is **stepping up**. Both are commonly called pinches in conversation; be precise when you are describing what happened.

### 4. The cross-corner (north-south) dump

```facts
Action: Shoot the puck hard diagonally into the far corner, or straight north down your own side, depending on where your speed is
Read: The retrieving defenceman is on your side, or the strong-side corner is crowded
Goal: Force their weak-side defenceman into a long retrieval on their backhand, facing their own boards, with a forechecker arriving — awkward by design, though no count ranks retrievals
Never: Dump cross-corner into nobody — your far-side winger has to have read it and be going there
```

From one side of the ice, shoot the puck hard **diagonally** across into the far corner, or straight north down your own side depending on where your speed is.

![](diagram:dump-cross-corner)

- **The read:** the retrieving defenceman is on your side, or the strong-side corner is crowded.
- **Why:** it moves the puck away from the defender best placed to get it and forces their weak-side defenceman into a long retrieval on their backhand, facing their own boards, with a forechecker arriving behind them. That is an awkward retrieval by design; which retrievals are hardest is coaching judgement rather than a counted ranking.
- **What you need:** your far-side winger to have read it and be going there. Cross-corner dumps into nobody are pure giveaways.

### 5. The flip / area dump over a trap

**In short:** When a trap clogs the neutral zone, flip the puck over it to land past the defence but short of the goal line.

```facts
Read: The opposition congesting the neutral zone — a trap, most commonly a 1-3-1 — and no route along the ice
Action: Lift the puck high off the ice so it travels over sticks and bodies and lands in open space behind the defence
Technique: Open the blade, get under the puck, and land it past the defence but before the goal line
Goal: Go over the three-player wall rather than through it, and hang the puck long enough for your forwards to run underneath it
Risk: An overshot flip that crosses the goal line untouched from behind the centre red line is icing; whether being shorthanded saves you depends on your book, as Rules Primer sets out
Rule: A team below their opponent's on-ice strength cannot be called for icing (NHL 81.6) — but USA Hockey grants that only "For all Youth 15-Only and above, Girls 16U and above, High School and Adult classifications only" (Rule 624(b)(1))
Rule: NHL 81.6 and PWHL 83.6 withdraw the shorthanded icing exemption where a major expires with the penalty box empty — "Icing will be called" — but only until the next whistle. Rule 20.3 wants a substitute seated at a stoppage before that expiry, immediately where minors are stacked on the major
Rule: On a kill, a clear released before the penalty expires is not icing however late it lands — "prior to the penalty expiring, the icing infraction shall not apply" (NHL 81.6). USA Hockey grants it only from Youth 15-Only and Girls 16U up, judged "at the time the penalty expires" (624(b)(1))
Rule: 🇬🇧 Britain plays the IIHF book, whose 81.6 runs to two paragraphs with no major-penalty case and no classification limit — so a British kill keeps both the shorthanded icing exemption and the release-before-expiry relief
Rule: 🇬🇧 A British kill keeps the icing exemption, but that does not make an empty penalty box free — IIHF Rule 20.3 wants the substitute seated "immediately", and Rule 68.2 prices a failure at a bench minor, which is a 5-on-3 if the whistle comes while the major is still running
```

Lift the puck high off the ice so it travels over sticks and bodies and lands in open space behind the defence.

![](diagram:dump-flip-over-trap)

- **The read:** the opposition are congesting the neutral zone — a **trap**, most commonly a **1-3-1** neutral zone structure with one forechecker, three players across the middle and one back — and there is no route along the ice.
- **Why:** a flip does not have to get through the three-player wall; it goes over it. It also hangs long enough for your forwards to run underneath it.
- **How:** open the blade, get under the puck, and land it **past** the defence but **before** the goal line — a puck crossing the goal line untouched from behind the centre red line is **icing** (some CARHA leagues use the blue lines instead, so ask yours).

**On the penalty kill, take the clear you are entitled to.** With two seconds left on the kill, fire it — if your league grants the shorthanded exemption: a clear *"released from the stick of the player shooting the puck down the ice prior to the penalty expiring"* is not icing (NHL 81.6). 🇬🇧 **Under the IIHF book British hockey plays, both the shorthanded exemption and that relief hold** — its 81.6 has no major-penalty paragraph and no classification limit.

**What it costs.** ⚠️ **Being shorthanded does not always save you from icing.**

- **USA Hockey** grants the exemption, and the same relief, only *"(For all Youth 15-Only and above, Girls 16U and above, High School and Adult classifications only)"* (Rule 624(b)(1)). Below those, a shorthanded team is called for icing like anyone else.
- **The NHL and the PWHL** withdraw it from a team short by a *major* that has *"neglected to ensure there is a player on the penalty bench to exit upon the expiry of the penalty"* — *"Icing will be called"* — from the expiry until the next stoppage (NHL 81.6, PWHL 83.6). If the penalised player was ejected or injured, the team may wait for a stoppage before the major expires to seat a substitute (20.3) — ⚠️ **unless that player also has minors running alongside it, when the substitute is required *"immediately"*.**
- 🇬🇧 **Under the IIHF book**, ⚠️ **an empty box is not free:** Rule 20.3 wants the substitute in the box *"immediately"*, and Rule 68.2 prices a failure as a bench minor — a 5-on-3 if the whistle comes while the major is still running.

See the [Rules Primer](../foundation/rules_primer.md) for the full icing rule and [Special Teams](special_teams.md) for every book's wording.

### How the trapezoid shapes where you aim

**In short:** Where a trapezoid applies, dump to the corners; where there is none, rim it hard and low, and chase the puck, never the goalie.

```facts
Rule: Under the NHL and IIHF books a goalie may not play the puck outside the designated area behind the net — a two-minute delay-of-game minor (NHL Rules 27.8 and 63.2(viii))
Rule: On a trapezoid violation what is judged is the position of the puck, not the goalie's; the only exception is playing it while maintaining skate contact with the crease (NHL Rule 27.8)
Rule: The trapezoid applies in the NHL and IIHF (IIHF Rule 27.7); KHL reportedly too, unverified here. In any British competition plan for their goalie to come for a dumped puck — England Ice Hockey's Rules & Regulations 22.3 call it "not currently enforced", its scope and standing unsettled
Key: USA Hockey, Hockey Canada and CARHA have no trapezoid, and the PWHL's 1.8 drops it though its 65.2(viii) keeps the minor — under all four, plan for the goalie to come out
Action: Dump to the corners, not to the goalie — the corners are legally out of bounds for the goalie, straight behind the net is not
Action: If you must put it behind the net, put it there hard — a rocketing puck is far harder to stop and distribute cleanly
Action: Where there is no trapezoid, rim it hard and low so the goalie cannot set up on it, or dump to the corner furthest from their stick hand
Rule: Where there is no trapezoid the forecheck still pays — USA Hockey 614(c) bars a goalie freezing the puck outside the "goalkeeper's privileged" area, which excludes the corners, and its Casebook Situation 6 penalises beating a lone attacker to a loose puck and falling on it
Rule: Hockey Canada's Interpretation 3 to Rule 10.1(a), clause v, makes freezing it a minor with no warning once the goalie has left the crease — and clause iv names your dump-in exactly: coming out to beat an attacking player to the puck and simply jumping on it
Read: The retrieval is not the save — a goalie covering a save out in front is a separate question (USA Hockey's Casebook excuses it under pressure; Hockey Canada's clauses conflict); see Goaltender
Rule: Chasing their goalie is no licence to hit them — six books say they are not "fair game" outside the crease (NHL, IIHF, PWHL 42.1, USA Hockey 607(d), Hockey Canada 8.5, CARHA 52(b)); charging one ejects you under Hockey Canada 8.5(b), and under USA Hockey 607(b) if reckless; plan on a match (602(a))
Rule: Chasing a goalie from behind changes which IIHF rule you are under — a hit landed on their back that puts them into their own net is checking from behind, where 43.2 leaves the referee no minor to award and 43.1 names the goal frame itself; CARHA 53(b) a match if they cannot protect themselves
Rule: CARHA 52(b) (CARHA adult leagues) mandates a major and game misconduct for charging a goalie in the crease and for any charge that injures, anywhere; 52(a)'s Note makes over two strides a charge. NHL and PWHL 42.5, EIHL 42 mandate the misconduct on a charging major for a face or head injury
Rule: Short of a charge, CARHA 66(b) makes contact impeding a goalie a minor, and deliberate contact "whether in or out of the goal crease" a minor or discretionary major. Injuring them by interference ejects you under Hockey Canada 8.5(b), and under CARHA 66(e) and 30(a), banned a game or more (32(d))
```

**Where the trapezoid applies, dump to the corners rather than straight behind the net.** The goalie cannot play it out there, so your chaser meets a defenceman rather than a goalie setting the puck up for one. A puck that dies straight behind the net is a puck the goalie can legally stop and set for a defenceman, which is exactly the help you were trying to deny them.

If you must put it behind the net, put it there **hard** — the goalie can play it, but stopping a rocketing puck and distributing it cleanly is a much harder job than corralling a soft one.

![](diagram:dump-and-the-trapezoid)

**How the rule works.** Under NHL Rule 27.8, *"[a] goalkeeper shall not play the puck outside of the designated area behind the net"*; doing so is a two-minute minor for delay of game. Two details matter tactically:

- **"The determining factor shall be the position of the puck."** The goalie may stand wherever they like; what is judged is where the puck is when they play it.
- **The only exception is skate contact with the crease** — the minor is not assessed *"when a goalkeeper plays the puck while maintaining skate contact with his goal crease."*

So **the corners are legally out of bounds for the goalie, and the area directly behind the net is not.** Take the painted dimensions from NHL Rule 1.8 — eight feet from each post at the goal line, eleven at the boards, 22 feet across widening to 28. Rule 27.8's "six feet from either goal post" is stale wording from before the 2014-15 widening. The corners are outside it either way.

**Where it applies:** the NHL and the IIHF (IIHF Rule 27.7: 6.80 m along the goal line widening to 8.60 m at the boards). The NHL's commonly given 2005-06 start date is in no rulebook held here, so treat it as received rather than checked. The KHL is said to have one too, unverified here: no KHL rulebook was available.

**In Britain, treat it as unsettled.** The IIHF book is in force and the In-House Rules do not amend Rule 27. But England Ice Hockey's own *Rules & Regulations* 22.3, a 2024-25 edition, say the restricted area is *"not currently enforced to allow ice rinks the necessary time to make the changes to implement this rule"*. Whether that reaches Scotland and Northern Ireland is unsettled. So in any British competition, plan as though their goalie **may** retrieve the puck, ask your league, and look behind the net to see whether the lines are painted.

**Where there is no trapezoid, plan for the goalie to come out.** USA Hockey, Hockey Canada and CARHA put no limit on where behind their goal line a goalie may play the puck. The PWHL's book is split: Rules 1.8 and 27.8 switch the trapezoid off, but Rule 65.2(viii) still writes the minor — so plan for her to come out. With no trapezoid a puck-handling goalie can go anywhere behind their goal line, so **rim it hard and low so the goalie cannot set up on it, or dump to the corner furthest from their stick hand.**

**Under USA Hockey, Hockey Canada or CARHA the forecheck still pays**, because none lets the goalie smother a dumped puck out there. CARHA makes a freeze behind the goal line with the goalie's body out of the crease a minor (58(b)). USA Hockey bars the goalie *freezing* it outside their privileged area, which excludes the corners. Its *Casebook* gives a minor to a goalie who beats a lone attacker to a loose puck and falls on it (614(c), Situation 6; Situation 12 makes the same cover a minor behind the goal line): *"[t]he goalkeeper has an obligation to play the puck with their stick, just like any other player"*. Hockey Canada gives a minor with no warning once the goalie has left the crease, and names your play: a goalie who comes out *"to beat an attacking player to the puck and simply jumps on the puck, causing a stoppage of play"* (Interpretation 3 to Rule 10.1(a)). This is about a retrieval, not a save — a cover straight after a save out in front is a separate question; [Goaltender](../positions/goaltender.md) sets it out.

**Chase the puck, not the man.** If the goalie comes out for it, peel off into the lane they want to pass into. ⚠️ **Make a real effort to avoid the goalie under every book:** the NHL, IIHF and PWHL allow incidental contact when they are playing the puck outside the crease, *"provided the attacking Player has made a reasonable effort to avoid such contact"*, and USA Hockey, Hockey Canada and CARHA write no such permission at all. And arrive on the puck, not on their back: a goalie turned to the boards for a rimmed puck is a player you would be hitting from behind.

**What it costs.** ⚠️ **The dump is no licence to hit the goalie — every book here says they are not "fair game".** USA Hockey 607(d): *"A goalkeeper is NOT 'fair game' because they are outside the privileged area."* Hockey Canada 8.5: *"A penalty under this rule will be called where an opposing player makes unnecessary contact with the goaltender anywhere on the ice."* IIHF 42.1: *"A goalkeeper is not 'fair game' just because they are outside the goal crease area"*, again in 69.4; the British In-House Rules leave both untouched. NHL 42.1, CARHA 52(b) and PWHL 42.1 print it too.

- **USA Hockey:** accidental contact goes to interference and deliberate contact to charging (607(d) Note 1). Its one carve-out is narrow: in a checking game its *Casebook* (607, Situation 5) lets you *"physically engage"* a goalie *"in an effort to gain possession"* of a puck they have outside their privileged area, but never with a check *"to intimidate or punish"* them, which is charging. Once the goalie loses the puck, any avoidable contact is a penalty. The privileged area runs from the end-zone dots to the end boards, so the ice behind the net is inside it, and a check there is *"[a] minor plus a misconduct or a major plus a game misconduct"* (607(c)). ⚠️ **607(b) makes the major plus a game misconduct mandatory for a reckless charge**, and plan on a match penalty too: Rule 602(a) says one *"shall be assessed"* for reckless endangerment, though 607(e) says only *"may also be assessed"*.
- **Hockey Canada:** ⚠️ **any charge on the goalie is an automatic ejection** — *"A Major penalty and Game Misconduct penalty **will** be assessed to any player who charges the goaltender"* (8.5(b); 8.5(e) and Interpretation 3 to Rule 7.4(b) say the same). Injuring the goalie with contact that would otherwise be a minor ejects you too. Its bar for charging reaches an ordinary hard chase: *"two or more strides immediately prior to making contact"*, a jump, an excessive distance, or a violent, unnecessary check (Rule 7.4) — and 7.4(c) makes a charge a **match penalty** where they cannot defend themselves, as a goalie turned to the boards cannot. Hit them from behind there and the price is the harsher of 7.5 (checking from behind) and 8.5.
- **CARHA:** a major and game misconduct is mandatory for charging a goalie *"within the goal crease"*, and for any charge that *"injures an opponent"*, anywhere (52(b)). Out of the crease, the Notes to 52 penalise unnecessary contact and count more than two strides as a charge. Rule 66(b) gives a minor to an attacker who is tripped or falls and fails to try to avoid the goalie, makes impeding them a minor, and makes deliberate contact *"whether in or out of the goal crease"* a minor or a discretionary major. 66(e) makes the major mandatory if interference injures them. ⚠️ **In CARHA each of these majors ejects you** (30(a)), with at least a one-game suspension on top (32(d)). A hit from behind that propels them into the boards or goal net unable to protect themselves is a match penalty (53(b)), with no goalie exception and no lesser substitute. (CARHA-affiliated adult leagues.)
- **NHL and PWHL:** the charging major is the referee's call, but a game misconduct is mandatory when it is for an injury to the face or head (42.5), and the EIHL Casebook's Rule 42 does the same in the Elite League.
- **IIHF:** a charge on a goalie in the crease is penalised (42.1), and a reckless one can go to a major plus a game misconduct (42.4). A hit on a goalie turned to the boards is ⚠️ **checking from behind**: Rule 43 reaches a hit *"from behind into the boards, the goal frame, or on open ice in any manner"*, leaves the referee *"no option to award a minor penalty"* (43.2), and makes the game misconduct automatic once the major is given (43.3). *(That Rule 43 reaches a goalie is a reading of how the book is assembled rather than a sentence it prints: Rule 5.1 makes "Player" cover goalies and 43.1 names the goal frame, but its glossary defines a vulnerable position for "a skater".)*

## 8. Chasing a dump properly

A dump-in is only as good as the chase. Most amateur chases fail before they start because the chaser skates at the puck.

### Angle, don't chase

**In short:** Take away the retriever's easy escape with your route and your stick, rather than skating straight at the puck.

```facts
Never: Skate at the puck — your route should take away the easy escape, not shorten the distance
Read: The easy escape is up the boards or a reverse behind the net to their partner; the hard one is being hemmed on the wall or forced to their backhand
Action: Skate to a point between the retriever and where they want to go, arriving at the puck at an angle rather than head-on
Action: Approach on the retriever's backhand side where you can — that is where turnovers come from
Position: Keep your route on the inside, between the puck and the middle, so if they escape they escape to the boards, not into the slot
Technique: Stick first — leading, on the ice, in the passing lane you are eliminating, before your body arrives
Never: Arrive on the player instead of the puck, or hit anyone who cannot see you coming; if you can see their numbers, pull up — whether you may hit at all is your league's call
Rule: A turned retriever has their back to you, so arriving through them is checking from behind — USA Hockey's Casebook (608 Situation 1) puts the major plus a game misconduct, or a match penalty, on a forceful check on a player standing along the boards; if reckless, plan on the match (602(a))
Rule: Never check a turned retriever — off their head or head first into boards, Hockey Canada 7.5 starts at a minor and game misconduct; on their head, major and game misconduct a must if hurt (7.6, minor and female), highly violent (7.7, Junior and Senior) or a high cross-check; a match if defenceless
Rule: Hockey Canada 7.5(c): a match for a hit from behind, off the head or head first, "into the boards or goal frame" leaving them "unable to protect or defend themselves"; 7.5(b) a major plus game misconduct on violence, a must on injury; on the head, head contact (7.6; 7.7 Junior/Senior), a match too
Rule: The boards are not what makes a check from behind a Match penalty under Hockey Canada — 7.5(c)'s second paragraph assesses one to any player who "deliberately attempts to or deliberately injures an opponent by checking them from behind", wherever the retriever ends up
Key: The measure of a good chase is whether the retriever's only remaining option was one you had covered
```

The retrieving defenceman has two escapes: the **easy** one (usually turning up the boards toward the neutral zone, or reversing behind the net to their partner) and the **hard** one (getting hemmed on the wall, or being forced to their backhand into traffic).

Your route should **take away the easy escape**, not shorten the distance to the puck.

- Skate to a point that puts you **between the retriever and where they want to go**, arriving at the puck at an angle rather than head-on.
- **Approach on the retriever's backhand side** where you can. A defenceman forced to make a backhand play under pressure, facing their own boards, is where turnovers come from.
- **Take a route that keeps you on the inside** — between the puck and the middle of the ice — so if they escape, they escape to the boards, not into the slot.
- **Stick first.** Your stick should be leading, on the ice, in the passing lane you are trying to eliminate, before your body arrives.
- **Name the hit before you arrive.** A defenceman turned to retrieve has their back to you and their head over the puck, and arriving through them is ⚠️ **checking from behind**. Play the puck, not the player: whether you may hit at all is your league's call, never hit anyone who cannot see you coming, and if you can see their numbers, pull up. The whole point of angling is to take the escape away rather than arrive through them; [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md) covers how.

The measure of a good chase is not whether you got the puck. It is whether the retriever's only remaining option was one you had covered.

**What it costs.** ⚠️ **A check from behind on a turned retriever is never cheap.** Under USA Hockey a forceful check on a player standing along the boards is a major plus a game misconduct or a match (Casebook 608, Situation 1); plan on a match and a likely multi-game suspension for a reckless one (602(a)), mandatory with excessive force on a vulnerable player, as a turned defenceman is (608(c)). A game misconduct there also costs you the team's next game (404(b)). Hockey Canada 7.5 starts at a minor **plus a game misconduct**, can go to a major and game misconduct on violence alone, and is a **match penalty** into the boards or goal frame when they cannot protect themselves. Under Hockey Canada a hit on their head is head contact, up to a match, and where you drive them head first into the boards, the harsher of 7.5 and 7.6 applies.

A hit from behind meant to injure is ⚠️ **a mandatory match** under NHL and PWHL 21.1, CARHA 48(a), USA Hockey 602(a) and Hockey Canada 7.5(c). Under the NHL and PWHL, a hit from behind that reaches the head costs the harsher of Rules 43 and 48; under the IIHF, plan on the major and game misconduct, head or not. [Forechecking during a delayed offside](#forechecking-during-a-delayed-offside) sets out every tier.

### F1, F2 and what they owe each other

```facts
Key: F1, F2 and F3 are roles, not people — defined by order of arrival, not by position
Convention: Under this guide's standard 2-1-2 forecheck, F1 goes to the puck and takes away the easy escape; F1's job is to force a decision, not to win the puck
Convention: F2 reads F1's angle and covers the side F1 forced them toward — F1 creates, F2 collects
Convention: F3 stays high, around the top of the circles, protecting against the breakout pass up the middle and the counter-attack — and that job ends if the puck escapes, because F3 is the first man back
Options: In a 1-2-2 F1 pressures but F2 and F3 hold higher, funnelling the puck to one side — fewer pucks recovered low, far fewer odd-man rushes
Options: In a 2-1-2 aggressive/overload both F1 and F2 attack the same side hard; in a 1-3-1 F1 pressures and three lock the neutral zone, which is a trap
Action: Find out which one your team plays — the same dump-in requires a different second man in each
```

**F1, F2 and F3 are roles, not people.** They are defined by order of arrival, not by position — whichever forward gets there first is F1, whoever is next is F2, regardless of whether they are the centre or a winger.

Under the **standard forecheck used throughout this guide — a 2-1-2** — the relationship on a dump-in retrieval is:

- **F1** goes to the puck and takes away the easy escape as described above. F1's job is to force a decision, not necessarily to win the puck.
- **F2** reads F1's angle and covers **the side F1 forced them toward** — usually the strong-side wall or the area behind the net. F2 is the one who actually recovers most pucks; F1 creates, F2 collects.
- **F3** stays high, generally around the top of the circles, protecting against the breakout pass up the middle and the counter-attack. **That job ends if the puck escapes the zone: F3 is the first man back.**

**Other systems assign this differently.** In a **1-2-2**, F1 pressures but F2 and F3 both hold higher, funnelling the puck to one side rather than chasing it; you will recover fewer pucks below the goal line but concede far fewer odd-man rushes. In a **2-1-2 aggressive/overload**, both F1 and F2 attack the same side of the ice hard. In a **1-3-1 forecheck** F1 pressures and three players lock the neutral zone, which is barely a forecheck at all — it is a trap. **Find out which one your team plays**, because the same dump-in requires a different second man in each.

See [Forechecking Systems](forechecking_systems.md) for the full treatment.

## 9. Deciding: carry, dump, or delay

You have roughly half a second. Read these four things in this order.

### 1. Gap

```facts
Read: Gap is the distance between you and the nearest defender — Defending the Rush covers the scale to read it against
Read: Large gap, defender well off and backing in — carry. They have given you the line, so take it and get to the dots
Read: Normal gap, closing — you need a move or an angle: attack the outside shoulder, or chip past them if they are committing
Read: Tight gap, stick on you, standing up at the line — carrying is a low-percentage play. Chip past, dump, or delay
Convention: The tighter gap figure you will also hear is a deliberate coaching choice carried by Neutral Zone Systems, not the default scale
```

**Gap** is the distance between you and the nearest defender.

Read these against the defender's own scale in [Defending the Rush](defending_the_rush.md): **two to three stick lengths is a normal neutral-zone gap**, about a stick and a half at the red line, and about a stick length at their own blue line. Those are coaching convention, and the published pages disagree — the only page found that anchors a distance to the centre red line prints two stick lengths there and one at the blue line, where a second page prints two. The tighter **one to one and a half stick lengths** you will also hear is not part of that scale: it is the coaching description of a deliberately *tight* gap, carried by [Neutral Zone Systems](neutral_zone_systems.md).

- **Large gap (defender more than about three stick-lengths off, backing in):** carry. They have given you the line. Take it and get to the dots.
- **Normal gap (two to three stick-lengths, closing):** you need a move or an angle. Attack the outside shoulder, or chip past them if they are committing.
- **Tight gap (one to one and a half stick-lengths, stick on you, standing up at the line):** carrying is a low-percentage play. Chip past, dump, or delay.

### 2. Support

```facts
Read: Two or more teammates at the line with you, at speed — carry, or pass in
Read: One teammate arriving late — delay. One second of curl usually converts this into the case above
Read: With no support at the line, dump to a place someone can get to, or delay if anyone at all is coming, curling into open ice, never turning your back to anyone coming at you near the wall
Never: Dump and change unless genuinely nobody is coming. Even then, recognise you have already lost this shift
```

- **Two or more teammates at the line with you, at speed:** carry, or pass in. This is what all the timing work in Section 4 was for.
- **One teammate, arriving late:** delay. One second of curl usually converts this into the case above.
- **Nobody:** dump to a place someone can get to, or delay if anyone at all is coming. If genuinely nobody is coming, put it in deep and change — but recognise you have already lost this shift, because dump-and-change was the worst-valued play in the games where Tulsky's group tracked it.

### 3. Score and time

```facts
Read: Tied, or early — play the percentages and be aggressive
Read: Protecting a one-goal lead late — dump it in, forecheck, burn clock, because a goal against costs more than a goal for gains
Read: Chasing a goal late — carry almost regardless; a dump-in with ninety seconds left surrenders possession you cannot afford to lose
Read: On a line change — the entry decision is secondary; get the puck deep or out of danger and change cleanly
Key: This is the one place where the analytics argument bends
```

This is the one place where the analytics argument bends. See [Game Management](game_management.md).

- **Tied, or early:** play the percentages. The break-even is around one-third confidence; be aggressive.
- **Protecting a one-goal lead late:** the cost of a turnover is asymmetric — a goal against costs you more than a goal for gains you. Dump it in, forecheck, burn clock.
- **Chasing a goal late:** carry almost regardless. A dump-in with ninety seconds left surrenders possession you cannot afford to lose.
- **On a line change:** the entry decision is secondary. Get the puck deep or out of danger and change cleanly.

### 4. Who you are

```facts
Read: The carry/dump threshold moves with team strength — Tulsky's group modelled this directly
Action: A skilled line against a weaker pair should be attempting carries at nearly any excuse (Tulsky's 26% figure)
Action: A fourth line against a top pair genuinely does have a higher bar (Tulsky's 44% figure)
Key: Even the 44% bar is still well below "only carry when it's certain"
Key: All three thresholds are modelled from one team's tracked games and an assumed shooting percentage — use the shape, not the number
```

Tulsky's group modelled this directly: the threshold moves with team strength. A skilled line against a weaker pair should attempt carries at nearly any excuse (their 26%); a fourth line against a top pair genuinely has a higher bar (their 44%). **Both are still well below "only carry when it's certain."** All three thresholds come off one decision curve, built from the Washington-game outcomes and an assumed shooting percentage — 9% for and 7% against for the stronger team — so they are a shape to think with, not a dial to set.

### Carry, dump or delay: the one-sentence version

*Carry if you have the line or the support; chip if they are committing; delay if support is one second away — in open ice, never turning your back to anyone coming at you near the wall; dump only to a place a teammate is already skating to; and never dump and change if you can help it.*

### When the entry fails

A failed carry is cheap only if the next few seconds go right, because all of its cost is the rush coming back the other way. So know in advance what each of you does when the puck is lost at the line:

- **The carrier and the nearest forward pressure at once.** The defender who has just won it is usually facing their own net or the boards, so pressure them with your stick, never into their back — a stick on their puck in the first second often wins it straight back or forces a rushed clear.
- **The other forward gets above the puck** — between it and your own net, in the middle of the ice — so a quick pass up the middle has somebody to go through.
- **The defencemen hold their gap rather than charge it.** They were following the entry up; on the turnover they stop and skate backwards instead of jumping at a puck they cannot reach first.

Some teams send only one forward to pressure and have the other two retreat at once, especially late in a game or protecting a lead. Which your team plays is a coaching choice — ask. [Defending the Rush](defending_the_rush.md) covers what to do if the rush gets going anyway.

## 10. Entering against specific structures

### Against a standing-up defence

```facts
Read: They hold the blue line, take away the middle, and dare you to go around
Action: Chip and chase past them — a defenceman standing still has no momentum to recover, so this is the ideal chip situation
Action: Attack the seam between the two of them — two defenders holding a line have a gap between them by definition
Action: Use a late player — standing up requires them to watch the puck, so a fourth attacker arriving behind the play is often unmarked
Action: Change the entry point — swing the puck to the far side and enter on the weak side
```

They hold the blue line, take away the middle, and dare you to go around.

![](diagram:entry-vs-standing-up)

- **Chip and chase past them.** A defenceman standing still at the line has no momentum to recover. This is the ideal chip situation.
- **Attack the seam between the two of them.** Two defenders holding a line have a gap between them by definition.
- **Use a late player.** Standing up requires them to watch the puck; a fourth attacker arriving behind the play is often unmarked.
- **Change the entry point.** Cross the line where they aren't — swing the puck to the far side and enter on the weak side.

### Against a 1-3-1 neutral zone

```facts
Read: One forechecker, three across the middle, one back — the three-across layer is designed to kill the puck at the red line
Never: Skate into the three — that is the trap working
Action: Use the flip / area dump over the wall — this is what it exists for
Action: Move the puck side to side quickly — the three-player layer is strong facing forward and slow rotating laterally
Action: Attack the outside of the wall — the 1-3-1 concedes the boards to protect the middle, so take what it gives
Action: Use a stretch player held high behind their three, which forces the back player to stay honest and thins the wall
```

One forechecker, three across the middle, one back. The three-across layer is designed to kill the puck at the red line.

- **Do not skate into the three.** That is the trap working.
- **Use the flip / area dump over the wall** described in Section 7. This is what it exists for.
- **Move the puck side to side quickly** — the three-player layer is strong facing forward and slow rotating laterally.
- **Attack the outside of the wall.** The 1-3-1 concedes the boards to protect the middle; take what it gives.
- **Use a stretch player.** A forward held high behind their three forces the back player to stay honest, which thins the wall.

See [Neutral Zone Systems](neutral_zone_systems.md).

### Against an aggressive gap

```facts
Read: They close hard and early, well before their own line
Action: Chip past them — an aggressive gap is a defender with committed momentum, and this is the best possible situation for it
Action: Get the puck off your stick early; make the play a stride before they arrive
Action: Use the space behind the defenders — send a teammate into that ice and pass it to them there
Technique: Change of pace beats a move — hard deceleration then re-acceleration ruins a defender who is closing hard
Convention: That a change of pace beats a move is coaching craft, not a measured finding — the IIHF's coach-development manual and Cutting Edge Ice Academy both teach it, and neither measures it against a move or against top speed
```

They close hard and early, well before their own line.

- **This is the best possible situation for the chip past.** An aggressive gap is a defender with committed momentum.
- **Get the puck off your stick early.** Do not skate into a closing defender; make the play a stride before they arrive.
- **Use the space behind them.** Aggressive gap means there is ice behind them by definition. Send a teammate into it and pass it to them there.
- **Change of pace beats a move.** A hard deceleration then re-acceleration ruins a defender closing hard, because their speed is now a liability. **That ranking is coaching craft, not a measured finding** — the IIHF's coach-development manual and Edge Ice Academy both teach the change of pace, and neither measures it against a move. See [Time and Space](../hockey-iq/time_and_space.md).

### On the power play

```facts
Convention: Four across the line, two wide lanes and two inside-dot lanes, so the carrier always has an option
Convention: Attack middle ice first — get the first pass to the dot line or the middle rather than pushing wide early
Options: The drop entry against a kill that sits back — attack the first killer to freeze them, then drop underneath
Options: Placement entries — a planned puck below the goal line, by chip or cross-corner, with the support routes already running
Options: Rim entries when pressure is all on the strong side; without a weak-side support player a rim is just a slow turnover
Key: Controlled entry still produces roughly two and a half times the shots of a dump-in on the power play — that figure is tracking of women's professional hockey, the PWHPA, not Tulsky's NHL sample
Key: The extra man narrows the penalty for getting an entry wrong rather than raising it — dump-ins get better on the power play, so the carry advantage shrinks
```

Entries on the power play are a distinct skill, and the most common failure of a bad power play is not the setup — it is that it cannot get in. That ranking is coaching emphasis, not a counted one.

Common coaching structures (these are choices, not laws):

- **Four across the line**, two wide lanes and two inside-dot lanes, so the carrier always has an option and the kill has to defend four threats.
- **Attack middle ice first.** Pushing the puck wide too early invites pressure and forces a chip. Get the first pass to the dot line or the middle.
- **The drop entry**, especially against a penalty kill that sits back: the carrier attacks straight at the first killer to freeze them, then drops underneath into the space that creates.
- **Placement entries** — a *planned* puck put below the goal line, by chip or cross-corner, into an area where you have a recovery advantage. Not a panic dump; the difference is that the support routes were already running when the puck was released.
- **Rim entries** when pressure is all on the strong side: a long rim to a weak-side support player. Without that support player, a rim is just a slow turnover.

The PWHPA data in Section 1 — women's professional hockey — gives power-play figures too: carry-ins **0.91** shots per entry and pass-ins **0.96**, against **0.38** for dump-ins. **They do not say what they are usually said to say.** Against the even-strength figures (carry-ins 0.73, dump-ins 0.14), the carry-in advantage **narrows** on the power play: from 0.59 to 0.53 shots per entry, and from more than five-to-one to under two-and-a-half-to-one. Dump-ins get *better* with the extra man, which makes sense — a four-player kill is worse at retrieving a puck than a five-player defence.

What survives is the direction: **a controlled entry still produces roughly two and a half times the shots of a dump-in on the power play**, so control still matters — but if your unit cannot enter cleanly against a set kill, a dump-in is a less costly fallback there than at even strength.

## 11. Defending the entry

Everything above, from the other side. This is the summary; [Defending the Rush](defending_the_rush.md) is the full document.

### Gap control

```facts
Key: Gap is the space between you and the puck carrier, and it is the whole game
Risk: Too much gap and they enter with speed, get their head up and make a play — you have conceded the controlled entry
Risk: Too little gap and one move or one chip beats you, and now they are behind you
Convention: The general target taught by most coaches is a gap that shrinks the whole way and never grows — Defending the Rush covers the distances
Key: Your gap should be whatever lets you match their next change of speed
Convention: That a change of speed is the harder thing for a defender to match is coaching craft rather than a measured finding — Time and Space carries the two coaching sources, neither of which measures it
Action: Skate backwards fast enough that you never have to turn — the moment you turn and run, the attacker chooses everything
```

**Gap** is the space between you and the puck carrier, and it is the whole game.

- **Too much gap** and they enter with speed, get their head up, and make a play. You have conceded the controlled entry Tulsky's tracking says is worth roughly double a dump-in in shot terms.
- **Too little gap** and one move or one chip beats you, and now they are behind you.
- **The general target**, on [Defending the Rush](defending_the_rush.md)'s scale, is **two to three stick lengths through the neutral zone, about a stick and a half at the red line, and about one stick length by your own blue line** — shrinking the whole way, never growing. Those distances are coaching convention, and the published pages disagree with them. The principle matters more: **your gap should be whatever lets you match their next change of speed.** That a change of speed is the harder thing to match is coaching craft rather than a measured finding — [Time and Space](../hockey-iq/time_and_space.md) carries the two coaching sources behind it.
- **Skate backwards fast enough that you never have to turn.** The moment a defender turns and runs, the attacker chooses everything.

### Standing up at the line

```facts
Action: Meet the entry at your own blue line rather than backing in
Goal: Kill controlled entries outright — if they cannot cross with the puck, they cannot get the controlled-entry shot rate
Risk: If you are beaten you are beaten badly — flat-footed at your own line with a forward behind you and only your goalie left
Key: It needs good reads, your forwards back, and a partner who slides with you rather than staying deep
```

Meeting the entry at your own blue line rather than backing in.

- **Upside:** it kills controlled entries outright. If they cannot cross with the puck, they cannot have the 0.53–0.62 unblocked shot attempts a successful carry-in was worth at 5-on-5.
- **Downside:** if you are beaten, you are beaten badly — you are flat-footed at your own line with a forward behind you and only your goalie left. It also needs your forwards to be back, because standing up assumes support behind you.
- **Needs:** good reads, and a partner who slides with you rather than staying deep.

### Forcing wide, and "receiving the entry"

```facts
Convention: The traditional approach — the defencemen stay roughly between the faceoff dots, mark the carrier and the slot, and steer the play outside
Goal: Make the entry happen where it is least dangerous
Key: Very hard to beat cleanly, and it keeps the middle protected
Risk: It concedes the entry — the attacker gets in with the puck, gets time on the wall, and can shoot with purpose looking for rebounds
```

The traditional approach, often taught as **receiving the entry**: the defencemen stay roughly between the faceoff dots, mark the carrier and the slot, and steer the play to the outside so the entry happens where it is least dangerous.

- **Upside:** very hard to beat cleanly, and it keeps the middle protected.
- **Downside:** it concedes the entry. The attacker gets in with the puck, gets time on the wall, and can shoot with purpose looking for rebounds.

### More aggressive alternatives — name them

```facts
Options: F1 pressure with the D holding the dots — the first forward back pressures the carrier while the defencemen hold their depth
Options: Squash-and-slide — the defenceman, not the backchecker, forces the carrier outside while the first forward back protects the slot
Technique: In squash-and-slide the two defencemen hold the same depth and slide across together like a foosball bar
Risk: Squash-and-slide demands a great deal from the forwards — one lazy backcheck and the weak side is wide open
Convention: These are systems, not laws — which one your team plays changes whether you step up, hold the dots, or take the puck
Action: Ask, and then talk to your partner about it before the puck drops
```

- **F1 pressure with the D holding the dots:** the first forward back pressures the carrier while the defencemen hold their depth. Requires the defencemen to talk and to step on the cross-ice pass.
- **Squash-and-slide** (as described publicly of Bruce Cassidy's Boston Bruins around 2018-19): the defenceman — not the backchecker — forces the carrier outside, while the first forward back protects the slot. The two defencemen hold the same depth and slide across together like a foosball bar, "squashing" the play against the wall if it goes wide and closing in on it if it goes inside. It buys time and space back at the cost of demanding a great deal from the forwards; one lazy backcheck and the weak side is wide open.

![](diagram:defend-squash-and-slide)

**These are systems, not laws.** Which one your team plays changes your job completely — whether you step up, whether you hold the dots, whether the first forward back takes the puck or the slot. Ask, and then talk to your partner about it before the puck drops.

### The defender's version of the offside rule

```facts
Key: You do not have to prevent the entry, only make it uncontrolled — a stick in the passing lane that forces a dump has done the same job as a hit
Action: Force them to cross before the puck — stepping up at exactly the right instant can make an onside attacker offside
Rule: The defending team passing or carrying the puck into the neutral zone nullifies a delayed offside and resets everything (NHL Rule 83.3(ii); IIHF Rule 83.3(II), the same)
Rule: Head up as you retrieve — the pressure and contact triggers protect the "defending puck carrier" only (NHL and IIHF 83.3, PWHL 85.3, USA Hockey 630(d)), so that protection lapses the instant you make the outlet pass; Hockey Canada 6.11(b)(ii) (the stop-play clause) and CARHA 72(e)(2) write neither
Rule: The shot that caused the delayed offside cannot score — not directly, not off you, the goalie, the boards or an official (NHL Rule 83.4; IIHF Rule 83.4 word-for-word)
Never: Treat your own net as safe here. Under NHL and IIHF 83.4 and PWHL 85.4, the one goal that can still count is the puck you put in yourself — so play it out cleanly
Rule: USA Hockey and Hockey Canada differ — USA Hockey's Casebook (Rule 630, Situation 41) disallows even a defending team's own goal, and Hockey Canada writes no own-goal sentence at all, so nothing settles it there
Rule: Read USA Hockey's Casebook Rule 630, Situation 41 to the end — it bars the goal while the delayed offside is live and on the offending shot, but allows any goal legally scored once the attackers have tagged up, so your own net is live again
```

Everything in Section 2 works for you too:

- **You do not have to prevent the entry, only make it uncontrolled.** A stick in the passing lane that forces a dump has done the same job as a hit.
- **Force them to cross before the puck.** A defender who steps up at exactly the right instant can make an onside attacker offside, because the judgement happens at the moment the puck crosses.
- **On a delayed offside, get the puck out — head up, and not as though the raised arm were a shield.** Passing or carrying it into the neutral zone ends the delayed offside. ⚠️ **No whistle stops a body already committed at speed**, and where the books protect you at all, they protect the *puck carrier*: the NHL, IIHF, PWHL and USA Hockey triggers name the *"defending puck carrier"*, so the protection lapses the instant you make the outlet pass. Hockey Canada and CARHA write no pressure or contact trigger at all. Know where the forechecker is before you put your head down over the puck.
- **Know the disallowed-goal rule, and its one exception.** The shot that caused the delayed offside cannot score, *"either directly or off the goalkeeper, a player, the boards, the glass, a piece of equipment or an official"*, even if they cleared the zone first. **But under the NHL, the IIHF and the PWHL one goal can still count — your own**, so play it out cleanly; that is the British rule too. Hockey Canada writes no own-goal sentence, so do not test it there. USA Hockey's *Casebook* disallows even your own goal off the offending shot, tag-up or not; with no shot on goal, a goal after the tag-up counts.

## Common Mistakes

- **Dumping the puck in because it feels responsible.** Carry it when you have the line or the support; dumping is a tool, not a default. Even counting every failed attempt, a carry was worth about double a dump-and-chase in the tracked games.
- **Dumping and changing.** If you need a change, change before you get the puck. In the tracked Washington games this was the *only* entry with a worse net value than a failed carry-in.
- **Dumping to nobody.** Dump only to a place a teammate is already skating to. A dump with no chaser is a pass to their defenceman.
- **Overshooting a flip dump, or wasting a clear on the kill.** **Land the flip past the defence and short of the goal line; a man down, take the clear you are entitled to.** A clear released before your penalty expires is not icing however late it lands. Check your book: USA Hockey gives no shorthanded exemption outside Youth 15-Only and above, Girls 16U and above, High School and Adult, and the NHL and PWHL withdraw it while an expiring major leaves nobody to come out of the box. 🇬🇧 Under the IIHF book, **seat a substitute in the penalty box as soon as a major empties it** — otherwise it is a bench minor.
- **Arriving at the line early and having to stop.** Be slower thirty feet out and faster at the line.
- **The puck carrier arriving first.** Arrive first and you are one player against two defenders. As the carrier, reach the line last, with wingers already there at speed and onside.
- **Never delaying.** Alone at the line, curl back, protect the puck and come again with a teammate. **Curl in open ice, not against the wall**, and if you are caught there, never turn your back to anyone coming at you, and never duck.
- **Blind drop passes.** If you cannot see the trailer, do not drop it. A drop into empty neutral ice is the worst turnover in the game, because your whole team is facing the wrong way.
- **Skating straight at the puck on the forecheck.** Angle to take away the escape, and arrive on the puck, not the player — never on the back of a defenceman turned to retrieve it, never on anyone who cannot see you coming. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call, and if you can see their numbers, pull up.
- **Dumping straight behind the net where the trapezoid applies.** Use the corners. Straight behind hands the goalie a legal touch.
- **Arriving on the goalie instead of the puck.** **Chase the puck, not the man; if the goalie comes out for it, peel off into the lane they want to pass into.** A goalie out of the crease playing a dumped puck is not *"fair game"* in any book here. If the goalie wins it and heads back, get out of their road: the NHL, IIHF and PWHL can penalise an attacker whose deliberate actions stop the goalie getting home, no contact needed. ⚠️ **Hitting the goalie is never cheap:** a charge on them is an automatic ejection under Hockey Canada, under CARHA in the crease or if it injures them, and under USA Hockey for a reckless one. An ordinary hard chase counts as a charge under Hockey Canada at two strides and under CARHA past two.
- **Coasting over the line on a delayed offside.** **Every attacker in the zone puts a skate on the line, together, and nobody re-enters until the zone is empty.** Being in the air over the line does not clear you, and one straggler keeps the whole team frozen. Under USA Hockey there is no tag-up at all outside Youth 15-Only and above, Girls 16U and above, High School and Adult — the whistle goes immediately.
- **Forechecking during a delayed offside, and expecting the whistle to protect you.** **Hold at the line, tag up, and wait for the arm to drop — under any book.** Only the NHL, the IIHF and the PWHL whistle you for being about to hit the defending puck carrier; elsewhere nothing you can rely on stops play before you arrive. ⚠️ **The carrier's back is to you, so hitting them is checking from behind** — thrown out of the game in most books. Drive them into the boards or goal frame unable to protect themselves and a match penalty is mandatory under Hockey Canada and CARHA; under USA Hockey it is mandatory with excessive force, and plan on one, and a likely multi-game suspension, for any reckless hit. Under Hockey Canada a hit that reaches their head is head contact, up to a match penalty; where it drives them head first into the boards, the harsher of the two applies. Under the NHL and PWHL, one on the head costs the harsher of Rules 43 and 48; under the IIHF, plan on the major and game misconduct, head or not. [Forechecking during a delayed offside](#forechecking-during-a-delayed-offside) sets out every tier.
- **Retrieving the puck on a delayed offside with your head down.** **Head up, and move the puck quickly — the raised arm is not a shield.** No whistle can stop a body already committed at speed, and any protection lapses the instant you pass.
- **Driving the middle with your head down.** **Head up is the condition of the drive, not an extra** — the middle seam is where both defencemen can stand you up. ⚠️ **Under the IIHF book, head up is what keeps your protection against a hit to the head**; skate head down toward the checker and a square check with no upward motion is not penalised as one.
- **Dragging the trail skate with your head down.** **Head up, because the drag puts your body, hands and stick over the line first** — into the ice a defenceman standing up at the line is defending.
- **Driving into the blue paint or the goalie, or arriving head down.** **Stop at the edge of the paint — off the red line around it, short of the goalie, head up — and never meet a post head first or duck into one.** A head-first hit on a post is the same neck and spine injury as one on the boards, and can happen at walking speed. A skate in the paint risks the goal under every book, and under USA Hockey can cost you the zone too. ⚠️ **Hitting the goalie costs more than the goal:** deliberate contact is a penalty in every book, in or out of the crease, and a charge on them can get you thrown out — automatically under Hockey Canada, under CARHA in the crease or if it injures them, and under USA Hockey for a reckless one. Under Hockey Canada and CARHA, even interference worth only a minor ejects you if it injures the goalie. Any CARHA major for contact with the goalie also brings at least a one-game suspension (32(d)).
- **Attacking straight at a defender.** Attack the space between two of them and make somebody choose.
- **Treating your team's entry rules as universal.** Lane discipline, who carries, whether you stand up at the line — these vary. Find out yours.

## Key Takeaways

1. **At the line with the puck, carry it in when you have the line or the support; the dump is a tool, not a default.** A carry is worth about twice a dump in shot attempts, a shape repeated across trackers and leagues — take the direction rather than the decimals.
2. **If you rate yourself better than about one in three to complete the carry, take it.** A failed carry costs far less than it feels, but its cost comes back as the rush off your turnover, so your line must be ready to pressure the moment the puck is lost.
3. **Attack the line at full speed, head up, and let the trailing skate keep you onside.** Your body can already be over the line. Keep the trailing blade on the ice unless your book allows it in the air: the NHL, the IIHF and the PWHL do; USA Hockey and Hockey Canada do not.
4. **On a delayed offside, tag up together; nobody re-enters until the zone is empty.** ⚠️ **Never close on the defenceman retrieving it:** their back is to you, so it is checking from behind — an ejection in most books, and a match penalty in several if they go into the boards or goal frame unable to protect themselves.
5. **Against two defenders, attack the space between them, head up.** Two sharing a seam have to decide who takes you, and the hesitation is your way in. ⚠️ **The seam is also where both can stand you up**, and under the IIHF book, head down costs you part of your protection against a hit to the head.
6. **As the carrier, be last to the line, with your support already waiting at it, onside.** **Be slower thirty feet out so your acceleration peaks as the puck crosses**; if you cross and have to slow down, you were early.
7. **Alone at the line, turn away instead of forcing it — protect the puck, look back up the ice, and come again with support.** **Curl in open ice, not against the wall**, and never turn your back to anyone coming at you there, and never duck.
8. **Never dump without a chaser already going there, and pick the dump to the read** — rim, soft dump, chip past, cross-corner or flip. **Land a flip short of the goal line.** A man down, take the clear you are entitled to, unless your book withholds it.
9. **Where there is a trapezoid, dump to the corners, not straight behind the net.** The NHL and IIHF have one. Under USA Hockey, Hockey Canada and CARHA, against a PWHL goalie, and in any British competition, plan for the goalie to come out: rim it hard and low, or away from their stick hand — under CARHA, with a backswing under fifteen inches.
10. **Angle your chase to cut off the easiest escape; take the puck, not the player.** If you see their numbers or they cannot see you, pull up. If their goalie comes out, peel into their passing lane, never into the goalie: ⚠️ **a charge ejects you under Hockey Canada, CARHA in the crease or if it injures, and USA Hockey if reckless.**
11. **Driving the net, stop at the blue paint's edge, head up; never meet a post head first or duck.** ⚠️ **Deliberate contact with the goalie is a penalty in every book.** A charge ejects you under Hockey Canada, CARHA in the crease and USA Hockey if reckless; under Hockey Canada and CARHA, so does interfering with them in a way that injures them.
12. **Decide in half a second — gap, then support, then score, then matchup.** Carry with the line or support, chip past a defender standing still or closing hard, delay if support is a second away, and dump only to where a teammate is skating. Late in a game, how far the score moves that line is your coach's call.
13. **Defending the entry, keep a gap that shrinks all the way to your own line, and aim to make the entry uncontrolled rather than stop it outright.** A stick that forces a dump does the job of a hit — and in a non-checking league it is the only one you have. Which system you play is your coach's call.
14. **Drop the puck only to a trailer you have already seen, into the space they are skating onto — then keep driving as if you still had it.** If you would be dropping it blind, throw it away instead.

*Limitations worth knowing: Tulsky's paper carries two different scopes and this document keeps them apart. The shots and goals per carried-in and dumped-in entry (Table 1: 0.53–0.62 against 0.22–0.28) are the full 330-game sample. Everything that involves a **failed** attempt, a dump-and-change or a net value across two entries (Table 3: 0.55, 0.48, 0.24, −0.08, +0.42, +0.12, the 14% turnover rate, and the 34/26/44% thresholds derived from them) comes from the **Washington games alone**, because those were the games in which unsuccessful attempts and dump-and-change plays were recorded — the paper labels that table "as measured in Capitals games". The paper's own strategic conclusion runs toward carrying more, not less, and concerns NHL players only: "in general NHL teams probably could benefit from being more aggressive at the blue line."
