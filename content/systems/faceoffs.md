## Overview

A **faceoff**, or **draw**, is how play starts and restarts: an official drops the puck between the sticks of two opponents, and whoever wins it gives their team possession. There are roughly **56 in an average NHL game** — 56.6 per game in 2024-25 and 56.0 in 2025-26, computed from the NHL's official statistics — and they are the only moments where you know in advance where the puck will be and when.

**Most of them matter far less than people think.** Across eleven NHL seasons, a team's faceoff win percentage explains only around 6% of the variation in the share of standings points it takes, and the relationship is unstable enough to have gone slightly negative in one of those seasons. That is a **team-level** figure, and it is the one people mean when they say faceoffs are overrated. So do not chase your faceoff percentage. Chase the ten or fifteen draws a game where the outcome actually changes something — a coaching estimate of how many carry real consequence, not a counted figure.

**The one loss that hurts immediately is a draw in your own end.** Lost cleanly, it puts a set opponent running a prepared play into the most dangerous ice on the rink while your five are stationary and facing the wrong way.

**The cheapest improvement available to any team is telling your linemates where they may stand.** If anyone but the two takers moves into the faceoff circle before the puck is dropped, under NHL rules it is your **centre** who is thrown out of the faceoff, not the player who moved. **Under the IIHF book — which every level of British hockey is played under — that used to be the largest split between the two books on faceoffs, and it has closed.** In the 2025/26 edition your centre stayed and the team spent its one warning for that draw instead; the 2026/27 edition ejects him, in the NHL's own words. Either way a second violation by your team on the same draw is a two-minute bench penalty. (One genuine split survives: the IIHF makes winning the draw with your skates a violation, and the NHL does not.) The standard is the NHL's and the IIHF's, in near-identical words: both skates outside the circle, and outside the short hash marks painted beside it, with contact on the line permitted and crossing it not, and your stick allowed inside either provided it touches no opponent or their stick. Under those two books you may reach; you may not step. **⚠️ Do not carry the reaching half into a North American game.** USA Hockey's Casebook answers it flatly — *"Both players must be completely behind their respective hash marks. This includes all sticks and all skates"* — Hockey Canada splits it, its glossary treating a stick *"inside the face-off circle"* as acceptable and a stick *"in the area between the hash marks"* as encroachment, and CARHA Rule 57(a) requires every other player to be on side *"and their sticks"* with them. "The rule everybody gets wrong" below sets all five out.

**Watch the official's hand, not your opponent** — you are training a reaction, not fighting a duel, and it repays the practice: among NHL centres who took at least 500 draws in 2025-26, win rates ran from 62.1% down to 39.7%, a spread far too wide to be luck. **That is a skater-level figure and the 6% above is a team-level one — a different unit of analysis, and the two are not comparable with each other.**

## Why Faceoffs Matter — Honestly

There is a bad version of this argument and a good one. The bad version says faceoffs win games. They do not, and pretending otherwise makes everything else you say less credible.

### What the evidence actually shows

**Team faceoff percentage barely predicts winning.** Computed from the NHL's official team statistics: across the eleven completed seasons from 2015-16 to 2025-26 (344 team-seasons), the correlation between a team's faceoff win percentage and its **points percentage** (the share of available standings points a team actually took — points earned divided by two per game played) is **r = +0.25**, meaning faceoff percentage accounts for **about 6% of the variation** in how many points teams take. It is positive — but it is weak, and it is unstable. In 2016-17 the relationship was actually *negative* (r = −0.24), and in 2025-26 it was essentially zero (r = +0.03).

**And at team level there is barely any spread to predict with.** Computed from the NHL's public team-summary data: across the **32 NHL teams** in the **2025-26 regular season, all situations**, team faceoff win percentage ran from about **45.9% to 54.5%**, with the league mean sitting at **50.0% by definition** — every draw is a win for one team and a loss for the other, so the teams must average out to even. That is a total range of under nine percentage points between the best and worst faceoff teams in the league — far narrower than people assume when they quote a team's faceoff rank.

**Do not confuse that with the spread between individual centres, which is much wider.** The team figure blends every skater a club sends to the dot — including the wingers who take emergency draws (eleven of the 120 skaters with 500+ faceoffs in 2025-26 were wingers) — so it pulls hard toward 50%. The skater-level range described in the next section — **62.1% down to 39.7%** across the 109 centres with 500 or more draws in 2025-26 — is a different unit of analysis and roughly two and a half times as wide. Team faceoff percentage tells you about a roster; skater faceoff percentage tells you about a player.

**Team faceoff percentage barely predicts possession either.** **Garret Hohl**, writing at Hockey Graphs, found an R² of about 0.20 between team faceoff percentage and Corsi percentage (shot-attempt share, the standard possession proxy), across every 82-game NHL team-season from 2007-08 on — a real relationship, but one that leaves four fifths of the variation unexplained.

**The best faceoff man in the league is worth a handful of goals.** Here the model and the man who applied it are two different people, and it is worth keeping them straight. The per-zone even-strength goal values are **Gabriel Desjardins'**; **Garret Hohl** applied them at Hockey Graphs to the faceoff differentials of the NHL's 100 most frequent draw-takers, in his own words *"using data originally brought up by Gabriel Desjardins."* That exercise put the best of them (Boyd Gordon) at **+4.34 goals** and the worst (Ryan Nugent-Hopkins) at **−2.73** — a spread of **7.07 goals** from the best draw-taker in the league to the worst. For comparison, the same piece set that against the gap between top-decile and bottom-decile possession players, which its chart puts at roughly **10–15 goals per 82 games** — *read off the figure, not stated as a number in the article text, so treat it as approximate.* (*The 82-game basis is stated for that comparison chart only; Hohl's player table gives no time base, so read +4.34 and −2.73 as season-scale rather than exactly per-82.*) Hohl's own conclusion is worth quoting: faceoffs "matter, but they do not matter on average much more than any other of the many puck battles that occur throughout the game."

**The advantage decays in seconds.** The finding is **Craig Tabita's**, published at hockeyprospectus.com: shot volumes after a draw even out to roughly the same level about ten seconds later, regardless of where the draw was. It reaches this document at two removes — Hockey Graphs' women's-hockey piece quotes **Alison Lukan's** 2017 summary of Tabita in *The Athletic*, which is where the sentence *"after 10 seconds of play, regardless of where a face-off occurs … shot volume evens out to be more or less the same"* comes from. The figure here rests on Lukan's summary rather than on Tabita's original. A faceoff win buys you about one possession's worth of head start, not a scoring chance.

### So why bother?

```facts
Priority: Prepare the situational draws, not the league-average one
Risk: A defensive-zone draw lost cleanly puts a set opponent into the most dangerous ice while you are stationary
Action: Fix the free failure modes — a winger creeping over a line is an information problem, not a skill one
Key: A 22-point spread between the best and worst NHL centres means this is learnable, not luck
Key: The spread between NHL centres is computed here from NHL official statistics — the 109 who took at least 500 draws in 2025-26, running from 62.1% down to 39.7%, median 51.8%
Never: Chase your faceoff percentage. The draws worth preparing are the ten or fifteen a game that change something; that count is a coaching estimate, not a measurement
```

Because *average* is the wrong frame. The honest case for faceoffs is not "win more draws, win more games." It is four narrower claims, and all four survive the evidence.

- **Situational draws are not average draws.** A defensive-zone draw while shorthanded, with your best penalty killers already tired, is not the same event as a neutral-zone draw at 3-0 in the first period. The league-average draw is worth almost nothing. The specific ones described later in this document are worth a great deal, and they are a small enough share of the total that a team can prepare for all of them.
- **A lost defensive-zone draw is a shot against, quickly.** The reason coaches obsess over these is not the possession — it is that losing one cleanly puts a set opponent with a set play into the highest-danger area of the ice while your five skaters are stationary and facing the wrong way. That is the one faceoff outcome that is genuinely, immediately dangerous.
- **The failure modes are free to fix.** Getting your centre ejected because a winger crept over a line is not a skill problem. It is an information problem, and it costs nothing to solve. See the violations section.
- **It is learnable, and the gap between good and bad is enormous.** Computed from the NHL's official skater statistics: 120 skaters took at least 500 draws in 2025-26, and 109 of them are listed as centres (the other eleven are wingers). Among those 109 centres the median win rate was **51.8%**, the tenth percentile 45.4%, the ninetieth 57.7%, and the range ran from **62.1% down to 39.7%** — a **22-point spread** on a single repeatable, isolated action. That is enormous, and it tells you this is learnable, because nothing that wide is luck. **What it does not tell you is that the draw beats everything else you could work at** — nobody has computed that spread for any other hockey skill, so treat "the most improvable skill in hockey" as a coaching claim and this number as the thing that is actually measured. (This is the figure [Switching Positions](../positions/switching_positions.md) leans on when it tells a winger moved to centre that the draw is worth working at.)

**The practical version: don't chase your faceoff percentage. Chase the ten or fifteen draws a game where the outcome actually changes something, and stop giving away the free ones.** *("Ten or fifteen" is a coaching estimate of how many draws carry real consequence, not a counted figure — nothing here counts them.)*

## The Nine Faceoff Locations

Every faceoff in the game happens at one of **nine spots** (NHL Rule 76.2). There is no such thing as a draw somewhere else — Rule 76.2 explicitly forbids one within fifteen feet of the goal or the side boards, or anywhere that isn't a marked spot.

### Where they are, and what's painted on the ice

```facts
Rule: Every faceoff happens at one of nine marked spots, and nowhere else — NHL and IIHF Rule 76.2, USA Hockey Rule 612(b)
Position: Wingers stand at the hash marks, and those marks are a hard boundary
Rule: The centre's restraining lines define where your feet may go (Rule 1.9)
Rule: The four neutral-zone dots have no circles and no hash marks — all players other than the centres stand "uniformly back" (NHL and IIHF Rule 76.7)
Key: Encroachment is called loosely in the neutral zone and tightly in the end zones
```

**The NHL, the IIHF and USA Hockey all say the same thing about the count, in three sets of words.** NHL Rule 76.2 opens *"All face-offs must be conducted on one of the nine (9) face-off spots located on the rink."* IIHF Rule 76.2 makes the identical provision with its own punctuation and its own singular — *"All 'face-off' must be conducted on one of the nine (9) face-off spots located on the rink"* — in the 2025/26 and 2026/27 editions alike. USA Hockey Rule 612(b) puts it differently again: *"All face-offs will be conducted at one of the nine face-off spots."* What is painted around those spots is Rule 1.9, and the table below is the NHL's version of it.

| Spot | Count | Markings (NHL Rule 1.9) |
|---|---|---|
| **Centre ice** | 1 | A 12-inch blue spot, with a circle of 15-foot radius |
| **Neutral-zone dots** | 4 | 2-foot red spots, 5 feet outside each blue line, 44 feet apart. **No circles** |
| **End-zone dots** | 4 | 2-foot red spots with 15-foot-radius circles, 20 feet out from the goal line and 22 feet either side of the middle |

*(The dimensions in that table are the rink's, and [Rink Map](../foundation/rink_map.md) covers them — it carries the same figures alongside the rest of the ice, with the IIHF metric equivalents.)*

Two markings inside the end-zone circles matter enormously and almost nobody can name them:

- **Hash marks** — red lines two inches wide and two feet long, running parallel to the goal line. Rule 1.9 puts two of them "at the outer edge of **both sides** of each face-off circle", set **5 feet 7 inches apart** — so **four per circle**, a pair on the boards side and a pair on the middle-of-the-ice side. These are where the wingers stand, and they are a hard boundary (see violations).
- **The centre's restraining lines** — the little painted "L" shapes a foot outside the spot: two lines four feet long and eighteen inches apart running parallel to the side boards, plus a line 2 feet 10 inches long parallel to the end boards. These define where the centre's feet may go.

**The four neutral-zone dots have no circles and no hash marks at all.** Rule 76.7 acknowledges this directly and tells linespersons to use judgement, requiring only that everyone stand "uniformly back" as if a circle were there. Practically: encroachment is called loosely in the neutral zone and tightly in the end zones.

### How often each zone comes up

Computed from the NHL's public team-faceoff data for the **2025-26 regular season** (**team-level, all situations**), a team's draws split roughly:

| Zone | Share of a team's draws | Draws per game |
|---|---|---|
| **Offensive zone** | **34.6%** | ~19–20 |
| **Defensive zone** | **34.6%** | ~19–20 |
| **Neutral zone** (including centre ice) | **30.8%** | ~17 |

Those come out of the roughly **56 draws in an average NHL game** (56.0 per game in 2025-26; see the Overview). The percentages are the computed figures; the per-game counts in the right-hand column are simply those percentages applied to 56 draws, rounded.

Three things follow from that table, and they are worth holding onto:

- **The offensive and defensive shares are identical, and they have to be.** Every offensive-zone draw for you is a defensive-zone draw for the other team, so once you average across the league the two numbers are the same number seen from opposite ends. If someone quotes you a team with more offensive- than defensive-zone draws, that is a real fact about *that* team's play; league-wide it cannot happen.
- **End-zone draws are about 69% of all draws** — so most faceoffs *are* in an end zone, and the defensive-zone draws described later in this document are not a rare event. You take about twenty of them a game.
- **Offensive-zone draws are only about a third of the total**, which matters when you read "sheltered usage" or zone-start percentage in a statistics table. A coach loading a favoured player with offensive-zone starts is redistributing within that third, spread across a whole bench — which is one reason the measured effect on any single player's season numbers is modest. See [How to Watch Hockey](../off-the-ice/how_to_watch_hockey.md) for how zone starts are used and misused as a context statistic.

### What sends the draw to which spot

![](diagram:faceoff-where-the-draw-goes)

```facts
Action: Work out where the next draw will be and start skating there before the whistle finishes
Rule: Centre ice takes period starts, draws after a goal, and an erroneous icing call (Rules 76.2, 81.2)
Rule: After an icing or a penalty the draw is in the offending team's end (NHL and IIHF Rules 81.2, 76.2); the other team's choice of dot is NHL and IIHF only, USA Hockey 612(b) and 624(a) giving none
Rule: Hockey Canada 6.4(b) gives the choice of end-zone dot to the attacking (non-offending) team, in Junior and Senior divisions only and only after an icing or a time penalty, and that team loses it unless it tells the referee its dot "prior to the start of the line change procedure"
Rule: A stoppage between the end-zone dots and the end boards draws at the spot on the side it happened, "unless otherwise expressly provided by these rules" (NHL and IIHF Rule 76.2) — USA Hockey 612(b) takes the nearest spot, but only where it calls for a last play face-off
Rule: A stoppage the attacking team caused in its attacking zone is a different draw under USA Hockey — 612(c) sends it to the nearest neutral-zone spot, and 612(g) moves any draw back toward the goal of the team that caused the stoppage
Rule: Offside on a carry-in draws at the neutral-zone dot outside that blue line (Rule 83.6)
Rule: Offside on a pass or shot sends the draw back to the zone the puck came from (Rule 83.6)
Rule: A post-whistle scrum that pulls players inside the circle's outer edge moves the draw to the neutral zone — the attacking team's point men and bench, and any non-offending player, under NHL and IIHF 76.2; any attacking player under USA Hockey 612(d), Hockey Canada 6.3(d)(vi)
Rule: A puck out of play draws at the nearest spot in the zone it was shot from (NHL 85.1); a puck shot into the players' bench draws no penalty, but the IIHF Handbook exempts only one shot straight in, not one clearing the glass into it (63.2(iii) NOTE; Situation 63.24)
Never: Go offside by firing the puck over the blue line — carrying it over and being whistled costs you far less
```

This is the part worth actually learning, because knowing where the next draw will be lets you start skating there before the whistle finishes.

**To centre ice:**
- The start of every period, and after every goal.
- A penalty assessed *after* a goal, or at the end or start of a period (Rule 76.2).
- A linesperson's **erroneous** icing call (Rule 81.2) — the one time a mistake gets fully neutralised.
- A premature-substitution violation when play was stopped beyond the centre red line (Rule 76.2).

**To an end-zone dot in the offending team's own end:**
- **Icing.** And here is the detail almost nobody knows: since 2019-20, **the attacking team chooses which of the two dots** (Rule 81.2). They will pick the one their centre is strong on. See "Situational Faceoffs".
- **A penalty.** One of the two dots in the penalised team's end — and again, **the team awarded the power play chooses which dot** (Rule 76.2). Three exceptions send it to centre ice or the neutral zone instead.
- **Intentional offside** — deliberately going offside to force a whistle (Rule 83.7).
- **A goalie freeze, or any stoppage low in the zone.** Rule 76.2: when the stoppage happens between the end-zone dots and the end of the rink, the draw is at the end-zone dot **on the side where the stoppage occurred**. So which side a goalie freezes the puck on determines which circle you play. Goalies know this. Note the rule's own escape clause, because it is what all the exceptions above and below hang off: the side-of-the-stoppage answer holds *"unless otherwise expressly provided by these rules"* — the IIHF prints the same qualification at its own 76.2 — so a stoppage that another rule has already spoken for goes where that rule sends it.
- **A defender or goalkeeper accidentally knocking their own net off.** **Rule 63.8** sends that draw *"to one of the end zone face-off spots in the defending zone"* — the offending team's own end — and **the attacking team chooses which of the two dots**, as it does after an icing. The same rule bars that team from making any substitution before the draw. **IIHF Rule 63.8 is the same rule under the same number**, writing *"a defending Player"* where the NHL writes *"a defending skater or goalkeeper"*, and it reads the same in the 2025/26 and 2026/27 editions.
- **The goalie freezing the puck on the back of the net with stick or glove, or a defender shielding an attacker from playing it off the back of the net** — Rule 85.2 sends these to "one of the face-off spots in the defending zone." Note that a puck **merely lodged in the outside netting** is *not* automatically a defensive-zone draw: that is Rule 85.5, which puts it at "either of the adjacent face-off spots or at the nearest face-off spot in the zone from which the puck was shot, unless otherwise covered in these rules."

**To a neutral-zone dot:**
- **Offside where the puck was carried over the line** — the draw is at the neutral-zone dot outside that blue line (Rule 83.6).
- **A stoppage in the neutral zone not attributable to either team** — the nearest dot outside the blue line; if it's genuinely unclear which is nearest, the tie goes to the spot giving the **home** team the greatest territorial advantage (Rule 76.2).
- **A goal illegally scored by hand, kick or high stick** (Rule 76.2).
- **A scrum after the whistle that draws the point men in.** Rule 76.2 does this in two sentences. The first catches the **attacking** team's point men and anyone coming off its bench; the second catches *"any of the non-offending players"* — any of them, not just the defencemen. Either way, coming inside the outer edge of the end-zone circle during an altercation moves the draw out to the neutral zone. This is a real and frequently-conceded cost: your team just turned a won defensive-zone draw into a neutral-zone one, or lost an offensive-zone draw entirely, by joining a shoving match.

**Puck out of play:** the nearest spot in the zone the puck was shot or deflected out from. If it came from the neutral or defending zone, the spot chosen is the one giving the **offending team the least territorial advantage** (Rules 85.1 and 85.5). ⚠️ **A puck lodged in the netting is not automatically a draw back in the shooting team's end.** Rule 85.5 gives that case its own sentence, together with a puck *"frozen between opposing players"*: the ensuing face-off is *"at either of the adjacent face-off spots or at the nearest face-off spot in the zone from which the puck was shot, unless otherwise covered in these rules."* Two useful exceptions: if the puck leaves the rink **directly off a faceoff**, the draw stays at the same spot and nobody is penalised (Rule 85.1, and the note to Rule 63.2). And a puck **shot into the players' bench** is not a delay-of-game penalty — that is the NOTE to Rule 63.2(iii) in its own words, *"When the puck is shot into the players' bench, the penalty will not apply."* **IIHF Rule 63.2(III) carries that Note in the same terms** — *"When the puck is shot into the Players' bench, the penalty will not apply"* — in the 2025/26 and 2026/27 editions alike. ⚠️ **That is not licence to aim at the bench, and under the IIHF it is narrower than it sounds:** only a puck shot **directly** into the bench where there is no glass escapes the minor, so a clearance that goes over the glass and drops into the bench is still two minutes. [Risk Management](../hockey-iq/risk_management.md) carries the situations that decide it. **And note which bench, because that is a separate rule:** Rule 85.1 says that if the puck enters the **opposing** team's bench *"through an open bench door"* (or makes contact with the gloves or body of an opposing player hanging over it), the draw is in the **neutral zone adjacent to that bench**, not back in the zone the shot came from. [Risk Management](../hockey-iq/risk_management.md) carries the delay-of-game rule across all four books in full.

**Offside where the puck was shot or passed in** (rather than carried): the draw goes back to the nearest spot in the zone the pass or shot came from, again picking the option giving the offending team the least territorial advantage (Rule 83.6). So a sloppy stretch pass from your own end that goes offside sends the draw all the way back into your own zone. Carrying it over the line and getting whistled costs you far less than firing it over. [Zone Entries](zone_entries.md) draws the tactical conclusion from that asymmetry.

**⚠️ USA Hockey and Hockey Canada send some of these draws somewhere else, and one of them makes you ask for what you want.** **USA Hockey gives no choice of dot at all.** Rule 612(b) sends a penalty draw to *"a defending zone face-off spot of the offending team"*, and Rule 624(a) sends an icing draw to *"an end zone face-off spot in the defending zone of the offending team"* — a spot, not a pick. That book also decides the stoppage low in the zone differently: where the NHL and the IIHF put the draw on the side it happened, USA Hockey Rule 612(b) sends a stoppage *"between the end zone face-off spots and the nearest end boards"* calling for a last play face-off to *"the nearest end zone face-off spot"*.

**That is narrower than it sounds, and the narrow version is the one that decides where you stand.** A **last play face-off** is USA Hockey's own term for a stoppage that is nobody's fault in particular, or both teams' — not for every stoppage low in the zone. **A stoppage the *attacking* team caused in the attacking zone is a different draw entirely**: Rule 612(c) sends that one to the nearest neutral-zone spot. And Rule 612(g) overrides on top of all of it — *"[a] team causing a stoppage of play shall not gain a territorial advantage with the ensuing face-off"*, which puts the draw at the nearest spot **closer to their own goal**. So under that book, the side that caused the whistle skates the wrong way for it.

**Hockey Canada does give a choice of dot — but not to you, if you were the one who caused the stoppage — and then rations it twice over.** Rule 6.4(b) grants it *"[i]n Junior and Senior divisions"* only, after an icing or a time penalty — and it is not automatic: *"[t]he attacking team must indicate their desired face-off location to the Referee prior to the start of the line change procedure. If they fail to do so, then the face-off will take place at the location prescribed by Rule 6.3 – Face-off Location."* **So under that book the choice belongs to the *attacking* team — the side that did not ice the puck or take the penalty — and even they lose it unless they name the dot early.**

**And the post-whistle scrum catches more attacking players in both of those books than it does in the NHL's — though the NHL's rule is wider than it is usually quoted.** Rule 76.2 spends one sentence on the **attacking** team's point men and anyone coming off its bench, and then adds a second that most summaries drop: *"Should any of the non-offending players enter into the attacking zone beyond the outer edge of the end zone face-off circle during an altercation, gathering or 'scrum,' the ensuing face-off shall take place in the neutral zone near the blue line of the defending team."* The IIHF prints the same sentence at its own 76.2, in near-identical wording — *"Should any of the non-offending Players enter the attacking zone…"* **What the other two books add is that on the attacking side it is not just the point men and the bench.** **USA Hockey Rule 612(d) catches any attacking player**: the draw moves to the nearest neutral-zone spot *"if any attacking player enters the end zone further than the outer edge of the face-off circles nearest the blue line"*. **Hockey Canada Rule 6.3(d)(vi) is the same**, moving it out when *"any player of the attacking team enters into the end-zone beyond the top of the face-off circles after play has stopped"*. So joining the shoving match as an attacking forward costs you the draw under USA Hockey and Hockey Canada where it might not under the NHL's book or the IIHF's — the reverse of the direction people expect.

## The Procedure and the Rules

### The sequence, start to finish

```facts
Rule: The visiting team gets up to five seconds to change, then the home team up to eight (NHL and IIHF Rule 82.2) — USA Hockey 204(a) and Hockey Canada 6.1(d) give the home team five, not eight
Rule: CARHA has no last change at all — Rule 19(g) gives both teams one shared ten-second window and names neither the home nor the visiting team, so there is no matchup to hunt off it
Rule: After the linesperson's whistle both teams have no more than five seconds to line up — NHL and IIHF Rule 76.3 and USA Hockey 613(c); Hockey Canada and CARHA write no five-second line-up clock at all
Rule: The sticks go down before the drop, and the order they go down in is not the same in every book (Rule 76.4)
Rule: The faceoff ends when the puck has been legally dropped (Rule 76.1)
Rule: The five-second limit is not enforced in the last two minutes of regulation or in overtime (NHL and IIHF Rule 76.3) — USA Hockey 613(c) writes no such exception and Hockey Canada and CARHA set no five-second line-up clock at all
Rule: Hockey Canada has no five-second line-up clock but does have an untimed one — after a referee's warning, failing to commence play or "unnecessarily delay[ing] the resumption of play" is delay of game (Hockey Canada 10.1(vii))
Rule: CARHA has no line-up clock either, and no warning — its Rule 57(c) lets the official replace a centre who is slow into position at that draw, so "no clock" is not "take your time"
Key: Last change is why the away centre so often finds himself against a specialist — in the four books that have last change at all
```

1. **Line change.** The referee gives the **visiting team up to five seconds** to change, raises a hand, then gives the **home team up to eight seconds** (Rule 82.2). This is the mechanism behind "last change" — the home team sees the visitors' personnel before committing its own, which is why home coaches can hunt matchups and visiting coaches cannot. It is also why the away team's centre often finds himself against a specialist. Note that the *right* itself comes from Rule 82.1 rather than 82.2, and it carries an icing exception — see the post-icing draw below.
2. **The referee lowers their hand.** No more changes.
3. **The linesperson blows the whistle.** Both teams now have **no more than five seconds** to line up (Rule 76.3).
4. **The sticks go down** (Rule 76.4). Order matters — see below.
5. **The puck is dropped** between the two blades. The faceoff formally "ends when the puck has been legally dropped" (Rule 76.1).

**And you have to go straight to the faceoff dot.** Rule 82.3: *"Players must proceed directly to the location of the face-off to participate in the ensuing face-off. Any attempts to delay the game by stalling or otherwise unnecessary actions by either team shall result in the assessment of a bench minor penalty for delaying the game."* The IIHF prints that paragraph in the same words. Dawdling to the dot to buy your bench a breath is a two-minute penalty rather than a tactic — and unlike the slow-to-the-dot warning in Rule 82.2(vii), this one carries no warning first.

**In the last two minutes of regulation and at any point in overtime, the five-second limit is not enforced** (Rule 76.3). The whistle still goes, but the linesperson will take the time to get it right. This changes how late-game draws feel: there is more time to set your alignment, more time to look at the bench, and more time for both centres to fidget for an advantage.

**⚠️ Three of the five books run that clock differently, so do not carry the eight seconds into a North American game.** **USA Hockey Rule 204(a)** gives the visitors five seconds and then *"the home team shall have 5 seconds to complete any player substitutions"* — five and five, not five and eight. **Hockey Canada Rule 6.1(d)** does the same, allowing *"a five-second period during which the Visiting Team may make a player change"* and then, arm still raised, *"a five-second period during which the Home Team may make a player change."* The home team still changes second under both, so last change survives; it is simply three seconds shorter. ⚠️ **CARHA is a third answer, and under it last change does not exist.** Its **Rule 19(g)** gives both teams a single shared window — the referee *"will then allow a ten-second period during which the teams may make player changes"*, after which an arm goes up and no further changes may be made. There is no visiting-team window, no home-team window and no order between them; the phrases *"Visiting Team"* and *"Home Team"* appear nowhere in that rule. **So in a CARHA game do not plan a matchup off last change — there is none to have.** **And the late-game relaxation is in none of the three — though only one of the three has a clock to relax.** USA Hockey Rule 613(c) states the five-second line-up limit with no exception for the last two minutes or for overtime, so expect to be hurried late in a tight game under that book. **Hockey Canada writes no five-second line-up limit at all** — its only five-second periods are the two line-change windows in 6.1(d) — **and CARHA writes none either**: its Rule 57 was read in full and sets no line-up clock, and the only counted period anywhere in that book is the shared ten seconds for changes in Rule 19(g). Under those two, what hurries you late is not a count but an official — Hockey Canada's untimed delay-of-game backstop, immediately below, and CARHA Rule 57(c), under which an official may *"order them replaced at that face-off"* the first time you are slow into position, with no warning first.

⚠️ **That last one is a missing clock, not a missing limit, and the difference is two minutes.** Hockey Canada Rule 10.1 lists among the acts that are delay of game a team which, *"after a warning by the Referee, fails to place the correct number of players on the ice and commence play"*, or where *"any player(s) of that team unnecessarily delays the resumption of play"* (10.1(vii)). Nothing is counted and nothing is timed — but a warning comes first, and after it, dawdling to the dot is a penalty like anywhere else. Do not read "no five-second limit" as "take as long as you like."

### Who puts their stick down first

```facts
Rule: At centre ice the visiting player places their stick first (NHL Rule 76.4)
Rule: At the other eight spots the defending player places first, then the attacking player (NHL Rule 76.4)
Rule: The IIHF book — the one every level of British hockey is played under — matches the NHL word for word at all nine spots, in both its 2025/26 and 2026/27 editions (IIHF Rule 76.4)
Rule: ⚠️ Britain overrides that — the IHUK In-House Rules 2026-27 say "Either centre can now put their stick down first for a face-off", so the order tells a British reader nothing (Section 10, Rule 76)
Rule: ⚠️ Britain requires the whole blade in the white, not the toe IIHF 76.7(IV) asks for — "the whole of their stick blades (not toe or heel)" (In-House Section 10, Rule 76); short of it is a violation, and a violation replaces your centre
Rule: USA Hockey reverses this — the attacking player places first everywhere but centre ice (USA Hockey Rule 613(a))
Rule: Hockey Canada is a third answer — the visiting player places first at every spot, attacking or defending, though a home player who wants to place first "are allowed to do so, if it does not cause a problem" (Rule 6.2(a), Interpretation 1)
Rule: CARHA prescribes no stick-down order at all — its Rule 57 names neither the visiting player nor the defending one, so under that book there is no blade to wait for and none to claim
Rule: The IIHF Situation Handbook 2026/27 reads the IIHF's own stick order as an option — an attacking centre may place first if they choose, and the linesperson allows it (Situation 76.53)
Key: Placing first shows your blade angle first, so under NHL and IIHF rules the advantage of going second is the attacking team's
Action: When you are the one who must show your hand, vary the angle you show
```

This is stated wrongly more often than almost any rule in hockey. The actual text of Rule 76.4:

- **At centre ice** — the **visiting** player places their stick first.
- **At the other eight spots** — the **defending** player places first, "followed immediately by the attacking player."

So the common shorthand "the visitor puts their stick down first" is true at exactly one of the nine spots. Everywhere else the **defending** player commits first — and putting your stick down first means showing your blade angle first, which is information you'd rather not give away. **So at the eight end-zone and neutral-zone spots the advantage of going second belongs to the *attacking* team** under the NHL and IIHF books, which is the opposite of what most players assume. If you are defending, expect to show your hand, and vary the angle you show.

At the four neutral-zone dots the rule still says "defending player," which means the player from the team defending the goal at that end. The rulebook does not spell this out further; that is the standard reading.

**The IIHF book agrees with the NHL here, and it still does.** IIHF Rule 76.4 carries the same sentence, word for word in both the 2025/26 and the 2026/27 editions: *"At the eight (8) face-off spots (excluding center ice face-off spot), the defending Player shall place their stick within the designated white area first followed immediately by the attacking Player. When the 'face-off' is conducted at the center ice face-off spot, the visiting Player shall place their stick on the ice first."* This is one of the places where the two books do **not** split — which is worth saying plainly, because the violation *consequences* further down this document were, until the IIHF's 2026/27 edition, one of the biggest splits between them. Same order of sticks; the cost of getting it wrong is set out below. ⚠️ **But not in Britain — the box at the end of this section has the British answer, and it is none of the three this comparison gives.**

**The IIHF's Situation Handbook reads that order as an option rather than an obligation, and says so for the first time in its 2026/27 edition.** Situation 76.53 asks what a linesperson should do when *"the attacking center player puts their stick down before the defending center player has a chance to do so"*, and answers: *"This rule gives the attacker an option to put their stick down second. However, if the attacker chooses to put their stick down first, the linesperson should allow this and drop the puck as soon as the defending center puts down their stick."* So the second-stick advantage is yours to keep or to give away, and giving it away is not a violation — useful if you are the attacking centre and would rather set your blade early than react to theirs. That situation is new: the 2025/26 handbook carried a different one at the same number, about how a team picks its dot after a penalty. ⚠️ **It is a handbook interpretation, not operative rule text** — the handbook's own Foreword says *"in case of any contradictions, the valid IIHF Rulebook is always the basis"* — though nothing in Rule 76.4 speaks to an attacker who volunteers, so there is no contradiction to resolve. **Hockey Canada arrives at the same place from the other side**, as its own paragraph two below sets out: there a *home* player who wants to place first *"are allowed to do so, if it does not cause a problem."*

**⚠️ USA Hockey reverses this, so check before you build a habit on it.** USA Hockey Rule 613(a) reads: *"The attacking player shall be the first to place their stick on the ice, except for a center ice face-off where the visiting team player shall be first to place their stick on the ice."* So under the NHL the **defending** centre shows his blade angle first at eight of the nine spots; under USA Hockey the **attacking** centre does. If you play in a North American rec or youth league, the advantage of going second is yours when you are *defending*, not attacking — the exact opposite of the NHL situation described above.

**⚠️ Hockey Canada is a third answer again, and the simplest of the five.** Hockey Canada Rule 6.2(a) says *"the player of the Visiting Team will place their stick within the designated white area first"*, and Interpretation 1 to that rule removes any doubt about scope: *"For all face-offs, the Visiting Team face-off player must place their stick down first for the face-off regardless of whether they are the attacking or defending team."* So under that book the order never depends on which end you are attacking — only on which bench you came out of, at all nine spots. The same interpretation is unusually relaxed about enforcement: a first violation is a warning, a second during the same stoppage *"may result in a Minor penalty"* for delay of game, and a home player who wants to put their stick down first *"are allowed to do so, if it does not cause a problem."*

**⚠️ CARHA is a fourth position, and it is silence.** Its Rule 57 was read in full and prescribes no order at all — it asks only that the two takers stand *"approximately one stick length apart"* with their blades in the white area, and the words *"Visiting Team"* and *"Home Team"* appear nowhere in that book's face-off rule. **So under CARHA there is no order to wait for and none to claim** — which makes it the one North American book where showing your blade first gives nothing away, because nobody was obliged to show theirs.

> **⚠️ If you play in Britain, none of the three orders above is yours either.** The amendment that settles it has two halves that pull in opposite directions — **the stick *order* is looser than the IIHF book, and the amount of *blade* you must put down is stricter** — so read both before you act on either. The **IHUK In-House Rules 2026-27**, under *Section 10 – Game Flow*, amend Rule 76 directly: *"Linespersons must ensure both centres are squared up correctly behind their marks with the whole of their stick blades (not toe or heel) in the white portion of the face-off circle. **Either centre can now put their stick down first for a face-off.**"* IHUK's own word "now" flags it as new for that season. **So under the In-House Rules the order carries no information at all** — you cannot read off it which end you are defending, and you cannot count on seeing the other blade before you show yours whichever team you are on. Set your blade when you are ready and watch the linesperson's hand.
>
> **This is a British departure, not an IIHF change.** Both IIHF editions still put the defending centre's stick down first at the eight spots, in the sentence quoted above, word for word; the In-House Rules are the *"overriding interpretation of how the IIHF Rule Book is to be applied domestically"*, so where they amend it, they win. **⚠️ And the other half of that same amendment cuts the opposite way**: a British linesperson is told to require *"the whole of their stick blades (not toe or heel)"* in the white, where IIHF Rule 76.7(IV) asks only that *"at least the toe of the blade of the stick"* be in the designated white area. **Put the whole blade down.** Meeting the IIHF standard is not enough to satisfy the instruction your linesperson has been given — and under the other half of the same amendment a violation has the linesperson replace your centre on the spot, with a second violation by your team on that draw still a bench minor under IIHF Rule 76.7, which IHUK does not amend.
>
> **What those In-House Rules cover, in their own words:** IHUK and the British Universities Ice Hockey Association adopt the IIHF book *"as the foundation for the rules of the game to be applied in England, Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland (the Home Countries)"*. The Rule 76 amendment carries no league restriction of its own — unlike the fighting provision in the same document, which is headed *"Applicable Leagues: NIHL (all leagues), SNL, and BUIHA Varsity"*. **The Elite League is not mentioned anywhere in that document**, so nothing here tells you which stick order an EIHL game uses; ask.

### Positioning requirements

```facts
Position: Square to your opponent's end of the rink, roughly one stick length apart (Rule 76.4)
Rule: Feet either side of the restraining lines that run parallel to the side boards — touching the lines is fine (NHL and IIHF Rule 76.7(iv), which defines this; 76.4 does not)
Rule: The toe of your skate blade must not cross the restraining lines perpendicular to the side boards (NHL and IIHF Rule 76.7(iv))
Rule: At least the toe of your stick blade must be in the white of the spot — NHL and IIHF Rule 76.7(iv) and Hockey Canada 6.2(a) — but not in Britain, not under CARHA 57(a) ("the full blade of their sticks flat on the ice"), and not under USA Hockey 613(a) at an end-zone spot
Rule: USA Hockey 613(a) is stricter and narrower at once — at end-zone spots both blades must be "in contact with the nearest white area of the face-off spot and clear of the center red area"; at centre ice and the neutral zone it sets no blade-placement standard
Rule: Under NHL and IIHF Rule 76.7(iv), the blade must remain in the white until the puck is dropped; lifting it early is a faceoff violation
Rule: ⚠️ In Britain the toe is not enough — put "the whole of their stick blades (not toe or heel) in the white portion of the face-off circle" (IHUK In-House Rules 2026-27, Section 10, Rule 76); falling short is a violation and the linesperson replaces your centre
Rule: No other player may enter the circle or come within fifteen feet of the two takers — NHL 76.6, USA Hockey 613(b), Hockey Canada 6.2(a) and CARHA 57(a); IIHF 76.6 writes it as 4.5 m
Rule: At end-zone draws the other eight skaters keep their bodies on their own side of the hash marks — the restraining lines on the outer edge of the circle (NHL and IIHF Rule 76.6, USA Hockey 613(b))
Rule: The attacking team's four other skaters set their positions first, then the defending team counters — NHL and IIHF 76.6 and Hockey Canada's Interpretation 1 to 6.2(a); USA Hockey 613(b) and CARHA 57(a) write no such right
Rule: The goalkeeper may not participate in a faceoff (Rule 76.1)
```

**For the centre** (Rule 76.4 and 76.7):
- Stand **squarely facing your opponent's end of the rink**, roughly **one stick length apart**.
- Place your feet on either side of the restraining lines running parallel to the side boards — touching the lines is fine.
- The **toe of your skate blade must not cross** the restraining lines running perpendicular to the side boards as you approach.
- At least the **toe of your stick blade** must be on the ice in the designated white part of the spot, and **must stay there until the puck is dropped**. **Four of the five books name the toe** — NHL and IIHF Rule 76.7(iv) ask that *"at least the toe of the blade of the stick"* be placed in the designated white area of the face-off spot *"and must remain there until the puck is dropped"*, **Hockey Canada Rule 6.2(a)** asks for *"the toe of the blade touching within the designated white area"*, and, for draws at the end-zone and neutral-zone spots, **CARHA Rule 57(a)** says it in near-identical words. ⚠️ **But CARHA asks for more of the blade than that, in the same rule, and the demand sits a paragraph away from the toe sentence.** Its opening paragraph, which is scoped to no spot at all, has the two players taking the draw stand *"with the full blade of their sticks flat on the ice"* — more of the blade than the toe sentence in its next paragraph asks for, and more than any of the other three books ask for anywhere. **The two halves do not contradict each other**: a blade lying flat on the ice with its toe in the white satisfies both, and the toe alone satisfies only the second. **So under CARHA, put the whole blade down** — the same instruction a British player is given, arrived at by a different route. ⚠️ **USA Hockey Rule 613(a) is the fifth answer, and it differs from the toe sentence in both directions at once.** At an end-zone spot it asks for more, and adds a clause no other book writes: *"[t]he stick blades of both players shall be in contact with the nearest white area of the face-off spot **and clear of the center red area**."* **So a blade with its toe in the white and the rest of it lying across the red satisfies the four books that name the toe and fails this one.** And it reaches less far than they do: at the centre-ice and neutral-zone spots the same rule asks only that the two players *"squarely face their opponent's end of the ice and stand approximately one stick length apart"*, and says nothing about the blade at all. **So under USA Hockey, get the whole blade into the white at an end-zone draw** — a third route to the same instruction.
- 🇬🇧 **In Britain, put the whole blade in the white.** The IHUK In-House Rules 2026-27 tell linespersons to ensure both centres are squared up *"with the whole of their stick blades (not toe or heel) in the white portion of the face-off circle"* — a stricter standard than the IIHF's toe. Improper stick placement is itself a faceoff violation under IIHF Rule 76.7(IV), and under the same In-House amendment a violation has the linesperson replace the centre — and IHUK does not amend IIHF Rule 76.7, so a **second** violation by your team on that same draw is still a bench minor for delay of game. So a British centre who does exactly what the IIHF book says, and puts only the toe down, is in violation; this is not a detail to leave to chance. **The order in which the two of you put your sticks down is free there**; see the box at the end of "Who puts their stick down first".

**For everyone else** (Rule 76.6):
- No other player may enter the faceoff circle or come within **fifteen feet** of the two players taking the draw. **All five books carry that distance**, two of them metric: the NHL writes *"fifteen feet (15')"*, **USA Hockey Rule 613(b)** *"15 feet"*, **Hockey Canada Rule 6.2(a)** *"at least 4.57 m (15 ft.)"*, **CARHA Rule 57(a)** *"at least 15 feet (4.57 m)"*, and **IIHF Rule 76.6** writes it as *"4.5m"*.
- At end-zone draws, all eight other skaters must keep their bodies **on their own side of the hash marks**. **The NHL and the IIHF use the same words for it** — bodies *"on their own side of the restraining lines marked on the outer edge of the face-off circles"* (Rule 76.6) — and **USA Hockey Rule 613(b)** says *"behind the hash marks on the outer edge of the face-off circle"*. **Hockey Canada draws the line by where a skate is rather than where a body is**: its glossary defines encroachment as standing *"with a skate inside the face-off circle, on the face-off circle, or in the area between the hash marks"*, adding that a player's stick may be inside the circle but not between the hash marks.
- Crucially: **the attacking team's four other skaters must establish their positions first**, and only then may the defending team counter and hold. If an attacker tries to reposition after that, the violation is charged to the *attacking* team — even if the defending centre backs out of the dot in response.

That last rule is a genuine tactical asset for the defending team, and it is the reason defensive-zone alignments can be reactive: you get to see where their net-front threat is going before you decide who takes them.

**⚠️ That asset is not in every book, so find out whether it is yours.** **Hockey Canada writes it in the same terms** — Interpretation 1 to Rule 6.2(a): *"When positioning players for end zone face-offs (other than the players taking the face-off), the attacking team must be first to take their positions; the defending team has the right to set up its defence."* **USA Hockey does not write it at all.** Its Rule 613(b) says only that no other player may *"enter the face-off circle or come within 15 feet of the players facing-off the puck"* and that *"[a]ll other players must position themselves behind the hash marks on the outer edge of the face-off circle or stand approximately one stick length apart from the opponent"* — nothing about who sets up first. So under that book, do not build a defensive-zone alignment on watching their net-front threat commit before you commit: you may not get to.

**The goalkeeper may not participate in a faceoff** (Rule 76.1). This matters when your goalie is pulled — see "Situational Faceoffs".

## Violations, Ejections and the Bench Minor

Get this exactly right, because the consequences are asymmetric and one of them is routinely misunderstood.

**Two different consequences, depending on your book — and, in the IIHF's case, on which edition of it.** The *list* of violations is essentially the same in the NHL and IIHF rulebooks — the same premature movement, the same encroachment, the same bad stick placement. What differs is what it costs you. The NHL throws your centre out of the circle. The **IIHF 2025/26** book issued **a warning to your team** and re-dropped the puck with the same centre still in the dot.

> ⚠️ **That IIHF answer changed with the 2026/27 edition, and this section was written to the older one.** Everything marked "IIHF" below is the **2025/26** position. The **IIHF Official Rule Book 2026/27**, published June 2026, moved to the NHL's ejection: its 76.6 has the encroaching team's centre *"ejected from the face-off circle"*, its 76.7 dropped the warning sentence for premature movement, swapped the warning for pre-drop physical contact for an order that the centre be *"replaced"*, and deleted the first warning its predecessor gave for helmet-to-helmet contact, and its 76.3 and 76.4 replace the offending centre rather than warning him. **If you are playing in Britain, read the NHL column, not the IIHF one, for who gets removed — and you can read it without first establishing which IIHF edition your league runs, because the IHUK In-House Rules 2026-27 write the removal in themselves**, on every draw but one that follows an icing. The 2025/26 text is kept below because the rest of it is unchanged — the standard for where a skate may be, the two-violation bench minor, the skate-win violation itself, the stick-placement order, and the once-a-game lateness carve-out, which survives in substance though the newer book rewrote its wording — and because knowing what moved is how you spot it if your league is still administering the old book. **Neither 2026/27 document has been read in full** — each was read for the face-off rules and for every other rule and situation stated here as reading the same in both editions, each of which carries its number where it is used — so nothing here says whether the rest of this document's IIHF layer is current under the newer book.

**If you play anywhere in Britain, an IIHF book is the one you live under** — see [UK and England Rules](../foundation/uk_rules.md) — **with the IHUK In-House Rules on top of it, and on faceoff violations those In-House Rules are what actually decide the question.** Read this section with that substitution in mind, and with the edition warning above; both are flagged again at each point where they change what you should do.

### What gets your centre thrown out of the dot

```facts
Rule: A faceoff violation normally ejects the offending team's centre from that draw, replaced "by any teammate then on the ice" (NHL Rules 76.4, 76.6)
Rule: Under the IIHF 2025/26 book a faceoff violation was a warning to the team and a re-drop, with the centre staying in the dot (IIHF 2025/26 Rules 76.6, 76.7)
Rule: The IIHF 2026/27 book ejects the centre from the circle for a violation instead, as the NHL does (IIHF 2026/27 Rule 76.6)
Rule: In Britain the IHUK In-House Rules 2026-27 settle it — the linesperson replaces the centre for every violation except on a draw following an icing (Section 10, Rule 76); IHUK does not amend IIHF 76.7, so a second violation on that draw is still a bench minor
Rule: Premature movement takes nobody out of the dot under the NHL or either IIHF book — the draw "must be conducted again" (Rule 76.7; the 2025/26 edition warns the team first) — but in Britain IHUK replaces the centre for it, as for any violation
Rule: Improper stick placement, or skates in the wrong place relative to the restraining lines, is a violation (NHL and IIHF Rule 76.7, in both IIHF editions)
Rule: A teammate putting a skate inside the circle or across the hash marks is a violation (NHL and IIHF 76.7, both editions) — but merely moving is not, and the premature-movement clause names the centre, not them
Rule: Physical contact before the drop is a violation whoever makes it, but the minor is the taker's — mandatory under USA Hockey 613(e), Hockey Canada 6.2(c) and CARHA 57(b), discretionary under NHL and IIHF 76.7; a teammate's contact ejects your centre instead (Hockey Canada 6.2(a), CARHA 57(a))
Rule: Under the IIHF 2026/27 book that contact costs you the centre as well — 76.7 has the linesperson "order the center of the offending team replaced", where the 2025/26 book only warned him (IIHF 76.7, both editions)
Rule: Lining up off-side for the draw is a violation in all five books — NHL and IIHF 76.3(V), Hockey Canada 6.2(a), CARHA 57(a), USA Hockey 613(d) — and in every one but the IIHF 2025/26 edition, which warned, it is your centre who is replaced
```

Under NHL rules, **most** faceoff violations result in the **offending team's centre being ejected from that draw** — replaced "by any teammate then on the ice" (Rule 76.4). ⚠️ **The first item on the list below is the exception, and it sits in the rule's own opening sentence:** a centre who moves early, or a puck dropped unfairly, costs a re-drop under the NHL and under both IIHF editions, and takes nobody out of the dot. 🇬🇧 **Britain is the exception to that, and the IHUK In-House Rules settle it without reference to which IIHF edition your league runs.** The **IHUK In-House Rules 2026-27**, at Section 10, Rule 76, have the linesperson replace the centre for every violation except on a draw following an icing, and those rules are the *"overriding interpretation of how the IIHF Rule Book is to be applied domestically."* The violations (Rule 76.7):

- **Moving prematurely** before the drop. ⚠️ **The rule names the centre, not everybody.** NHL Rule 76.7 opens *"[i]f a center should move prematurely prior to the face-off, or if the Referee or Linesperson shall have dropped the puck unfairly, the face-off shall be considered a face-off violation and it must be conducted again"* — so that clause is about the two takers, and ⚠️ **it is the one violation on this list the NHL and the IIHF remove nobody for:** the remedy written into that sentence is the re-drop, and neither book takes a centre out for it. 🇬🇧 **Britain does, and it is the In-House Rules that do it rather than the IIHF** — their linesperson *"shall immediately replace the centre for all violations"* bar post-icing draws, and this is one of them. **The IIHF prints that sentence word for word in its 2026/27 edition** — but ⚠️ **not in the 2025/26 edition, which is the one the IHUK In-House Rules' Introduction names**, and which breaks the sentence to add a step: *"the 'face-off' shall be considered a 'face-off' violation. A warning shall be issued to the offending team and the 'face-off' must be conducted again."* A winger who twitches is caught by the **encroachment** clauses instead, and only where the movement puts a skate somewhere it may not be: USA Hockey's Casebook says in terms that *"[a] player not taking the face-off may be moving, provided they are outside the circle and on-side"* (Rule 613, Situation 7). What the centre's own stick may not do is leave the white — 76.7(iv) requires the blade to *"remain there until the puck is dropped."*
- **Improper stick placement or positioning** — failing to get the blade into the white area, lifting it before the drop, or having your skates in the wrong place relative to the restraining lines.
- **Encroachment by a teammate.** A player other than the centre putting a skate inside the circle, or across the hash marks, before the puck is dropped.
- **Physical contact with an opponent** before the drop — a violation whoever makes it. ⚠️ **For the two players in the dot it is never merely a free violation: a minor can come with it in every book, and under USA Hockey, Hockey Canada and CARHA it must.** Under the NHL and the IIHF the penalty is the referee's to give: NHL Rule 76.7 provides that *"[f]or violation of this rule, the Referee may, at his discretion impose a minor penalty or penalties on the player(s) whose action(s) caused the physical contact"*, and IIHF Rule 76.7 prints the same sentence in its own words — *"the Referee may, at their discretion impose a minor penalty or penalties on the Player(s) whose action(s) caused the 'physical contact'"* — in the 2025/26 and 2026/27 editions alike. Both books then add that it counts as a faceoff violation as well — and in the **2026/27** IIHF edition that half of it got harder. Where the 2025/26 book had the centre *"warned by the Linesperson that the team has committed a 'face-off' violation"*, the 2026/27 book has the *"Linesperson shall order the center of the offending team replaced."* **So under the current IIHF book, leaning on the other centre costs you the draw outright, before the referee decides anything about a minor.** **Under the other three there is no discretion.** USA Hockey Rule 613(e) says *shall*: *"[a] minor penalty for interference shall be assessed to any player facing-off who makes any physical contact with their opponent's body by means of their own body or by their stick"* before the draw is complete. Hockey Canada Rule 6.2(c) says *will*: *"the Referee will assess a Minor penalty or penalties to the player(s) whose action(s) caused the physical contact."* **CARHA Rule 57(b) says *shall* and writes no discretion clause at all**: *"[f]or violation of this rule, the Referee shall assess a Minor penalty on the player(s) whose action(s) caused the physical contact."* So the difference between the books is *mandatory* against *discretionary*, not *penalty* against *no penalty*. Wherever you play, leaning on the other centre before the puck lands can cost you two minutes as well as the dot. ⚠️ **All five of those penalty clauses are scoped to a player taking the draw, not to the players on the hash marks** — USA Hockey's assesses the minor *"to any player facing-off"*, and the NHL's, the IIHF's, Hockey Canada's and CARHA's each open by providing that no player facing off shall make the contact (the four write the hyphen differently and Hockey Canada writes none) — **so a winger who leans on the opponent beside him is not the one who gets the minor.** What his contact costs is the draw: it is a violation in its own right, named for a player other than the taker at **Hockey Canada 6.2(a)** and **CARHA 57(a)**, and it is **your centre** who is ejected for it. [Winger](../positions/winger.md) sets that out for the player it lands on.
- **Lining up in an off-side position** for the ensuing draw (Rule 76.3(v)). ⚠️ **This is the one item on the list where all five books agree, and in every one of them but the IIHF's superseded 2025/26 edition it is your centre who pays for it.** NHL Rule 76.3(v) and IIHF Rule 76.3(V) both list *"[a]ny player who lines up for the face-off in an off-side position"*, and the NHL's consequence is that *"the Linesperson shall have the offending center(s) replaced immediately prior to dropping the puck"* — the 2026/27 IIHF edition says the same, where the 2025/26 edition had the linesperson assess a warning instead. **Hockey Canada Rule 6.2(a)** and **CARHA Rule 57(a)** both catch a player other than the taker who *"moves off-side"* (CARHA prints it *"moves offside"*), and both then eject *"the offending team's player taking the face-off"*. **USA Hockey Rule 613(d)** reaches it through position rather than by name: *"[i]f a player, other than the player facing off, fails to maintain their proper position, the center of their team shall be ejected from the face-off."* So this is the violation least worth arguing about — line up on-side, everywhere.

#### Being late to the dot — the one softening, and who writes it

```facts
Rule: Being late to the faceoff location, and therefore off-side for the draw, is warned once in the game under NHL and IIHF 76.7 — the warning going to the team and its coach — and the rule says the centre "is not ejected from the face-off"
Rule: Timing your arrival is not the same as being late — a centre who "attempts to arrive at the face-off spot just as the five seconds elapses in an attempt to gain an advantage" is removed and replaced under NHL and IIHF 2026/27 Rule 76.4, unless the draw follows an icing
Rule: Once your team has had its one lateness warning, being slow to the dot again is a bench minor for delay of game — "Slow Proceeding to Face-off Location" (NHL and IIHF Rule 76.7)
Rule: USA Hockey 613(d) and CARHA 57(c) write no warning at all — each lets the official order a player facing off who fails to take their proper position when directed "replaced" for that draw, first time, at discretion (CARHA writes "the proper position", USA Hockey "their")
Rule: Hockey Canada reaches the same place in two steps, which is a reading of two rules together and not one sentence — 6.5(a) names "late arrival" a face-off violation and 6.2(d) ejects the taker; 6.5(a)'s warning is Junior divisions after an icing only
Rule: In Britain the two texts point opposite ways — IIHF 76.7 says a late centre is not ejected, the IHUK In-House Rules 2026-27 replace the centre for all violations bar post-icing draws (Section 10, Rule 76) — and nothing published resolves it
Rule: The carve-out survived the IIHF edition change and changed currency — the 2025/26 book said the late centre "will not receive a warning", the 2026/27 book says the centre "is not ejected from the 'face-off'" (IIHF Rule 76.7, both editions)
Action: Be at the dot on time — the safe play is the same under every book, and it is free
```

**One softening worth knowing, and it belongs to two books rather than to all five:** under the NHL and the IIHF, players who are simply **late to the faceoff location**, and therefore off-side for the draw, get **one warning per game** — given to the offending team and to its coach — and Rule 76.7 is explicit that "in this situation, the offending team's center is not ejected from the face-off." Any subsequent instance is a bench minor, announced as "Delay of Game – Slow Proceeding to Face-off Location." So being slow off the bench is not the same offence as being early on the drop: the first one costs you a warning, the second costs you your centre. ⚠️ **USA Hockey and CARHA write no warning into this at all, and they write it in near-identical sentences.** **USA Hockey Rule 613(d)** provides that where a player facing off *"fails to take their proper position immediately when directed by the Official, the Official may order them replaced for that face-off by any teammate then on the ice"*; **CARHA Rule 57(c)** says the same of the Referee — where a player facing off *"fails to take the proper position immediately when directed by the Referee, the official may order them replaced at that face-off by any player on the ice."* First time, no warning, at the official's discretion. **Under those two books, late is late**, and what it costs is the draw rather than nothing. ⚠️ **Hockey Canada arrives at the same place in two steps rather than one, so treat this as a reading of two rules together rather than a single sentence**: its Rule 6.5(a) names *"late arrival"* among face-off violations, and Rule 6.2(d) has the official eject the offending team's draw-taker for a face-off violation — with the warning in 6.5(a) reserved to Junior divisions on a draw following an icing. The safe play is the same under all three, and it is free: be at the dot on time. 🇬🇧 **⚠️ That is the NHL and IIHF answer; in Britain the IHUK In-House Rules point the other way and nothing published settles which wins — so do not plan on keeping the dot, and get there on time.**

⚠️ **That protection is for arriving late, not for timing your arrival — and the sentence that takes it away is in a different rule.** **NHL Rule 76.4** deals separately with the centre who plays the clock: *"[i]f the center attempts to arrive at the face-off spot just as the five seconds elapses in an attempt to gain an advantage to win the face-off, he is to be removed from the face-off and replaced, resulting in a face-off violation."* **The IIHF's 2026/27 Rule 76.4 prints the same sentence** in its own pronouns — its 2025/26 edition had the centre *"warned"* there instead, because that book had no removal to apply. **Both books then carve out exactly one draw**: *"[i]f the face-off is a result of an icing infraction and the center attempts to arrive at the face-off spot just as the five seconds elapse to gain an advantage to win the face-off, he shall not be removed from the face-off"*, and is warned instead. **So the free attempt described under "The icing exception" below is the only one either book gives you.** Try the same thing on any other draw and you lose the dot — the once-a-game warning above does not cover it, because it is written for players who are simply late.

**Under the IIHF 2025/26 book this softening was worth more, not less.** That edition's Rule 76.7 carried the same once-a-game lateness warning, but phrased the carve-out for a book that had no ejections: *"In this situation, the offending team's center will not receive a warning for a 'face-off' violation."* Because a warning was the currency in that book — the thing that put you one twitch from a bench minor — that sentence meant being slow to the dot did **not** burn the warning you might still need on the same draw. Your one free violation stayed intact.

**The 2026/27 edition keeps the carve-out and rewrites it for a book that now does eject.** Its 76.7 reads: *"In this situation, the offending team's center is not ejected from the 'face-off'."* So the protection survives the edition change — being slow to the dot still does not cost you your centre — but what it protects you from is now a removal rather than a warning.

> 🇬🇧 **⚠️ In Britain that protection and the In-House Rules do not obviously agree, and this document cannot tell you which one wins.** IIHF Rule 76.7 says in terms that on a late arrival *"the offending team's center is not ejected from the 'face-off'"*, and the 2026/27 Situation Handbook repeats it at Situation 82.4. But the **IHUK In-House Rules 2026-27** say *"[w]ith the exception of face-offs following icings, the linesperson shall immediately replace the centre for all violations"* — and being late to the location puts you in an off-side position for the draw, which is a violation. On this one case the two texts point opposite ways: the IIHF hands you a once-a-game warning, and the In-House sentence carves out post-icing draws and nothing else.
>
> **Nothing published resolves it.** The In-House Introduction cuts both ways in two consecutive sentences: those rules are the *"overriding interpretation of how the IIHF Rule Book is to be applied domestically"*, which points at the centre being replaced — but *"[u]nless otherwise stated, all rules will be enforced in line with the IIHF Rule Book"*, and the IIHF book does state otherwise for exactly this case. **One further hook, offered as a reading and not as a ruling:** the IIHF lateness warning is given by the **Referee**, where the In-House sentence instructs the **linesperson**, so the two may not be addressing the same act at all. ⚠️ **That is a reading, not a ruling — ask your league rather than relying on it.** In the meantime the safe play is the same under either answer, and it is free: be at the dot on time.


**One trap if you go and check this in your own book, and it is edition-specific.** The IIHF rulebook states the same lateness carve-out twice, in two different vocabularies. In the **2025/26** edition, Rule 76.7 used the warning wording quoted above while Rule 82.2(VII) used ejection wording — *"In this situation, the offending team's center is not ejected from the 'face-off'."* In that edition, that was the **only** appearance of the word "ejected" anywhere in the face-off material, and it was a sentence saying an ejection did *not* happen; it was not evidence that 2025/26 IIHF face-off ejections existed somewhere else, because the word appeared nowhere else in that book's Rule 76. **⚠️ Do not carry that reasoning into the 2026/27 book, where it is simply wrong.** There the word appears throughout Rule 76 and describes a real consequence: 76.6 ejects the centre for a teammate's encroachment, and 76.4 speaks of the replacement for *"a Player… ejected from the face-off."* The two rules now agree because both were rewritten, not because neither meant it.

### Getting low without putting your head in it

```facts
Rule: Helmet-to-helmet contact with the other centre before the drop is named in the rulebook as a faceoff violation (IIHF 76.7(III), both editions); the 2025/26 book gave it a first warning, the 2026/27 book deleted that warning
Rule: The IIHF Situation Handbook 2026/27 tells the linesperson to "immediately eject the center that is responsible for the helmet to helmet contact", and to eject both where they cannot tell who caused it — the 2025/26 handbook said warn (Situation 76.43)
Never: Lean in until your head is against the other centre's — heads "almost ear to ear" is IIHF Situation 76.42, and both editions of the handbook say it should not be allowed
Rule: A centre who will not move back when the linesperson breaks up an ear-to-ear stance is warned under the IIHF 2025/26 Situation Handbook and removed from the face-off under the 2026/27 edition (Situation 76.42)
Rule: In Britain do not plan on that first warning — the In-House Rules' Introduction names the 2025/26 edition, but the IHUK In-House Rules 2026-27 have the linesperson replace the centre for every violation bar a post-icing draw, and helmet-to-helmet contact is one (Section 10, Rule 76)
Rule: Helmet-to-helmet contact at the dot is physical contact before the drop as well, so the referee's discretionary minor under NHL and IIHF 76.7 sits on top of the violation — and that minor is mandatory under USA Hockey 613(e), Hockey Canada 6.2(c) and CARHA 57(b)
Action: Get your chest over the puck, not your head over the dot — depth comes from the knees and hips, and ear-to-ear is the position the IIHF handbook tells linespersons to break up
```

**"Get low, chest over the puck" is the most repeated instruction in this document, and it is the one instruction here that can put your head somewhere the rulebook does not want it.** Two opposing centres both doing it well finish with their heads close together over the dot, and the IIHF Situation Handbook addresses that position twice in succession.

**Situation 76.42** takes the case where *"[b]oth centers lean in over the face-off dot such that their heads are side by side, almost ear to ear"* and asks whether it should be allowed. Both editions open identically — *"[t]his should not be allowed. The Linesperson should instruct both players to move back"* — and then divide on what follows a refusal: in the **2025/26** handbook *"one or both should be warned"*, and in the **2026/27** handbook *"one or both should be removed from the face-off."*

**Situation 76.43** is the same geometry after contact has actually happened: *"[b]oth centers lean in over the face-off creating helmet to helmet contact."* Again *"[t]his is not permitted"*, and again the consequence moved. The 2025/26 handbook had the linesperson *"immediately warn the center that is responsible for the helmet to helmet contact"* and, where they could not tell, *"warn both centers."* The 2026/27 handbook has them *"immediately eject the center that is responsible for the helmet to helmet contact"* and, failing that, *"eject both centers."*

**The rulebook singles the head out too, and that is the change that matters most.** Rule 76.7(III) covers *"[a]ny physical contact with an opponent prior to the puck being dropped"* and then names this case specifically. Under the **2025/26** edition a centre making helmet-to-helmet contact *"must receive a first 'face-off' violation warning"*, and where the linesperson could not tell who initiated it, *"both Players must receive a first 'face-off' violation warning."* The **2026/27** edition deletes the warning from both limbs: the player *"has committed a 'face-off' violation"*, and both players *"have committed a 'face-off' violation."*

**So the free first head bump is gone, and what replaces it depends on which book you are under.** The 2026/27 handbook says eject; the IHUK In-House Rules 2026-27 say replace the centre for all violations bar a post-icing draw; and the discretionary minor in the same rule sits on top either way — **mandatory rather than discretionary under USA Hockey 613(e), Hockey Canada 6.2(c) and CARHA 57(b)**, which write no exception for the head and catch any pre-drop contact at all.

**What to do about it is small, and it costs you nothing on the draw.** Depth comes from bending your knees and dropping your hips, not from pushing your head out over the dot; the low stance this document recommends is a loaded one, not a leaning one. And if the other centre leans into you, the linesperson's first instruction is to tell **both** of you to move back — so move when told, because the second sentence of both situations is about the centre who does not.

### The rule everybody gets wrong

```facts
Rule: A teammate moving into the circle ejects the centre, not the teammate (NHL Rule 76.6)
Rule: Under the IIHF 2025/26 book a teammate's encroachment cost the team its one warning for that draw, not the centre (IIHF 2025/26 Rule 76.6)
Rule: The IIHF 2026/27 book ejects the centre for a teammate's encroachment, and the IHUK In-House Rules 2026-27 replace the centre for every violation but a post-icing one, leaving the team one mistake from the bench minor of IIHF 76.7 (IIHF 2026/27 Rule 76.6; In-House Section 10, Rule 76)
Rule: Both skates stay outside the circle and outside the hash marks (NHL and IIHF Rule 76.7, in both IIHF editions)
Rule: Skate contact with the line is permitted — crossing it is what does it (NHL and IIHF Rule 76.7, in both IIHF editions)
Rule: Your stick may be inside the circle or the hash marks if it touches no opponent or their stick (NHL and IIHF Rule 76.7, in both IIHF editions)
Rule: USA Hockey allows no reaching stick — its Casebook answers "No" to a stick between the hash marks: "Both players must be completely behind their respective hash marks. This includes all sticks and all skates" (Rule 613, Situation 8)
Rule: Hockey Canada splits it — its glossary calls a stick "inside the face-off circle" acceptable but one "in the area between the hash marks" encroachment, and counts a skate "on the face-off circle" as encroachment too
Action: Tell your linemates — the NHL and IIHF let your stick reach inside the circle and the hash marks, never your skates; USA Hockey and Hockey Canada do not let the stick reach the hash marks
```

**Encroachment by a teammate gets the CENTRE ejected, not the teammate.** Two rules work together here, which is why you will see both cited. **Rule 76.6** supplies the consequence: *"If a player other than the player taking the face-off moves into the face-off circle prior to the dropping of the puck, then the offending team's player taking the face-off shall be ejected from the face-off circle. This shall be considered a face-off violation."* **Rule 76.7** then enumerates what counts as encroachment — **(i)** any player other than the centre entering the face-off circle, and **(ii)** any player crossing into the area between the hash marks — and supplies the escalation, quoted here exactly as printed, dropped word and all: *"When a [sic] least two face-off violations have been committed by the same team during the same face-off, this team shall be penalized with a bench minor penalty to the offending team."* The missing word is the rulebook's own — it means *at* least two.

In both of those clauses the line itself is safe — clause (i) puts it as *"skate contact with the line is permitted"* — it is a skate **crossing** the line that does it. And under those two books your stick may be inside the circle or inside the hash marks provided there is no contact with an opponent or their stick. **⚠️ Both halves of that are NHL and IIHF rules, and the other three books answer differently; the full comparison is set out below.**

So if your winger leans over the hash marks trying to time a jump, **you lose your best draw-taker for the most important draw of the game.** The winger stays. It costs nothing to act on: tell your linemates.

**⚠️ In Britain this is the sentence that changed with the IIHF's 2026/27 edition, and it changed toward the NHL.** The **2025/26** book set out the identical offence with a softer consequence: *"If a Player other than the Player taking the 'face-off' moves into the face-off circle prior to the dropping of the puck, then the offending team taking the 'face-off' shall receive a warning for a 'face-off' violation."* Your centre stayed, and what your winger had spent was the team's **one free violation on that draw**.

**The 2026/27 book replaced that sentence with the NHL's.** IIHF 2026/27 Rule 76.6: *"If a Player other than the Player taking the 'face-off' moves into the face-off circle prior to the dropping of the puck, then the offending team's player taking the face-off shall be ejected from the face-off circle. This shall be considered a face-off violation."* **And in Britain the IHUK In-House Rules 2026-27 reach the same place directly, without going through either IIHF edition**, on every draw but one following an icing. **So a British winger creeping over the hash marks costs his team its centre on that draw, exactly as in the NHL.** The paragraph above about losing your best draw-taker on the most important draw of the game applies to you, and it no longer needs a British asterisk. IIHF 76.7 still puts a bench minor on a second violation by the same team on the same draw, so the margin is gone as well as the specialist. The instruction to your linemates never changed: under either IIHF edition you may reach with your stick, and under neither may you step.

The specific standard is the same in the NHL's book and the IIHF's, and is worth quoting once, because it is the part people get wrong. **Both skates** must stay outside the circle and outside the hash marks. **Skate contact with the line is permitted** — it is crossing it that's illegal. Your **stick** may be inside the circle or inside the hash marks, provided it makes no contact with an opponent or an opponent's stick. So under those two books you can reach; you cannot step. NHL Rule 76.7(i) reads *"Players on the perimeter of the face-off circle must keep both skates outside the face-off circle (skate contact with the line is permitted)"*, and both clauses close the same way — *"[a] player's stick may be inside the face-off circle"*, and at (ii) inside *"the area between the hash marks"*, *"provided there is no physical contact with his opponent or his opponent's stick."* IIHF Rule 76.7(I)–(II) prints all of it in the same words in both editions, changing only the pronouns and the punctuation of that parenthesis.

**⚠️ The reaching stick is an NHL and IIHF permission, and the other three books do not hand it to you.** This is the half of the rule most worth checking before you repeat it to a linemate, because you will hear it stated as though it were a constant of the sport.

- **USA Hockey: no reach at all.** Rule 613(b) runs to two sentences, and no stick permission appears in either: *"No other player shall be allowed to enter the face-off circle or come within 15 feet of the players facing-off the puck. All other players must position themselves behind the hash marks on the outer edge of the face-off circle or stand approximately one stick length apart from the opponent."* **That fifteen-foot standoff is not a USA Hockey peculiarity, and it travels with you** — NHL Rule 76.6 sets the same distance in feet, IIHF Rule 76.6 sets it at 4.5 m in both editions, and Hockey Canada Rule 6.2(a) has every other player in the draw stand *"at least 4.57 m (15 ft.)"* from the two takers. What is particular to this book is that its Casebook then takes the reaching stick head-on. **Rule 613, Situation 8** asks *"[m]ay a player have their stick between the hash marks that are meant to separate them from their opponent during a face-off?"* and answers: *"**No.** Rule Reference 613(b). Both players must be completely behind their respective hash marks. **This includes all sticks and all skates.**"*
- **Hockey Canada: half a reach.** That book puts the limits in its **Glossary**, in the definition of the term Rule 6.2(a) uses. **ENCROACHMENT** is a player other than the centre standing *"with a skate inside the face-off circle, on the face-off circle, or in the area between the hash marks. It is acceptable for the player's stick to be inside the face-off circle but not in the area between the hash marks."* So a stick in the circle is fine there and a stick between the hash marks is not — and 6.2(a) attaches the same consequence to it as to anything else, the *"offending team's player taking the face-off will be ejected from the face-off."*
- ⚠️ **And read what that same Hockey Canada sentence does to the skate half, because it reverses it.** It counts a skate *"on the face-off circle"* as encroachment, where NHL and IIHF 76.7 expressly permit skate contact with the line. **So "touching the line is fine" is an NHL and IIHF answer rather than a universal one** — under Hockey Canada, keep the skate off the paint. The same entry also reaches a player who has not settled — it *"applies to players who are in motion while a face-off is taking place, before the puck is legally dropped"* — **whereas USA Hockey takes the moving player head-on and permits them**, its Casebook asking *"[s]hould a Linesperson allow a player not taking the face-off to be in motion, and not in a stationary position?"* and answering *"**Yes.** Rule Reference 613(b). A player not taking the face-off may be moving, provided they are outside the circle and on-side"* (Rule 613, Situation 7). **So under USA Hockey motion is expressly legal and your position is what decides, while Hockey Canada names motion inside the definition of the offence itself** — drifting toward the paint before the drop leaves you relying on which book you are playing under.

**The instruction that survives all five books is the short one: keep your skates and your stick behind your own hash mark.** That is legal everywhere, it needs no edition and no book named, and the reach it gives up is worth nothing set against what a violation costs your centre.

### The second violation

```facts
Rule: Two faceoff violations by the same team on the same draw is a bench minor under NHL and IIHF Rule 76.7
Rule: USA Hockey routes it to the offender instead — 613(d) makes a team's second violation "of any of the provisions of this subsection" on the same draw "a minor penalty for delay of game being assessed to the player committing the second violation", not a bench minor
Rule: Hockey Canada 6.2(e) is discretionary — a team's second violation of any of the provisions of (a) to (d) on the same draw "may be assessed a Minor penalty by the Referee" under Rule 10.1 (viii)
Rule: CARHA 57(d) is discretionary and limb-scoped — a second violation "of any of the provisions of Section (a)" "may be assessed a Minor penalty"; Section (a) names a non-taker who "moves offside, makes physical contact with an opponent, or encroaches on the face-off circle"
Rule: So a winger's pre-drop contact under CARHA does feed 57(d). What sits outside that limb is the taker's own contact, because 57(b) — scoped to "no player facing-off" — already makes that a mandatory minor on the first offence
Rule: USA Hockey 613(d) is limb-scoped too, to "any of the provisions of this subsection" — 613(d)'s own. Both books scope it, but not to the same content: 613(d) reaches a non-taker's contact only through "fails to maintain their proper position"
Rule: ⚠️ Hockey Canada's discretion vanishes if the second violation runs into the official — a player who contacts the linesperson before they have "reasonable time to vacate the face-off location" "will be assessed a Minor penalty" (Hockey Canada 6.2(e))
Rule: Any of the four on-ice officials may identify a faceoff violation, not just the linesperson dropping the puck (NHL Rule 76.7)
Action: With a replacement in the dot take the simplest approach — a clean tie-up, nothing clever
```

**Two faceoff violations by the same team on the same draw is a bench minor** for "Delay of Game – Face-off Violation" (Rule 76.7). This is why the replacement taker has to be immaculate: your team is now one twitch away from a penalty. It is also why, when your centre is tossed, the replacement should take the simplest possible approach — a clean tie-up, nothing clever.

**⚠️ The other three books do not write it that way — one moves it onto a different person, and two make it discretionary.** **USA Hockey Rule 613(d)**: a second violation by the same team during the same face-off *"shall result in a minor penalty for delay of game being assessed to the player committing the second violation"* — an individual minor rather than a bench minor, so the skater who did it sits, not whoever your bench nominates. **Hockey Canada Rule 6.2(e)** looks softer: a team committing a second violation during the same face-off *"may be assessed a Minor penalty by the Referee"* — permissive, where the NHL and the IIHF are mandatory. **CARHA Rule 57(d) is permissive too, and limb-scoped as well:** a team's second violation on the same face-off *"may be assessed a Minor penalty for 'face-off violation' by the Referee"*, and only a second violation *"of any of the provisions of Section (a)"*. ⚠️ **Read what Section (a) actually covers before you decide what is outside it, because it is wider than positioning.** It reaches *"a player, other than the player taking the face-off"* who *"moves offside, **makes physical contact with an opponent**, or encroaches on the face-off circle prior to the dropping of the puck"* — **so a winger's pre-drop contact is squarely inside that limb, and it does count toward 57(d)'s second-violation minor.** What sits outside is the **taker's** own contact, and only because CARHA Rule 57(b) has already dealt with it: that one is a mandatory minor on the first offence, with no second violation needed, and 57(b) is scoped to *"no player facing-off"* so it never reaches the winger at all. ⚠️ **Do not read that scoping as CARHA's peculiarity, because USA Hockey writes the same restriction and it is easy to miss.** Its 613(d) prices *"[a] second violation **of any of the provisions of this subsection** by the same team during the same face-off"* — so that book's individual minor reaches only 613(d)'s own provisions, and not the pre-drop contact its 613(e) deals with separately, which is mandatory there on the first offence. **Two of the five books scope the second-violation penalty to a single sub-rule, then, so this is not a CARHA peculiarity — but the two limbs do not hold the same things.** CARHA's Section (a) names a non-taker's physical contact in so many words; USA Hockey's 613(d) reaches it only indirectly, through *"fails to maintain their proper position"*.

⚠️ **But Hockey Canada's Rule 6.2(e) has a second paragraph, and it is not permissive at all.** The same second violation on the same draw, *"where the offending player subsequently contacts the Linesperson before the Linesperson has had reasonable time to vacate the face-off location[,] will be assessed a Minor penalty by the Referee under Rule 10.1 (a) – Delay of Game."* **"Will be assessed" — the referee's discretion is gone the moment you make contact with an official.** That is the likeliest way to lose it, too: on a re-drop your eyes are on the puck and on the other centre, and the linesperson is backing out of a space you are already moving into. **So under Hockey Canada, after a first violation, let the official clear before you commit.** The instruction to the replacement taker does not change under any of the books: take the re-drop straight.

**The two-violation bench minor is the rule that did all the work in Britain under the older IIHF edition, because it was the only one with teeth — and it is unchanged in the newer one.** IIHF Rule 76.7 carries the escalation in the NHL's own terms — *"When two (2) 'face-off' violations have been committed by the same team during the same 'face-off', this team shall be penalized with a bench minor penalty"* — and the IIHF Situation Handbook confirms it is served like any bench minor, by any skater the team designates. Its Situation 17.4 is precisely the case this document keeps warning you about — in the **2026/27** handbook, *"Team A has already had their center removed for a face-off violation. The winger for Team A encroaches and Team A is penalized for committing a second face-off violation"* — and the answer is that any skater may serve it (Situations 17.3 and 17.4, citing Rules 17.1 and 76.6). **That one word is the whole edition change in miniature**: the **2025/26** handbook set out the identical situation with the centre *"warned"* rather than removed, and the handbook was rewritten the same way throughout — fifteen situations across five rules swap the warning for a removal, and the 2026/27 edition adds a new Situation 81.18 that puts the question and answers it in terms: *"Is the center removed or just warned? ANSWER The offending center is removed."* The interpretation layer moved with the rule, which is the strongest evidence available here that the change is deliberate rather than an editing slip. **This is the part of the British picture the 2026/27 edition did *not* change** — the two-violation bench minor is worded the same in both IIHF editions and in the NHL book. What changed is what sits between the two violations. Under the **2025/26** book your first violation cost you a re-drop and your second cost you two minutes, with no ejection anywhere in between, so the discipline the NHL bought by removing your centre, that book bought by putting the whole five one mistake from a penalty kill. **Under the 2026/27 book you now get both**: the first violation removes your centre *and* leaves the team one mistake from the bench minor. The instruction to your line is the same either way, and under the current book there is more riding on it: after the first violation, nobody moves early, nobody leans, nobody reaches.

Note that any of the **four on-ice officials** may identify a faceoff violation, not just the linesperson dropping the puck. IIHF Rule 76.7 says the same in the same parenthesis.

### The icing exception — which books grant it, and to whom

```facts
Rule: After an icing the first faceoff violation by either team does not remove the centre (NHL Rule 76.4)
Rule: The centre is warned instead, and any subsequent violation by that team is a bench minor (NHL Rule 76.4)
Rule: The IIHF 2025/26 book had no post-icing carve-out because it needed none — every first violation on every draw was already a warning (IIHF 2025/26 Rules 76.6, 76.7)
Rule: The IIHF 2026/27 book adopted the NHL's post-icing carve-out in the NHL's own words, because it also adopted the ejection it exempts you from (IIHF 2026/27 Rule 76.4)
Rule: Britain has the carve-out too — the IHUK In-House Rules 2026-27 replace the centre for every violation "with the exception of face-offs following icings" (Section 10, Rule 76); it is not a free draw, because IHUK does not amend IIHF 76.7 and a second violation is still a bench minor
Rule: Hockey Canada writes the carve-out but rations it by division — in Junior divisions only, any post-icing faceoff violation is a warning and a subsequent one a minor (Hockey Canada Rules 6.5(a), 6.5(b))
Rule: USA Hockey writes no post-icing carve-out at all — after an icing the centre comes off exactly as on any other draw (USA Hockey Rule 613)
Key: The warning is per team, so each side has its own free one
Action: After an icing, use your free attempt to cheat the timing, and expect their centre to use theirs — but find out which book, and under Hockey Canada which division, before you plan on one
```

**On a draw that follows an icing, the *first* faceoff violation does not get anybody's centre removed — and that applies to *both* teams, not just the team that iced.** Rule 76.4: *"When an icing infraction occurs, the first face-off violation by either team will not result in the center being removed. The center will be warned by the Linesperson that the team has committed their first face-off violation, and any subsequent violation by that team will result in a bench minor penalty for delay of game - face-off violation being assessed."*

Read the two limits carefully. It is the **first** violation only — not "any" violation — and the warning is per team, so each side has its own free one. Any subsequent violation by that team is a bench minor. But on a defensive-zone draw after your own icing, with a tired line that cannot change, you get one free attempt to cheat the timing, and so does the team that has just pinned you. Good centres use it and expect it.

> **⚠️ This is the clearest place to see what the IIHF's 2026/27 edition did, because the rule arrived and the reason arrived with it.** In the **2025/26** book there was no post-icing carve-out anywhere in Rules 76.3 to 76.7 and no equivalent sentence in Rule 81 — that book's Rule 76 mentioned icing exactly once, in a different context (the post-whistle scrum that moves a draw to the neutral zone). **That absence was not an omission but redundancy.** The NHL needs a special exemption after an icing because its default is ejection; the 2025/26 IIHF default was already a warning, on every draw, all game, so a British reader had the free first violation the NHL rations to post-icing draws, and had it on all fifty-six of them.
>
> **⚠️ Both halves of that changed together in the 2026/27 edition, and a British reader must now read this section as written.** Having adopted the NHL's ejection, the IIHF adopted the NHL's exemption from it. IIHF 2026/27 Rule 76.4: *"When an icing infraction occurs, the first face-off violation by either team will not result in the center being removed. The center will be warned by the Linesperson that the team has committed their first face-off violation, and any subsequent violation by that team will result in a bench minor penalty for delay of game - face-off violation being assessed."* The same edition adds a narrower companion sentence covering the centre who times his arrival at the dot to gain an advantage: after an icing, *"the center shall not be removed from the face-off"* and is warned instead.
>
> **⚠️ And the British answer no longer depends on that edition question at all, because IHUK carved out the same draw itself.** The **IHUK In-House Rules 2026-27** amend Rule 76 in one sentence, and the exception is the first thing in it — face-offs following icings, carved out of a replacement that otherwise reaches every violation. So on a British post-icing draw the linesperson does not take your centre off. **What happens instead comes from the IIHF book underneath, and both editions land in the same place — a warning** — so do not read the carve-out as a free pass: IHUK does not amend Rule 76.7, and a **second** violation by your team on the same draw is still a bench minor for delay of game. The In-House sentence also says nothing about the "first violation only" limit that IIHF 76.4 writes into its own post-icing rule, so treat one attempt as what you have.
>
> **So the loophole is now yours, and so is the rationing.** Under the current rules — the IIHF's 2026/27 book, and the British amendment alike — the free first violation is not something you carry into every draw — it is something you get on a post-icing draw, once per team, exactly as an NHL centre does. On every other draw the first violation costs you your centre. That is a straight reversal of the 2025/26 position, and it cuts both ways: expect the centre across from you to cheat the drop after an icing and to be careful everywhere else.

⚠️ **The other three North American books split on this, and only one of them gives you the free attempt.** **Hockey Canada writes the carve-out and then rations it by division.** Its Rule 6.5 is titled *Face-off Violation – Junior*, and 6.5(a) provides that *"[i]n Junior divisions, on a face-off following an icing, any face-off violation, including but not limited to late arrival, encroachment, or early movement, will result in a warning"* — delivered by the linesperson blowing the whistle and raising an arm with the index finger extended. **Note that it is wider than the NHL's in what it forgives** — it names encroachment and early movement, not just the late arrival — **and narrower in who gets it**, because outside Junior divisions the rule does not apply and Rule 6.2(a) takes your centre off after an icing as it would on any other draw. Rule 6.5(b) then makes *"[a]ny subsequent violation by the same team on the same stoppage of play"* a minor penalty under Rule 10.1(vii). **Neither USA Hockey nor CARHA writes a post-icing carve-out at all.** USA Hockey Rule 613 sets out the faceoff procedure with no icing exception anywhere in it, and both USA Hockey volumes were searched for a warning attached to a post-icing draw without finding one — the only faceoff-adjacent warning in the Casebook is for a *player change* violation, which it expressly distinguishes: *"Players facing off should only be removed for actual face-off violations."* **CARHA Rule 57 was likewise read in full: no warning, no icing exception, and the word *"warn"* appears nowhere in it.** **So three of the five books soften the draw after an icing, neither USA Hockey nor CARHA does, and Hockey Canada's softening reaches only part of its own membership.** Find out which book — and under Hockey Canada, which division — you are actually in before you plan on a free attempt.

### Closing your hand on the puck is a *penalty* under the NHL and IIHF books, not a violation

```facts
Rule: Everyone on the ice may catch a puck out of the air, provided they "immediately place it or knock it down to the ice" — but not the two takers, not until a third player has touched the puck (NHL and IIHF Rules 67.2, 76.5)
Rule: Not the two takers, though — the note to NHL and IIHF Rule 67.2, printed again at Rule 76.5, bars them from playing the puck with a hand "until such time as a third player (from either team) has at least touched the puck"
Rule: The bar on the two faceoff takers is on playing the puck with the hand at all, not merely on batting it or closing on it (NHL and IIHF Rule 76.5)
Rule: Closing your hand on the puck is a minor penalty under the NHL and IIHF (Rule 67.2), but under USA Hockey Rule 618(a) it is a stoppage and a last-play faceoff rather than a penalty at all — outside the crease only
Rule: A taker who attempts to win the draw by batting the puck with their hand takes a minor for delay of game (NHL and IIHF Rule 67.2)
Rule: An open hand placed over the puck to conceal it is a minor under NHL and IIHF Rule 67.2(ii), but a penalty shot instead when the puck is inside your own goal crease — or an awarded goal if your net is empty (Rules 67.4, 67.5, 63.6)
Rule: USA Hockey escalates in the crease too — 618(a) and 614(b) make a hand holding the puck there a penalty shot or optional minor, and 614(b), an awarded goal on an empty net where the act "has prevented an obvious and imminent goal"
Rule: Deliberately falling on or gathering the puck into your body is a minor (Rule 63.2(v))
Never: Put your hands or your body on the puck in the scrum. Tie up the stick and win the puck with your stick instead
Rule: An ordinary face-off violation cannot be challenged after a goal, but a draw won with the hand can be, as a missed game stoppage — IIHF Situations 38.20 (new in 2026/27) and 38.11 (identical in both 2025/26 editions, the book British hockey plays under)
Rule: Winning the draw with your skates is legal in the NHL but a face-off violation under the IIHF book British hockey plays at every level — it cost a warning in the 2025/26 edition and gets the centre replaced in the 2026/27 one (IIHF Rule 76.3(VI), both editions)
```

**Start with what you are allowed to do, because it is the half of this rule that decides whether there is an offence at all.** NHL and IIHF **Rule 67.2** open with a permission that belongs to every player on the ice: *"A player shall be permitted to catch the puck out of the air but must immediately place it or knock it down to the ice."*

**⚠️ At a draw that permission is switched off — for the two players taking it, and for nobody else on the ice.** The note to NHL Rule 67.2, printed again inside the face-off rule itself as **Rule 76.5**, reads: *"The two players involved in the actual face-off (the centers) are not permitted to play the puck with their hand without incurring a penalty under this rule until such time as a third player (from either team) has at least touched the puck."* The IIHF writes it in materially identical words, at its own 67.2 and again at its 76.5. **Note how wide the bar is: it is *playing* the puck with your hand — not merely batting it, and not merely closing on it.** From the drop until a third player touches the puck, the free option everyone else has is not yours. Once the face-off is complete and a winner is clear, both books hand you back to Rule 79 and ordinary hand-pass rules.

**Now the price, and it is a different category entirely.** Under NHL and IIHF Rule 67.2, a player who closes their hand on the puck takes a **minor penalty** — and a **penalty shot** rather than a minor if the puck he closes on is inside his own goal crease, or an **awarded goal** against him if his net is empty (Rules 67.4, 67.5). **⚠️ USA Hockey draws the first of those lines somewhere else entirely:** under **Rule 618(a)** a closed hand that is not immediately opened is *"Play shall be stopped and a last play face-off shall occur"* — a stoppage, not a penalty — with that book's minor attached instead to picking the puck up off the ice, though **618(a) and 614(b) still reach a penalty shot or optional minor when the puck is in the crease**, and an awarded goal on an empty net where the act *"has prevented an obvious and imminent goal."* And specifically for draws: a minor for "delay of game – face-off violation" is assessed to a player taking the faceoff who "attempts to win the face-off by batting the puck with their hand."

The practical line: **in the scrum after the drop, your hands and your body stay off the puck.** For the two takers that is the rule itself; for everybody else it is advice, because the catch-and-knock-it-down permission survives in a scrum even though acting on it there is a poor bet. An *open* hand is not a loophole — **NHL Rule 67.2(ii)** assesses a minor *"if he places his hand over the puck while it is on the ice in order to conceal it from or prevent an opponent from playing the puck"*, and the note printed directly beneath that clause escalates it exactly where a defensive-zone scrum happens: *"When this is done in his team's goal crease area, a penalty shot shall be assessed (67.4) or a goal awarded (67.5)."* **The IIHF's 67.2(II) is the same clause and the same note, in both its editions.** Nor is your body a loophole — **NHL and IIHF Rule 63.2(v)** assess a minor on *"a player other than the goalkeeper who deliberately falls on or gathers the puck into his body"*, which Rule 63.6 escalates to a penalty shot on the same test, and **it is the puck that has to be in the crease, not you**: *"The significant factor … is the location of the puck at the time it was held, grabbed or gathered into the body. If the puck is in the crease, penalty shot."* What **is** legal is tying up your opponent's stick and winning the puck with your own **stick**. Do not gamble on this one — it is a penalty, not a do-over. **Your skates are a separate problem**: winning the draw with them is legal in the NHL, but IIHF Rule 76.3(VI) makes it a face-off violation where *"one of the centers wins the face-off by using the skates"* — and that is true in **both** the 2025/26 and the 2026/27 editions, so under the book every level of British hockey is played under, kicking the puck back is not available to you. **What the 2026/27 edition changed is the price.** The 2025/26 book had the linesperson *"assess a warning for a 'face-off' violation prior to dropping the puck"*; the 2026/27 book has the linesperson *"have the offending center(s) replaced immediately prior to dropping the puck."* Either way the next violation by anyone in your five is the bench minor — but under the current book the skate win also costs you the man taking the draw. See the flag below.

⚠️ **Under the IIHF's rules a hand at the draw can cost you the goal itself, on review, minutes after nobody on the ice noticed — and this is not a new rule you can wait out.** The **2025/26** Situation Handbook already decided it, at **Situation 38.11**, which is word-for-word identical in both 2025/26 editions and the 2026/27 one. An attacking centre *"plays the puck on a face-off with the glove and it goes undetected. The puck goes directly to a teammate who scores"*; the defending team challenges for a missed game stoppage; and the answer is *"Yes, while this is a missed penalty infraction it does not cancel out that this is also a missed game stoppage event (glove pass to a teammate in the attacking zone)."* 🇬🇧 **Version 1.1 is the edition the In-House Rules' Introduction names**, so this ruling reaches a reader in Britain now, and not at some future edition change. **The 2026/27 handbook adds the other half of the answer** at **Situation 38.20**, new in that edition: a coach challenges a goal on the ground that the attacking centre committed a face-off violation on the draw before it, and the answer is *"No, a face-off violation cannot be challenged as a missed game stoppage event. However, if the attacking center wins the face-off by using their hand, this can be challenged as a missed game stoppage for a hand pass."* **So under the current IIHF book every ordinary violation in this document dies with the drop** — encroachment, early movement, a stick down out of turn, none of them can be reached by a review afterwards. **The hand is the one that can**, because the handbook treats it as a hand pass rather than as a face-off violation, and Rule 38.2(II) makes a *"Missed Game Stoppage Event in the Attacking Zone Leading to a Goal"* reviewable, with Rule 38.9 listing Rule 79, Hand Pass, first among the infractions that qualify — in both editions alike. **Read the zone into that**: Rule 79.2 says *"[p]lay will not be stopped for any hand pass by Players in their own defending zone"*, so it is the attacking-zone draw this reaches, which is also the draw you are most likely to be scoring off. ⚠️ **It does not sit obviously with Rule 76.5, and nothing held here settles that.** Rule 76.5 sends the two takers' hand play to *"a penalty under ➔ Rule 67.2 – Handling Puck"* until a third player has touched the puck, and hands you to Rule 79 only *"[o]nce the face-off is deemed complete"*; the handbook reaches for Rule 79 anyway. Its own Foreword ranks it below the book — *"in case of any contradictions, the valid IIHF Rulebook is always the basis"* — so treat it as how the review is run rather than as the rule. 🇬🇧 **In much of British hockey there is nobody to run it:** the IHUK In-House Rules 2026-27 provide that *"a Coach's Challenge is not permitted in EIH or SIHA competition"*, a sentence that names those two competitions and no others, so it does not settle a BUIHA or an Elite League fixture. **None of it changes the instruction, which is the same under every book: your hands stay off the puck.**

### When your centre gets tossed — and the edition that changed where

```facts
Rule: Any teammate then on the ice may take an ejected centre's place, and the bench cannot help — all five books bar substitution until the draw is done (NHL and IIHF 76.8, USA Hockey 204(a), Hockey Canada 6.2(d), CARHA 57(c)), unless a penalty changes the on-ice strength
Rule: The replacement centre must come into position quickly or risk the puck being dropped without them set — or being ejected themselves, which counts as a second faceoff violation on the same draw and costs the team a bench minor for delay of game (NHL Rule 76.4)
Rule: No time-out shall be granted following a faceoff violation (Rule 87.1; IIHF Rule 87.1 in the same words, unchanged across both IIHF editions)
Rule: Under the IIHF 2025/26 book a violation was a warning to the team, not an ejection, and the warned centre stayed for the re-drop (IIHF 2025/26 Rules 76.4, 76.6, 76.7)
Rule: The IIHF 2026/27 book removes the centre for a violation instead — ejected under 76.6, replaced under 76.3 and 76.4 — so a British reader should expect the centre to come off, as under NHL rules
Rule: And Britain no longer needs the IIHF edition settled to know that — the IHUK In-House Rules 2026-27 replace the centre for all violations bar those on post-icing draws (Section 10, Rule 76)
Rule: The IHUK In-House Rules govern "in England, Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland" and their Rule 76 amendment names no league — but the Elite League appears nowhere in the document, so ask (IHUK In-House Rules 2026-27)
Rule: IIHF counts winning the draw with your skates as a violation in both editions (IIHF Rule 76.3(VI))
Rule: USA Hockey keeps the ejection but makes the second violation a minor on the individual (Rule 613(d))
Rule: Under Hockey Canada the replacement may not be the offender — 6.2(d) has "the player who encroached" identified clearly as the cause and "not eligible to take the face-off", and permits no change of players unless a penalty makes a team shorthanded
Action: Agree the designated replacement in advance — under Hockey Canada it can never be the player who caused the violation — and make the plan a tie-up rather than a set play
```

**Under NHL rules**, when the violation is yours the centre goes and somebody else takes the draw:

- **Any teammate on the ice may step in** (Rule 76.4) — in practice, a winger.
- **They must get set quickly.** Rule 76.4: the replacement "must come into position quickly or risk having the puck dropped by the Linesperson without the player being set," or being ejected themselves, which is the second violation and therefore a bench minor.
- **Agree the backup in advance.** Every winger should know whether they are the designated replacement, and every line should have a default plan for the draw they take without their centre. That plan should almost always be a **tie-up**, not a set play.
- **No timeout can save you.** Rule 87.1: "No time-out shall be granted following a face-off violation." **IIHF Rule 87.1 says it in the same words**, so that one is true wherever you play.

⚠️ **Under Hockey Canada the replacement comes off a shorter list than you would expect — not the ejected centre, not the goaltender, and not the player who caused the violation — so a line whose designated backup is the winger who just encroached has no plan.** **Hockey Canada Rule 6.2(d)** sets out the whole procedure in a single paragraph: *"When a player commits a face-off violation, the official will eject the player from the offending team who was taking the face-off. Any other player of that team, other than the goaltender, may now take the face-off. **The player who encroached must be identified clearly as the player causing the violation and that player is not eligible to take the face-off.** No change of players will be permitted, even if the puck has been physically dropped, unless a penalty has been assessed to make a team shorthanded."* **Two things follow.** The official does not simply take your centre off — he **names** the offender, out loud, and that named player is barred from the dot. And because no change of players is permitted, **you cannot solve it from the bench either**: the replacement has to come from the four skaters already out there, minus the one who is named. ⚠️ **So under that book a line needs two names, not one.** What it costs if the named player steps in anyway is not spelled out, and this is a reading rather than a ruling: Rule 6.2(e) prices *"a second violation of any of the provisions of (a), (b), (c), or (d) during the same face-off"* at *"a Minor penalty by the Referee under Rule 10.1 (viii) – Delay of Game"*, and taking a draw you are not eligible to take is a departure from (d). Ask your officials rather than finding out on a penalty kill.

**CARHA writes the second half of that and not the first — and the second half is not Hockey Canada's peculiarity at all, because all five books write it.** **CARHA Rule 57(c)** carries the bar on substitution — *"No substitution of players shall be permitted until the face-off has been completed and play has been resumed, except when a penalty is imposed which will make a team short-handed"* — so there too the replacement comes from the ice rather than the bench, but that book names no ineligible player, and any of the four may take the re-drop. **The NHL and both IIHF editions write the same bar in a rule of its own, Rule 76.8** — *"No substitution of players shall be permitted until the face-off has been completed and play has resumed except when a penalty is imposed which affects the on-ice strength of either team"* (the NHL's words; the IIHF prints the same sentence at its own 76.8, *Face-off Procedure – Line Changes*, in both editions) — **and USA Hockey writes it at Rule 204(a)**, which closes the line-change procedure with *"no additional player substitutions shall be permitted until the face-off has been completed legally and play has resumed, except when a penalty or penalties are imposed that affect the on-ice strength of either or both teams."* ⚠️ **So nobody comes over the boards to take a re-drop in any of the five books.** **What the other four do not write is the ineligible player.** **The NHL and both IIHF editions say only that the replacement is "any teammate then on the ice"** (Rule 76.4); the phrase *"not eligible"* appears nowhere in either book's face-off material, and **USA Hockey Rule 613(d)** likewise offers replacement *"by any teammate then on the ice"* with no exclusion attached. **So the two-name plan is Hockey Canada's requirement specifically** — but it costs nothing to carry everywhere, and a line that has agreed a second name has also agreed who is *not* taking the draw, which is the part that goes wrong in the four seconds you have to sort it out.

> **⚠️ In Britain this used to be the section you could skip, and it is not any more.** An IIHF rule book is the base rule set at every level of UK hockey — the Elite League, the NIHL, the English and Scottish recreational and junior game, university hockey and the GB sides ([UK and England Rules](../foundation/uk_rules.md)) — and until the summer of 2026 **which edition your league was administering decided whether your centre could be thrown out of a draw.**
>
> **That question is now answered by a British document rather than by an IIHF one.** The **IHUK In-House Rules 2026-27** (Revision 1, published 25 July 2026) add a *Section 10 – Game Flow* amendment to Rule 76 that the 2025-26 edition did not have at all: *"With the exception of face-offs following icings, the linesperson shall immediately replace the centre for all violations."* Those In-House Rules are, in their own words, the *"overriding interpretation of how the IIHF Rule Book is to be applied domestically"*, so **it does not matter which IIHF edition sits underneath: in a game played under them, a faceoff violation costs you your centre — on every draw except one that follows an icing, which that sentence expressly excepts.** The rest of this box explains what changed underneath and why, because knowing that is how you recognise an official still administering the older procedure.
>
> **Two limits on that, and neither is small.** First, **scope**: the In-House Rules apply the IIHF book *"in England, Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland (the Home Countries)"*, the Rule 76 amendment carries no league restriction of its own — where the same document's fighting provision is headed *"Applicable Leagues: NIHL (all leagues), SNL, and BUIHA Varsity"* — and **the Elite League is not mentioned in the document anywhere**. Nothing here tells you what an EIHL game does. Second, **edition honesty**: the 2026-27 In-House Rules name different IIHF editions on their cover page and in their Introduction, so they are not evidence of which IIHF book British hockey is on generally. **That contradiction does not reach the faceoff answer**, because IHUK wrote the replacement into its own text. [Rules Primer](../foundation/rules_primer.md) sets the inconsistency out.
>
> **The 2025/26 edition had no faceoff ejection anywhere in Rule 76.** A violation was a **warning to the offending team**, and the draw was taken again with the same centre — for premature movement, for a teammate entering the circle, and for bad positioning alike. **⚠️ The 2026/27 edition, published in June 2026, replaced every one of those warnings with a removal**, at 76.4, 76.6 and 76.7: the offending team's draw-taker is *"ejected from the face-off circle"*, and where the older book had the official assess a warning the newer one has him *"replaced for that face-off by any teammate then on the ice."* **So under the current book the NHL paragraphs above are yours: the centre goes, any teammate on the ice may step in, and they must get set quickly.** Two violations by the same team on the same draw is a bench minor under both editions and under the NHL (IIHF Rule 76.7).
>
> **What that changes about how you play, and it is a straight reversal.** Under the 2025/26 book your specialist took every draw he lined up for, there was no "designated replacement" problem, and what you protected was the warning. **Under the 2026/27 book you need the back-up plan the NHL section describes**: every winger should know whether they are the designated replacement, and the plan for a draw taken without your centre should be a **tie-up**, not a set play. ⚠️ **If you learned "my specialist always takes the draw" under the old book, that is the habit to unlearn** — and in Britain you can now unlearn it without asking anyone, because the In-House Rules say so directly. What is still worth asking about is **enforcement**: the change is recent enough that a coach or an official may still describe the old procedure from memory.
>
> **The IIHF also lists a violation the NHL does not have, and it deletes a technique — in both editions.** IIHF Rule 76.3(VI): a violation occurs where **"one of the centers wins the face-off by using the skates"**. Under NHL rules, kicking the puck back is legal; under either IIHF book, it is not. **The price is what moved.** The 2025/26 book had the Linesperson *"assess a warning for a 'face-off' violation prior to dropping the puck"*; the 2026/27 book has the Linesperson *"have the offending center(s) replaced immediately prior to dropping the puck."* So a skate win used to burn your team's warning and now costs you the draw-taker instead, with the *next* violation by anyone in your five still the bench minor either way. **The In-House Rules do not amend 76.3, and their own sentence reaches "all violations"** — so in Britain a skate win is a violation and the violation costs you your centre. **Take "kick it back" out of your repertoire** — it is in the technique list below because the document is written to NHL rules, and it is not available to you under either edition.
>
> **USA Hockey keeps the ejection but changes the escalation** — this flag is not yours if you play in Britain. Rule 613(d) has the same headline consequence as the NHL — *"if a player, other than the player facing off, fails to maintain their proper position, the center of their team shall be ejected from the face-off"* — so a winger still costs a draw-taker. What differs is the second violation: USA Hockey assesses *"a minor penalty for delay of game … to the player committing the second violation"*, a minor on an individual rather than the NHL's bench minor on the team. Same two minutes off the clock, different player in the box.
>
> Many rec, beer and youth leagues enforce faceoff violations loosely or not at all, and some simply re-drop with nobody removed. **Under the 2025/26 IIHF book that *was* the rule; under the 2026/27 book the puck is still re-dropped, but your centre is out of the circle when it is.** So a re-drop with your centre still in the dot is now the officials being lenient rather than the book being followed — **with one exception you should recognise before you complain about it: after an icing, in Britain and under the NHL and the 2026/27 IIHF book alike, leaving the centre in the dot is exactly right.** How strictly the rest of this is enforced at your level is a matter for your officials. Ask.

## Faceoff Technique

A draw is hockey's nearest thing to a closed skill: same start position, same cue, same distance, every single time. That is exactly the kind of thing humans get good at through reps.

### The stance

```facts
Technique: Get low — deep knee bend, chest over the puck, weight on the balls of your feet
Goal: React faster from a loaded position, and be impossible to push off the dot
Position: Feet either side of the restraining lines, staggered rather than square, back foot loaded
Position: Square to the opponent's end, hips already cocked toward where you intend to send the puck
Key: Eyes on the linesperson's hand, not the opponent — watching the opponent means you are always second
Never: Get low by pushing your head out over the dot — depth comes from knees and hips, and the IIHF handbook has linespersons break up centres leaning in "almost ear to ear" (Situation 76.42)
```

- **Get low.** This is the single most repeated instruction from coaching sources, and for two reasons: you react faster from a loaded position, and you cannot be pushed off the dot. Deep knee bend, chest over the puck, weight on the balls of your feet. ⚠️ **Get the depth from your knees and hips, not by pushing your head out over the dot.** Chest over the puck is the cue; head over the dot is the position the IIHF Situation Handbook tells linespersons to break up, and under the 2026/27 handbook a centre who will not move back is removed from the draw (Situations 76.42 and 76.43).
- **Feet either side of the restraining lines**, staggered rather than square, with your back foot loaded — that's the foot you'll drive off.
- **Square to the opponent's end**, as the rule requires, but with your hips already cocked toward where you intend to send the puck.
- **Eyes on the linesperson's hand, not the opponent.** This is the highest-leverage habit in the whole skill. You are reacting to the puck's release, not to your opponent's movement. Watching the opponent means you are always second.

### The grip

```facts
Technique: Bottom hand right down near the blade — that is where the leverage, speed and power come from
Options: Top hand halfway down the shaft or up on top of it; the coaching sources disagree, so try both
Technique: For the reverse grip, roll your bottom hand over so your knuckles face down
Read: Take the reverse grip when you have committed to a backhand pull and want raw speed and strength
Risk: The reverse grip telegraphs, and gives you almost nothing on the forehand
Options: Some players roll both hands into a "kayak paddle" grip for maximum backhand snap
```

- **Standard draw grip:** the two How To Hockey pieces cited here disagree on the top hand. Their faceoff-tips article describes "their top hand about halfway down the stick, and their bottom hand right near the blade"; their pro-tips article quotes former NHL player Jeff Serowik as "one hand on top of shaft and the other hand really low on shaft for leverage." **What both agree on is the part that matters: the bottom hand comes right down near the blade.** That is where the leverage, speed and power come from. Try both top-hand positions — neither is your normal skating grip and neither should feel like it.
- **The reverse grip (backhand grip):** roll your bottom hand over so your knuckles face down — the coaching cue from How To Hockey is to "hold the stick as if you were going to cross-check someone in the teeth." This gives you far more pulling power and quickness on the backhand, and it's what centres use when they intend to yank the puck backward hard rather than sweep it.
- **When to use the reverse grip:** when you have committed to a backhand pull and want raw speed and strength — typically defensive-zone draws you must not lose, and offensive-zone draws set up for a **one-timer** (a shot taken straight off a pass, without stopping the puck first). Its cost is that it telegraphs, and it gives you almost nothing on the forehand. A centre who lines up in a reverse grip every time is a centre who can be read.
- Some players use a two-handed **"kayak paddle"** grip — both hands rolled, stick held like a paddle — for maximum backhand snap.

### Faceoff technique: the techniques

```facts
Key: There is no single correct draw — the good centres own three or four
Technique: Pull the puck back on the backhand, usually with the reverse grip — pulling is a stronger and faster motion than pushing
Convention: How often each technique wins is not something this document can tell you — the NHL faceoff data behind it records zone, strength and winner, not technique
Options: Tie up their blade instead of playing the puck, turning a 1-on-1 into a scramble your wingers lean into
Risk: The stick lift is the higher-reward version and it fails badly if you miss
```

There is no single correct draw. There are seven or eight, and the good centres own three or four.

- **The clean backward win (the draw proper).** Sweep or yank the puck behind you to a designated teammate. This is the one that shows up on highlight reels and it is genuinely the highest-value outcome — but it is also the lowest-percentage, because it requires beating the other centre outright.
- **The backhand pull.** Rotate and pull the puck back on the backhand side, usually with the reverse grip — How To Hockey's cue for that grip is that it *"helps you improve your quickness and power"*. Pulling is a stronger and faster motion than pushing, and that is the whole case for building a draw around it. Whether it wins more often than the other techniques is not something this document can tell you: the NHL faceoff data behind it records which zone a draw was in, which strength situation, and who won it — nothing at all about how.
- **The forehand pull.** The same idea on the forehand. Weaker and slower than the backhand equivalent for most players, which is precisely why your handedness matters (below).
- **Tying up the stick.** Instead of playing the puck, get your blade over or under your opponent's and neutralise it. You are not winning the draw; you are ensuring **nobody** wins it, and converting a 1-on-1 into a 4-on-4 scramble that your wingers are already leaning into. Against a superior draw-taker this is the correct play, not a surrender.
- **The stick lift.** A more aggressive version: lift their blade off the ice entirely, then take the puck. Higher reward, and it fails badly if you miss.

### The techniques that use your body, your feet or your hands

```facts
Rule: Contact before the drop is a violation under NHL and IIHF 76.7 and can draw a minor on the taker at the referee's discretion as well, where USA Hockey 613(e), Hockey Canada 6.2(c) and CARHA 57(b) make that minor mandatory; afterwards they permit contact only "in the course of playing the puck"
Rule: Only Hockey Canada defines when a face-off is complete — Rule 6.2, the puck "contacts the ice surface before contacting the stick or any part of the body of the player taking the face-off", so there contact timed to the drop is contact before completion
Never: Lean on the man before the puck lands; no book in this document makes that free
Rule: Where body checking is banned you cannot go through the man — USA Hockey 604(a) prohibits it at 12 & under and below, in "all Girls'/Women's age classifications and all non-check Adult classifications", and Hockey Canada 7.3 at U13 and below, in female hockey and in any Member-approved division
Read: Try the "lose it forward" play at the neutral-zone dots, where there is space behind the other centre — go round the other centre, not through them
Rule: ⚠️ Never skate through the other centre to reach a puck pushed past them — in women's hockey IIHF 101.1 obliges you to "skate around the stationary Player", at least a minor and up to a major plus an automatic game misconduct
Never: Use "lose it forward" in your own end
Rule: Batting the drop is legal with the stick; a taker who tries to win the draw by batting it with the hand takes a minor (NHL and IIHF Rule 67.2)
```

The remaining four put your body, your feet or your hands into the draw, and each of them has a rule attached. Three are here; the fourth — using your feet — has a section of its own after them, because what the five books say about a skate at the dot runs a long way past the draw.

- **Going through the man.** Lean into your opponent once the puck is on the ice, taking the ice and the puck together. **Both halves of that are the rule, not a coaching preference.** *Lean, not drive*: the only contact the books permit is contact *"in the course of playing the puck after the face-off has been completed"* (Rule 76.7), so the puck rather than the man has to be the thing you are going for. *And once it is down, not as it falls*: contact **before** the drop is a face-off violation under NHL and IIHF Rule 76.7, and the minor that goes with it is **discretionary rather than absent** — both books have the referee *"may, at their discretion impose a minor penalty or penalties"* (the IIHF's wording; the NHL's is the same sentence in the masculine), and both then treat the contact as a faceoff violation in its own right whether or not the penalty is called — the NHL and the IIHF's 2026/27 edition having the linesperson order the offending centre replaced, where the 2025/26 edition warned the team instead. Under **USA Hockey 613(e), Hockey Canada 6.2(c) and CARHA 57(b) that minor is mandatory.** So there is no book in which leaning on the man before the puck lands is free. ⚠️ **Only one of the five books says when a face-off is complete, and it draws the line at the ice rather than at the drop.** Hockey Canada's **Rule 6.2**: the face-off is complete *"when the puck has been legally and fairly dropped, meaning the puck contacts the ice surface before contacting the stick or any part of the body of the player taking the face-off."* The NHL and the IIHF say only that *"the face-off ends when the puck has been legally dropped"*; CARHA Rule 57 says neither, defining only when the *conduct* of a face-off commences; USA Hockey's Glossary defines when a face-off *commences* — *"The face-off commences with the dropping of the puck"* — and never says when it is completed. **So under Hockey Canada, a body timed to the drop is a body before completion. Wait for the puck to land.**
- **The "lose it forward" play.** Deliberately push the puck *forward* past your opponent and go after it. Used most often at the neutral-zone dots, where there is space behind the other centre and no immediate danger if it fails. In your own end it is a bad idea; in the offensive zone it can beat a centre who is committed to pulling backward. ⚠️ **Go round the other centre, not through them, and in women's hockey that is a rule rather than a preference.** You will hear this play described as "skate through him onto it"; do not. IIHF Rule 101.1, in Section 11 — Women's Ice Hockey, and word for word in the 2025/26 and 2026/27 editions alike, provides that *"[a] Player, who is stationary, is entitled to that area of the ice. It is up to the opponent to avoid body contact with such a Player. If that Player is stationed between the opponent and the puck, the opponent is obliged to skate around the stationary Player"*, and that *"[a]ny move by a Player to step or glide into an opposing Player will be assessed at least a minor penalty (2') for an 'illegal hit'."* That is exactly this play's fact pattern: the puck is past the other centre and the other centre is between you and it. The scale runs to *"[a] major penalty (5') and automatic game misconduct penalty"*, and in Britain the **IHUK In-House Rules 2026-27**, Section 11, add that in women's hockey *"[a]ny player who commits a foul that is assessed a Major Penalty will, in all cases, also receive an additional Game Misconduct Penalty and be ejected from the game."* **In the men's game the same play is contact for the puck rather than a check** — the IIHF Glossary allows skaters near the puck to *"push and lean into each other provided that possession of the puck remains the sole object of the contact"* — but body checking is prohibited outright in **USA Hockey**'s 12 & under classifications, *"all Girls'/Women's age classifications and all non-check Adult classifications"* (Rule 604(a)), and that book counts *"physically forcing the opponent off the puck and with no effort to legally play the puck"* as an illegal check (Rule 604 Note 1). **Hockey Canada bars it across a different list**, its Rule 7.3 stating that the body-checking rule *"applies only in divisions of U13 and below, in female hockey, and any other divisions approved by a Member of Hockey Canada"* — so in those, deliberate use of the body to gain an advantage is a penalty and incidental contact in pursuit of the puck is not. **Chase the puck, not the ice the other centre is standing on.**
- **Batting it out of the air.** Advanced, and legal with the **stick** — several coaching sources describe attacking the puck mid-drop. With the **hand** it is a minor for delay of game — face-off violation, assessed to a taker who *"attempts to win the face-off by batting the puck with their hand"* (NHL and IIHF Rule 67.2) — and the note to that rule keeps your hand off the puck altogether until a third player has touched it. Timing this requires watching the linesperson's release, not the ice.

#### Using your feet — the puck, the man, and the goal

```facts
Rule: Kicking the puck back is legal in the NHL and a face-off violation under IIHF 76.3(VI), so it is not available to you in Britain at any level (IIHF Rule 76.3(VI), both editions)
Rule: USA Hockey, Hockey Canada and CARHA write no face-off bar on kicking the puck, so the technique rests on a general kicking rule rather than a face-off one — USA Hockey 627(c), Hockey Canada 6.10(a), CARHA 69
Rule: A kick-back that gets tipped in is no goal under USA Hockey 627(c) or Hockey Canada 6.10(b), which disallow a goal off a deflected kick, or under CARHA Rule 60(c) — no goal where "after being kicked, the puck deflects off any player or stick into the net"
Rule: Hockey Canada scopes its own no-goal rule twice — 6.10's preamble exempts a puck that "deflects off a player's skate, without a deliberate kicking action", and 6.10(b) excepts "a defending player kick[ing] the puck into their own goal", which at your own end zone draw is your net
Rule: Kicking or attempting to kick an opponent is a match penalty under NHL 49.3, USA Hockey 627(b), Hockey Canada 7.1(c)(iii) and CARHA 48(c); under IIHF 49.3 the referee may assess a major, and the game misconduct is automatic once they do
Rule: Under USA Hockey a skate does not need a kicking motion to be an offence — 627(a) is a major plus a game misconduct for using the skate to "push off" an opponent, which its Note 2 defines as skate contact with an opponent "in a non-kicking motion"
Never: Send the skate at the man. The permission at the dot is for the puck, in every one of the five books
Action: Before you build an offensive-zone draw around the kick-back, find out which book your league runs — the tip behind it is a goal in only two of the five
```

**Using your feet.** Kick the puck back to a teammate, or open your skate as a backstop so a tied-up puck squirts the right way. Legal in the NHL. ⚠️ **But every one of the five books permits kicking the *puck* and penalises kicking the *man* at the top of its scale — and a faceoff is the one place on the ice where two opponents deliberately put their skates and sticks within inches of each other.** Kicking or attempting to kick an opponent is a **match penalty** under NHL Rule 49.3 (*"A match penalty shall be imposed on any player who kicks or attempts to kick another player"*), USA Hockey Rule 627(b) (*"A match penalty shall be assessed to any player or goalkeeper who kicks, attempts to kick or recklessly endangers an opponent by kicking"*) Hockey Canada Rule 7.1(c)(iii) (*"Attempting to kick or deliberately kicking an opponent"*) and CARHA Rule 48(c) (*"A player who attempts to kick or deliberately kicks an opposing player shall be assessed a Match penalty"*); under IIHF Rule 49.3, in both the 2025/26 and 2026/27 editions, the referee *"may assess a major penalty and an automatic game misconduct penalty"* for the same act. ⚠️ **USA Hockey does not require a kicking motion at all, which is what makes the opened-skate half of this technique the risky half in that book.** Its Rule 627(a): *"A major plus a game misconduct penalty shall be assessed to any player or goalkeeper who uses their skate to 'push off' an opponent"*, with Note 2 to the same rule defining that push-off as *"the action where a player uses their skate(s) in a non-kicking motion to make contact with the opponent."* **Both of those clauses sit immediately above the permission quoted below, in a rule USA Hockey titles *Kicking Opponent or Puck*. Send the skate at the puck, never at the man.** **A violation under IIHF rules, and therefore not a technique available in British hockey at any level** — IIHF Rule 76.3(VI) makes it a violation where *"one of the centers wins the face-off by using the skates"*. Note the wording: it is *winning* the draw with the skates that is caught, so opening a skate as a backstop is a finer line than kicking the puck back, and neither is worth learning if this is your book. See the flag above. **⚠️ The other three books write no face-off bar of their own — but read what actually permits the technique there before you build a play on it.** USA Hockey Rule 613, Hockey Canada Rule 6.2 and CARHA Rule 57 were each read in full and none says anything about skates at the dot. What speaks is each book's **general kicking rule**, which is not a face-off rule at all: **USA Hockey Rule 627(c)** — *"Kicking the puck shall be permitted provided the puck is not kicked by an attacking player and entered the goal either directly or after deflecting off any player including the goalkeeper"*, the same rule that bars the *"kick shot"* of driving your own stick with your foot — **Hockey Canada Rule 6.10(a)** — *"Kicking the puck will be permitted in all zones."* — and **CARHA Rule 69** — *"Kicking the puck shall be permitted in all zones. This action cannot result in a goal being scored."* So under those three books the draw technique is available **by inference**, from a general permission plus the absence of a face-off-specific bar, rather than because either book says anything about a face-off. ⚠️ **And Hockey Canada says something about the opened skate in particular, in the preamble above its own clauses**: *"Kicking refers to a player propelling the puck by way of a deliberate action with their foot. A puck that deflects off a player's skate, without a deliberate kicking action on the part of that player, will not be considered kicking."* **So under that book a skate opened as a backstop is not a kick at all** — which is a relief rather than a permission, and it does not reach the deliberate kick-back, which is a kick on the rule's own definition.

⚠️ **And the goal-scoring half of those kicking rules splits between the books, which is what decides whether the kick-back is worth anything at an offensive-zone draw.** **Under the NHL and the IIHF a kicked puck that deflects off a stick — your own or a teammate's — is a good goal** (Rule 49.2(ii) and (iii) in both books). **Two of the three North American books answer that fact pattern the other way**, Hockey Canada at Rule 6.10(b) — *"when an attacking player kicks the puck and it deflects off any other player or object and goes into the goal"* — and USA Hockey at Rule 627(c), *"either directly or after deflecting off any player including the goalkeeper"*, with its Casebook's Situations 4 and 5 to that rule disallowing the goal where the kick deflects in off the kicker's own stick or off a teammate. **So under USA Hockey and Hockey Canada a kick-back that gets tipped in is not a goal** — the kick has to end in a teammate's possession and a clean shot. That takes the tip play off the menu behind it under those two books. ⚠️ **CARHA answers it too, and the answer is in a different rule from the one that permits the kick** — which is why it is easy to report that book as silent. Its **Rule 69** is two sentences entire and carries no deflection clause; the clause is at **Rule 60(c)**, under *Goals and Assists*: *"A goal shall NOT be allowed if the puck has been directed into the net as a result of a distinct kicking motion by an attacking player, **or after being kicked, the puck deflects off any player or stick into the net.**"* **So all three North American books disallow the tipped kick, in near-identical terms** — and in CARHA's case the sentence is nowhere near the rule that permits the kick, so a reader checking Rule 69 alone will not find it. ⚠️ **Hockey Canada scopes its own no-goal rule in two places that matter at a draw**, and both make it narrower than a flat ban. Rule 6.10's preamble exempts a puck that *"deflects off a player's skate, without a deliberate kicking action on the part of that player"* from counting as kicking at all; and 6.10(b) opens *"Kicking the puck cannot result in a goal, **unless a defending player kicks the puck into their own goal.**"* **Read that second one at your own end**: a defensive-zone draw is where your skate is nearest your own net, and under that book a puck you kick into it counts. How much North American rec and youth hockey runs on USA Hockey and Hockey Canada is a question about leagues rather than about books, and this document has no count for it, so find out which book your league uses before you plan an offensive-zone draw around the tip. [Passing and Receiving](../technique/passing_and_receiving.md) carries the same four-book split for the loose puck in your feet.

## Reading and Beating Your Opponent

### Everyone has a tell

```facts
Action: Watch other lines' draws from the bench, not just your own
Read: A rolled-over bottom hand means a backhand pull is coming
Read: Hands high on the shaft means a sweep or a tie-up rather than a yank
Read: Which foot is loaded tells you which way they intend to rotate
Read: Stick blade angle tells you where they want the puck to go
Read: A tight grip and low coiled stance means a clean-win attempt; relaxed and upright means a tie-up
```

Most centres have exactly one preferred technique and one strong side. Two shifts of attention is usually all it takes to find them.

- **Watch other lines' draws, not just your own.** You get roughly 56 opportunities a game to scout the two or three centres you'll face, and most of them happen while you're on the bench.
- **Read the grip.** A rolled-over bottom hand means a backhand pull is coming. Hands high on the shaft means a sweep or a tie-up rather than a yank.
- **Read the stance.** Which foot is loaded tells you which direction they intend to rotate. Stick blade angle tells you where they want the puck to go.
- **Read the tension.** A tight grip and a low, coiled stance usually means an aggressive attempt at a clean win; a relaxed, upright posture often means they're planning to tie you up.

### Watch the hand, not the man

```facts
Key: Look at the linesperson's hand and the puck, not your opponent's stick
Goal: Turn the draw into a pure reaction-time problem, which you can train
Risk: Reacting to your opponent leaves you a step behind and open to feints
Options: Some coaching sources watch the official's elbow instead, on the grounds that it moves fractionally earlier
Read: Linespersons have tendencies too — height of drop, speed of drop, how long they hold
```

This is the one universal. **Look at the linesperson's hand and the puck, not at your opponent's stick.** Reacting to the drop is a pure reaction-time problem you can train; reacting to your opponent means you are always one step behind, and it makes you vulnerable to feints. Some coaching sources recommend watching the official's **elbow** rather than the hand, on the grounds that the elbow moves fractionally earlier.

Linespersons also have tendencies — height of drop, speed of drop, how long they hold. Notice them.

### Vary your approach

```facts
Action: Change the technique, not just the target — pull one back, tie the next up, sweep the third
Risk: Win three straight draws to the same defenceman and the fourth will be jumped
Key: Even a technique you are worse at has value if it stops an opponent pre-loading against your best one
Technique: A shoulder feint, hip fake or delayed stick approach can draw an opponent into moving early — which, if they move before the drop, is a faceoff violation
Risk: The feint can be charged to you instead — 76.7 names the centre moving prematurely, and a feint that becomes contact is a minor, mandatory under USA Hockey, Hockey Canada and CARHA
Goal: Make them move before the drop — that is a faceoff violation, but the remedy NHL Rule 76.7 writes for it is a re-drop, not a removal
Rule: Neither IIHF edition removes their centre for a premature move — the 2025/26 book warned their team and re-dropped, the 2026/27 book re-drops (IIHF 76.7, both); the ejections live in 76.6 and in 76.7's contact paragraph, not in its opening sentence
Rule: ⚠️ Britain is where the feint actually pays — the IHUK In-House Rules 2026-27 have the linesperson "immediately replace the centre for all violations" except after an icing, and a premature move is a violation (Section 10, Rule 76)
```

The counter to a scouted centre is unpredictability. If you win three straight draws pulling to the same defenceman, the fourth one will be jumped. Change the *technique*, not just the target: pull one back, tie the next one up, sweep the third. Even a technique you're worse at has value if it prevents the opponent from pre-loading against your best one. (Kicking it back is a fourth option **under the NHL, but not under the IIHF's** — and under USA Hockey, Hockey Canada and CARHA it is available **by inference**, from a general kicking permission plus the absence of any face-off bar, rather than because those books say anything about a draw. In every one of the five, kicking the *opponent* rather than the puck is a match penalty in four of them, and under **IIHF Rule 49.3** the referee *"may assess a major penalty and an automatic game misconduct penalty"* — discretionary as to the major, automatic as to the misconduct once it is given. See "Using your feet — the puck, the man, and the goal" above.)

Deception belongs here too: a small feint of the shoulder, a hip fake, or a deliberately delayed stick approach can draw an opponent into moving early — which, if they move before the drop, is a faceoff violation. **What that is worth depends on your book — and here it is the British answer that is the generous one, which is the other way round from every other violation on the list.** ⚠️ **Under NHL rules, drawing him early does not take him out of the dot.** Rule 76.7 opens on premature movement and writes its own remedy into the same sentence, and that remedy is the re-drop — the face-off *"must be conducted again"* — while the removals in that book are written for other things: for encroachment by a teammate and for the establish-position order at 76.6, and for pre-drop contact in 76.7's own later paragraph. (Rule 76.3(i) does have a linesperson replace a centre *"not positioned for the face-off"* immediately before the drop. **Whether that reaches a centre who is positioned and then twitches is not written down** — the book sets out two remedies and never says which governs, and there is no published NHL casebook to settle it, so do not plan on either.) **Both IIHF editions do the same as the NHL here**: the 2025/26 book added a warning to their team before the re-drop, the 2026/27 book deleted the warning, and neither one removes anybody for it. 🇬🇧 **Britain is where the feint actually pays, and it pays because IHUK wrote it rather than because the IIHF did** — the In-House Rules 2026-27 have the linesperson *"immediately replace the centre for all violations"*, and a premature move is a violation, so there you do end up facing a winger. That sentence opens *"[w]ith the exception of face-offs following icings"*, so on the post-icing draw expect it not to arrive. **Under either IIHF edition the second violation on the same draw is still the bench minor**, so a centre who has just been caught cheating the drop cannot afford to try anything at the re-drop. Bait him once, then take a clean, straight draw.

⚠️ **Two things belong with that, because a feint is the one movement at the dot that can land on you rather than on him.** First, **the premature-movement clause names the centre, and you are one.** NHL and IIHF Rule 76.7 open with *"[i]f a center should move prematurely prior to the face-off"*, so a shoulder that goes early is your violation on exactly the terms it would be his — and it costs you what it would have cost him: a re-drop under the NHL and under both IIHF editions, and **your own place in the circle in Britain**, where the In-House Rules replace the centre for every violation but a post-icing one. ⚠️ **It also spends one of the two violations that make a bench minor**, whichever book you are under. Second, **a feint that touches him is a penalty, not a violation.** You are about one stick length apart, so the shoulder is the movement most likely to become pre-drop physical contact, and no book makes that free. Under NHL and IIHF Rule 76.7 the referee *"may, at his discretion impose a minor penalty"*; under **USA Hockey Rule 613(e)** it is mandatory — *"[a] minor penalty for interference **shall** be assessed to any player facing-off who makes any physical contact with their opponent's body by means of their own body or by their stick"*; **Hockey Canada Rule 6.2(c)** is the same, the referee *"**will** assess a Minor penalty or penalties to the player(s) whose action(s) caused the physical contact"*; and **CARHA Rule 57(b)** writes no discretion clause at all — *"[f]or violation of this rule, the Referee **shall** assess a Minor penalty on the player(s) whose action(s) caused the physical contact."* **So feint with the shoulder, never into him.** Under any of those three books the margin for getting that wrong is two minutes rather than a re-drop.

### Handedness and which side you're strong on

```facts
Convention: Coaching sources call the strong-side draw a right-shot centre on the right side, or a left-shot on the left
Key: Your strong side is the circle where your natural backward pull sends the puck toward the boards
Action: Take ten draws in each circle and you will know within a minute
Risk: On your weak side the board-side pull is a forehand — slower and weaker for most players
Never: Solve that by pulling to your backhand in your own end — it sends the puck to the middle, the worst place it can go
Options: Match the circle to the centre, flip your hands, or put a left-shot and a right-shot centre out together
```

A centre's shot handedness determines which circle they're better in, and every coaching staff builds around it.

**One term, two meanings in this document — keep them apart.** "Strong side" for a *centre*, in this section, is about handedness: the circle where his natural backward pull sends the puck to the boards. Elsewhere in this document — the defenceman "on the draw side" at an offensive-zone draw, or the corner the puck goes to after a defensive-zone loss — "strong side" carries the ordinary hockey meaning used throughout this corpus: whichever side of the ice the puck is on. The two usually coincide, because a team tries to put its centre on his handedness strong side, but they are not the same claim, and this section's meaning is the handedness one.

- The consensus in coaching sources (Ice Hockey Systems, Hockey's Arsenal) is that a **strong-side draw** is a **right-shot centre on the right side** of the ice, or a **left-shot centre on the left side**.
- On your **strong side**, you can pull the puck backward on your **backhand toward the boards** — the most powerful motion available to you, sending the puck to the safest part of the ice.
- On your **weak side**, that same board-side pull has to be made on the **forehand**, which is slower and weaker for most players. The alternative is to pull on your backhand — but that sends the puck toward the **middle of the ice**, which in your own end is the worst place it can go.

> **Flagged:** the sources above state the mapping (right shot / right side) but do not spell out the reference frame — "right side" as you stand at the dot facing the opponent's end. That is the standard reading and it's the one used here, but the sources are not explicit about it. **The reliable self-test that doesn't depend on anyone's convention: your strong side is the circle where your natural backward pull sends the puck toward the boards.** Take ten draws in each circle and you'll know within a minute.

![](diagram:faceoff-strong-side-dot)

**What teams do about it:**
- **Match the circle to the centre.** This is why coaches send a specific centre out for a specific dot, and why the rules that let you *choose* the dot (after an icing, or starting a power play) are more valuable than they look.
- **Flip your hands.** Some NHL centres — Vincent Trocheck is the frequently-cited example — take weak-side draws holding the stick the other way round, recreating strong-side mechanics on the wrong side of the ice. It is a real technique, and it is a lot of work: you are learning a second, mirror-image skill. Worth it if you take 1,000 draws a season; probably not if you take 100.
- **Use two centres.** Many teams have a left-shot and a right-shot centre on the ice together in key situations, and simply swap who takes the draw depending on the circle. This is free, and almost no rec team does it.

## Full Five-Player Alignments

> **This entire section describes coaching choices, not rules of hockey.** Every team's faceoff alignment is different, and the differences are large. What follows is the most common default at each location, with realistic alternatives named. **Find out what your team runs before your first shift** — and specifically, find out what the *call* is for each draw. A faceoff alignment that half the line is guessing at is worse than no alignment.
>
> The defaults below assume the house system used throughout this guide: a **low zone collapse (zone) defensive-zone coverage** and a **2-1-2 forecheck** (see [Defensive Zone Coverage](defensive_zone_coverage.md) and [Forechecking Systems](forechecking_systems.md)).
>
> **A word on "left" and "right," because that confusion is exactly what this section is for.** Every end of the rink has two faceoff circles, one nearer each side board, and which one is on your left changes with which net you're defending and which way you're facing — it isn't fixed. That is why the alignments below are written in terms that do not depend on which way you are facing. Left and right are not banned — you will hear them constantly, and this document uses them further down where a fixed reference makes them safe — but they need that reference agreed first, and at a draw it usually is not. Two things about the two circles *are* fixed, and everything below is written in those terms instead: which one is nearer the boards you'll exit toward — called **boards-side** below, with the other called **inside** — and which one suits your centre's shot hand, his **strong side**, covered in "Handedness and which side you're strong on" above. Learn boards-side/inside and strong-side/weak-side rather than left and right, and the alignment below applies unchanged at whichever circle you're actually standing at: it's a mirror image at the other one.

### Defensive-zone draws

```facts
Priority: Do not lose this cleanly — a tie-up is acceptable, a clean loss to their strong-side point is not
Position: Boards-side winger on the outside hash mark; inside winger on the inside hash mark, facing the slot
Position: Boards-side defenceman behind and outside that winger; slot defenceman in front of the goalie
Action: On a clean win, pull to the boards-side defenceman to rim it out or hit the boards-side winger
Action: On a clean loss, wingers release to the points on the drop; the centre covers the man who beat him, boards-side D takes the corner or half-wall, and the slot D holds the net front and blocks the lane
Convention: This whole defensive-zone alignment is the house zone system, including who takes the corner and who holds the net front — under man-to-man each winger follows his man and those assignments change, so ask which your team plays
Action: On a tie-up everyone jumps; the inside winger and the boards-side defenceman are usually closest
Never: Pull the puck to the middle — a draw won into the slot is barely better than a loss
```

The highest-stakes alignment in hockey. It has to work on a win, a loss and a tie-up, because you get all three.

**The standard setup:**

- **Centre** — in the dot. Your job is stated simply: **do not lose this cleanly.** A tie-up is an acceptable outcome; a clean loss to their strong-side point is not.
- **Boards-side winger** — on the **outside hash mark**, the one nearer the boards. Default is the winger whose natural side matches the circle. You are the primary breakout outlet on a win and the first man to the opposing point on a loss.
- **Inside winger** — on the **inside hash mark**, facing the **slot** (the lane of ice straight out from the net between the two faceoff circles — the *high slot* runs from the dots up to the top of the circles, the *goalmouth* is the immediate net front). You have the harder job: on a loss you must get to the far point, and on a scramble you are the closest body to the most dangerous ice.
- **Boards-side defenceman** — behind and outside the boards-side winger, near the hash marks, ready to retrieve anything pulled back to the wall or to get behind the net.
- **Slot defenceman** — in the slot in front of the goalie, responsible for the net front and for blocking a point shot.

**Common variation worth knowing:** many teams set their defencemen on their **"off" sides** for defensive-zone draws — the right-shot D on the left boards and vice versa — so that a puck pulled back to the wall arrives on their forehand and the breakout pass up the boards is a forehand pass. It costs you a little on defensive coverage and gains you a lot on the exit. Ask which your team does.

**Which circle you're actually at matters as much as who's on it.** Boards-side and inside above are relative to whichever circle the draw is at, not to a fixed side of the rink — the alignment doesn't change from one circle to the other. What does change is which circle is your centre's strong side, and a team that gets to choose its dot — after an icing, or to start a power play — uses that choice to put him there; see "Handedness and which side you're strong on" above.

![](diagram:faceoff-dzone-alignment)

![](diagram:faceoff-dzone-alignment-other-circle)

![](diagram:faceoff-dzone-clean-loss)

![](diagram:faceoff-dzone-tie-up)

**On a clean win:**
- Pull to the **boards-side defenceman**, who rims it out or hits the boards-side winger for a direct exit. This is the default because the boards are the safe half of the ice.
- The **wingers release** the moment the puck is secured — you are not needed in the battle, you are needed out on the wall **between the hash marks and the goal line**, which is the outlet spot [Breakouts](breakouts.md) covers. That is at or just below the dot line, roughly twenty feet out from the goal line — not thirty feet up the wall, which would put you above the outlet and make the pass long, flat and into your back.
- **Never pull the puck to the middle.** A defensive-zone draw won into the slot is barely better than a loss.

**On a clean loss:**
- **Both wingers go straight to the points.** In the house zone system assumed here, this is the call, and you make it on the drop rather than after you see where the puck went — it is the only thing standing between you and a shot from the top of the circle with your goalie screened. **Under man-to-man coverage the job changes:** each winger takes the specific opponent they were assigned before the draw and follows him, which may mean staying low if his man stays low. A hybrid ("zone with man-on-man in the low slot") splits the difference — the wingers still release to the points, but the low defender stays glued to a specific body. Ask which one your team plays.
- **Centre finds their centre.** Having lost the draw, your job is now to cover the man who beat you, who will be heading for the **high slot** (the area from the faceoff dots up to the top of the circles) or the **net front** (the immediate goalmouth).
- **Slot defenceman holds the net front and blocks the lane.** Do not chase the puck to the point; your winger has it.
- **Boards-side defenceman takes the strong-side corner or half-wall.** The **half-wall** is the boards level with the faceoff dot — about 20 feet out from the goal line (Rule 1.9), so barely a third of the way to the blue line, which sits 64 feet out (Rule 1.5). It is *not* the same thing as the **point**, which is the area just inside the blue line, not the line itself.

**On a tie-up — the most common outcome, though that is a coaching estimate rather than a counted figure:**
- **Everyone jumps.** A tied-up draw is a loose puck in a two-foot area with eight players around it, and it belongs to whoever moves first. This is the single most under-coached moment in hockey — coaching observation rather than a counted ranking — and it shows: players wait to see what happens instead of moving on the drop.
- The **inside winger** and the **boards-side defenceman** are usually the two closest bodies and should be the two who attack it.
- **Call it.** "Loose!" from anyone with eyes on the puck. See [On-Ice Communication](../foundation/on_ice_communication.md).


### Offensive-zone draws

```facts
Position: Strong-side defenceman at the point, feet already set and stick already loaded — this is the shooter
Position: Weak-side defenceman at the far point, holding the line as the safety against a counterattack
Action: Centre wins it back rather than forward
Read: The inside winger is the usual target for a quick pull-back, and the net-front driver if the draw is lost
Priority: On a clean loss the priority flips instantly to not conceding a counterattack
Action: On a clean loss, wingers pressure the puck rather than retreat, the strong-side D recovers to the line, and the weak-side D holds — a 2-1-2 forecheck already in position
Key: An offensive-zone tie-up is a free 50-50 puck in the other team's end — a good outcome, not a failure
Convention: The offensive-zone alignment described here is a common default, not a rule — they differ from team to team, so find out what your team runs
```

The one place in hockey where you get to run a genuine set play with a stationary defence and a known start position. **What follows is a common default rather than a rule of hockey.** Offensive-zone alignments differ from team to team; find out what your team runs before your first shift.

**The standard setup:**

- **Centre** — in the dot, trying to win it back rather than forward.
- **Boards-side winger** — outside hash mark, ready to attack the wall or spin off it.
- **Inside winger** — inside hash mark, the most common recipient of a quick pull-back for a shot from the circle, and the primary net-front driver if the draw is lost.
- **Strong-side defenceman** — at the point on the draw side, **feet already set, stick already loaded**. This is the shooter.
- **Weak-side defenceman** — at the far point, holding the line as the safety against a counterattack.

**The same mirroring applies here as in your own end.** Boards-side and inside are relative to whichever circle hosts the draw, not to a fixed side of the ice — and "strong-side" for the point man here means the draw side, not necessarily his shooting hand (see "Handedness and which side you're strong on" above, which is where the two meanings of "strong side" in this document are kept apart).

![](diagram:faceoff-ozone-alignment)

**On a clean win:** run the set play (see below). The important thing is that the shooter's feet are already planted — this is one of the very few shots in hockey where you don't have to receive, settle and set. That is worth more than a slightly better shooting angle.

**On a clean loss:** the priority flips instantly to **not conceding a counterattack**. The weak-side defenceman holds; the strong-side defenceman recovers to the line; the wingers pressure the puck rather than retreating, because you have four skaters above the puck and their exit options are limited. This is the moment a 2-1-2 forecheck is at its most effective, because everyone is already in position for it.

**On a tie-up:** attack the puck with the two nearest bodies and keep the two defencemen honest at the line. An offensive-zone tie-up is a free 50-50 puck in the other team's end — a good outcome, not a failure.

### Neutral-zone draws

```facts
Position: Both wingers wide in their lanes, level with or just behind the dot
Position: Both defencemen back toward your own blue line, one either side of the middle
Action: On a win advance the puck immediately, and look to carry or pass into the zone with control
Risk: A won neutral-zone draw that ends in a dump-in has produced very little — one author's tracking of the 2018-19 Swiss National League, not the NHL, so read the direction rather than the multiple
Action: On a loss, gap up immediately and defend the rush
Mindset: This is where you experiment — a lost neutral-zone draw costs you almost nothing
Convention: The neutral-zone alignment described here is a common default, not a rule — they differ from team to team, so find out what your team runs
```

The lowest-stakes draws, and the ones most worth taking a risk on. **The setup below is a common default rather than a rule of hockey**, and neutral-zone alignments differ from team to team; find out what your team runs.

**The standard setup:**

- **Centre** in the dot; **both wingers** wide, level with or just behind the dot, in their lanes.
- **Both defencemen** back toward your own blue line, one either side of the middle, ready to defend a rush if the draw goes the wrong way.

**On a win:** advance the puck immediately. Neutral-zone possession is worth having but it decays fast — **Daniel Weinberger**, writing at Hockey Graphs, found that neutral-zone faceoff wins mostly turn into **dump-ins**, and that possessions containing a dump-in produced 5.5 shots and 0.17 **expected goals** per 100 possessions versus 32 shots and 0.68 expected goals for those without one. (*Expected goals* is a model estimate of how many goals an average shooter would score from those shots, given where and how they were taken — it values chance quality, not just chance count.) **⚠️ Note the league before you carry these numbers anywhere.** Weinberger's data is **InStat tracking of the 2018-19 Swiss National League**, not the NHL — he says so in the article — so this is the one set of figures in this document that is not measured on the league the rest of it is written to. **The four figures are quoted exactly as the article gives them** — but note what a roughly six-fold gap implies: this is one author's tracked sample of possessions following neutral-zone faceoff wins in one league in one season, not a league-wide measurement, and part of the gap is selection rather than causation (a possession that never generated a controlled entry is *why* it ended in a dump-in). Treat the direction as solid and the multiple as approximate. A won neutral-zone draw that ends in a dump-in has produced very little. Look to carry or pass into the zone with control. See [Zone Entries](zone_entries.md).

![](diagram:faceoff-neutral-zone)

**On a loss:** your defencemen are the whole plan. Gap up immediately and defend the rush — see [Defending the Rush](defending_the_rush.md).

**Why this is where you experiment:** a lost neutral-zone draw costs you almost nothing. It is the right place to try the "lose it forward" play — going **round** the other centre rather than through them, which in women's hockey is IIHF Rule 101.1 rather than a preference (see "The techniques that use your body, your feet or your hands") — the weak-side flipped grip you're learning, or a technique you want your opponent to see now so it's in their head for the defensive-zone draw later.

## Set Plays

Set plays off draws are the closest hockey gets to American football. They also fail constantly, and the reason is usually complexity rather than execution — a coaching observation, not a measurement; nobody counts why set plays fail.

> Everything in this section is a **coaching choice**. These are the common patterns; your team's playbook will differ.

### Offensive-zone plays

```facts
Options: The D one-timer, the winger walk-out, the tip play, or a board-side variation
Key: The point of the D one-timer is that the shooter's feet are set before the puck moves
Risk: Every defensive alignment expects the D one-timer, so someone must clear the inside winger's shooting lane
Read: Use the winger walk-out against a team that immediately pressures the point
Options: The tip play is the safest, because a point shot into traffic rarely turns into a counterattack
Risk: Board-side loops and swaps work precisely once per game against an attentive opponent
```

**The D one-timer.** The centre pulls the puck straight back to the strong-side defenceman, who shoots first-time. The whole point is that the shooter's feet are set and traffic is already in place before the puck moves. Its weakness is that it is the play every penalty kill and every defensive alignment expects — the inside winger's shooting lane needs to be cleared by someone, usually the net-front player stepping across.

**The winger walk-out.** The centre pulls the puck to the inside winger in the circle, who takes a stride toward the middle and shoots or feeds the slot. Better than the D one-timer against a team that immediately pressures the point, because you're attacking the space they vacated.

![](diagram:faceoff-ozone-d-one-timer)

![](diagram:faceoff-ozone-winger-walkout)

**The tip play.** The pull goes to the point, but the intended outcome is a deflection rather than a clean shot — someone screens, someone tips. Lower expected value per shot, higher rebound and chaos value, and it works when the goalie can see everything else you do. This is also the safest play, because a point shot into traffic rarely turns into a counterattack.

**The board-side variations.** Coaching sources describe plays where the boards-side winger loops **over the top of the circle to the inside hash mark** for a one-timer, or where the winger and defenceman **swap positions** before the draw to create an unfamiliar passing lane. These work precisely once per game against an attentive opponent.

### Defensive-zone plays

```facts
Options: The quick rim, the direct exit, the reverse, or off the glass and out
Key: Every opposing forechecker is stationary at the whistle, so a hard rim or a direct exit is past them before they move
Rule: Under NHL and IIHF rules — the KHL reportedly too, unverified — your goalie may only play the puck behind the goal line inside the trapezoid (Rule 1.8; penalty NHL 27.8 / 63.2(viii), IIHF 27.7 / 63.2(VIII)); the sole exception is skate contact with the crease
Rule: The goalkeeper's restricted area is IIHF Rule 27.7, not the NHL's 27.8; England Ice Hockey's Rules & Regulations 22.3 say it is "not currently enforced" in England and Wales — either way, keep your goalie inside it
Read: Play it as though it binds — your goalie stays inside the trapezoid, so a rim that dies in the corner is yours to fetch, not theirs
Rule: USA Hockey, Hockey Canada and the CARHA rec book write no trapezoid, so under those three a rim that dies in your own corner is your goalie's to fetch — but none of them lets him sit on it out there
Rule: Freezing the puck away from the net is a minor in three books — USA Hockey 614(c) behind the goal line with the body "entirely outside of the goal crease" or outside its "goalkeeper's privileged" area; CARHA 58(b) only where deliberate; Hockey Canada's Interpretation 3 to Rule 10.1(a), clause v
Key: Closer in, and in front of the goal line, your goalie has considerably more latitude under every book — Goaltender owns that test, not this section
Never: Rim it halfway. Rim it hard enough to reach your winger instead, or tight enough for the goalie to stop it behind the net
Read: Use the reverse against a team that overloads the boards side
Action: Call off the glass and out when you are tired, pinned, or a man down
```

**The quick rim.** Win the puck to the boards-side defenceman, who rims it hard around the boards immediately. No possession, no risk, and you are out. What makes it better than it looks is the standing start: every opposing forechecker is stationary at the whistle, so a hard rim is past them before anyone is moving. **The trapezoid does bear on this play, and the goalie it constrains is yours.** On a defensive-zone draw your own goalie is twenty feet away, and under NHL and IIHF rules he may only play a puck behind the goal line inside the marked trapezoid (Rule 1.8 in both books for the geometry; NHL 27.8 / 63.2(viii) and IIHF 27.7 / 63.2(VIII) for the penalty). So a rim that dies in your own corner is not his to fetch — a defenceman has to go and get it, under pressure, with his back to the forecheck. **The KHL is commonly said to have one as well, though that one is unverified here: no KHL rulebook was available to check it against.** **⚠️ USA Hockey, Hockey Canada and the CARHA rec book write no trapezoid at all**, so under those three the same rim *is* your goalie's to fetch. **What none of them lets him do is sit on it out there** — each restricts freezing the puck away from the net on a minor penalty, and Hockey Canada's carries no warning first. ⚠️ **And the trigger that bites this play is not the one about the privileged area.** **USA Hockey Rule 614(c)** lists **four**, any one of which makes it a minor, and the first is the corner: *"[t]he puck is behind the goal line and their body is entirely outside of the goal crease."* (The others are failing to play it with the stick when able before being pressured, being outside the *"goalkeeper's privileged"* area its Note defines, and holding the puck against the goal frame, the boards or the back of the netting.) **CARHA Rule 58(b) reaches the same corner, and not on the same terms** — a minor for a goalkeeper who *"deliberately falls on or gathers the puck into their body, or holds or places the puck against any part of the goal or boards when the puck is behind the goal line and the goalkeeper's body is entirely outside the boundaries of the goal crease area."* ⚠️ **Three differences from USA Hockey's, and they do not all run the same way:** CARHA needs the act to be **deliberate**, where 614(c) writes no intent word at all; USA Hockey needs the freeze to have **caused a stoppage of play**, where CARHA does not; and CARHA writes no privileged area anywhere in its book, so it has that one behind-the-goal-line trigger where USA Hockey has four. **Whether CARHA's *"when"* clause conditions both of its limbs or only the second, the book does not say.** ⚠️ **Its corners in front of the goal line are not free either: Rule 61(b) is a minor for a goalkeeper who *"deliberately holds the puck and in the opinion of the Referee, is causing an unnecessary stoppage of play"*, with no location condition at all** — so under CARHA what limits your goalie out there is the referee's judgement rather than the goal line. **So a goalie who chases a dead rim into the corner under either of those books has to play it, not cover it.** Hockey Canada reaches the same place from the crease rather than from the goal line: Interpretation 3 to Rule 10.1(a), clause v — *"[w]hen a goaltender leaves their crease, they will not be allowed to freeze the puck. If they do, they will be assessed a Minor penalty. No warning will be issued."* **In front of the goal line and closer in your goalie has considerably more latitude** — see [Goaltender](../positions/goaltender.md), which owns the test, and [Rules Primer](../foundation/rules_primer.md) for the rules themselves. That reverses the play: check your book before you decide who chases a dead rim. **Rim it hard enough to carry all the way round to your winger, or tight enough that the goalie can legally stop it behind the net and leave it.** Never in between. Full treatment in the trapezoid-and-retrieval section of [Breakouts](breakouts.md). **🇬🇧 In England and Wales, treat that as unsettled.** The IIHF book carries the restricted area at Rule 27.7, but England Ice Hockey's *Rules & Regulations* 22.3 (2024-25) say it is *"not currently enforced"* and neither the 2025-26 In-House Rules nor the current 2026-27 edition mentions it — both were searched, flattened as well as raw, for *trapezoid*, *restricted area* and Rule 27, with no hit in either — though their Introduction says *"unless otherwise stated, all rules will be enforced in line with the IIHF Rule Book"*, which points the other way. Play it as though it binds — your goalie stays inside the trapezoid, so a rim that dies in the corner is yours to fetch.

**The direct exit.** Win the puck to the boards-side defenceman, who hits the boards-side winger already breaking up the wall. Retains possession, and it is fast — the whole play happens before the opposing forecheck is organised, because they are also stationary at the whistle.

**The reverse.** Win the puck behind you and let a defenceman take it behind the net to change the angle. Good against a team that overloads the boards side on defensive-zone draws.

**Off the glass and out.** Not a play so much as an outcome, but it should be the explicit call when you're tired, pinned, or a man down. Nobody has ever been beaten by a puck in the neutral zone.

### Keeping it simple enough to work

```facts
Convention: Two or three plays maximum, per zone
Action: One-word calls from the centre at the dot, acknowledged by everyone — a play nobody heard is not a play
Key: Every play needs a defined failure branch: if we lose it, X does Y
Never: Get clever when there is confusion, no call, or a replacement taking the draw. Tie up in your own end instead, point shot in theirs
```

The failure mode is universal: teams install six plays, run each of them badly, and the players spend the drop trying to remember which one is on.

- **Two or three plays maximum**, per zone. A team that runs one play excellently beats a team that runs six adequately.
- **One-word calls.** The centre calls it at the dot; everyone acknowledges. A play nobody heard is not a play.
- **Every play needs a defined failure branch.** "If we lose it, X does Y." Most set plays are drawn up only for the win, which is why teams that lose an offensive-zone draw look so disorganised.
- **The default is always the simple one.** If there's confusion, no call, or a replacement taking the draw: tie-up in your own end, point shot in theirs.

## Special Teams Faceoffs

Special-teams draws are about 10 per game (9.6 in 2024-25, 10.1 in 2025-26, from NHL official data) — a small share of the total, and a hugely disproportionate share of the ones that matter. See [Special Teams](special_teams.md) for the formations themselves.

### Power-play draws

```facts
Rule: The team awarded the power play chooses which end-zone dot the draw starts at — NHL and IIHF Rule 76.2; USA Hockey gives no such choice (Rules 612(b), 624(a))
Rule: USA Hockey gives no dot choice here or after an icing (Rules 612(b), 624(a)); the IIHF follows the NHL
Action: Where you have the choice, pick your centre's strong side or the side your one-timer threat shoots from
Key: The entry you avoid is worth more than the draw you win
Priority: Win the draw, or make sure you do not lose it cleanly — a tie-up that keeps the puck in the zone is fine
Never: Fire a fancy set play that ends in a clearance
```

**The team awarded the power play chooses which end-zone dot the draw starts at** (Rule 76.2). Use it: pick your centre's strong side, or the side your one-timer threat shoots from. A surprising number of teams don't think about this at all — coaching observation, not a count.

> **⚠️ USA Hockey gives you no such choice — neither here nor after an icing.** Rule 612(b) sends a penalty draw simply to *"a defending zone face-off spot of the offending team"*, and Rule 624(a) sends an icing draw to *"an end zone face-off spot in the defending zone of the offending team"*. Neither rule offers a selection, and the phrase "choice of" appears nowhere in the 2025-29 book in this context. **The IIHF does follow the NHL and give you the choice, so this right is yours everywhere in Britain** — IIHF Rule 76.2: *"The team awarded the 'Power-Play' will have the choice of which end zone spot the 'face-off' will take place at to start the 'Power-Play'."* So if you play North American rec or youth hockey, everything below about picking your dot is unavailable to you — the official picks it, and your preparation goes into being ready for either side rather than into choosing.

League-wide, power-play units win about **54% of their draws** (53.6% in 2024-25, 54.4% in 2025-26, computed from NHL official data), which reflects both better personnel and the fact that the kill often has a winger in the dot.

**The entry you avoid is worth more than the draw you win.** This is the real argument for offensive-zone power-play draws, and it isn't about the set play at all. If you win the draw, you keep the zone. If you lose it and the puck gets cleared, you have to break out, regroup, and re-enter against a **set** penalty kill standing on its blue line, with four killers already in their structure. Zone-entry research is consistent that controlled entries generate roughly **twice** the shot volume of dump-ins at even strength — [Zone Entries](zone_entries.md) covers those figures, the strength state each was measured at and where each came from, including the separate and narrower power-play comparison — and a power play forced to re-enter against a set kill mostly gets dump-ins. A won draw is worth perhaps one extra possession's shot volume; a lost draw that costs you the zone can cost you a third of your two minutes.

So on the power play: **win the draw, or make sure you don't lose it cleanly.** A tie-up that keeps the puck in the zone is a fine power-play faceoff outcome. Firing a fancy set play that ends in a clearance is not.

### Penalty-kill draws

```facts
Mindset: You are not trying to create — you are trying to end the possession
Risk: A defensive-zone draw shorthanded is where the disadvantages pile up — a man down, in your own end, against a set unit with a rehearsed play
Key: A tie-up is a win here too — anything but their point man shooting into a screen is acceptable
Action: Put your best draw man out even if he is not a natural killer
```

Everything is inverted. You are not trying to create; you are trying to end the possession.

- **A defensive-zone draw shorthanded is where the disadvantages pile up.** You are one man down, in your own end, against a set unit with a rehearsed play and a shooter with his feet planted.
- **Winning it clean is enormous**, because of the icing exemption — which carries enough rule with it to have its own section below.
- **A tie-up is a win here too.** Any outcome that isn't "their point man shoots into a screen" is acceptable.
- **Put your best draw man out even if he isn't a natural killer.** The draw is worth more than the twenty seconds of coverage.

#### The icing exemption at a shorthanded draw — and the one that has nothing to do with being shorthanded

```facts
Rule: Under the NHL, IIHF and Hockey Canada you may ice freely while below your opponent's on-ice count (Rule 81.6; Hockey Canada 6.7(b)), so a won draw taken below that count is a penalty-free clear — the count decides, not whether a penalty is running, with one NHL exception after a major expires
Rule: USA Hockey grants the shorthanded icing exemption only at Youth 15-Only and above, Girls 16U and above, High School and Adult (Rule 624(b)(1)); below those levels a won shorthanded draw still has to be cleared legally
Rule: The NHL's major-penalty icing exception bites at the expiry, not during the major — where the penalised player was ejected or hurt, Rule 20.3 wants a substitute seated before the major expires, immediately if minors are stacked on it
Rule: Missing the NHL's substitute deadline costs twice — a bench minor for illegal substitution under Rule 68.2, and no clear until the next stoppage (81.6, 20.3)
Rule: IIHF Rule 81.6 carries neither carve-out — no age restriction and no major-penalty exception — but it runs on the on-ice count: a puck released before your penalty expires is never icing, and an expiry ends the exemption only where it leaves you level, so on a 5-on-3 you are 4-on-5 and exempt
Rule: An empty penalty box is priced under the IIHF too — Rule 20.3 wants the substitute seated immediately and Rule 68.2 charges a bench minor; what a British team does not also lose is the icing exemption
Rule: CARHA grants the shorthanded exemption too and rations it no further — Rule 65(d), the count taken "at the instant the puck is shot", with no age classification and no major-penalty exception anywhere in Rule 65
Rule: A puck sent the length of the ice directly off the draw is not icing in any book and at any strength — NHL and IIHF 81.5, Hockey Canada 6.7(c)(iv), USA Hockey 624(b)(3), CARHA 65(e); four say "directly", USA Hockey "as a result of" the taker — treat any other touch as ending it
```

**Winning it clean is enormous — where the shorthanded icing exemption applies.** You may **ice the puck freely while shorthanded** (Rule 81.6), so a won draw taken while you are below their on-ice numbers is a penalty-free clear — and a chance to change tired killers. (How much time that burns depends entirely on how quickly they retrieve and re-enter; no published figure puts a number on it, so don't trust the "fifteen seconds" figure you sometimes hear.) There is no other situation in hockey where a won faceoff so directly converts into time off the clock. **Two carve-outs before you plan the shift around it**, both owned by [Special Teams](special_teams.md): USA Hockey gives the exemption only at Youth 15-Only and above, Girls 16U and above, High School and Adult (Rule 624(b)(1)), and the NHL withdraws it in one narrow case — **which bites *after* the major has ended, not while you are killing it, so that carve-out is not what takes the clear away during the major.** Where the player serving a major **has been ejected or is injured**, **NHL Rule 20.3** gives his team until *"a stoppage of play prior to the expiration of the major penalty"* to seat a substitute on the penalty bench — ⚠️ **unless that player is also serving minor penalties on the clock alongside the major, when the same rule wants the substitute *"immediately"*** — and fail to, and from that expiry until the next stoppage they *"continue[] to play short-handed but are not permitted to ice the puck as they are no longer short-handed by reason of a penalty"*. ⚠️ **And losing the clear is the second half of that bill rather than the first** — **Rule 68.2** makes the failure to seat anybody an illegal substitution *"calling for a bench minor penalty."* Below those USA Hockey levels the won draw still has to be cleared legally. **Neither carve-out is yours in Britain — the bench minor is.** ⚠️ **IIHF Rule 68.2 carries the NHL's sanction sentence word for word, and IIHF Rule 20.3 wants the substitute in the box *"immediately"* rather than by the expiry, so an empty box costs two minutes under both books and the clear under only one.** IIHF Rule 81.6 grants the exemption in its opening sentence — *"If the puck was so shot by a Player of a side below the numerical strength of the opposing team, play shall continue, and the 'icing' violation shall not be called"* — and then says nothing further about majors or empty penalty benches. ⚠️ **Do not read that as no condition at all, though, because the condition the rule *does* carry is a faceoff condition, and it is the one that actually catches killers.** The same rule's second paragraph — word for word in the 2025/26 and the 2026/27 editions alike — fixes the moment the count is taken, not the test being applied: *"When a team is 'short-handed' as the result of a penalty and the penalty is about to expire, the decision as to whether there has been an 'icing' shall be determined at the instant the penalty expires"*, and *"[t]he action of the penalized Player remaining in the penalty box will not alter the ruling."* ⚠️ **That last sentence is about the one player whose penalty has just run out, and not about a teammate still serving a live one.** All the expiry does is count the returning player back in, so **it ends the exemption only where that leaves you level.** **Kill a lone minor and it does: fire the puck down the ice after your own clock has run out and you are called for icing even though that player is still sitting in the box** — and what it costs you is the thing this whole section is about: a defensive-zone draw, in your own end, that you cannot change lines before. **On a 5-on-3 it does not: when the first penalty expires you are 4-on-5, still below their strength, and you may still ice the puck.** **The relief runs the other way too, and a killer told only about the risk will stop making a legal play:** the same paragraph provides that *"[s]hould the puck be released from the stick of the Player shooting the puck down the ice prior to the penalty expiring, the 'icing' infraction shall not apply"*, so a puck you get away before your penalty expires is fine even if it expires while the puck is still travelling. **That is still the simpler rule** — one condition rather than three — **but it is not an unconditional one.** **Hockey Canada is with the IIHF on the head count, and attaches no level restriction:** Rule 6.7(b) provides that *"[i]cing will not be called and play will continue if the team shooting the puck is below the numerical strength of the opposing team at the instant the puck is shot"* — **note that it takes the count at the shot rather than at the expiry** — and its **Note 1** supplies the same box limb in plainer words, *"A team will no longer be considered shorthanded once the penalty time expires, irrespective of whether or not the penalized player has left the Penalty Bench"*. ⚠️ **Never quote that Note on its own, because read alone it sounds absolute.** The same rule's **Interpretation 3** supplies what it leaves out, in a single parenthesis: the moment a player's own time runs out they are *"considered to have completed their penalty and… on the ice, **for the purpose of equal numbers (or not)**."* **USA Hockey writes the count test into the very sub-clause that carries its classification limit, and is the one book that carries both moments in a single sentence**: Rule 624(b)(1) requires that *"[t]he offending team is short-handed (below the on-ice numerical strength of their opponent) when the puck is shot"*, adds that *"[t]he determination is made at the time the penalty expires"*, and provides that *"if the puck was shot prior to the penalty time expiration, regardless as to the position of the penalized player, no icing shall be called."* **So the head count is common ground in every book and is not a British quirk, even though the books do not all take that count at the same moment** — what divides them is the two carve-outs, not the test; [Rules Primer](../foundation/rules_primer.md) sets it out book by book. ⚠️ **CARHA is the fifth book, and it grants the exemption with neither of the two carve-outs attached.** Its **Rule 65(d)** puts it flat: *"'Icing the puck' shall not be called if a team is below the numerical strength of the opposing team **at the instant the puck is shot**"* — the count taken at the shot, as Hockey Canada takes it, and **Rule 65 was read in full: it writes no age classification and no major-penalty exception anywhere in it.** Its **Note 2** carries the same box limb the other books write — *"A team shall not be considered below the numerical strength of its opponent by reason of a penalized player remaining in the penalty box after their penalty has expired."* So of the five books it is USA Hockey alone that withholds the exemption from part of its own membership, and it withholds it by age classification rather than by penalty type.

⚠️ **And there is a second icing carve-out that belongs to this document rather than to an icing section, because it is about the draw itself — every one of the five books writes it, and none of them makes it conditional on being shorthanded.** A puck that goes the length of the ice **directly off the draw** is not icing at all. **NHL Rule 81.5**: *"If the puck shall go beyond the goal line in the opposite half of the ice **directly from either of the players while facing-off**, it shall not be considered a violation of this rule."* **IIHF Rule 81.5** carries that sentence word for word, in the 2025/26 and 2026/27 editions alike, with one difference of house style: it prints *"Players"* capitalised, as it capitalises that defined term throughout. **Hockey Canada Rule 6.7(c)(iv)** puts it among the circumstances where *"[i]cing will not be called"* — *"[i]f the action that causes the icing is directly from a player taking the face-off."* **USA Hockey Rule 624(b)(3)** has no icing where *"[t]he puck travels the length of the ice as a result of either player participating in a face-off."* And **CARHA Rule 65(e)**: *"Play shall continue and the 'icing the puck' infraction shall not be called if it is caused directly from either player taking the face-off."* ⚠️ **Four of the five print the same limiting word — *directly*. USA Hockey is the one that does not**, tying its carve-out instead to the puck travelling the length of the ice *"as a result of"* one of the two takers. **Play all five as though the strict word were in every one of them**, because four of them write it and the fifth does not say otherwise. This is the puck your own stroke sends down the ice from the dot — not a puck you win back to a defenceman who then clears it, and not one that clips a stick on the way. **The moment anybody else touches it, you are back on the ordinary rule.** ⚠️ **And note what it is not: it is not a shorthanded rule.** It holds at even strength, for both teams, in every one of the five books — which is the half a killer already relying on Rule 81.6 will not think to look for, and the half that matters at a defensive-zone draw you are taking at full strength.

### 4-on-4 and 3-on-3

```facts
Convention: 4-on-4 is two forwards and two defencemen, or three forwards and one, depending on the coach
Priority: At 4-on-4 keep both back players honest — a lost draw is a genuine odd-man threat
Key: 3-on-3 draws are close to the highest-leverage moment in the game, because a loss is often a chance within ten seconds
Rule: The five-second setup limit is not enforced in overtime (NHL and IIHF Rule 76.3) — use the time; USA Hockey 613(c) writes no such exception
```

- **4-on-4** — two forwards and two defencemen, or three forwards and one defenceman depending on the coach. The ice is enormous and a lost draw is a genuine odd-man threat, so the alignment is generally more conservative than at even strength: keep both back players honest.
- **3-on-3 overtime** — draws are close to the highest-leverage moment in the game, because a lost draw at 3-on-3 is often a scoring chance within ten seconds and the game ends the moment somebody scores. Note that in overtime **the five-second setup limit is not enforced** (Rule 76.3), so you have time to get this exactly right. Use it. **That relaxation is the NHL's and the IIHF's** — both write it into Rule 76.3 in the same words — **and it is not USA Hockey's**: Rule 613(c) sets the five-second limit with no exception for overtime at all, so under that book the whistle still starts a clock.

## Situational Faceoffs

### After an icing

```facts
Rule: The team that iced cannot change lines (NHL and IIHF Rules 81.4, 82.1) and cannot take a time-out (NHL and IIHF Rule 87.1)
Rule: The freeze falls on the team that iced, so the home team keeps last change when the visitors ice it (NHL and IIHF Rule 82.1)
Rule: After an icing the IIHF imposes both the no-change rule and the time-out ban in the NHL's words; USA Hockey imposes neither, but bounds both by the same line change procedure — five seconds a side to change, and the time-out requested before that procedure ends (Rules 624, 204(a), 636(f))
Rule: Hockey Canada is a fourth answer — 6.7(d) applies the no-change rule only in U18AAA, Junior and Senior at the Member's option, and 6.7(d)(iii) lets either team take its time-out after an icing, though the team that iced may not substitute during it
Rule: Under NHL and IIHF rules the attacking team picks the dot (Rule 81.2); USA Hockey gives no choice (Rule 624(a))
Rule: The first faceoff violation after an icing ejects nobody's centre, on either team (NHL Rule 76.4)
Rule: The free first faceoff violation after an icing is Hockey Canada's only in Junior divisions (Rule 6.5(a)), and USA Hockey does not write one at all
Rule: The IIHF 2025/26 book had no post-icing carve-out and needed none, because its first violation on any draw was already a warning (IIHF 2025/26 Rules 76.6, 76.7)
Rule: The IIHF 2026/27 book adopted the NHL ejection and the NHL post-icing carve-out together, so in Britain the free first violation is now rationed to post-icing draws (IIHF 2026/27 Rule 76.4)
Action: Play fast — their disadvantage is fatigue, and it evaporates the moment they get to breathe
Action: When you iced it, get the puck deep or off the glass at the first opportunity; nothing clever
```

The single most exploitable situation in the game, and it works in both directions.

**When they iced it:**
- **The team that iced the puck cannot change lines** (Rules 81.4 and 82.1), and **cannot take a timeout** (Rule 87.1). Whoever is on the ice for them, stays on the ice — and they are usually tired, because teams ice the puck when they are pinned. **This one travels to the IIHF book intact** — IIHF Rules 81.4 and 87.1 impose both restrictions, 87.1's icing sentence in the NHL's own words and 81.4 in the NHL's own words *plus* one added exception, for which see "With the goalie pulled" below — so it is available to you in British and European league hockey. **USA Hockey imposes neither** (Rule 624 restricts no substitution, Rule 204 gives the normal change windows, Rule 636(f) attaches no icing condition to time-outs), so under that book there is no edge here at all. ⚠️ **Neither permission is open-ended, though, and one procedure closes both.** Rule 204(a) gives the visiting team five seconds to change and then the home team five, after which *"no additional player substitutions shall be permitted until the face-off has been completed legally and play has resumed, except when a penalty or penalties are imposed that affect the on-ice strength of either or both teams"*, with a subsequent *"undue delay by either team in placing their lineup on the ice or changing players"* warned and then a minor for delay of game — and the time-out is bounded by that same window, Rule 636(f) requiring that *"[t]ime-outs must be requested during a stoppage of play prior to the conclusion of the line change procedure."* So a coach under that book genuinely may ice the puck and then take the time-out; what they may not do is ask late. The Glossary closes the procedure when the referee lowers their arm at the end of the home team's five seconds, and *"[a]t this time, no further player changes may be made until play has resumed or a penalty has been assessed prior to face-off."*

  **⚠️ Hockey Canada is a fourth answer, and under most of that book there is no freeze to exploit at all.** Its Rule 6.7(d) applies the *"'no-change on icing' rule"* only *"[i]n categories of U18AAA, Junior, and at the option of the Member in Senior"* — so below those categories the team that iced changes on the ordinary line-change procedure exactly as you do, and the matchup edge this section describes does not exist for you. Being *late* still costs you there, as it does everywhere. Where it does apply, the exceptions are the NHL's three, and **the time-out survives where the other three books take it away**: 6.7(d)(iii) provides that *"[e]ither team is permitted to take their time-out … following an icing, but during that time out, the team in violation of icing will not be permitted to make player substitutions."* [Rules Primer](../foundation/rules_primer.md) owns the full four-book comparison, including what an attempted change costs under that book.
- **You can change; they cannot.** The team that iced is frozen — Rule 82.1: *"A team that is in violation of… Rule 81 – Icing shall not be permitted to make any player substitutions prior to the ensuing face-off."* So you get to answer whoever they are stuck with, which is one of the largest matchup edges available to a coach, and it lasts one shift.

  **And the icing team's line-change freeze holds at home as well as on the road.** Rule 82.1's other clause is often quoted short, and quoted short it looks like a problem: the home team may make any desired substitution *"except in cases following an icing"* — whose icing? The full sentence is *"The home team may then make any desired substitution, except in cases following an icing, which does not result in the delay of the game."* That exception is the same bar the rule spells out later — *"A team that is in violation of Rule 63.8 – Delaying the Game or Rule 81 – Icing shall not be permitted to make any player substitutions prior to the ensuing face-off"* — and that Rule 81.4 restates for icing alone, putting *"this rule"* where 82.1 names two of them: it falls on **the team that iced the puck**, not on both benches. So if the visitors ice it, the home team changes normally and keeps last change; if the home team ices it, the home team is frozen and the visitors get the free matchup. **The IIHF Situation Handbook settles this directly rather than by inference**, at the situation it prints twice, as 81.6 and again as 82.1: after Team A ices the puck and coincidental fighting majors leave the strength unchanged, *"[t]he team that iced the puck (Team A) is only permitted to make a substitution for the penalized player. Team B may make a complete line change if they so choose"* — the same words in the 2025/26 v1.1 and 2026/27 handbooks. See [Special Teams](special_teams.md) for the same point from the special-teams side.
- **You choose the dot — under NHL and IIHF rules, so this one is yours in Britain.** Since 2019-20 the attacking team picks which of the two end-zone dots the draw happens at (Rule 81.2), and IIHF Rule 81.2 says the same in one line: *"Following an 'icing', the attacking team will have the choice of which end zone dot the 'face-off' will take place."* Pick your centre's strong side. **Under USA Hockey there is no choice** (Rule 624(a) sends it to a defending-zone spot, full stop).
- **Play fast.** The advantage is their fatigue, and it evaporates the moment the whistle-to-whistle stoppage lets them breathe. Win the draw and attack immediately.

**When you iced it:** you are tired, stuck, in your own end, and cannot rest. Get the puck deep or off the glass at the first opportunity; nothing clever. Remember that **the first faceoff violation after an icing does not eject the centre** (Rule 76.4) — you have one free warning. So does the other team, so do not count on their centre being tossed either. **In Britain this now reads exactly as written**: the IIHF 2026/27 book carries the same post-icing carve-out in the NHL's words, so the free violation is yours on this draw and rationed to it. Under the older 2025/26 edition you had it on every draw instead, because that book had no ejection to make an exception from. ⚠️ **Do not plan on that free violation in a North American game without checking first:** Hockey Canada gives it only in Junior divisions (Rule 6.5(a)), and **USA Hockey does not give it at all.** See "The icing exception" above.

### In the last minute

```facts
Rule: The five-second setup limit is not enforced in the final two minutes of regulation (NHL and IIHF Rule 76.3); USA Hockey 613(c) writes no such exception
Read: Expect more re-drops and more violations called
Key: A defensive-zone draw protecting a one-goal lead late is worth more than the previous fifty draws combined
Action: With a lead, accept the tie-up — a scramble that burns eight seconds beats a 50-50 clean-win attempt
Priority: Get the alignment right before you get the play right
```

- **The five-second setup limit is not enforced** in the final two minutes of regulation (Rule 76.3), so both centres will take their time and the linesperson will manage the alignment more carefully. Expect more re-drops and more violations called. **Check your book before you count on that time**: NHL and IIHF Rule 76.3 both suspend the limit in the last two minutes, and USA Hockey Rule 613(c) writes no such exception.
- **Everything is about the location.** A defensive-zone draw protecting a one-goal lead with 40 seconds left is worth more than the previous 50 draws combined, because there is no time to recover from losing it.
- **This is where you accept the tie-up.** With a lead, a scramble that burns eight seconds is a better outcome than a 50-50 attempt at a clean win.
- **Get the alignment right before you get the play right.** Late-game draws are lost to confusion more often than to the other centre.

### After a timeout

```facts
Read: Expect the opponent to have a plan too, and expect it to differ from their default
Rule: No time-out is granted following a faceoff violation (NHL and IIHF Rule 87.1)
Rule: No time-out for the defensive team following an icing, after their goalie stops a shot from beyond the centre red line, or after a defender accidentally dislodges the net (NHL Rule 87.1)
Rule: A net accidentally displaced by a defending skater or goalkeeper costs the line change as well as the time-out — the offending team may not substitute before the draw (NHL and IIHF Rule 63.8)
Rule: USA Hockey attaches no icing condition to its one 60-second time-out, but requires it be requested during the stoppage and before the line change procedure concludes (Rule 636(f))
Rule: IIHF Rule 87.1 carries both the faceoff-violation and the post-icing time-out restrictions; USA Hockey Rule 636(f) carries neither, gives sixty seconds, and closes the request window at the end of the line change procedure
```

A timeout buys both teams the same thing: a rest and a chance to draw up the alignment. So expect the opponent to have a plan too, and expect it to be different from their default.

Note the restrictions (Rule 87.1): **no timeout after a faceoff violation**, and **no timeout for the defensive team following an icing** or after their goalie causes a stoppage from a shot beyond the centre red line. So a team cannot ice the puck and then buy itself a rest — the two rules work together, which is exactly why icing is punished the way it is. **IIHF Rule 87.1 carries both restrictions in the same words**, so this is the position in British and European league hockey too. **USA Hockey Rule 636(f) carries neither**, and gives a sixty-second timeout rather than thirty — so under that book a team *can* ice the puck and then buy itself the rest. ⚠️ **It has to ask promptly, though.** Rule 636(f) requires that *"[t]ime-outs must be requested during a stoppage of play prior to the conclusion of the line change procedure"*, and the book's Glossary ends that procedure when the referee lowers their arm after the home team's five seconds. There is no icing condition on the USA Hockey time-out; there is a clock on it.

### With the goalie pulled

```facts
Rule: The goalkeeper may not participate in a faceoff (Rule 76.1)
Rule: A team that pulled its goalie may still substitute after an icing to put the goalie back in (NHL and IIHF Rule 81.4)
Rule: Besides putting a pulled goalie back, the NHL's exceptions to the post-icing no-change rule are an injured player and a penalty that changes on-ice strength (NHL Rule 81.4)
Rule: IIHF 81.4 adds a fourth, "a Player with a broken skate", which IIHF 82.1 does not repeat and no Situation names — treat it as one an official may allow, not one to count on: a change the referee refuses costs a warning your team may already have spent, then a bench minor (IIHF 81.4, 82.1, 82.2(V))
Position: The extra attacker goes to the net front or the high slot, with both defencemen at the points
Priority: The centre must win it back cleanly — there is no safe outlet and no recovery from a loss
Key: Defending against a pulled goalie, winning the draw effectively ends the game
```

- **The goalkeeper may not participate in a faceoff** (Rule 76.1) — a non-issue when they're on the bench, but worth knowing.
- **A team that pulled its goalie may still substitute after an icing** (Rule 81.4) — specifically to put the goalie back in. That is one of **three** exceptions to the no-change rule; the other two are replacing an injured player, and any penalty that changes the on-ice strength of either team.
- **⚠️ The IIHF book writes a fourth exception, and then contradicts itself about it — so do not plan a change around it.** Its Rule 81.4 also permits a substitution for *"a Player with a broken skate"*; its Rule 82.1 restates the same ban and lists only the NHL's three. **Both sentences read exactly that way in the 2025/26 and the 2026/27 editions alike, neither says which governs, and the sport has not published an answer.** The IIHF's own Situation Handbook cites the two rules **jointly** rather than as rivals — at Situations 81.6, 81.8 and 82.1, in both editions — which points away from a real contradiction, **but no situation in either Handbook mentions a broken skate at all**, and Situation 81.7 answers a lengthy-delay question by shortening the list further still, to the penalty case alone. So treat the broken skate as a change an official may allow rather than one to count on, and **do not send a player over the boards on it without the official's say-so**. **Nothing British amends this**: neither edition of the IHUK In-House Rules touches Rule 81 or 82 substitution, and where the 2026-27 edition mentions substitution at all it defers — *"Penalties, player eligibility, and substitutions continue per the Playing Rules."*
- **⚠️ And know what a refused change costs, because it is not a warning you can count on having.** Where an attempted change is not permitted, the Referee *“will send the Players who have attempted to change back to their Players' bench”* and *“then issue a warning to the offending team (through the Coach)”* — but **that warning is one per team per game, and it is not reserved for this**. IIHF Rule 82.2(V) warns that *“any subsequent violations during the rest of the game (including overtime), shall result in a bench minor penalty for ‘delaying the game’”* announced as a bench minor penalty for *“Delay of Game” – Improper Line Change*. So if your bench has already been warned for changing after the signal, for putting too many players out, or for any other personnel change, there is nothing left to spend and the next one is the bench minor. **Rule 82.2(VIII) does not save you late**: it disapplies only points (vi) and (vii) — the five-second line-up whistle and the slow-to-the-dot warning — in the last two minutes and in overtime, which is exactly when your goalie is out. And dawdling over it costs more again: **Rule 82.3** requires that *“Players must proceed directly to the location of the face-off to participate in the ensuing face-off”* and makes *“[a]ny attempts to delay the game by stalling or otherwise unnecessary actions by either team”* a bench minor — announced, in the NHL's printing of the same paragraph, as *“Delay of Game – Slow Proceeding to Face-off Location (or, Slow Proceeding to Players' Bench)”*, and with no warning first.
- **⚠️ Under the NHL, a visiting team may get no warning at all.** NHL Rule 82.3 opens *“Any attempt by the visiting team to make a change after the Referee's signal shall result in the assessment of a bench minor penalty for delay of game”* — a flat first-offence bench minor — where that same book's Rule 82.2(v) warns *“either team”* first. **The NHL book says both things and does not say which governs**, and the IIHF's 82.3 has a different first paragraph again, under which a failure or tardiness to comply by either team *“will result first in a warning from the Referee and then a bench minor penalty”*. So do not rely on the warning in a North American game either.
- **Six skaters changes the alignment entirely.** The extra attacker usually goes to the net front or the high slot, and both defencemen are at the points. The centre is under maximum pressure to win it back cleanly, because there is no safe outlet and no recovery from a loss.
- **Defending against a pulled goalie:** win the draw and the game is effectively over — you get an empty net at the other end. This is the single highest-leverage faceoff in hockey and it should get the most practice time of any of them, which in most organisations it does not.

## How to Practise Faceoffs

Here is the case, plainly. **A faceoff comes nearer than anything else you do dozens of times a game to a fixed starting position, a fixed cue, a fixed distance, and a binary outcome.** Everything else in open play — a shot, a pass, a battle, a gap — happens from a different position every time. A penalty shot has a fixed start too, but you may take a handful in a season; it is the *frequency* that makes the draw the one worth drilling. So the draw is hockey's nearest thing to a closed skill in an open-skill sport — nearest, not pure, because the drop is deliberately unpredictable and what you are training is a reaction from a fixed position. That is the combination that repays repetition.

The 22-point spread between the best and worst NHL centres cited at the top of this document is not a talent gradient. Faceoff percentage is one of the few statistics in hockey where the people at the top are, mostly, the people who worked at it.

### What actually to do

```facts
Action: Reps in pairs with a real drop — correct height, unpredictable timing, because you are training reaction
Action: End every practice with ten minutes of draws, both circles, both grips
Priority: Practise the technique you are worst at — your weak-side draws are where the points are
Action: Rehearse alignments with all five skaters: run the win, run the loss, run the tie-up
Action: Have every winger take draws in practice, because every winger will take one in a game
Key: Teach your wingers the encroachment rule — thirty seconds, and it stops a winger's early step costing your centre his place in the draw in all five books, the IIHF's since its 2026/27 edition
Rule: In Britain that lesson does not wait on the IIHF edition — the IHUK In-House Rules 2026-27 replace the centre for every violation bar one following an icing, so a winger's early step costs you the centre there too (Section 10, Rule 76)
```

- **Reps, in pairs, with a real drop.** Not a coach handing the puck over — someone dropping it from the correct height with unpredictable timing. Prediction is the enemy; you are training reaction.
- **End every practice with draws.** Ten minutes at the end of the session, both circles, both grips. This is the standard advice from coaching sources and it works because it is repeatable and nobody has to plan it.
- **Practise the technique you're worst at**, rather than the strong-side backhand pull you already trust. Your weak-side draws are where the points are.
- **Rehearse alignments, not just the draw.** Have all five skaters on the ice. Run the win, run the loss, run the tie-up. Most faceoff goals against come from four players not knowing what to do after the puck moves, not from the centre losing it.
- **Practise as the replacement.** Every winger should take draws in practice, because every winger will take one in a game after their centre is tossed — and some of you will be moved to centre permanently, which [Switching Positions](../positions/switching_positions.md) covers.
- **Off-ice: grip and hand speed.** Coaching sources recommend wrist and grip strengthening and hand-eye work (dribbling a golf ball with a stick is the classic). The premise those recommendations rest on is that a draw is decided in a fraction of a second and is a hand-speed event before it is a strength event — **coaching craft; there is no published measurement of faceoff reaction times, so do not quote a figure for it.** See [Conditioning and Recovery](../off-the-ice/conditioning_and_recovery.md).
- **Scout from the bench.** Watch the opposing centres take draws while you're resting. This is free, and almost nobody does it.
- **Teach your wingers the encroachment rule.** Thirty seconds, and it is what stops a winger's early step costing your centre his place in the circle — under NHL Rule 76.6, under IIHF Rule 76.6 as the 2026/27 edition now writes it, and in Britain under the IHUK In-House Rules 2026-27, which replace the centre for every violation bar one following an icing. **Only under the superseded IIHF 2025/26 edition did it cost the team its one free violation instead**, and that is the version a British coach may still be teaching.

See [Practice and Development](../off-the-ice/practice_and_development.md) for how to structure deliberate practice generally.

## Common Mistakes

- **Chasing your faceoff percentage instead of the important draws.** **Computed for this document** from the NHL's official team statistics across the eleven seasons from 2015-16 to 2025-26, a team's faceoff win percentage explains only about 6% of the variation in its **points percentage** — the share of available standings points it takes — and the relationship is unstable, having gone negative in 2016-17. That is a **team-level** figure, not a skater-level one. The defensive-zone draw on the penalty kill explains rather more.
- **Wingers creeping over the hash marks.** It ejects your **centre**, not you, and it happens on the draws where you can least afford it. **That now includes Britain**, and it is settled there by a British document rather than by an IIHF one: the IHUK In-House Rules 2026-27 have the linesperson replace the centre for every violation except on a draw following an icing. (The IIHF's own 2026/27 Rule 76.6 ejects the centre in the NHL's words too, where the 2025/26 edition spent the team's one warning instead.) Either way the team is left one mistake from a bench minor. Both skates outside the line — and *touching* the line is fine under the NHL and both IIHF editions, but not under Hockey Canada, whose glossary counts a skate *"on the face-off circle"* as encroachment. Your stick is a second question with a different answer in each pair of books: it may reach inside the circle and the hash marks under the NHL and the IIHF, may reach the circle but not the hash marks under Hockey Canada, and may reach neither under USA Hockey, whose Casebook answers *"No … This includes all sticks and all skates"* (Rule 613, Situation 8), nor under CARHA, whose Rule 57(a) puts the sticks on side with the players. **Keep skates and stick behind your own hash mark and you are legal in all five.**
- **Putting only the toe of your blade in the white — in Britain, under CARHA, and under USA Hockey at an end-zone draw.** The IIHF book asks for *"at least the toe of the blade of the stick"* (Rule 76.7(IV)); the **IHUK In-House Rules 2026-27** tell linespersons to require *"the whole of their stick blades (not toe or heel) in the white portion of the face-off circle"* (Section 10, Rule 76). **So the IIHF standard is not enough where those rules run** — and improper stick placement is a face-off violation, which under that same amendment has the linesperson replace your centre, with a second violation by your team on the same draw still a bench minor under IIHF 76.7. Whole blade, flat in the white, and leave it there until the puck lands. ⚠️ **CARHA is the other book that asks for more than the toe**, and it is easy to miss because the demand sits a paragraph away from the toe sentence: **Rule 57(a)** opens by having both players taking the draw stand *"with the full blade of their sticks flat on the ice"*, and only then, for the end-zone and neutral-zone spots, asks for *"the toe of the blade touching within the designated white area"*. The toe alone meets the second sentence and not the first, so put the whole blade down there too. ⚠️ **And USA Hockey is the third, at an end-zone draw, with a clause no other book writes:** **Rule 613(a)** requires that *"[t]he stick blades of both players shall be in contact with the nearest white area of the face-off spot **and clear of the center red area**."* A toe in the white with the rest of the blade lying across the red satisfies the four books that name the toe and fails that sentence. At the centre-ice and neutral-zone spots the same rule sets no blade standard at all — there it asks only that you *"squarely face their opponent's end of the ice and stand approximately one stick length apart."*
- **Getting low by pushing your head out over the dot.** Chest over the puck is the cue; head over the dot is not. The IIHF Situation Handbook addresses it twice — heads *"almost ear to ear"* at Situation 76.42, and *"helmet to helmet contact"* at 76.43 — and both moved with the 2026/27 edition: where the older handbook had the linesperson warn, the current one has them *"immediately eject the center that is responsible"*, and eject both where they cannot tell who caused it. Rule 76.7(III) names helmet-to-helmet contact as a violation in both editions and the 2026/27 book deleted the first warning the older one gave it, so **there is no free head bump any more**. 🇬🇧 **Nor is there one in Britain, where the older edition is the one the In-House Rules' Introduction names** — IHUK takes the IIHF book at *"Version 1.1, published July 2025"* and then amends Rule 76 so the linesperson replaces the centre for every violation bar a post-icing draw, and this is one. And the referee's minor for pre-drop contact sits on top, mandatory under USA Hockey 613(e), Hockey Canada 6.2(c) and CARHA 57(b).
- **Waiting to see what happens on a tie-up.** Most draws end in a scramble — a coaching estimate rather than a counted figure — and scrambles are won by whoever moves on the drop rather than after it.
- **Watching your opponent instead of the linesperson's hand.** If you're reacting to them, you're second.
- **Winning a defensive-zone draw into the middle of the ice.** A puck pulled to the slot in your own end is barely better than a loss.
- **Standing still after the draw.** The wingers' jobs — points on a loss, wall on a win — start on the drop, not after the puck is settled.
- **Using the same technique every time.** Three straight wins to the same defenceman guarantees the fourth gets jumped.
- **Trying something clever with the replacement taker.** Your centre is out and a second violation is a bench minor — a minor on the individual under USA Hockey Rule 613(d), which costs you the same skater, and a discretionary minor under Hockey Canada Rule 6.2(e) and CARHA Rule 57(d). Tie it up. **There is a replacement taker in Britain too**, under the IHUK In-House Rules 2026-27 and under the IIHF's own 2026/27 book, where the 2025/26 edition had none and left your own centre in the dot after a warning. The restraint is the same in both cases: take the re-drop straight. ⚠️ **And under Hockey Canada, check who is stepping in before you worry about what they do.** **Rule 6.2(d)** requires that *"[t]he player who encroached must be identified clearly as the player causing the violation and that player is not eligible to take the face-off"*, and adds that *"[n]o change of players will be permitted"* unless a penalty makes a team shorthanded — so the replacement comes from the four skaters already on the ice, minus the one the official has just named. If your pre-agreed backup is the player who caused it, you need a second name. ⚠️ **And under Hockey Canada, let the linesperson get clear first.** Rule 6.2(e) makes a second violation on the same draw a minor the referee *"may"* assess — except where the offending player then contacts the linesperson *"before the Linesperson has had reasonable time to vacate the face-off location"*, which *"will be assessed a Minor penalty"*. Running into the official is the one branch of that rule with no discretion in it.
- **Putting a hand or your body on the puck in the scrum.** ⚠️ **Catching a puck out of the air is not the offence** — NHL and IIHF Rule 67.2 permit that to anyone who *"immediately place[s] it or knock[s] it down to the ice"* — **but if you took the draw it is, until a third player has touched the puck**, because the note to that rule (printed again at Rule 76.5) bars the two takers from playing the puck with a hand at all until then. An open hand over the puck is a minor under NHL and IIHF Rule 67.2(ii) — but a **penalty shot** when the puck is inside your own crease, and an awarded goal if your net is empty (Rules 67.4, 67.5) — and falling on it or gathering it into your body is a minor under Rule 63.2(v) that escalates the same way, by where the puck is rather than where you are (Rule 63.6). **Under USA Hockey Rule 618(a) the closed hand outside the crease is a stoppage and a last-play faceoff rather than a penalty at all**, though inside the crease that book reaches the penalty shot too (618(a), 614(b)). Tie up the stick and win it with your stick instead — **not your skates**, which is legal in the NHL but a face-off violation under the IIHF book British hockey plays at every level (IIHF Rule 76.3(VI), in both the 2025/26 and 2026/27 editions) — it spent your team's one warning under the older edition and gets the centre replaced under the current one.
- **Not choosing your dot, where you have one.** Under NHL and IIHF rules you choose after an icing and at the start of a power play — take your centre's strong side, or the side your one-timer threat shoots from. **Under USA Hockey you get no choice at all** — Rules 624(a) and 612(b) fix the spot. ⚠️ **And under Hockey Canada the choice exists but is rationed twice**: Rule 6.4(b) gives it to the **attacking** team — the side that did not ice the puck or take the penalty — *"[i]n Junior and Senior divisions"* only, and even there the team loses it unless it *"indicate[s] their desired face-off location to the Referee prior to the start of the line change procedure."* So check your book, and under that book your division, before you build a routine on it — and if the choice is yours there, ask for it early rather than at the dot.
- **Joining a post-whistle scrum in the offensive zone.** If your point men come inside the circle's outer edge, the draw is moved to the neutral zone and you've thrown away the zone. **The rule is wider than the point men in every book**: NHL and IIHF Rule 76.2 catch *"any of the non-offending players"* in a second sentence, and USA Hockey 612(d) and Hockey Canada 6.3(d)(vi) catch **any** attacking player. Whoever you are, stay outside the circle.
- **Going through the other centre instead of round them on a "lose it forward".** You pushed the puck past them, so they are now standing between you and it — and the instruction that is legal everywhere is to skate *around* them. In **women's hockey** it is a written rule: IIHF Rule 101.1 says a stationary player *"is entitled to that area of the ice"* and that *"the opponent is obliged to skate around the stationary Player"*, with *"[a]ny move by a Player to step or glide into an opposing Player"* at least a minor and up to a major plus an automatic game misconduct — which in Britain the IHUK In-House Rules 2026-27 turn into an ejection from the game. **USA Hockey prohibits body checking outright** in 12 & under, *"all Girls'/Women's age classifications and all non-check Adult classifications"* (Rule 604(a)). Chase the puck, not the ice they are standing on.
- **Sending your skate at the man instead of the puck.** The kicking permission every rulebook gives you is a permission to kick the *puck*. Kicking or attempting to kick an **opponent** is a **match penalty** under NHL Rule 49.3, USA Hockey Rule 627(b), Hockey Canada Rule 7.1(c)(iii) and CARHA Rule 48(c) (*"A player who attempts to kick or deliberately kicks an opposing player shall be assessed a Match penalty"*); under **IIHF Rule 49.3** — Britain's book at every level — the referee *"may assess a major penalty and an automatic game misconduct penalty"*, so the major is at the referee's discretion and the game misconduct follows it automatically. ⚠️ **And USA Hockey needs no kicking motion at all** — its Rule 627(a) assesses a major plus a game misconduct on a player who *"uses their skate to 'push off' an opponent"*, which Note 2 to that rule defines as skate contact with an opponent *"in a non-kicking motion"*. A faceoff is where two players' skates are closest together all game, so if you use your feet at the dot, use them on the puck.
- **Practising only your strong side.** That's the one you're already good at.

## Key Takeaways

1. **A teammate stepping into the faceoff circle before the drop gets your CENTRE thrown out of the draw, not them** — NHL Rule 76.6, USA Hockey Rule 613(d), Hockey Canada Rule 6.2(a) and CARHA Rule 57(a) all eject the centre — Hockey Canada's is the plainest and CARHA copies it almost word for word: *"the offending team's player taking the face-off will be ejected from the face-off"*, where CARHA writes *"shall be ejected"*. **All five books now do**, the IIHF having joined them in its 2026/27 edition. ⚠️ **Hockey Canada then adds a sentence none of the other four has, and it decides who may replace him:** Rule 6.2(d) requires that *"[t]he player who encroached must be identified clearly as the player causing the violation and that player is not eligible to take the face-off"*, and permits *"[n]o change of players"* unless a penalty makes a team shorthanded. **So under that book the winger who caused it is named by the official and barred from the dot, and the replacement has to come from the three other skaters on the ice** — which means a line needs two designated names, not one. (All five books bar substitution until the draw is done — the NHL and both IIHF editions at Rule 76.8, USA Hockey at 204(a), CARHA at 57(c) — but only Hockey Canada names an ineligible player; the other four offer the draw to *"any teammate then on the ice."*) ⚠️ **Three of the five ration you a free first violation on the draw after an icing, and they do it on different terms** — NHL Rule 76.4 and IIHF 2026/27 Rule 76.4 warn the centre instead of removing him, once per team, and **Hockey Canada Rule 6.5(a) does the same in Junior divisions only**, where it forgives any violation rather than only a late arrival. **Neither USA Hockey nor CARHA writes such a carve-out.** Everywhere else — and in every USA Hockey and CARHA game — the centre is the one who comes off. ⚠️ **The positional standard quoted here is the NHL's and the IIHF's** (Rule 76.7 in both, and unchanged across both IIHF editions): both skates outside the circle and outside the hash marks; contact with the line is permitted, crossing it is not; you may reach with your stick, but you may not step. **The other three books do not hand you the reaching stick.** USA Hockey's Casebook answers it *"No … Both players must be completely behind their respective hash marks. This includes all sticks and all skates"* (Rule 613, Situation 8), Hockey Canada's glossary allows a stick *"inside the face-off circle"* but not one *"in the area between the hash marks"* — and counts a skate *"on the face-off circle"* as encroachment, where the NHL and the IIHF permit line contact — and CARHA Rule 57(a) requires every other player to be on side *"and their sticks"* with them. **The instruction that is legal in all five books is the short one: keep your skates and your stick behind your own hash mark.** Telling your linemates where they may stand is the cheapest improvement available to any team.
2. **⚠️ The IIHF changed this rule, and if you play in Britain the answer you may have been taught is the old one.** Under the **IIHF Official Rule Book 2025/26**, nobody was ejected from a draw: a violation was a warning to the team, the puck was dropped again with the same centre, and you had one free violation per team on every draw rather than only after an icing. **The IIHF Official Rule Book 2026/27, published in June 2026, adopted the NHL's ejection** — its Rule 76.6 has the offending team's draw-taker *"ejected from the face-off circle"* — and adopted the NHL's post-icing exemption alongside it, so the free first violation is now rationed to draws following an icing. **⚠️ And in Britain you no longer have to work out which edition your league is administering, because IHUK wrote the answer into its own rules.** The **IHUK In-House Rules 2026-27**, published 25 July 2026, add a Rule 76 amendment the previous season's edition did not contain: *"With the exception of face-offs following icings, the linesperson shall immediately replace the centre for all violations."* Those rules govern the game *"in England, Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland"* and are the *"overriding interpretation"* of the IIHF book domestically; **they do not mention the Elite League, so they do not answer for it.** Unchanged across both editions: winning the draw with your skates is itself a violation under the IIHF (Rule 76.3(VI)), so "kick it back" is not a technique you own, and two violations by your team on one draw is a bench minor. **The 2026/27 book has been read only in part — for the face-off rules, and for the other rules this document quotes as reading the same in both editions** — the rest of this document's IIHF layer describes 2025/26 and has not been rechecked, and the In-House Rules settle faceoff violations only, not anything else in this document.
3. **Two violations by the same team on the same draw is a bench minor under both the NHL and the IIHF books; USA Hockey Rule 613(d) makes it a minor on the individual instead, and Hockey Canada Rule 6.2(e) and CARHA Rule 57(d) make it discretionary rather than automatic.** ⚠️ **Hockey Canada's discretion has an exception, and it is the one a replacement taker is most likely to trip over.** Rule 6.2(e) has a second paragraph: where the same second violation is followed by the offending player contacting the linesperson *"before the Linesperson has had reasonable time to vacate the face-off location"*, the team *"will be assessed a Minor penalty by the Referee"* — mandatory, under Rule 10.1(a) rather than 10.1(viii). **After a first violation, let the official clear the dot before you commit.** ⚠️ **CARHA's second-violation minor is limb-scoped as well as discretionary** — it reaches only a second violation *"of any of the provisions of Section (a)"*. ⚠️ **That limb is wider than positioning, and the sentence to check is Section (a)'s own:** it names *"a player, other than the player taking the face-off"* who *"moves offside, **makes physical contact with an opponent**, or encroaches on the face-off circle"*, **so a winger's pre-drop contact is inside it and does count toward 57(d)**. What is outside is the **taker's** contact, because **Rule 57(b)** — scoped to *"no player facing-off"* — already makes that a mandatory minor on its own, with no second violation needed. **USA Hockey limb-scopes its own too**, to *"any of the provisions of this subsection"* — 613(d)'s — so the scoping is not a CARHA peculiarity, though the two limbs do not hold the same things: 613(d) reaches a non-taker's contact only through *"fails to maintain their proper position"*. **So do not carry "a second violation is discretionary" into a CARHA game as though it covered leaning on the other centre: that one is two minutes the first time.** **And pre-drop contact with the other centre is never a free violation:** the NHL and the IIHF let the referee impose a minor for it at their discretion on top of the violation (Rule 76.7), and USA Hockey Rule 613(e), Hockey Canada Rule 6.2(c) and CARHA Rule 57(b) make that minor mandatory. ⚠️ **Under the IIHF 2026/27 book it also costs you the centre** — 76.7 has the linesperson *"order the center of the offending team replaced"* where the 2025/26 book warned him. **Head contact is named separately and lost its free warning:** 76.7(III) makes helmet-to-helmet contact before the drop a violation in both editions, the 2025/26 book gave it a *"first 'face-off' violation warning"* and the 2026/27 book deleted that, and the 2026/27 Situation Handbook tells the linesperson to *"immediately eject the center that is responsible"* — or both, where they cannot tell (Situation 76.43). 🇬🇧 **Do not read the older edition's warning as the British answer, even though the older edition is the one the In-House Rules' Introduction names:** the IHUK In-House Rules 2026-27 amend Rule 76 so the linesperson replaces the centre for every violation bar a post-icing draw, and a helmet-to-helmet is a violation under 76.7(III) in both editions. **Get low from the knees and hips, not by pushing your head over the dot.** And a hand or a body on the puck in the scrum is a minor penalty rather than a faceoff violation **under the NHL and IIHF books** — a **penalty shot** if the puck is inside your own crease (Rules 67.4, 63.6) — while **USA Hockey Rule 618(a) makes the closed hand outside the crease a stoppage and a last-play faceoff rather than a penalty at all**, and a penalty shot or optional minor inside it. Win the draw with your stick. ⚠️ **And if you use your skate at the dot, use it on the puck:** kicking or attempting to kick an opponent is a **match penalty** under NHL Rule 49.3, USA Hockey Rule 627(b) and Hockey Canada Rule 7.1(c)(iii); under IIHF Rule 49.3 the referee *"may assess a major penalty and an automatic game misconduct penalty"*, so the major is at their discretion and the game misconduct follows it — and USA Hockey Rule 627(a) reaches a skate that *"push[es] off"* an opponent with no kicking motion at all, at a major plus a game misconduct. **Catching a puck out of the air and knocking it straight down is legal for everyone else on the ice, but not for the two takers until a third player has touched it** (the note to NHL and IIHF Rule 67.2, printed again at Rule 76.5). ⚠️ **And if you push the puck past the other centre, go round them rather than through them.** IIHF Rule 101.1, in the women's section of both editions, obliges a player to *"skate around the stationary Player"* who stands between them and the puck, and makes *"[a]ny move by a Player to step or glide into an opposing Player"* at least a minor and up to a major plus an automatic game misconduct — which the IHUK In-House Rules 2026-27 turn into an ejection from the game. **USA Hockey Rule 604(a) prohibits body checking outright** in the 12 & under classifications, *"all Girls'/Women's age classifications and all non-check Adult classifications"*.
4. **Watch the linesperson's hand, not your opponent.** You are training a reaction, not fighting a duel.
5. **Faceoff percentage matters far less than people think.** Computed for this document from the NHL's official team statistics across the eleven seasons from 2015-16 to 2025-26 (344 team-seasons), it explains only about 6% of the variation in team points percentage — the share of available standings points a team takes — and the relationship is unstable, having gone negative in 2016-17 and to about zero in 2025-26. So stop chasing the percentage and spend your attention on the draws where the outcome changes something.
6. **Faceoffs are unusually learnable, because a draw is hockey's nearest thing to a closed skill: same position, same cue, every time.** Computed for this document from the NHL's official skater statistics, the 109 centres among the 120 skaters who took at least 500 draws in 2025-26 ran from 62.1% down to 39.7% — a 22-point spread. That is a single season and a skater-level figure, not comparable with team faceoff percentages, and nobody has computed the same spread for any other skill, so it says the draw is worth working at rather than that it beats everything else.
7. **Know what sends the next draw where, and be there first.** Under NHL and IIHF rules the attacking team chooses which dot after an icing (Rule 81.2) and the team awarded a power play chooses where it starts (Rule 76.2) — take your centre's strong side. **USA Hockey gives no choice in either case** (Rules 624(a) and 612(b)). **Hockey Canada gives one and rations it twice**: Rule 6.4(b) hands it to the attacking team *"[i]n Junior and Senior divisions"* only, and that team forfeits it unless it names the dot to the referee *"prior to the start of the line change procedure."*
8. **At eight of the nine spots the defending player puts their stick down first under NHL Rule 76.4 — and IIHF Rule 76.4 says it in the same words, in both its 2025/26 and 2026/27 editions.** Only at centre ice, in both of those books, does the visitor go first. **USA Hockey Rule 613(a) reverses it** — the attacking player first everywhere but centre ice — and **Hockey Canada Rule 6.2(a) does something different again**: the visiting player first at every spot, attacking or defending. So seeing the other blade before you show yours favours the attacking team under the NHL and IIHF, the defending team under USA Hockey, and neither in particular under Hockey Canada. **CARHA makes silence a fourth answer**: its Rule 57 was read in full and writes no order at all, so there is nothing to wait for — **though the same rule asks for more of the blade than the IIHF book does, opening with *"the full blade of their sticks flat on the ice"*.** Four answers in five books — check yours before you build a habit on it. **⚠️ And in Britain there is no order to check: the IHUK In-House Rules 2026-27 provide that *"Either centre can now put their stick down first for a face-off"*, overriding the IIHF sentence.** The same amendment tells linespersons to require *"the whole of their stick blades (not toe or heel) in the white portion of the face-off circle"*, which is more of the blade than IIHF Rule 76.7(IV)'s *"at least the toe"* — **so a British centre who does exactly what the IIHF book says is in violation.** That is a penalty rule, not a preference: improper stick placement is a face-off violation under IIHF 76.7(IV), the same In-House amendment has the linesperson replace your centre for it, and a second violation by your team on that draw is a bench minor under IIHF 76.7, which IHUK does not amend. **Free choice about *when*; put the whole blade down.**
9. **Your strong circle is the one where your natural backward pull sends the puck toward the boards.** Coaching sources map that to a right shot on the right side, without stating the reference frame, so test it rather than trusting it. On your weak side, learn the forehand pull, flip your hands, or put a centre who shoots the other way on that dot. Never solve it by pulling to your backhand in your own end — that sends the puck to the middle, the worst place it can go.
10. **Every alignment needs a plan for the win, the loss and the tie-up — and the tie-up is the most likely of the three, on coaching estimate rather than a counted figure.** All of it is a coaching choice rather than a rule: the common defaults assume a low zone collapse, and under man-to-man each winger follows his own man instead. Find out what your team runs before your first shift.

*Verification note — the IIHF and UK layer, 29 July 2026. ⚠️ **This note describes the IIHF Official Rule Book 2025/26; the edition note that follows it describes what the 2026/27 edition changed, and the two must be read together.** **Every IIHF claim in this document was read in the IIHF Official Rule Book 2025/26 itself**, not taken at second hand. Against that book all of them hold, including the two most load-bearing: that a face-off violation is a warning to the team rather than an ejection (IIHF Rules 76.4, 76.6, 76.7) and that winning the draw with the skates is itself a violation (IIHF Rule 76.3(VI)). Three further IIHF/NHL divergences came out of the same reading and are stated where they belong: the IIHF book has **no post-icing face-off-violation carve-out** of the kind NHL Rule 76.4 provides, because its first violation is always a warning; IIHF Rule 81.4 lists a **fourth** exception to the post-icing substitution ban (a player with a broken skate) that the NHL's list does not have — apart from that clause its 81.4 is the NHL's 81.4 word for word — **and the IIHF's own Rule 82.1 restates the same ban with only the three**, a disagreement inside one book that neither edition resolves. Searched for a resolution and none found: both IIHF editions were read at 81.4, 82.1 and 63.8 and searched for a precedence provision ("takes precedence", "shall prevail", "in case of any contradictions", "conflict between", and the front matter entire), and both editions of the Situation Handbook return **no occurrence of "broken skate" at all**. ⚠️ **That negative was re-run flattened — hyphen joins removed and whitespace collapsed — across both editions and the superseded Version 1.0, and it holds; but note what a phrase search of that kind cannot see.** Situation 81.7 contradicts the exception **without naming it**, answering a glass-breakage question with *"[o]nly time a line change is permitted is when a penalty is assessed which affects the on-ice strength of either team"*. It was found by reading the Rule 81 situations in sequence, not by any search term used for this note — and every negative recorded here is a phrase search, blind the same way. Situation 81.7 is quoted in the body, where the reading is set out, because it is adverse to it. The Handbook's only ranking sentence is between documents rather than between rules: *"in case of any contradictions, the valid IIHF Rulebook is always the basis."* What the Handbook does show is that the IIHF reads the two rules **together**: it cites them jointly at Situations 81.6, 81.8 and 82.1, in both editions, and neither rule writes its list as closed. The document's reading — that the two are cumulative and the broken skate stands — is set out where it is used, with the grounds for it, and is labelled there as a reading rather than a ruling. **And nothing British amends it, as a checked negative rather than an assumption**: both editions of the IHUK In-House Rules were searched for Rule 81 and Rule 82 substitution material and contain none, the 2026-27 edition's only substitution sentence deferring to the Playing Rules. And IIHF Rule 81.6 grants the short-handed icing exemption **without** the NHL's major-penalty carve-out. **The IHUK In-House Rules 2025-26 Rev 1 were searched in full and amend nothing about face-offs** — the only occurrence of the phrase in that document is the team-sheet deadline ("no later than 20 minutes before the scheduled face-off"), which [UK and England Rules](../foundation/uk_rules.md) covers. Silence in the In-House Rules means the IIHF rule stands unamended, so everything above marked "IIHF" is the operative rule wherever those In-House Rules run. ⚠️ **That scope has since widened, and this note was written before it did.** The 2025-26 edition the reading was done against adopted the IIHF book *"as the foundation for the rules of the game to be applied in England and Scotland"*; the 2026-27 edition applies it *"in England, Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland (the Home Countries)"*. **So a Welsh or Northern Irish reader is inside it now and was not named then.** University hockey is inside both, BUIHA being a party to each. Nothing here is attributed to the EIHL Casebook, which has not been obtained. ⚠️ **That silence has since ended — see the In-House note below.***

*⚠️ In-House note — the IHUK In-House Rules 2026-27, 29 August 2026. **The 2025-26 In-House Rules amend nothing about face-offs. The 2026-27 edition does.** IHUK published **In-House Rules 2026-27, Revision 1, on 25 July 2026**, and that document adds, under *Section 10 – Game Flow*, a Rule 76 section its predecessor did not contain. It is two paragraphs, quoted here in full: *"With the exception of face-offs following icings, the linesperson shall immediately replace the centre for all violations."* and *"Linespersons must ensure both centres are squared up correctly behind their marks with the whole of their stick blades (not toe or heel) in the white portion of the face-off circle. Either centre can now put their stick down first for a face-off."* **Both were read in the document itself, and the 2025-26 edition was re-searched for a Rule 76 section and has none** — its only occurrence of "face-off" remains the team-sheet deadline. **That settles two things for a British reader.** First, a British reader's centre is removed for a face-off violation, and the conclusion no longer depends on which IIHF edition the league runs, because IHUK wrote the removal into its own text. Second, the stick-placement order in IIHF Rule 76.4 does **not** apply in Britain, which is a British departure rather than an IIHF change — that sentence of 76.4 is word-for-word identical in the 2025/26 and 2026/27 editions, and both were checked. ⚠️ **Two things it does not settle.** **Scope:** the In-House Rules apply *"in England, Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland (the Home Countries)"*; the Rule 76 amendment carries no league restriction, unlike the Section 6 fighting provision headed *"Applicable Leagues: NIHL (all leagues), SNL, and BUIHA Varsity"*; and **the Elite League appears nowhere in the document**, so nothing here speaks to an EIHL game. **Which IIHF edition sits underneath:** the 2026-27 cover page reads *"in conjunction with the **Current** IIHF Rule Book"* where the 2025-26 cover named *"the 2025–26 IIHF Rule Book"*, but the 2026-27 **Introduction** still adopts *"the latest IIHF Official Rule Book (Version 1.1, published July 2025)"* — and Version 1.1 of July 2025 is the **2025/26** book, the 2026/27 one giving its own version as 1.0, June 2026. **The document contradicts itself on that point**, so it is not evidence that British hockey has moved to the 2026/27 IIHF book generally, and nothing in this document treats it as such. Five other In-House provisions were compared between the two editions and carry forward unchanged: Rules 37 and 38 — video review *"is not available in EIH or SIHA competition"* and *"a Coach's Challenge is not permitted in EIH or SIHA competition"*, **both scoped to those two competitions in both editions** rather than to the Home Countries generally, Rule 69.1, Rule 84 (overtime), the Section 6 fighting provision, and the **Section 11 women's** bullet turning any Major Penalty into a Game Misconduct and an ejection, which is word for word the same in both.*

*⚠️ Edition note — the IIHF 2026/27 Rule Book, 29 August 2026. **The verification note above remains accurate about the book it names, and that book is no longer the current one.** The IIHF published its **Official Rule Book 2026/27, Version 1.0, June 2026**, after that reading was done. **These were read in the 2026/27 edition for this document and compared line by line with the 2025/26 text: Rules 38.2, 38.9, 49.3, 63.2(III), 67.2, 76.2, 76.3, 76.4, 76.5, 76.6, 76.7, 79.2, 81.4, 82.1, 82.2, 82.3, 87.1 and 101.1, plus its Appendix VII pilot rule.** ⚠️ **38.9, 49.3, 67.2 and 76.2 were read in both editions on 11 September 2026 and are word-for-word identical**, page furniture aside; this enumeration and the one in the Sources entry above are the same list, save for Rule 81.6, which that entry carries and this one does not, for the reason given below. **Six** things changed, and all six move the IIHF toward the NHL: 76.6 now ejects the offending team's draw-taker for a teammate's encroachment where the older edition warned the team; 76.6 has the linesperson order the centre replaced on the establish-position-first violation where the older edition assessed a warning; 76.7 deletes the warning sentence for premature movement and an unfair drop, leaving only the re-drop; 76.7 also replaces the warning for pre-drop physical contact — where the 2025/26 book had the centre *"warned by the Linesperson that the team has committed a 'face-off' violation"*, the 2026/27 book has the *"Linesperson shall order the center of the offending team replaced"*; 76.7(III) drops the first warning the older edition gave for helmet-to-helmet contact, so that a player who makes it *"has committed a 'face-off' violation"* where he *"must receive a first 'face-off' violation warning"* before, and both players *"have committed"* one where the linesperson cannot tell who initiated it (*"they has"* is the IIHF's own slip and is quoted as printed); and 76.3 and 76.4 replace the offending centre where the older edition warned him. **76.3 is worth reading closely, because its trailing clause governs all six of its limbs** — including (IV), *"Any Player makes physical contact with an opponent"* — and in the 2026/27 edition it reads *"the Linesperson shall have the offending center(s) replaced immediately prior to dropping the puck"*, where the 2025/26 edition carried a half-edited sentence the newer book resolved: *"the Linesperson shall have the offending center(s) may assess a warning for a 'face-off' violation prior to dropping the puck"*, quoted as printed. So pre-drop contact loses its warning in 76.3 and in 76.7 alike, which is further reason to read the change as deliberate. ⚠️ **The two 76.7 items were missed by an earlier reading of this document that declared the rule compared, and are recorded here because a gap inside a rule stated as fully read is worse than an unread rule.** The 2026/27 edition also **adds** the post-icing carve-out the older one lacked, in the NHL's own words, together with a narrower companion sentence covering a centre who times his arrival at the dot after an icing. **Several are word-for-word what they were**: 81.4 (the post-icing substitution ban, including the fourth exception for a player with a broken skate — and Rule 82.1's differing three-exception restatement of the same ban is word-for-word what it was too, so the disagreement between them is as stable as the rules are), 87.1 (the time-out bans, including the one following a face-off violation) 82.2(VII) (the once-a-game lateness warning), and 82.3 in all three of its paragraphs — the last-change warning-then-bench-minor, the bench minor for stalling on the way to the dot, and the bench minor for a player who retires from the ice through any exit other than the players' bench. ⚠️ **Note one consequence of that last one**: 82.2(VII) already used ejection wording — *"the offending team's center is not ejected from the 'face-off'"* — in the 2025/26 book, where 76.7 stated the same carve-out in warning wording. The two now agree because 76.7 was rewritten to match, not because 82.2 moved. **One thing outside the rules proper was read and is cited**: Appendix VII, *Developmental rules for piloting*, whose three-on-three overtime “No Return” concept writes its post-violation substitution ban with 81.4's four-item list, broken skate included. That is evidence of which list the IIHF reaches for when it drafts the ban fresh, and nothing more — the appendix holds *“concepts for potential future rule changes that the IIHF deems relevant for piloting and evaluation”* and is not in force. ⚠️ **One rule has been added to that comparison since it was made, and separately: Rule 81.6, the short-handed icing exemption, was read in both editions on 14 September 2026 and is word-for-word identical** — the grant, the *"at the instant the penalty expires"* timing test, the release-before-expiry relief and the *"remaining in the penalty box will not alter the ruling"* sentence, all four present in each, and the NHL's third paragraph withdrawing the exemption on a major with an empty penalty bench absent from both.
