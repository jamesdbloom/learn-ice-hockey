## Key focus

**Watch the linesperson's hand, not the other centre, and own three or four draws.** Go on the drop, not on his move, or you are always second. Get low from the knees and hips, chest over the puck, bottom hand low and back foot loaded. Mix the backhand pull, the forehand pull, the tie-up and the lift: win three straight to the same defenceman and the fourth gets jumped.

**Against a centre who beats you clean, tie him up and let your wingers win it.** Lift or press his stick with yours, then play the puck. Stick on stick only — never a hand on his stick, never your stick on his hands. In your own end and on the kill, a tie-up counts as a win. The alternative is a different taker who shoots the other way; ask your bench which your team tries first.

**The four skaters not in the dot decide most draws, so they move on the drop.** Most draws end in a scramble (a coaching estimate, not a count). The common own-end default: wingers to the points on a loss and to the wall between the hash marks and the goal line on a win, the slot defenceman holding the net front, the two nearest bodies attacking a tie-up. **That is a coaching choice, not a rule** — under man-to-man each winger follows his man — so ask your bench what your team runs.

**Win a defensive-zone draw to the boards, never into the middle.** A puck pulled into your own slot is barely better than a loss, and on your weak side your backhand pull sends it there — so learn the forehand pull, flip your hands, or use a centre who shoots the other way. Then rim it hard enough to reach your winger, or tight enough for your goalie to legally stop it behind the net — never in between.

**Prepare the draws that change something, not your faceoff percentage.** A team's faceoff win rate explains only a small share of its results. The draws that matter are the own-end draw, the shorthanded draw, the draw protecting a late lead and the draw after an icing. A clean loss in your own end lets a set opponent run a prepared play while your five stand still, so prepare those above all.

**Tell your linemates where to stand: skates and sticks behind your own hash mark, skates off the circle line.** That is legal in every book here. A teammate who creeps into the circle early gets your **centre** thrown out of the draw, not himself, and a second violation on the same draw can cost a penalty.

**Scout the centre opposite from the bench.** Most centres have one favourite draw and one strong side, and two shifts usually show them. A rolled-over bottom hand means a backhand pull; hands high on the shaft mean a sweep or a tie-up; the loaded foot shows which way he will turn.

**Run two or three set plays per zone, each with a failure branch.** The centre calls it in one word and everyone acknowledges it. Agree "if we lose it, X does Y" before you line up. With no call, or a replacement in the dot, keep it simple: tie up in your own end, point shot in theirs.

**When they ice the puck, win the draw and attack at once.** Under the NHL, IIHF and PWHL books they can neither change their line nor take a time-out, but that edge fades once the stoppage lets them breathe. Where your book lets you pick the dot, ask early and take your centre's strong side or your one-timer's side — not every book gives you the choice.

## Overview

A **faceoff**, or **draw**, starts and restarts play: an official drops the puck between the sticks of two opponents. There are about **56 in an average NHL game** — 56.6 per game in 2024-25 and 56.0 in 2025-26, computed from the NHL's official statistics. It is the one moment you know where the puck will be and when, so it is the one moment you can plan for completely.

**Most draws matter less than people think, and a few matter a great deal.** Across eleven NHL seasons, a team's faceoff win percentage explains only around 6% of the variation in its share of standings points — the figure people mean when they say faceoffs are overrated. So prepare the ten or fifteen draws a game that change something (a coaching estimate), above all in your own end, where a clean loss lets a set opponent run a prepared play while your five face the wrong way.

**Winning the draw is a reaction, not a duel.** Watch the official's hand, go on the drop from a low, loaded stance, and keep three or four techniques so the other centre cannot pre-load against one. It repays practice: among NHL centres with at least 500 draws in 2025-26, win rates ran from 62.1% down to 39.7%. That is a skater-level figure, not comparable with the team-level 6%. Against a centre you cannot beat, tie him up or change the taker — your bench decides which. And scout centres from the bench: most have one favourite draw and one strong side.

**Then the four players not in the dot decide what the draw was worth.** Most draws end in a scramble (a coaching estimate), settled by whoever moves first, so their jobs start on the drop — most faceoff goals against come from those four not knowing what to do. **Their jobs are a coaching choice, not a rule:** the defaults here assume a low zone collapse, and under man-to-man each winger follows his man, so ask your bench. Either way, send a win to the boards in your own end, back to a set shooter in theirs, and never into your own slot.

**The cheapest improvement is telling your linemates where to stand: skates and stick behind your own hash mark, skates off the circle line — legal in all five books.** If anyone but the two takers enters the circle before the drop, your **centre** is thrown out, not the player who moved, and your team is one more mistake from two minutes. ⚠️ **Do not carry the looser NHL version into a North American rec or youth game** — a stick reaching into the circle and a skate on the line are NHL and IIHF permissions, not every book's. "The rule everybody gets wrong" and "The second violation" set out all five books.

**The rest of this document** covers where the next draw will be, how to stand, grip and read the other centre, what the other four do at every dot, the set plays worth running, special-teams and late-game draws — and, as background, the rules that decide who stays in the dot.

## Why Faceoffs Matter — Honestly

There is a bad version of this argument and a good one. The bad version says faceoffs win games. They do not, and pretending otherwise makes everything else you say less credible.

### What the evidence actually shows

**Team faceoff percentage barely predicts winning.** Computed from the NHL's official team statistics: across the eleven completed seasons from 2015-16 to 2025-26 (344 team-seasons), the correlation between a team's faceoff win percentage and its **points percentage** (the share of available standings points a team actually took — points earned divided by two per game played) is **r = +0.25**, meaning faceoff percentage accounts for **about 6% of the variation** in how many points teams take. It is positive — but it is weak, and it is unstable. In 2016-17 the relationship was actually *negative* (r = −0.24), and in 2025-26 it was essentially zero (r = +0.03).

**And at team level there is barely any spread to predict with.** Computed from the NHL's public team-summary data: across the **32 NHL teams** in the **2025-26 regular season, all situations**, team faceoff win percentage ran from about **45.9% to 54.5%**, with the league mean sitting at **50.0% by definition** — every draw is a win for one team and a loss for the other, so the teams must average out to even. That is a total range of under nine percentage points between the best and worst faceoff teams in the league — far narrower than people assume when they quote a team's faceoff rank.

**Do not confuse that with the spread between individual centres, which is much wider.** The team figure blends every skater a club sends to the dot — including the wingers who take emergency draws (eleven of the 120 skaters with 500+ faceoffs in 2025-26 were wingers) — so it pulls hard toward 50%. The skater-level range described in the next section — **62.1% down to 39.7%** across the 109 centres with 500 or more draws in 2025-26 — is a different unit of analysis and roughly two and a half times as wide. Team faceoff percentage tells you about a roster; skater faceoff percentage tells you about a player.

**Team faceoff percentage barely predicts possession either.** **Garret Hohl**, writing at Hockey Graphs, found an R² of about 0.20 between team faceoff percentage and Corsi percentage (shot-attempt share, the standard possession proxy), across every 82-game NHL team-season from 2007-08 on — a real relationship, but one that leaves four fifths of the variation unexplained.

**The best faceoff man in the league is worth a handful of goals.** Here the model and the man who applied it are two different people, and it is worth keeping them straight. The per-zone even-strength goal values are **Gabriel Desjardins'**; **Garret Hohl** applied them at Hockey Graphs to the faceoff differentials of the NHL's 100 most frequent draw-takers, in his own words *"using data originally brought up by Gabriel Desjardins."* That exercise put the best of them (Boyd Gordon) at **+4.34 goals** and the worst (Ryan Nugent-Hopkins) at **−2.73** — a spread of **7.07 goals** from the best draw-taker in the league to the worst. For comparison, the same piece set that against the gap between top-decile and bottom-decile possession players, which its chart puts at roughly **10–15 goals per 82 games** — *read off the figure, not stated as a number in the article text, so treat it as approximate.* (*The 82-game basis is stated for that comparison chart only; Hohl's player table gives no time base, so read +4.34 and −2.73 as season-scale rather than exactly per-82.*) Hohl's own conclusion is worth quoting: faceoffs "matter, but they do not matter on average much more than any other of the many puck battles that occur throughout the game."

**The advantage decays in seconds.** The finding is **Craig Tabita's**, published at hockeyprospectus.com: shot volumes after a draw even out to roughly the same level about ten seconds later, regardless of where the draw was. It reaches this document at two removes — Hockey Graphs' women's-hockey piece quotes **Alison Lukan's** 2017 summary of Tabita in *The Athletic*, which is where the sentence *"after 10 seconds of play, regardless of where a face-off occurs … shot volume evens out to be more or less the same"* comes from. The figure here rests on Lukan's summary rather than on Tabita's original. A faceoff win buys you about one possession's worth of head start, not a scoring chance.

### So why bother?

```facts
Priority: Prepare the situational draws, not the league-average one
Risk: A defensive-zone draw lost cleanly puts a set opponent into the most dangerous ice while you are stationary
Action: Fix the free failure modes — a winger creeping over a line is an information problem, not a skill one
Key: A 22-point spread between the best and worst NHL centres in 2025-26 — one season — and coaches treat the draw as learnable
Key: The spread between NHL centres is computed here from NHL official statistics — the 109 who took at least 500 draws in 2025-26, running from 62.1% down to 39.7%, median 51.8%
Never: Chase your faceoff percentage. The draws worth preparing are the ten or fifteen a game that change something; that count is a coaching estimate, not a measurement
```

Because *average* is the wrong frame. The honest case for faceoffs is not "win more draws, win more games." It is four narrower claims, and all four survive the evidence.

- **Situational draws are not average draws.** A defensive-zone draw while shorthanded, with your best penalty killers already tired, is not the same event as a neutral-zone draw at 3-0 in the first period. The league-average draw is worth almost nothing. The specific ones described later in this document are worth a great deal, and they are a small enough share of the total that a team can prepare for all of them.
- **A lost defensive-zone draw is a shot against, quickly.** The reason coaches obsess over these is not the possession — it is that losing one cleanly puts a set opponent with a set play into the highest-danger area of the ice while your five skaters are stationary and facing the wrong way. That is the one faceoff outcome that is genuinely, immediately dangerous.
- **The failure modes are free to fix.** Getting your centre ejected because a winger crept over a line is not a skill problem. It is an information problem, and it costs nothing to solve. See the violations section.
- **It is learnable, and the gap between good and bad is enormous.** Computed from the NHL's official skater statistics: 120 skaters took at least 500 draws in 2025-26, and 109 of them are listed as centres (the other eleven are wingers). Among those 109 centres the median win rate was **51.8%**, the tenth percentile 45.4%, the ninetieth 57.7%, and the range ran from **62.1% down to 39.7%** — a **22-point spread** on a single repeatable, isolated action, in a single season. That is a wide gap between centres, and coaches read it as a skill that rewards work. **What it does not tell you is that the draw beats everything else you could work at** — nobody has computed that spread for any other hockey skill, so treat "the most improvable skill in hockey" as a coaching claim and this number as the thing that is actually measured. (This is the figure [Switching Positions](../positions/switching_positions.md) leans on when it tells a winger moved to centre that the draw is worth working at.)

**The practical version: don't chase your faceoff percentage. Chase the ten or fifteen draws a game where the outcome actually changes something, and stop giving away the free ones.** *("Ten or fifteen" is a coaching estimate of how many draws carry real consequence, not a counted figure — nothing here counts them.)*

## The Nine Faceoff Locations

Every faceoff in the game happens at one of **nine spots** (NHL Rule 76.2). There is no such thing as a draw somewhere else — Rule 76.2 explicitly forbids one within fifteen feet of the goal or the side boards, or anywhere that isn't a marked spot.

### Where they are, and what's painted on the ice

```facts
Rule: Every faceoff happens at one of nine marked spots, and nowhere else — NHL and IIHF Rule 76.2, USA Hockey Rule 612(b)
Position: Wingers stand at the hash marks, and those marks are a hard boundary
Rule: The centre's restraining lines define where your feet may go (Rule 1.9)
Rule: The four neutral-zone dots have no circles and no hash marks — all players other than the centres stand "uniformly back" (NHL and IIHF Rule 76.7)
Key: Encroachment — a player other than the two centres moving in before the drop — is called loosely in the neutral zone and tightly in the end zones
```

**The NHL, the IIHF and USA Hockey all say the same thing about the count, in three sets of words.** NHL Rule 76.2 opens *"All face-offs must be conducted on one of the nine"* face-off spots *"located on the rink."* IIHF Rule 76.2 makes the identical provision with its own punctuation and its own singular — *"All 'face-off' must be conducted on one of the nine"* face-off spots *"located on the rink"* — in the 2025/26 and 2026/27 editions alike. USA Hockey Rule 612(b) puts it differently again: *"All face-offs will be conducted at one of the nine face-off spots."* What is painted around those spots is Rule 1.9, and the table below is the NHL's version of it.

| Spot | Count | Markings (NHL Rule 1.9) |
|---|---|---|
| **Centre ice** | 1 | A 12-inch blue spot, with a circle of 15-foot radius |
| **Neutral-zone dots** | 4 | 2-foot red spots, 5 feet outside each blue line, 44 feet apart. **No circles** |
| **End-zone dots** | 4 | 2-foot red spots with 15-foot-radius circles, 20 feet out from the goal line and 22 feet either side of the middle |

*(The dimensions in that table are the rink's, and [Rink Map](../foundation/rink_map.md) covers them — it carries the same figures alongside the rest of the ice, with the IIHF metric equivalents.)*

Two markings inside the end-zone circles matter enormously and almost nobody can name them:

- **Hash marks** — red lines two inches wide and two feet long, running parallel to the goal line. Rule 1.9 puts two of them "at the outer edge of **both sides** of each face-off circle", set **5 feet 7 inches apart** — so **four per circle**, a pair on the boards side and a pair on the middle-of-the-ice side. These are where the wingers stand, and they are a hard boundary (see violations).
- **The centre's restraining lines** — the little painted "L" shapes a foot outside the spot: two lines four feet long and eighteen inches apart running parallel to the side boards, plus a line 2 feet 10 inches long parallel to the end boards. These define where the centre's feet may go.

**The four neutral-zone dots have no circles and no hash marks at all.** Rule 76.7 acknowledges this directly and tells linespersons to use judgement, requiring only that everyone stand "uniformly back" as if a circle were there. Practically: encroachment — anyone but the two centres moving in before the drop — is called loosely in the neutral zone and tightly in the end zones.

### How often each zone comes up

Computed from the NHL's public team-faceoff data for the **2025-26 regular season** (**team-level, all situations**), a team's draws split roughly:

| Zone | Share of a team's draws | Draws per game |
|---|---|---|
| **Offensive zone** | **34.6%** | ~19–20 |
| **Defensive zone** | **34.6%** | ~19–20 |
| **Neutral zone** (including centre ice) | **30.8%** | ~17 |

Those come out of the roughly **56 draws in an average NHL game** (56.0 per game in 2025-26; see the Overview). The percentages are the computed figures; the per-game counts in the right-hand column are simply those percentages applied to 56 draws, rounded.

Three things follow from that table, and they are worth holding onto:

- **The offensive and defensive shares are identical, and they have to be.** Every offensive-zone draw for you is a defensive-zone draw for the other team, so once you average across the league the two numbers are the same number seen from opposite ends. If someone quotes you a team with more offensive- than defensive-zone draws, that is a real fact about *that* team's play; league-wide it cannot happen.
- **End-zone draws are about 69% of all draws** — so most faceoffs *are* in an end zone, and the defensive-zone draws described later in this document are not a rare event. You take about twenty of them a game.
- **Offensive-zone draws are only about a third of the total**, which matters when you read "sheltered usage" or zone-start percentage in a statistics table. A coach loading a favoured player with offensive-zone starts is redistributing within that third, spread across a whole bench — which is one reason the measured effect on any single player's season numbers is modest. See [How to Watch Hockey](../off-the-ice/how_to_watch_hockey.md) for how zone starts are used and misused as a context statistic.

### What sends the draw to which spot

![](diagram:faceoff-where-the-draw-goes)

```facts
Action: Work out where the next draw will be and start skating there before the whistle finishes
Rule: Centre ice takes period starts, draws after a goal, and an erroneous icing call (Rules 76.2, 81.2)
Rule: After an icing or a penalty the draw is in the offending team's end (NHL and IIHF Rules 81.2, 76.2); the other team's free choice of dot is NHL, IIHF and PWHL only, at PWHL 83.2 — USA Hockey 612(b) and 624(a) give none, CARHA none, Hockey Canada 6.4(b) a rationed one
Rule: Hockey Canada 6.4(b) gives the choice of end-zone dot to the attacking (non-offending) team, in Junior and Senior divisions only and only after an icing or a time penalty, and that team loses it unless it tells the referee its dot "prior to the start of the line change procedure"
Rule: A stoppage between the end-zone dots and the end boards draws at the spot on the side it happened, "unless otherwise expressly provided by these rules" (NHL and IIHF Rule 76.2) — USA Hockey 612(b) takes the nearest spot, but only where it calls for a last play face-off
Rule: A stoppage the attacking team caused in its attacking zone is a different draw under USA Hockey — 612(c) sends it to the nearest neutral-zone spot, and 612(g) moves any draw back toward the goal of the team that caused the stoppage
Rule: Offside on a carry-in draws at the neutral-zone dot outside that blue line (Rule 83.6)
Rule: Offside on a pass or shot sends the draw back to the zone the puck came from (Rule 83.6)
Rule: A post-whistle scrum that pulls players inside the circle's outer edge moves the draw to the neutral zone — the attacking team's point men and bench, and any non-offending player, under NHL and IIHF 76.2; any attacking player under USA Hockey 612(d), Hockey Canada 6.3(d)(vi)
Rule: A puck out of play draws at the nearest spot in the zone it was shot from (NHL 85.1); a puck shot into the players' bench draws no penalty, but the IIHF Handbook exempts only one shot straight in, not one clearing the glass into it (63.2(iii) NOTE; Situation 63.24)
Never: Go offside by firing the puck over the blue line — carrying it over and being whistled costs you far less
```

This is the part worth actually learning, because knowing where the next draw will be lets you start skating there before the whistle finishes.

**To centre ice:**
- The start of every period, and after every goal.
- A penalty assessed *after* a goal, or at the end or start of a period (Rule 76.2).
- A linesperson's **erroneous** icing call (Rule 81.2) — the one time a mistake gets fully neutralised.
- A premature-substitution violation when play was stopped beyond the centre red line (Rule 76.2).

**To an end-zone dot in the offending team's own end:**
- **Icing.** And here is the detail almost nobody knows: since 2019-20, **the attacking team chooses which of the two dots** (Rule 81.2). They will pick the one their centre is strong on. See "Situational Faceoffs".
- **A penalty.** One of the two dots in the penalised team's end — and again, **the team awarded the power play chooses which dot** (Rule 76.2). Three exceptions send it to centre ice or the neutral zone instead.
- **Intentional offside** — deliberately going offside to force a whistle (Rule 83.7).
- **A goalie freeze, or any stoppage low in the zone.** Rule 76.2: when the stoppage happens between the end-zone dots and the end of the rink, the draw is at the end-zone dot **on the side where the stoppage occurred**. So which side a goalie freezes the puck on determines which circle you play. Goalies know this. Note the rule's own escape clause, because it is what all the exceptions above and below hang off: the side-of-the-stoppage answer holds *"unless otherwise expressly provided by these rules"* — the IIHF prints the same qualification at its own 76.2 — so a stoppage that another rule has already spoken for goes where that rule sends it.
- **A defender or goalkeeper accidentally knocking their own net off.** **Rule 63.8** sends that draw *"to one of the end zone face-off spots in the defending zone"* — the offending team's own end — and **the attacking team chooses which of the two dots**, as it does after an icing. The same rule bars that team from making any substitution before the draw. **IIHF Rule 63.8 is the same rule under the same number**, writing *"a defending Player"* where the NHL writes *"a defending skater or goalkeeper"*, and it reads the same in the 2025/26 and 2026/27 editions.
- **The goalie freezing the puck on the back of the net with stick or glove, or a defender shielding an attacker from playing it off the back of the net** — Rule 85.2 sends these to "one of the face-off spots in the defending zone." ⚠️ **That draw-only answer is the NHL's, the IIHF's and the PWHL's (87.2) — USA Hockey and Hockey Canada both add a minor penalty on top of it, and CARHA 58(b) does whenever the goalie's body is entirely outside the crease**, so do not read "just a faceoff" as the price in every book — nor in those three for a goalie freezing it deliberately outside the crease, a minor under NHL 63.2(i), IIHF 63.2(I) and PWHL 65.2(i); [Goaltender](../positions/goaltender.md) has the full comparison. Note that a puck **merely lodged in the outside netting** is *not* automatically a defensive-zone draw: that is Rule 85.5, which puts it at "either of the adjacent face-off spots or at the nearest face-off spot in the zone from which the puck was shot, unless otherwise covered in these rules."

**To a neutral-zone dot:**
- **Offside where the puck was carried over the line** — the draw is at the neutral-zone dot outside that blue line (Rule 83.6).
- **A stoppage in the neutral zone not attributable to either team** — the nearest dot outside the blue line; if it's genuinely unclear which is nearest, the tie goes to the spot giving the **home** team the greatest territorial advantage (Rule 76.2).
- **A goal illegally scored by hand, kick or high stick** (Rule 76.2).
- **A scrum after the whistle that draws the point men in.** Rule 76.2 does this in two sentences. The first catches the **attacking** team's point men and anyone coming off its bench; the second catches *"any of the non-offending players"* — any of them, not just the defencemen. Either way, coming inside the outer edge of the end-zone circle during an altercation moves the draw out to the neutral zone. This is a real and frequently-conceded cost: your team just turned a won defensive-zone draw into a neutral-zone one, or lost an offensive-zone draw entirely, by joining a shoving match.

**Puck out of play:** the nearest spot in the zone the puck was shot or deflected out from; from the neutral or defending zone, the spot giving the **offending team the least territorial advantage** (Rules 85.1 and 85.5). ⚠️ **A puck lodged in the netting is not automatically a draw back in the shooting team's end** — Rule 85.5 allows *"either of the adjacent face-off spots or at the nearest face-off spot in the zone from which the puck was shot"*. If the puck leaves the rink **directly off a faceoff**, the NHL and the IIHF re-drop at the same spot and penalise nobody (Rule 85.1, and the note to Rule 63.2(iii)) — but neither says whether that covers a centre who does it on purpose, and Rule 63.2(ii) makes a deliberate shot out a minor *"from anywhere on the ice surface"*, so never aim one out off the draw. A puck **shot into the players' bench** is not a delay-of-game penalty (the NOTE to NHL Rule 63.2(iii); IIHF 63.2(III) the same, in both editions). ⚠️ **That is no licence to aim at the bench, and under the IIHF it is narrower than it sounds:** only a puck shot **directly** into a bench with no glass escapes the minor, so a clearance that goes over the glass and drops into the bench is still two minutes. And if the puck enters the **opposing** bench *"through an open bench door"*, the draw is in the **neutral zone adjacent to that bench** (Rule 85.1). [Risk Management](../hockey-iq/risk_management.md) carries the delay-of-game rule and the situations that decide it.

**Offside where the puck was shot or passed in** (rather than carried): the draw goes back to the nearest spot in the zone the pass or shot came from, again picking the option giving the offending team the least territorial advantage (Rule 83.6). So a sloppy stretch pass from your own end that goes offside sends the draw all the way back into your own zone. Carrying it over the line and getting whistled costs you far less than firing it over. [Zone Entries](zone_entries.md) draws the tactical conclusion from that asymmetry.

**USA Hockey and Hockey Canada send some of these draws somewhere else, and one of them makes you ask for what you want.** **⚠️ USA Hockey gives no choice of dot at all.** Rule 612(b) sends a penalty draw to *"a defending zone face-off spot of the offending team"*, and Rule 624(a) sends an icing draw to *"an end zone face-off spot in the defending zone of the offending team"* — a spot, not a pick. That book also decides the stoppage low in the zone differently: where the NHL and the IIHF put the draw on the side it happened, USA Hockey Rule 612(b) sends a stoppage *"between the end zone face-off spots and the nearest end boards"* calling for a last play face-off to *"the nearest end zone face-off spot"*.

**That is narrower than it sounds, and the narrow version is the one that decides where you stand.** A **last play face-off** is USA Hockey's own term for a stoppage that is nobody's fault in particular, or both teams' — not for every stoppage low in the zone. **A stoppage the *attacking* team caused in the attacking zone is a different draw entirely**: Rule 612(c) sends that one to the nearest neutral-zone spot. And Rule 612(g) overrides on top of all of it — *"[a] team causing a stoppage of play shall not gain a territorial advantage with the ensuing face-off"*, which puts the draw at the nearest spot **closer to their own goal**. So under that book, the side that caused the whistle skates the wrong way for it.

**Hockey Canada does give a choice of dot — but not to you, if you were the one who caused the stoppage — and then rations it twice over.** Rule 6.4(b) grants it *"[i]n Junior and Senior divisions"* only, after an icing or a time penalty — and it is not automatic: *"[t]he attacking team must indicate their desired face-off location to the Referee prior to the start of the line change procedure. If they fail to do so, then the face-off will take place at the location prescribed by Rule 6.3 – Face-off Location."* **So under that book the choice belongs to the *attacking* team — the side that did not ice the puck or take the penalty — and even they lose it unless they name the dot early.**

**And the post-whistle scrum catches more attacking players in both of those books than in the NHL's — though the NHL's rule is wider than it is usually quoted.** Rule 76.2's second sentence, which most summaries drop, moves the draw to the neutral zone if *"any of the non-offending players"* go beyond the outer edge of the end-zone circle during a scrum, and the IIHF's 76.2 says the same. **USA Hockey Rule 612(d) and Hockey Canada Rule 6.3(d)(vi) catch *any* attacking player** who goes beyond the top of the circles after the whistle, not just the point men and the bench. So joining the shoving match as an attacking forward costs you the draw under those two books where it might not under the NHL's or the IIHF's.

Use whichever form suits you; they are the same rules.*

## Faceoff Technique

A draw is hockey's nearest thing to a closed skill: same start position, same cue, same distance, every single time. That is exactly the kind of thing humans get good at through reps.

### The stance

```facts
Technique: Get low — deep knee bend, chest over the puck, weight on the balls of your feet
Goal: React faster from a loaded position, and be impossible to push off the dot
Position: Feet either side of the restraining lines, staggered rather than square, back foot loaded
Position: Square to the opponent's end, hips already cocked toward where you intend to send the puck
Key: Eyes on the linesperson's hand, not the opponent — watching the opponent means you are always second
Never: Get low by pushing your head out over the dot — depth comes from knees and hips, and the IIHF handbook has linespersons break up centres leaning in "almost ear to ear" (Situation 76.42)
```

- **Get low.** This is the single most repeated instruction from coaching sources, and for two reasons: you react faster from a loaded position, and you cannot be pushed off the dot. Deep knee bend, chest over the puck, weight on the balls of your feet. ⚠️ **Get the depth from your knees and hips, not by pushing your head out over the dot.** Chest over the puck is the cue; head over the dot is the position the IIHF Situation Handbook tells linespersons to break up, and under the 2026/27 handbook a centre who will not move back is removed from the draw (Situations 76.42 and 76.43).
- **Feet either side of the restraining lines**, staggered rather than square, with your back foot loaded — that's the foot you'll drive off.
- **Square to the opponent's end**, as the rule requires, but with your hips already cocked toward where you intend to send the puck.
- **Eyes on the linesperson's hand, not the opponent.** This is the highest-leverage habit in the whole skill. You are reacting to the puck's release, not to your opponent's movement. Watching the opponent means you are always second.

### The grip

```facts
Technique: Bottom hand right down near the blade — that is where the leverage, speed and power come from
Options: Top hand halfway down the shaft or up on top of it; the coaching sources disagree, so try both
Technique: For the reverse grip, roll your bottom hand over so your knuckles face down
Read: Take the reverse grip when you have committed to a backhand pull and want raw speed and strength
Risk: The reverse grip telegraphs, and gives you almost nothing on the forehand
Options: Some players roll both hands into a "kayak paddle" grip for maximum backhand snap
```

- **Standard draw grip:** the two How To Hockey pieces cited here disagree on the top hand. Their faceoff-tips article describes "their top hand about halfway down the stick, and their bottom hand right near the blade"; their pro-tips article quotes former NHL player Jeff Serowik as "one hand on top of shaft and the other hand really low on shaft for leverage." **What both agree on is the part that matters: the bottom hand comes right down near the blade.** That is where the leverage, speed and power come from. Try both top-hand positions — neither is your normal skating grip and neither should feel like it.
- **The reverse grip (backhand grip):** roll your bottom hand over so your knuckles face down — the coaching cue from How To Hockey is to "hold the stick as if you were going to cross-check someone in the teeth." This gives you far more pulling power and quickness on the backhand, and it's what centres use when they intend to yank the puck backward hard rather than sweep it.
- **When to use the reverse grip:** when you have committed to a backhand pull and want raw speed and strength — typically defensive-zone draws you must not lose, and offensive-zone draws set up for a **one-timer** (a shot taken straight off a pass, without stopping the puck first). Its cost is that it telegraphs, and it gives you almost nothing on the forehand. A centre who lines up in a reverse grip every time is a centre who can be read.
- Some players use a two-handed **"kayak paddle"** grip — both hands rolled, stick held like a paddle — for maximum backhand snap.

### Faceoff technique: the techniques

```facts
Key: There is no single correct draw — the good centres own three or four
Technique: Pull the puck back on the backhand, usually with the reverse grip — pulling is a stronger and faster motion than pushing
Convention: How often each technique wins is not something this document can tell you — the NHL faceoff data behind it records zone, strength and winner, not technique
Options: Lift or press their stick with yours, then play the puck — a 1-on-1 becomes a scramble for your wingers. Never a hand on their stick or your stick on their hands; never hook it or hold it down
Risk: The stick lift is the higher-reward version and it fails badly if you miss
```

There is no single correct draw. There are seven or eight, and the good centres own three or four.

- **The clean backward win (the draw proper).** Sweep or yank the puck behind you to a designated teammate. This is the one that shows up on highlight reels and it is genuinely the highest-value outcome — but it is also the lowest-percentage, because it requires beating the other centre outright.
- **The backhand pull.** Rotate and pull the puck back on the backhand side, usually with the reverse grip — How To Hockey's cue for that grip is that it *"helps you improve your quickness and power"*. Pulling is a stronger and faster motion than pushing, and that is the whole case for building a draw around it. Whether it wins more often than the other techniques is not something this document can tell you: the NHL faceoff data behind it records which zone a draw was in, which strength situation, and who won it — nothing at all about how.
- **The forehand pull.** The same idea on the forehand. Weaker and slower than the backhand equivalent for most players, which is precisely why your handedness matters (below).
- **Tying up the stick.** Instead of trying to win the puck outright, lift their stick or press it for an instant with your own — over or under the blade — then play the puck yourself. You are not winning the draw; you are ensuring **nobody** wins it, and converting a 1-on-1 into a 4-on-4 scramble that your wingers are already leaning into. Against a superior draw-taker this is the common answer, not a surrender — the alternative is to change the taker, and your bench decides which. **Do it stick on stick and nothing else: never a hand on their stick, never your stick on their hands, and never hook it or hold it down.** A hand on an opponent's stick is a minor for holding the stick under NHL and IIHF Rule 54.2, PWHL 55.2, USA Hockey 622, Hockey Canada 8.1(a) and CARHA 63(a), though 63(a) may also allow a major (its text is unclear). A hold that injures is a mandatory major under Hockey Canada 8.1(b) and CARHA 63(b), and either one puts you out of the game — 8.1(b) adds a game misconduct, and under CARHA 30(a) any major ejects. A stick on their hands is hooking — IIHF Rule 55.1 names a stick *"against the opponent's hands / or near the opponent's hands"* outright, and Hockey Canada's Interpretation 1 to Rule 8.2(a) allows the lift only *"for the purpose of making a play on the puck"*, making it hooking once it contacts the hands and impedes their ability to play the puck. Under USA Hockey and Hockey Canada a blade that hooks their stick is hooking too: USA Hockey 623 counts a pulling or tugging blade on *"any part of an opponent's body or stick"*, and Hockey Canada's Rule 8.2 says the hooking action *"may apply to any part of an opponent's body or stick"*. That is why you play the puck straight after the lift.
- **The stick lift.** A more aggressive version: lift their blade off the ice entirely, then take the puck. Higher reward, and it fails badly if you miss.

### The techniques that use your body, your feet or your hands

```facts
Rule: Contact before the drop is a violation under NHL and IIHF 76.7 and can draw a minor on the taker at the referee's discretion as well, where USA Hockey 613(e), Hockey Canada 6.2(c) and CARHA 57(b) make that minor mandatory; afterwards they permit contact only "in the course of playing the puck"
Rule: Only Hockey Canada defines when a face-off is complete — Rule 6.2, the puck "contacts the ice surface before contacting the stick or any part of the body of the player taking the face-off", so there contact timed to the drop is contact before completion
Never: Lean on the man before the puck lands; no book in this document makes that free
Rule: Where body checking is banned you cannot go through the man — USA Hockey 604(a) at 12 & under, in all girls'/women's and non-check adult classifications; Hockey Canada 7.3 at U13 and below, female and Member-approved divisions; CARHA 49(a) in every game
Read: Try the "lose it forward" play at the neutral-zone dots, where there is space behind the other centre — go round the other centre, not through them
Rule: ⚠️ **Never skate through the other centre to reach a puck pushed past them** — in women's hockey IIHF 101.1 and PWHL 52.1 oblige you to skate around a stationary player — at least a minor, a major brings a game misconduct, and a PWHL match is possible
Never: Use "lose it forward" in your own end
Rule: Batting the drop is legal with the stick; a taker who tries to win the draw by batting it with the hand takes a minor (NHL and IIHF Rule 67.2)
```

The remaining four put your body, your feet or your hands into the draw, and each of them has a rule attached. Three are here; the fourth — using your feet — has a section of its own after them, because what the six books say about a skate at the dot runs a long way past the draw.

- **Going through the man.** Lean into your opponent once the puck is on the ice, taking the ice and the puck together — except in a CARHA game, where Rule 49(a) penalises body contact a player *"does not avert"*, so there you play the puck and avoid the man. **Both halves are the rule, not a coaching preference.** *Lean, not drive*: the only contact the books permit is *"in the course of playing the puck after the face-off has been completed"* (Rule 76.7). *Once it is down, not as it falls*: contact **before** the drop is a face-off violation under NHL and IIHF 76.7, with a discretionary minor, and the NHL and the IIHF 2026/27 edition replace the offending centre for it. Under **USA Hockey 613(e), Hockey Canada 6.2(c) and CARHA 57(b) the minor is mandatory.** No book makes leaning on the man before the puck lands free. ⚠️ **Hockey Canada alone says when a face-off is complete, and it draws the line at the ice:** Rule 6.2 — complete *"when the puck has been legally and fairly dropped, meaning the puck contacts the ice surface before contacting the stick or any part of the body of the player taking the face-off."* The NHL and the IIHF say only that it *"ends when the puck has been legally dropped"*; CARHA and USA Hockey define only when it starts. **So under Hockey Canada a body timed to the drop is a body before completion. Wait for the puck to land.**
- **The "lose it forward" play.** Deliberately push the puck *forward* past your opponent and go after it. Used most often at the neutral-zone dots, where there is space behind the other centre and no immediate danger if it fails. In your own end it is a bad idea; in the offensive zone it can beat a centre who is committed to pulling backward. ⚠️ **Go round the other centre, not through them, and in women's hockey that is a rule rather than a preference.** You will hear this play described as "skate through him onto it"; do not. IIHF Rule 101.1, in Section 11 — Women's Ice Hockey, and word for word in the 2025/26 and 2026/27 editions alike, provides that *"[a] Player, who is stationary, is entitled to that area of the ice. It is up to the opponent to avoid body contact with such a Player. If that Player is stationed between the opponent and the puck, the opponent is obliged to skate around the stationary Player"*, and that *"[a]ny move by a Player to step or glide into an opposing Player will be assessed at least a minor penalty (2') for an 'illegal hit'."* That is exactly this play's fact pattern: the puck is past the other centre and the other centre is between you and it. The scale runs to *"[a] major penalty (5') and automatic game misconduct penalty"*, and in Britain the **IHUK In-House Rules 2026-27**, Section 11, add that in women's hockey *"[a]ny player who commits a foul that is assessed a Major Penalty will, in all cases, also receive an additional Game Misconduct Penalty and be ejected from the game."* **PWHL Rule 52.1 says the same, and a major there ejects you too (Rule 52.5).** **In the men's game it is the same instruction under a different rule.** With the puck pushed past him, the other centre does not have it, and NHL Rule 56.1 gives a player *"the ice he is standing on"* and says he *"is not required to move in order to let an opponent proceed"*; IIHF Rule 56.1 makes it interference to obstruct an opponent *"without possession of the puck"*. Where body contact is allowed at all, leaning on each other while you both play the loose puck is legal — the IIHF Glossary allows skaters near the puck to *"push and lean into each other provided that possession of the puck remains the sole object of the contact"* — but driving through him to reach it is not. And body checking is prohibited outright in **USA Hockey**'s 12 & under classifications, *"all Girls'/Women's age classifications and all non-check Adult classifications"* (Rule 604(a)), and that book defines an illegal check as intentional contact *"using overt hip, shoulder, forearm or torso action"*, including *"physically forcing the opponent off the puck and with no effort to legally play the puck"* (Rule 604 Note 1). **Hockey Canada bars it across a different list**, its Rule 7.3 stating that the body-checking rule *"applies only in divisions of U13 and below, in female hockey, and any other divisions approved by a Member of Hockey Canada"* — so in those, deliberate use of the body to gain an advantage is a penalty and incidental contact in pursuit of the puck is not. **CARHA goes furthest, in every game it governs**: Rule 49(a) penalises intentional body contact and a player who *"does not avert body contact with an opponent"*, with accidental contact spared (49(c)) unless it reaches the head, face or neck, which is still a minor (50(a)), or puts a player from behind into the boards or net, which is a match penalty (53(b)). **Chase the puck, not the ice the other centre is standing on.**
- **Batting it out of the air.** Advanced, and legal with the **stick** — several coaching sources describe attacking the puck mid-drop. With the **hand** it is a minor for delay of game — face-off violation, assessed to a taker who *"attempts to win the face-off by batting the puck with their hand"* (NHL and IIHF Rule 67.2) — and the note to that rule keeps your hand off the puck altogether until a third player has touched it. Timing this requires watching the linesperson's release, not the ice.

#### Using your feet — the puck, the man, and the goal

```facts
Rule: Kicking the puck back is legal in the NHL and PWHL and a face-off violation under IIHF 76.3(VI), so it is not available to you in Britain at any level (IIHF Rule 76.3(VI), both editions)
Rule: USA Hockey, Hockey Canada and CARHA write no face-off bar on kicking the puck, so the technique rests on a general kicking rule rather than a face-off one — USA Hockey 627(c), Hockey Canada 6.10(a), CARHA 69
Rule: A kick-back that gets tipped in is no goal under USA Hockey 627(c) or Hockey Canada 6.10(b), which disallow a goal off a deflected kick, or under CARHA Rule 60(c) — no goal where "after being kicked, the puck deflects off any player or stick into the net"
Rule: Hockey Canada scopes its own no-goal rule twice — 6.10's preamble exempts a puck that "deflects off a player's skate, without a deliberate kicking action", and 6.10(b) excepts "a defending player kick[ing] the puck into their own goal", which at your own end zone draw is your net
Rule: Kicking or attempting to kick an opponent is a match penalty under NHL and PWHL 49.3, USA Hockey 627(b), Hockey Canada 7.1(c)(iii) and CARHA 48(c); under IIHF 49.3 the referee may assess a major, and the game misconduct is automatic once they do
Rule: Under USA Hockey a skate does not need a kicking motion to be an offence — 627(a) is a major plus a game misconduct for using the skate to "push off" an opponent, which its Note 2 defines as skate contact with an opponent "in a non-kicking motion"
Never: Send the skate at the man. Any kicking permission at the dot is for the puck only, and under IIHF 76.3(VI) a centre may not win the draw with a skate at all
Action: Before you build an offensive-zone draw around the kick-back, find out which book your league runs — the tip behind it is a goal in only three of the six
```

**Using your feet.** Kick the puck back to a teammate, or open your skate as a backstop so a tied-up puck squirts the right way. Legal in the NHL, and in the PWHL, whose Rule 78 writes no skate bar at the dot. ⚠️ **But every one of the six books permits kicking the *puck* and penalises kicking the *man* at the top of its scale — and the dot is where two opponents put their skates inches apart.** Kicking or attempting to kick an opponent is a **match penalty** under NHL Rule 49.3, USA Hockey 627(b), Hockey Canada 7.1(c)(iii), CARHA 48(c) and PWHL 49.3, the PWHL adding a five-minute time penalty *"[w]hether or not an injury occurs"*; under IIHF Rule 49.3, in both editions, the referee *"may assess a major penalty and an automatic game misconduct penalty"*. ⚠️ **USA Hockey does not even need a kicking motion, which makes the opened skate the risky half there:** Rule 627(a) gives a major plus a game misconduct to a player who *"uses their skate to 'push off' an opponent"*, and its Note 2 defines that as contact *"in a non-kicking motion"*. **Send the skate at the puck, never at the man.**

**Under IIHF rules it is a violation, and therefore not a technique available in British hockey at any level** — IIHF Rule 76.3(VI) catches a centre who *"wins the face-off by using the skates"*. It is *winning* with the skates that is caught, so an opened backstop is a finer line than a kick-back, but neither is worth learning under this book; "When your centre gets tossed", below, sets out the cost. **⚠️ USA Hockey, Hockey Canada and CARHA write no face-off bar** — Rules 613, 6.2 and 57 were each read in full and say nothing about skates at the dot — so there the technique rests **by inference** on each book's general kicking permission: USA Hockey 627(c), Hockey Canada 6.10(a) (*"Kicking the puck will be permitted in all zones."*) and CARHA Rule 69. ⚠️ **Hockey Canada's preamble to 6.10 adds that a puck deflecting off a skate *"without a deliberate kicking action on the part of that player"* is not a kick** — so an opened backstop is not a kick there, though a deliberate kick-back still is.

**And the goal-scoring half of those kicking rules splits between the books,** ⚠️ **which decides whether the kick-back is worth anything at an offensive-zone draw.** **Under the NHL, the IIHF and the PWHL a kicked puck that deflects in off a stick — yours or a teammate's — is a good goal** (Rule 49.2(ii) and (iii) in all three books). **All three North American amateur books disallow it:** Hockey Canada at Rule 6.10(b); USA Hockey at Rule 627(c), whose Casebook Situations 4 and 5 disallow a kick deflected in off the kicker's own stick or a teammate's; and CARHA — ⚠️ **not in Rule 69, which permits the kick, but at Rule 60(c)** under *Goals and Assists*, where no goal is allowed if *"after being kicked, the puck deflects off any player or stick into the net."* **So under those books the kick has to end in a teammate's possession and a clean shot — the tip play is off the menu.** ⚠️ **Hockey Canada narrows its no-goal rule twice at a draw:** a skate deflection without a deliberate kick is not a kick at all, and 6.10(b) excepts the case where *"a defending player kicks the puck into their own goal"* — so at a defensive-zone draw, a puck you kick into your own net counts. This document has no count of how much rec and youth hockey runs on each book, so find out yours before you plan a tip off a draw. [Passing and Receiving](../technique/passing_and_receiving.md) carries the same split for the loose puck in your feet.

## Reading and Beating Your Opponent

### Everyone has a tell

```facts
Action: Watch other lines' draws from the bench, not just your own
Read: A rolled-over bottom hand means a backhand pull is coming
Read: Hands high on the shaft means a sweep or a tie-up rather than a yank
Read: Which foot is loaded tells you which way they intend to rotate
Read: Stick blade angle tells you where they want the puck to go
Read: A tight grip and low coiled stance means a clean-win attempt; relaxed and upright means a tie-up
```

Most centres have exactly one preferred technique and one strong side. Two shifts of attention is usually all it takes to find them.

- **Watch other lines' draws, not just your own.** You get roughly 56 opportunities a game to scout the two or three centres you'll face, and most of them happen while you're on the bench.
- **Read the grip.** A rolled-over bottom hand means a backhand pull is coming. Hands high on the shaft means a sweep or a tie-up rather than a yank.
- **Read the stance.** Which foot is loaded tells you which direction they intend to rotate. Stick blade angle tells you where they want the puck to go.
- **Read the tension.** A tight grip and a low, coiled stance usually means an aggressive attempt at a clean win; a relaxed, upright posture often means they're planning to tie you up.

### Watch the hand, not the man

```facts
Key: Look at the linesperson's hand and the puck, not your opponent's stick
Goal: Turn the draw into a pure reaction-time problem, which you can train
Risk: Reacting to your opponent leaves you a step behind and open to feints
Options: Some coaching sources watch the official's elbow instead, on the grounds that it moves fractionally earlier
Read: Linespersons have tendencies too — height of drop, speed of drop, how long they hold
```

This is the one universal. **Look at the linesperson's hand and the puck, not at your opponent's stick.** Reacting to the drop is a pure reaction-time problem you can train; reacting to your opponent means you are always one step behind, and it makes you vulnerable to feints. Some coaching sources recommend watching the official's **elbow** rather than the hand, on the grounds that the elbow moves fractionally earlier.

Linespersons also have tendencies — height of drop, speed of drop, how long they hold. Notice them.

### Vary your approach

```facts
Action: Change the technique, not just the target — pull one back, tie the next up, sweep the third
Risk: Win three straight draws to the same defenceman and the fourth will be jumped
Key: Even a technique you are worse at has value if it stops an opponent pre-loading against your best one
Technique: A shoulder feint, hip fake or delayed stick approach can draw an opponent into moving early — which, if they move before the drop, is a faceoff violation
Risk: The feint can be charged to you instead — 76.7 names the centre moving prematurely, and a feint that becomes contact is a minor, mandatory under USA Hockey, Hockey Canada and CARHA
Goal: Make them move before the drop — that is a faceoff violation, but the remedy NHL Rule 76.7 writes for it is a re-drop, not a removal
Rule: Neither IIHF edition removes their centre for a premature move — the 2025/26 book warned their team and re-dropped, the 2026/27 book re-drops (IIHF 76.7, both); the ejections live in 76.6 and in 76.7's contact paragraph, not in its opening sentence
Rule: ⚠️ **Under the IHUK In-House Rules 2026-27 the feint actually pays** — the linesperson must "immediately replace the centre for all violations" except after an icing, and a premature move is one. Not in the Elite League — its own Casebook writes no Rule 76, so IIHF 76.7 only re-drops
```

The counter to a scouted centre is unpredictability. If you win three straight draws pulling to the same defenceman, the fourth one will be jumped. Change the *technique*, not just the target: pull one back, tie the next one up, sweep the third. Even a technique you're worse at has value if it prevents the opponent from pre-loading against your best one. (Kicking it back is a fourth option **under the NHL, but not under the IIHF's** — and under USA Hockey, Hockey Canada and CARHA it is available **by inference**, from a general kicking permission plus the absence of any face-off bar, rather than because those books say anything about a draw. Kicking the *opponent* rather than the puck is a match penalty under the NHL, PWHL, USA Hockey, Hockey Canada and CARHA, and under **IIHF Rule 49.3** the referee *"may assess a major penalty and an automatic game misconduct penalty"* — discretionary as to the major, automatic as to the misconduct once it is given. See "Using your feet — the puck, the man, and the goal" above.)

Deception belongs here too: a small feint of the shoulder, a hip fake, or a deliberately delayed stick approach can draw an opponent into moving early — which, if they move before the drop, is a faceoff violation. **What that is worth depends on your book — and here it is the British answer that is the generous one, which is the other way round from every other violation on the list.** ⚠️ **Under NHL rules, drawing him early does not take him out of the dot.** Rule 76.7 opens on premature movement and writes its own remedy into the same sentence, and that remedy is the re-drop — the face-off *"must be conducted again"* — while the removals in that book are written for other things: for encroachment by a teammate and for the establish-position order at 76.6, and for pre-drop contact in 76.7's own later paragraph. (Rule 76.3(i) does have a linesperson replace a centre *"not positioned for the face-off"* immediately before the drop. **Whether that reaches a centre who is positioned and then twitches is not written down** — the book sets out two remedies and never says which governs, and there is no published NHL casebook to settle it, so do not plan on either.) **Both IIHF editions do the same as the NHL here**: the 2025/26 book added a warning to their team before the re-drop, the 2026/27 book deleted the warning, and neither one removes anybody for it. 🇬🇧 **The Home Countries are where the feint actually pays, and it pays because IHUK wrote it rather than because the IIHF did** — the In-House Rules 2026-27 have the linesperson *"immediately replace the centre for all violations"*, and a premature move is a violation, so there you do end up facing a winger — on every draw except one following an icing. ⚠️ **Not in the Elite League, where a centre you draw early simply takes the re-drop** — the In-House Rules never name it, and its own Casebook writes no Rule 76 and leaves IIHF 76.7 ruling. **Under either IIHF edition the second violation on the same draw is still the bench minor** — two minutes against the team, so a centre who has just been caught cheating the drop cannot afford to try anything at the re-drop. Bait him once, then take a clean, straight draw.

**Two things belong with that,** ⚠️ **because a feint is the one movement at the dot that can land on you rather than on him.** First, **the premature-movement clause names the centre, and you are one.** NHL and IIHF Rule 76.7 open with *"[i]f a center should move prematurely prior to the face-off"*, so a shoulder that goes early is your violation on exactly the terms it would be his — and it costs you what it would have cost him: a re-drop under the NHL and under both IIHF editions, and **your own place in the circle** under the In-House Rules, which replace the centre for every violation but a post-icing one — though not in the Elite League, which they never name. ⚠️ **It also spends one of the two violations that can cost two minutes**, whichever book you are under. Second, **a feint that touches him is a penalty, not a violation.** You are about one stick length apart, so the shoulder is the movement most likely to become pre-drop physical contact, and no book makes that free. Under NHL and IIHF Rule 76.7 the referee *"may, at his discretion impose a minor penalty"*; under **USA Hockey Rule 613(e)** it is mandatory — *"[a] minor penalty for interference **shall** be assessed to any player facing-off who makes any physical contact with their opponent's body by means of their own body or by their stick"*; **Hockey Canada Rule 6.2(c)** is the same, the referee *"**will** assess a Minor penalty or penalties to the player(s) whose action(s) caused the physical contact"*; and **CARHA Rule 57(b)** writes no discretion clause at all — *"[f]or violation of this rule, the Referee **shall** assess a Minor penalty on the player(s) whose action(s) caused the physical contact."* **So feint with the shoulder, never into him.** Under any of those three books the margin for getting that wrong is two minutes rather than a re-drop.

### Handedness and which side you're strong on

```facts
Convention: Coaching sources call the strong-side draw a right-shot centre on the right side, or a left-shot on the left
Key: Your strong side is the circle where your natural backward pull sends the puck toward the boards
Action: Take ten draws in each circle and you will know within a minute
Risk: On your weak side the board-side pull is a forehand — slower and weaker for most players
Never: Solve that by pulling to your backhand in your own end — it sends the puck to the middle, the worst place it can go
Options: Match the circle to the centre, flip your hands, or put a left-shot and a right-shot centre out together
```

A centre's shot handedness determines which circle they're better in, and every coaching staff builds around it.

**One term, two meanings in this document — keep them apart.** "Strong side" for a *centre*, in this section, is about handedness: the circle where his natural backward pull sends the puck to the boards. Elsewhere in this document — the defenceman "on the draw side" at an offensive-zone draw, or the corner the puck goes to after a defensive-zone loss — "strong side" carries the ordinary hockey meaning used throughout this corpus: whichever side of the ice the puck is on. The two usually coincide, because a team tries to put its centre on his handedness strong side, but they are not the same claim, and this section's meaning is the handedness one.

- The consensus in coaching sources (Ice Hockey Systems, Hockey's Arsenal) is that a **strong-side draw** is a **right-shot centre on the right side** of the ice, or a **left-shot centre on the left side**.
- On your **strong side**, you can pull the puck backward on your **backhand toward the boards** — the most powerful motion available to you, sending the puck to the safest part of the ice.
- On your **weak side**, that same board-side pull has to be made on the **forehand**, which is slower and weaker for most players. The alternative is to pull on your backhand — but that sends the puck toward the **middle of the ice**, which in your own end is the worst place it can go.

> **Flagged:** the sources above state the mapping (right shot / right side) but do not spell out the reference frame — "right side" as you stand at the dot facing the opponent's end. That is the standard reading and it's the one used here, but the sources are not explicit about it. **The reliable self-test that doesn't depend on anyone's convention: your strong side is the circle where your natural backward pull sends the puck toward the boards.** Take ten draws in each circle and you'll know within a minute.

![](diagram:faceoff-strong-side-dot)

**What teams do about it:**
- **Match the circle to the centre.** This is why coaches send a specific centre out for a specific dot, and why the rules that let you *choose* the dot (after an icing, or starting a power play) are more valuable than they look.
- **Flip your hands.** Some NHL centres — Vincent Trocheck is the frequently-cited example — take weak-side draws holding the stick the other way round, recreating strong-side mechanics on the wrong side of the ice. It is a real technique, and it is a lot of work: you are learning a second, mirror-image skill. Worth it if you take 1,000 draws a season; probably not if you take 100.
- **Use two centres.** Many teams have a left-shot and a right-shot centre on the ice together in key situations, and simply swap who takes the draw depending on the circle. This is free, and almost no rec team does it.

## Full Five-Player Alignments

> **This entire section describes coaching choices, not rules of hockey.** Every team's faceoff alignment is different, and the differences are large. What follows is the most common default at each location, with realistic alternatives named. **Find out what your team runs before your first shift** — and specifically, find out what the *call* is for each draw. A faceoff alignment that half the line is guessing at is worse than no alignment.
>
> The defaults below assume the house system used throughout this guide: a **low zone collapse (zone) defensive-zone coverage** and a **2-1-2 forecheck** (see [Defensive Zone Coverage](defensive_zone_coverage.md) and [Forechecking Systems](forechecking_systems.md)).
>
> **A word on "left" and "right," because that confusion is exactly what this section is for.** Every end of the rink has two faceoff circles, one nearer each side board, and which one is on your left changes with which net you're defending and which way you're facing — it isn't fixed. That is why the alignments below are written in terms that do not depend on which way you are facing. Left and right are not banned — you will hear them constantly, and this document uses them further down where a fixed reference makes them safe — but they need that reference agreed first, and at a draw it usually is not. Two things about the two circles *are* fixed, and everything below is written in those terms instead: which one is nearer the boards you'll exit toward — called **boards-side** below, with the other called **inside** — and which one suits your centre's shot hand, his **strong side**, covered in "Handedness and which side you're strong on" above. Learn boards-side/inside and strong-side/weak-side rather than left and right, and the alignment below applies unchanged at whichever circle you're actually standing at: it's a mirror image at the other one.

### Defensive-zone draws

```facts
Priority: Do not lose this cleanly — a tie-up is acceptable, a clean loss to their strong-side point is not
Position: Boards-side winger on the outside hash mark; inside winger on the inside hash mark facing the slot; boards-side defenceman behind and outside him; slot defenceman in front of the goalie
Action: On a clean win, pull to the boards-side defenceman to rim it out or hit the boards-side winger
Action: On a clean loss, wingers release to the points on the drop; the centre covers the man who beat him, boards-side D takes the corner or half-wall, and the slot D holds the net front and blocks the lane
Convention: This whole defensive-zone alignment is the house zone system, including who takes the corner and who holds the net front — under man-to-man each winger follows his man and those assignments change, so ask which your team plays
Action: On a tie-up everyone jumps; the inside winger and the boards-side defenceman are usually closest
Never: Pull the puck to the middle — a draw won into the slot is barely better than a loss
Never: Take the contact with your back to the checker on a defensive-zone draw retrieval, and never duck. Skates parallel to the boards, forearm and hip to the wall, head up and chin off your chest
```

The highest-stakes alignment in hockey. It has to work on a win, a loss and a tie-up, because you get all three.

**The standard setup:**

- **Centre** — in the dot. Your job is stated simply: **do not lose this cleanly.** A tie-up is an acceptable outcome; a clean loss to their strong-side point is not.
- **Boards-side winger** — on the **outside hash mark**, the one nearer the boards. Default is the winger whose natural side matches the circle. You are the primary breakout outlet on a win and the first man to the opposing point on a loss.
- **Inside winger** — on the **inside hash mark**, facing the **slot** (the lane of ice straight out from the net between the two faceoff circles — the *high slot* runs from the dots up to the top of the circles, the *goalmouth* is the immediate net front). You have the harder job: on a loss you must get to the far point, and on a scramble you are the closest body to the most dangerous ice.
- **Boards-side defenceman** — behind and outside the boards-side winger, near the hash marks, ready to retrieve anything pulled back to the wall or to get behind the net.
- **Slot defenceman** — in the slot in front of the goalie, responsible for the net front and for blocking a point shot.

**Common variation worth knowing:** many teams set their defencemen on their **"off" sides** for defensive-zone draws — the right-shot D on the left boards and vice versa — so that a puck pulled back to the wall arrives on their forehand and the breakout pass up the boards is a forehand pass. It costs you a little on defensive coverage and gains you a lot on the exit. Ask which your team does.

**Which circle you're actually at matters as much as who's on it.** Boards-side and inside above are relative to whichever circle the draw is at, not to a fixed side of the rink — the alignment doesn't change from one circle to the other. What does change is which circle is your centre's strong side, and a team that gets to choose its dot — after an icing, or to start a power play — uses that choice to put him there; see "Handedness and which side you're strong on" above.

![](diagram:faceoff-dzone-alignment)

![](diagram:faceoff-dzone-alignment-other-circle)

![](diagram:faceoff-dzone-clean-loss)

![](diagram:faceoff-dzone-tie-up)

**On a clean win:**
- Pull to the **boards-side defenceman**, who rims it out or hits the boards-side winger for a direct exit. This is the default because the boards are the safe half of the ice. ⚠️ **It is the safe half for the puck, not automatically for the player fetching it.** The tempting way to collect it is facing the wall with a forechecker arriving behind him — do not. That is the geometry every checking-from-behind and boarding rule is written about. **The posture you take that contact in is not a preference: never turn your back to the checker, and never duck.** Get your skates parallel to the boards instead, forearm and hip to the wall — not the point of your shoulder, and not the whole shoulder if you can help it — feet apart, knees bent, head up and chin off your chest. Go in on an angle; if you are caught already facing the wall, turn parallel if you possibly can, and if you cannot, get your stick and gloves up and spread on the glass. See [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md).
- The **wingers release** the moment the puck is secured — you are not needed in the battle, you are needed out on the wall **between the hash marks and the goal line**, which is the outlet spot [Breakouts](breakouts.md) covers. That is at or just below the dot line, roughly twenty feet out from the goal line — not thirty feet up the wall, which would put you above the outlet and make the pass long, flat and into your back.
- **Never pull the puck to the middle.** A defensive-zone draw won into the slot is barely better than a loss.

**On a clean loss:**
- **Both wingers go straight to the points.** In the house zone system assumed here, this is the call, and you make it on the drop rather than after you see where the puck went — it is the only thing standing between you and a shot from the top of the circle with your goalie screened. **Under man-to-man coverage the job changes:** each winger takes the specific opponent they were assigned before the draw and follows him, which may mean staying low if his man stays low. A hybrid ("zone with man-on-man in the low slot") splits the difference — the wingers still release to the points, but the low defender stays glued to a specific body. Ask which one your team plays.
- **Centre finds their centre.** Having lost the draw, your job is now to cover the man who beat you, who will be heading for the **high slot** (the area from the faceoff dots up to the top of the circles) or the **net front** (the immediate goalmouth).
- **Slot defenceman holds the net front and blocks the lane.** Do not chase the puck to the point; your winger has it.
- **Boards-side defenceman takes the strong-side corner or half-wall.** The **half-wall** is the boards level with the faceoff dot — about 20 feet out from the goal line (Rule 1.9), so barely a third of the way to the blue line, which sits 64 feet out (Rule 1.5). It is *not* the same thing as the **point**, which is the area just inside the blue line, not the line itself.

**On a tie-up — the most common outcome, though that is a coaching estimate rather than a counted figure:**
- **Everyone jumps.** A tied-up draw is a loose puck in a two-foot area with eight players around it, and it belongs to whoever moves first. This is the single most under-coached moment in hockey — coaching observation rather than a counted ranking — and it shows: players wait to see what happens instead of moving on the drop.
- The **inside winger** and the **boards-side defenceman** are usually the two closest bodies and should be the two who attack it.
- **Call it.** "Loose!" from anyone with eyes on the puck. See [On-Ice Communication](../foundation/on_ice_communication.md).


### Offensive-zone draws

```facts
Position: Strong-side defenceman at the point, feet already set and stick already loaded — this is the shooter
Position: Weak-side defenceman at the far point, holding the line as the safety against a counterattack
Action: Centre wins it back rather than forward
Read: The inside winger is the usual target for a quick pull-back, and the net-front driver if the draw is lost — drive to the net, not into the goalie
Priority: On a clean loss the priority flips instantly to not conceding a counterattack
Action: On a clean loss, wingers pressure the puck rather than retreat, the strong-side D recovers to the line, and the weak-side D holds — a 2-1-2 forecheck already in position
Key: An offensive-zone tie-up is a free 50-50 puck in the other team's end — a good outcome, not a failure
Convention: The offensive-zone alignment described here is a common default, not a rule — they differ from team to team, so find out what your team runs
```

The one place in hockey where you get to run a genuine set play with a stationary defence and a known start position. **What follows is a common default rather than a rule of hockey.** Offensive-zone alignments differ from team to team; find out what your team runs before your first shift.

**The standard setup:**

- **Centre** — in the dot, trying to win it back rather than forward.
- **Boards-side winger** — outside hash mark, ready to attack the wall or spin off it.
- **Inside winger** — inside hash mark, the most common recipient of a quick pull-back for a shot from the circle, and the primary net-front driver if the draw is lost. Get to the front of the net and stay off the goalie: avoiding contact is your job, and a bump there can cost you the goal or a penalty.
- **Strong-side defenceman** — at the point on the draw side, **feet already set, stick already loaded**. This is the shooter.
- **Weak-side defenceman** — at the far point, holding the line as the safety against a counterattack.

**The same mirroring applies here as in your own end.** Boards-side and inside are relative to whichever circle hosts the draw, not to a fixed side of the ice — and "strong-side" for the point man here means the draw side, not necessarily his shooting hand (see "Handedness and which side you're strong on" above, which is where the two meanings of "strong side" in this document are kept apart).

![](diagram:faceoff-ozone-alignment)

**On a clean win:** run the set play (see below). The important thing is that the shooter's feet are already planted — this is one of the very few shots in hockey where you don't have to receive, settle and set. That is worth more than a slightly better shooting angle.

**On a clean loss:** the priority flips instantly to **not conceding a counterattack**. The weak-side defenceman holds; the strong-side defenceman recovers to the line; the wingers pressure the puck rather than retreating, because you have four skaters above the puck and their exit options are limited. This is the moment a 2-1-2 forecheck is at its most effective, because everyone is already in position for it.

**On a tie-up:** attack the puck with the two nearest bodies and keep the two defencemen honest at the line. An offensive-zone tie-up is a free 50-50 puck in the other team's end — a good outcome, not a failure.

### Neutral-zone draws

```facts
Position: Both wingers wide in their lanes, level with or just behind the dot
Position: Both defencemen back toward your own blue line, one either side of the middle
Action: On a win advance the puck immediately, and look to carry or pass into the zone with control
Risk: A won neutral-zone draw that ends in a dump-in has produced very little — one author's tracking of the 2018-19 Swiss National League, not the NHL, so read the direction rather than the multiple
Action: On a loss, gap up immediately and defend the rush
Mindset: This is where you experiment — a lost neutral-zone draw costs you almost nothing
Convention: The neutral-zone alignment described here is a common default, not a rule — they differ from team to team, so find out what your team runs
```

The lowest-stakes draws, and the ones most worth taking a risk on. **The setup below is a common default rather than a rule of hockey**, and neutral-zone alignments differ from team to team; find out what your team runs.

**The standard setup:**

- **Centre** in the dot; **both wingers** wide, level with or just behind the dot, in their lanes.
- **Both defencemen** back toward your own blue line, one either side of the middle, ready to defend a rush if the draw goes the wrong way.

**On a win:** advance the puck immediately. Neutral-zone possession is worth having but it decays fast — **Daniel Weinberger**, writing at Hockey Graphs, found that neutral-zone faceoff wins mostly turn into **dump-ins**, and that possessions containing a dump-in produced 5.5 shots and 0.17 **expected goals** per 100 possessions versus 32 shots and 0.68 expected goals for those without one. (*Expected goals* is a model estimate of how many goals an average shooter would score from those shots, given where and how they were taken — it values chance quality, not just chance count.) **⚠️ Note the league before you carry these numbers anywhere.** Weinberger's data is **InStat tracking of the 2018-19 Swiss National League**, not the NHL — he says so in the article — so this is the one set of figures in this document that is not measured on the league the rest of it is written to. **The four figures are quoted exactly as the article gives them** — but note what a roughly six-fold gap implies: this is one author's tracked sample of possessions following neutral-zone faceoff wins in one league in one season, not a league-wide measurement, and part of the gap is selection rather than causation (a possession that never generated a controlled entry is *why* it ended in a dump-in). Treat the direction as solid and the multiple as approximate. A won neutral-zone draw that ends in a dump-in has produced very little. Look to carry or pass into the zone with control. See [Zone Entries](zone_entries.md).

![](diagram:faceoff-neutral-zone)

**On a loss:** your defencemen are the whole plan. Gap up immediately and defend the rush — see [Defending the Rush](defending_the_rush.md).

**Why this is where you experiment:** a lost neutral-zone draw costs you almost nothing. It is the right place to try the "lose it forward" play — going **round** the other centre rather than through them, which in women's hockey is IIHF Rule 101.1 rather than a preference (see "The techniques that use your body, your feet or your hands") — the weak-side flipped grip you're learning, or a technique you want your opponent to see now so it's in their head for the defensive-zone draw later.

## Set Plays

Set plays off draws are the closest hockey gets to American football. They also fail constantly, and the reason is usually complexity rather than execution — a coaching observation, not a measurement; nobody counts why set plays fail.

> Everything in this section is a **coaching choice**. These are the common patterns; your team's playbook will differ.

### Offensive-zone plays

```facts
Options: The D one-timer, the winger walk-out, the tip play, or a board-side variation
Key: The D one-timer works because the shooter's feet are set first — in a CARHA league keep the backswing under fifteen inches, or it is a slap shot: a minor, and a major and ejection if it injures
Risk: Every defensive alignment expects the D one-timer, so someone must clear the inside winger's shooting lane
Read: Use the winger walk-out against a team that immediately pressures the point
Options: The tip play is the safest, because a point shot into traffic rarely turns into a counterattack
Risk: Board-side loops and swaps work precisely once per game against an attentive opponent
```

**The D one-timer.** The centre pulls the puck straight back to the strong-side defenceman, who shoots first-time. The whole point is that the shooter's feet are set and traffic is already in place before the puck moves. **In a CARHA league keep that backswing short** — bring the stick back more than fifteen inches behind the puck and it is a slap shot there — a minor, and a major if it injures an opponent, which ends your game (Rules 79(a) and 30(a)). Its weakness is that it is the play every penalty kill and every defensive alignment expects — the inside winger's shooting lane needs to be cleared by someone, usually the net-front player stepping across.

**The winger walk-out.** The centre pulls the puck to the inside winger in the circle, who takes a stride toward the middle and shoots or feeds the slot. Better than the D one-timer against a team that immediately pressures the point, because you're attacking the space they vacated.

![](diagram:faceoff-ozone-d-one-timer)

![](diagram:faceoff-ozone-winger-walkout)

**The tip play.** The pull goes to the point, but the intended outcome is a deflection rather than a clean shot — someone screens, someone tips. Lower expected value per shot, higher rebound and chaos value, and it works when the goalie can see everything else you do. This is also the safest play, because a point shot into traffic rarely turns into a counterattack.

**The board-side variations.** Coaching sources describe plays where the boards-side winger loops **over the top of the circle to the inside hash mark** for a one-timer, or where the winger and defenceman **swap positions** before the draw to create an unfamiliar passing lane. These work precisely once per game against an attentive opponent.

### Defensive-zone plays

```facts
Options: The quick rim, the direct exit, the reverse, or off the glass and out of your zone — every forechecker is stationary at the whistle, so a hard rim or direct exit beats them
Rule: Under NHL and IIHF rules — the KHL reportedly too, unverified — your goalie may only play the puck behind the goal line inside the trapezoid (Rule 1.8; penalty NHL 27.8 and 63.2(viii), IIHF 27.7 and 63.2(VIII)); the sole exception is skate contact with the crease
Rule: The goalkeeper's restricted area is IIHF Rule 27.7, not the NHL's 27.8. Nothing the Elite League publishes switches it off; England Ice Hockey's Rules & Regulations 22.3 call it "not currently enforced" in its own competitions — keep your goalie inside it
Read: Play it as though it binds — your goalie stays inside the trapezoid, so a rim that dies in the corner is yours to fetch, not theirs
Rule: USA Hockey, Hockey Canada and the CARHA rec book write no trapezoid, so there a rim dying in your corner is your goalie's to fetch, though none lets him sit on it; a PWHL goalie plays as if it binds — Rules 1.8 and 27.8 switch it off, but 65.2(viii) keeps the minor
Rule: Neither the three books without a trapezoid nor the PWHL lets your goalie sit on a dead rim — USA Hockey 614(c) and CARHA 58(b) behind the goal line, body clear of the crease; CARHA 61(b) anywhere; PWHL 65.2(i) outside the crease; Hockey Canada Interpretation 3 to 10.1(a), clause v
Key: Closer in, and in front of the goal line, your goalie has considerably more latitude under every book — Goaltender owns that test, not this section
Never: Rim it halfway. Rim it hard enough to reach your winger instead, or tight enough for the goalie to stop it behind the net
Read: Use the reverse against a team that overloads the boards side
Action: Call off the glass and out of your zone when you are tired, pinned, or a man down — off it, not over it
```

**The quick rim.** Win the puck to the boards-side defenceman, who rims it hard around the boards at once. No possession, no risk, and you are out — and because every forechecker is standing still at the whistle, a hard rim is past them before anyone moves. **The trapezoid bears on this play, and the goalie it constrains is yours.** Under NHL and IIHF rules your goalie may play a puck behind the goal line only inside the trapezoid (Rule 1.8 in both books; NHL 27.8 and 63.2(viii) and IIHF 27.7 and 63.2(VIII) for the penalty). The KHL is commonly said to have one too; no KHL rulebook was available to check it. So a rim that dies in your corner is not his to fetch — a defenceman has to get it with the forecheck coming. ⚠️ **That pressure is worse than on an ordinary wall retrieval: the forechecker was stationary at the whistle and arrives at full speed.** **So when the checker arrives, never have your back to him, and never duck** — skates parallel to the boards, forearm and hip to the wall (not the point of the shoulder), head up and chin off the chest ([Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md)).

Three books run it the other way — ⚠️ **USA Hockey, Hockey Canada and CARHA write no trapezoid**, so under them the rim *is* your goalie's to fetch. *(A PWHL goalie should play as if it binds: the PWHL switches it off in Rules 1.8 and 27.8, but its Rule 65.2(viii) still writes the minor — [Goaltender](../positions/goaltender.md) sets out that contradiction.)* **But none of the four lets him sit on it out there** — each makes freezing the puck away from the net a minor, and Hockey Canada's carries no warning first:

- **PWHL** Rule 65.2(i): a minor for any player, *"including the goalkeeper"*, who freezes the puck *"in such a manner as to deliberately cause a stoppage of play"* — reaching a goalkeeper outside her crease.
- **USA Hockey** Rule 614(c): a minor on any of four triggers, and the first is the corner — *"[t]he puck is behind the goal line and their body is entirely outside of the goal crease."* The others are not playing it with the stick when able before being pressured, being outside the privileged area, and holding it against the goal frame, the boards or the netting.
- **CARHA** Rule 58(b): a minor for a goalkeeper who *"deliberately falls on or gathers the puck into their body, or holds or places the puck against any part of the goal or boards"* when the puck is behind the goal line and his body is entirely outside the crease. ⚠️ **It differs from USA Hockey's in both directions:** *deliberately* qualifies falling on or gathering, but not plainly holding against the goal or boards, so do not count on intent excusing those; it does not need the freeze to have caused a stoppage; and CARHA has no privileged area. The book does not say whether its *"when"* clause conditions both limbs or only the second. ⚠️ **In front of the goal line CARHA still limits him:** Rule 61(b) is a minor for a goalkeeper who *"deliberately holds the puck"* and is, in the referee's opinion, *"causing an unnecessary stoppage of play"*, with no location condition; and Rule 74(b) is a minor for *"any player who deliberately holds the puck against the boards or any part of the goal in any manner, unless they are being checked by an opponent"* — the Glossary makes the goalkeeper a player. Being checked is no licence to freeze it either: Rule 55(a) puts a bench minor on deliberate delay, and its Note 2 names *"freezing the puck"*.
- **Hockey Canada** reaches it from the crease: Rule 10.1(a) Interpretation 3, clause v — *"[w]hen a goaltender leaves their crease, they will not be allowed to freeze the puck. If they do, they will be assessed a Minor penalty. No warning will be issued."*

**So a goalie who chases a dead rim into the corner under USA Hockey or CARHA has to play it, not cover it.** Closer to the net he has more latitude — [Goaltender](../positions/goaltender.md) owns the test, and [Rules Primer](../foundation/rules_primer.md) the rules. Check your book before you decide who chases a dead rim. **Rim it hard enough to carry all the way round to your winger, or tight enough that the goalie can legally stop it behind the net and leave it** — never in between. Full treatment in [Breakouts](breakouts.md). **🇬🇧 In Britain, keep him inside it.** Nothing the Elite League publishes switches it off — its Casebook adopts the 2026-27 IIHF book — and while England Ice Hockey's *Rules & Regulations* 22.3 (2024-25) call the restricted area *"not currently enforced"* in its own competitions, the In-House Rules never mention it and say *"[u]nless otherwise stated, all rules will be enforced in line with the IIHF Rule Book"*. Play it as though it binds: a rim that dies in the corner is yours to fetch.

**The direct exit.** Win the puck to the boards-side defenceman, who hits the boards-side winger already breaking up the wall. Retains possession, and it is fast — the whole play happens before the opposing forecheck is organised, because they are also stationary at the whistle.

**The reverse.** Win the puck behind you and let a defenceman take it behind the net to change the angle. Good against a team that overloads the boards side on defensive-zone draws.

**Off the glass and out.** Not a play so much as an outcome, but it should be the explicit call when you're tired, pinned, or a man down. Nobody has ever been beaten by a puck in the neutral zone.

#### Who may hit the defenceman fetching it

```facts
Never: Take the contact with your back to the checker fetching a dead rim in your own corner, and never duck. Skates parallel to the boards, forearm and hip to the wall, head up and chin off your chest
Never: Hit a player who has turned toward the wall as the forechecker. Where checking is allowed at all, take the puck or angle him off it instead
Rule: A rim forechecker's hit on you is at least a minor in USA Hockey Competitive Contact (604(c)), Hockey Canada U13 or below, female or Member-approved play (7.3(a)) and any CARHA game, avoidable contact too (49(a)). CARHA excuses an accident, except to the head or from behind into the boards or net
Rule: A minor is the floor for an illegal check on a rim retrieval — Hockey Canada 7.3(b) mandates a major and game misconduct once it injures, and 7.3(c) is a match penalty for a deliberate attempt to injure. No IHUK regulation prices a breach of its own band
Rule: Under USA Hockey a reckless check on a rim retrieval is a major plus a game misconduct in a Competitive Contact game (604(d)), and in any game Rule 602(a) says a match "shall be assessed" for reckless endangerment — plan on the match
Rule: In the PWHL a forechecker may angle her off a rim skating the same way, but extending "an arm, shoulder, or hip after angling an opponent", or stepping or gliding into her, is an illegal hit — at least a minor (Rule 52.1)
Rule: In Britain checking is set by your competition's Rules of Competition, not a rule book — IHUK's four: junior U14 up and NIHL 1 and 2 checking, U10, U12 and WNIHL not. Those four settle no other league — not the EIHL, NIHL National, BUIHA, the PNIHL or the SNL. ⚠️ **Ask; until you know, do not hit**
```

**Whether the forechecker chasing a dead rim is allowed to hit your defenceman at all is a separate question from how he takes the contact** — ⚠️ **it turns on the classification, not the play.** In a **USA Hockey** Competitive Contact category — 12 & under and below, all Girls'/Women's classifications, all non-check Adult classifications — a body check on that retrieval is a minor under **Rule 604(c)**; under **Hockey Canada Rule 7.3(a)** a player who *"intentionally body-checks, bumps, shoves, or pushes any opposing player"* takes a minor at U13 and below, in female hockey and in any division a Member has approved. ⚠️ **That minor is the floor, not the price.** Hockey Canada **7.3(b)** allows *"a Major penalty and Game Misconduct penalty for body-checking"* on *"the degree of violence of the impact"*, and makes them **mandatory** — *"must be assessed"* — where a player is injured by a check that would otherwise be a minor; **7.3(c)** is a Match penalty for a player who *"deliberately attempts to or deliberately injures an opponent by body-checking."* **USA Hockey 604(d)** makes a major plus a game misconduct mandatory for a player who *"recklessly endangers an opponent with a body check in a Competitive Contact category"*, **604(e)** lets the referee go to a match penalty for reckless endangerment, and **Rule 602(a)** says a match *"shall be assessed"* to a player who *"recklessly endangers or attempts to injure"* an opponent — so plan on the match for a reckless hit. **A forechecker who takes a run at that retrieval risks his night, not two minutes — and the player he hits pays for it.**

**Under CARHA's adult book he may not hit him in any game.** Rule 49(a) penalises intentional body contact and contact a player *"does not avert"*; 49(c) spares only accidental contact, and not to the head, face or neck (still a minor, 50(a)) or from behind into the boards or net (a match penalty, 53(b)). A major there ends his game (Rule 30(a)), and a foul that puts the defenceman into the boards becomes a double minor (49(b)) — two of the three penalties that eject a CARHA player (32(a)).

**None of that takes anything off the posture, because the wall still arrives.** Hockey Canada's Rule 7.3 assesses no penalty for *"incidental contact of two opposing players in pursuit of the puck on the ice in the same direction"*, and the Note to USA Hockey 604(c) puts legal competitive contact *"in front of the goal or along the boards."* ⚠️ **And if you are the forechecker in a game that allows checking, never hit a player who has turned toward the wall** — take the puck or angle him off it instead.

🇬🇧 **In Britain the answer is in a competition regulation, not a rule book.** The IIHF book and the IHUK In-House Rules write no classification ban; the **IHUK Rules of Competition 2026-27**, one per competition, do. Junior hockey is **non-checking at U10 and U12 and checking at U14, U16 and U19** — *"Bodychecking is NOT allowed in U10 matches."* **NIHL Division 1 and 2 are checking. Every WNIHL women's division is non-checking.** ⚠️ **Those four regulations settle no other British league** — not the EIHL, NIHL National, BUIHA, the PNIHL, the SNL or other Scottish domestic hockey — and the Elite League's Casebook, searched in full, does not mention body checking. ⚠️ **For any of them, ask your league before you hit anyone, and until you have an answer, do not.** ⚠️ **Nor do the four price a breach:** none writes a penalty for the check itself — the nearest is the U10 procedure, where the official *"can remove a player from the game for the remainder of the shift"* and a repeat offender comes off for the game — and the IIHF book has no non-check classification to price it under. **Treat the band as the instruction and the price as unsettled.** The wall still arrives in a British non-checking game: the IIHF Glossary, in both editions, lets players near the puck *"push and lean into each other provided that possession of the puck remains the sole object of the contact."*

In women's hockey, chasing that rim, ⚠️ **do not use the boards on her:** IIHF Rule 101.1 says players *"are not allowed to use the boards to make contact with an opponent to eliminate her from the play, push her into the boards, or pin her along the boards"*, and makes *"[a]ny move by a Player to step or glide into an opposing Player"* at least a minor. **The PWHL is looser and still not free:** Rule 52.1 lets a forechecker angle her off the puck skating the same way, but extending *"an arm, shoulder, or hip after angling an opponent"* is an illegal hit, and so is stepping or gliding into her — at least a minor. [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md) has the league-by-league table of who may check, and [UK and England Rules](../foundation/uk_rules.md) quotes the four British regulations in full.

### Keeping it simple enough to work

```facts
Convention: Two or three plays maximum, per zone
Action: One-word calls from the centre at the dot, acknowledged by everyone — a play nobody heard is not a play
Key: Every play needs a defined failure branch: if we lose it, X does Y
Never: Get clever when there is confusion, no call, or a replacement taking the draw. Tie up in your own end instead, point shot in theirs
```

The failure mode is universal: teams install six plays, run each of them badly, and the players spend the drop trying to remember which one is on.

- **Two or three plays maximum**, per zone. A team that runs one play excellently beats a team that runs six adequately.
- **One-word calls.** The centre calls it at the dot; everyone acknowledges. A play nobody heard is not a play.
- **Every play needs a defined failure branch.** "If we lose it, X does Y." Most set plays are drawn up only for the win, which is why teams that lose an offensive-zone draw look so disorganised.
- **The default is always the simple one.** If there's confusion, no call, or a replacement taking the draw: tie-up in your own end, point shot in theirs.

## The Procedure and the Rules

**"The five books" below means five, and there is a sixth book here.** Every cross-book comparison in this document sets the **NHL**, the **IIHF**, **USA Hockey**, **Hockey Canada** and **CARHA** against each other, with the British amendments flagged separately — and one survey, the closing-hand one, runs to six. **The sixth is the PWHL's own rule book, and on face-offs it is the NHL's chapter renumbered rather than rewritten**: Rule 78 where the NHL has 76, Rule 83 where it has 81, Rule 84 where it has 82. Its Rules 78, 83.2 and 84 were read in full against the NHL's Rules 76, 81.2 and 82 for this document, clause by clause, and **no divergence was found** — the same nine spots, the same five-and-eight-second line change, the same stick order, the same blade standard, the same fifteen feet, the same encroachment ejection, the same two-violation bench minor and the same post-icing carve-outs. **So wherever the face-off rules below say "the NHL", a PWHL reader can read along at the other number** — two higher here, though the gap is not the same throughout that book — and a rule-number search of that book will find nothing, which is why the face-off survey sentences below say five. **⚠️ Where a sentence says a rule is some books’ *only*, though, the PWHL is named and numbered there instead** — reading along cannot repair a word that shuts you out.

🇬🇧 **If you play U10 or U12 in IHUK's junior leagues, much of this section is not your game yet.** The U10 Rules of Competition restart every stoppage with a quick draw at centre ice, refereed by a coach who is told to *"not wait for teams to be set-up in a face-off formation"* and to *"[c]all the nearest player from each team"*; penalties there are *"the same as with other EIH junior leagues"*, but *"no penalties are recorded"* — the official removes the offending player for the rest of the shift instead. The U12 format has *"[n]o icing calls"*, and offside does not stop play — the attacking team is called out and must clear the zone, and *"[f]ailure to retreat quickly"* is a delay-of-game penalty — so the post-icing and offside draws below never happen there. From U14 up, the draw is the one this document describes; [UK and England Rules](../foundation/uk_rules.md) sets out the junior formats in full.

### The sequence, start to finish

```facts
Rule: The visiting team gets up to five seconds to change, then the home team up to eight (NHL and IIHF Rule 82.2) — USA Hockey 204(a) and Hockey Canada 6.1(d) give the home team five, not eight
Rule: CARHA has no last change at all — Rule 19(g) gives both teams one shared ten-second window and names neither the home nor the visiting team, so there is no matchup to hunt off it
Rule: After the linesperson's whistle both teams have no more than five seconds to line up — NHL and IIHF Rule 76.3 and USA Hockey 613(c); Hockey Canada and CARHA write no five-second line-up clock at all
Rule: The sticks go down before the drop, and the order they go down in is not the same in every book (Rule 76.4)
Rule: The faceoff ends when the puck has been legally dropped (Rule 76.1)
Rule: The five-second limit is not enforced in the last two minutes of regulation or in overtime (NHL and IIHF Rule 76.3) — USA Hockey 613(c) writes no such exception and Hockey Canada and CARHA set no five-second line-up clock at all
Rule: Hockey Canada has no five-second line-up clock but does have an untimed one — after a referee's warning, failing to commence play or "unnecessarily delay[ing] the resumption of play" is delay of game (Hockey Canada 10.1(vii))
Rule: CARHA has no line-up clock either, and no warning — its Rule 57(c) lets the official replace a centre who is slow into position at that draw, so "no clock" is not "take your time"
Rule: The PWHL renumbers rather than rewrites — its Rule 84.2 gives the visitors five seconds and the home team eight, exactly as NHL and IIHF 82.2 do, and its whole face-off chapter is the NHL's at Rule 78
Key: Last change is why the away centre so often finds himself against a specialist — in every book read here but CARHA, which has none
```

1. **Line change.** The referee gives the **visiting team up to five seconds** to change, raises a hand, then gives the **home team up to eight seconds** (Rule 82.2). This is the mechanism behind "last change" — the home team sees the visitors' personnel before committing its own, which is why home coaches can hunt matchups and visiting coaches cannot. It is also why the away team's centre often finds himself against a specialist. Note that the *right* itself comes from Rule 82.1 rather than 82.2, and it carries an icing exception — see the post-icing draw below.
2. **The referee lowers their hand.** No more changes.
3. **The linesperson blows the whistle.** Both teams now have **no more than five seconds** to line up (Rule 76.3).
4. **The sticks go down** (Rule 76.4). Order matters — see below.
5. **The puck is dropped** between the two blades. The faceoff formally "ends when the puck has been legally dropped" (Rule 76.1).

**And you have to go straight to the faceoff dot.** Rule 82.3: *"Players must proceed directly to the location of the face-off to participate in the ensuing face-off. Any attempts to delay the game by stalling or otherwise unnecessary actions by either team shall result in the assessment of a bench minor penalty for delaying the game."* The IIHF prints that paragraph in the same words. Dawdling to the dot to buy your bench a breath is a two-minute penalty rather than a tactic — and unlike the slow-to-the-dot warning in Rule 82.2(vii), this one carries no warning first.

**In the last two minutes of regulation and at any point in overtime, the five-second limit is not enforced** (Rule 76.3). The whistle still goes, but the linesperson will take the time to get it right. This changes how late-game draws feel: there is more time to set your alignment, more time to look at the bench, and more time for both centres to fidget for an advantage.

**Three of the five books run that clock differently,** ⚠️ **so do not carry the eight seconds into a North American game.** **USA Hockey Rule 204(a)** gives the visitors five seconds and then *"the home team shall have 5 seconds to complete any player substitutions"* — five and five, not five and eight. **Hockey Canada Rule 6.1(d)** does the same, allowing *"a five-second period during which the Visiting Team may make a player change"* and then, arm still raised, *"a five-second period during which the Home Team may make a player change."* The home team still changes second under both, so last change survives; it is simply three seconds shorter. ⚠️ **CARHA is a third answer, and under it last change does not exist.** Its **Rule 19(g)** gives both teams a single shared window — the referee *"will then allow a ten-second period during which the teams may make player changes"*, after which an arm goes up and no further changes may be made. There is no visiting-team window, no home-team window and no order between them; the phrases *"Visiting Team"* and *"Home Team"* appear nowhere in that rule. **So in a CARHA game do not plan a matchup off last change — there is none to have.** **And the late-game relaxation is in none of the three — though only one of the three has a clock to relax.** USA Hockey Rule 613(c) states the five-second line-up limit with no exception for the last two minutes or for overtime, so expect to be hurried late in a tight game under that book. **Hockey Canada writes no five-second line-up limit at all** — its only five-second periods are the two line-change windows in 6.1(d) — **and CARHA writes none either**: its Rule 57 was read in full and sets no line-up clock, and the only counted period anywhere in that book is the shared ten seconds for changes in Rule 19(g). Under those two, what hurries you late is not a count but an official — Hockey Canada's untimed delay-of-game backstop, immediately below, and CARHA Rule 57(c), under which an official may *"order them replaced at that face-off"* the first time you are slow into position, with no warning first.

**That last one is a missing clock, not a missing limit, and the difference is two minutes.** Hockey Canada Rule 10.1 lists among the acts that are delay of game a team which, *"after a warning by the Referee, fails to place the correct number of players on the ice and commence play"*, or where *"any player(s) of that team unnecessarily delays the resumption of play"* (10.1(vii)). Nothing is counted and nothing is timed — but a warning comes first, and after it, dawdling to the dot is a penalty like anywhere else. ⚠️ **Do not read "no five-second limit" as "take as long as you like."**

### Who puts their stick down first

```facts
Rule: At centre ice the visiting player places their stick first (NHL Rule 76.4)
Rule: At the other eight spots the defending player places first, then the attacking player (NHL Rule 76.4)
Rule: The IIHF book — the one every level of British hockey is played under — matches the NHL word for word at all nine spots, in both its 2025/26 and 2026/27 editions (IIHF Rule 76.4)
Rule: ⚠️ **The IHUK In-House Rules 2026-27 override that across the Home Countries — England, Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland, but not the Elite League — saying "Either centre can now put their stick down first for a face-off"** (Section 10, Rule 76)
Rule: The Elite League is the exception: those In-House Rules never name it and the EIHL Casebook writes no Rule 76 at all, so an EIHL draw sits on IIHF 76.4 unamended and the defending centre's stick still goes down first at the eight spots
Rule: ⚠️ **The In-House Rules require the whole blade in the white**, not the toe IIHF 76.7(IV) asks for — "the whole of their stick blades (not toe or heel)" (Section 10, Rule 76); short of it, your centre is replaced unless the draw follows an icing. The EIHL, which they never name, keeps the IIHF toe
Rule: USA Hockey reverses this — the attacking player places first everywhere but centre ice (USA Hockey Rule 613(a))
Rule: Hockey Canada is a third answer — the visiting player places first at every spot, attacking or defending, though a home player who wants to place first "are allowed to do so, if it does not cause a problem" (Rule 6.2(a), Interpretation 1)
Rule: CARHA prescribes no stick-down order at all — its Rule 57 names neither the visiting player nor the defending one, so under that book there is no blade to wait for and none to claim
Rule: The IIHF Situation Handbook 2026/27 reads the IIHF's own stick order as an option — an attacking centre may place first if they choose, and the linesperson allows it (Situation 76.53)
Key: Placing first shows your blade angle first, so under NHL and IIHF rules the advantage of going second is the attacking team's
Action: When you are the one who must show your hand, vary the angle you show
```

This is stated wrongly more often than almost any rule in hockey. The actual text of Rule 76.4:

- **At centre ice** — the **visiting** player places their stick first.
- **At the other eight spots** — the **defending** player places first, "followed immediately by the attacking player."

So the common shorthand "the visitor puts their stick down first" is true at exactly one of the nine spots. Everywhere else the **defending** player commits first — and putting your stick down first means showing your blade angle first, which is information you'd rather not give away. **So at the eight end-zone and neutral-zone spots the advantage of going second belongs to the *attacking* team** under the NHL and IIHF books, which is the opposite of what most players assume. If you are defending, expect to show your hand, and vary the angle you show.

At the four neutral-zone dots the rule still says "defending player," which means the player from the team defending the goal at that end. The rulebook does not spell this out further; that is the standard reading.

**The IIHF book agrees with the NHL here, and it still does.** IIHF Rule 76.4 carries the same sentence, word for word in both the 2025/26 and the 2026/27 editions: *"At the eight"* face-off spots *"(excluding center ice face-off spot), the defending Player shall place their stick within the designated white area first followed immediately by the attacking Player. When the 'face-off' is conducted at the center ice face-off spot, the visiting Player shall place their stick on the ice first."* **That is the IIHF's text**, which writes *"Player"* and *"their"* where the NHL writes *"player"* and *"his"*; the provision is the same one. This is one of the places where the two books do **not** split — which is worth saying plainly, because the violation *consequences* further down this document were, until the IIHF's 2026/27 edition, one of the biggest splits between them. Same order of sticks; the cost of getting it wrong is set out below. **But not in Britain — the box at the end of this section has the British answer, and it is none of the three this comparison gives.**

**The IIHF's Situation Handbook reads that order as an option rather than an obligation, and says so for the first time in its 2026/27 edition.** Situation 76.53 asks what a linesperson should do when *"the attacking center player puts their stick down before the defending center player has a chance to do so"*, and answers: *"This rule gives the attacker an option to put their stick down second. However, if the attacker chooses to put their stick down first, the linesperson should allow this and drop the puck as soon as the defending center puts down their stick."* So the second-stick advantage is yours to keep or to give away, and giving it away is not a violation — useful if you are the attacking centre and would rather set your blade early than react to theirs. That situation is new: the 2025/26 handbook carried a different one at the same number, about how a team picks its dot after a penalty. **It is a handbook interpretation, not operative rule text** — the handbook's own Foreword says *"in case of any contradictions, the valid IIHF Rulebook is always the basis"* — though nothing in Rule 76.4 speaks to an attacker who volunteers, so there is no contradiction to resolve. **Hockey Canada arrives at the same place from the other side**, as its own paragraph two below sets out: there a *home* player who wants to place first *"are allowed to do so, if it does not cause a problem."*

**USA Hockey reverses this,** ⚠️ **so check before you build a habit on it.** USA Hockey Rule 613(a) reads: *"The attacking player shall be the first to place their stick on the ice, except for a center ice face-off where the visiting team player shall be first to place their stick on the ice."* So under the NHL the **defending** centre shows his blade angle first at eight of the nine spots; under USA Hockey the **attacking** centre does. If you play in a North American rec or youth league, the advantage of going second is yours when you are *defending*, not attacking — the exact opposite of the NHL situation described above.

**Hockey Canada is a third answer again, and the simplest of the five.** Hockey Canada Rule 6.2(a) says *"the player of the Visiting Team will place their stick within the designated white area first"*, and Interpretation 1 to that rule removes any doubt about scope: *"For all face-offs, the Visiting Team face-off player must place their stick down first for the face-off regardless of whether they are the attacking or defending team."* ⚠️ **So under that book the order never depends on which end you are attacking — only on which bench you came out of, at all nine spots.** The same interpretation is unusually relaxed about enforcement: a first violation is a warning, a second during the same stoppage *"may result in a Minor penalty"* for delay of game, and a home player who wants to put their stick down first *"are allowed to do so, if it does not cause a problem."*

**CARHA is a fourth position, and it is silence.** Its Rule 57 was read in full and prescribes no order at all — it asks only that the two takers stand *"approximately one stick length apart"* with their blades in the white area, and the words *"Visiting Team"* and *"Home Team"* appear nowhere in that book's face-off rule. **So under CARHA there is no order to wait for and none to claim** — which makes it the one North American book where showing your blade first gives nothing away, because nobody was obliged to show theirs.

> **If you play in Britain, which order is yours depends on your league — and across the Home Countries none of the three above is.** The amendment pulls two ways: **the stick *order* is looser than the IIHF's, and the amount of *blade* stricter.** The **IHUK In-House Rules 2026-27**, *Section 10 – Game Flow*, amend Rule 76: *"Linespersons must ensure both centres are squared up correctly behind their marks with the whole of their stick blades (not toe or heel) in the white portion of the face-off circle. **Either centre can now put their stick down first for a face-off.**"* **⚠️ So under the In-House Rules the order tells you nothing** — not which end you are defending, and not whether you will see the other blade first. Set your blade when you are ready and watch the linesperson's hand. ⚠️ **The Elite League is the exception:** the In-House Rules never name it, so an EIHL draw keeps the IIHF order — the defending centre's stick down first at the eight spots, the visiting centre's at centre ice.
>
> **This is a British departure, not an IIHF change** — both IIHF editions keep the defending-centre order word for word, and the In-House Rules, as the *"overriding interpretation of how the IIHF Rule Book is to be applied domestically"*, win where they amend it. **⚠️ The blade half cuts the other way:** IIHF Rule 76.7(IV) asks only for *"at least the toe of the blade of the stick"* in the white, but a British linesperson is told to require the whole blade. **Put the whole blade down.** A violation there has your centre replaced on the spot unless the draw follows an icing, and a second violation by your team on that draw is still a bench minor under IIHF Rule 76.7, which IHUK does not amend.
>
> **What those In-House Rules cover, in their own words:** IHUK and the British Universities Ice Hockey Association adopt the IIHF book *"as the foundation for the rules of the game to be applied in England, Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland (the Home Countries)"*. The Rule 76 amendment carries no league restriction of its own — unlike the fighting provision in the same document, which is headed *"Applicable Leagues: NIHL (all leagues), SNL, and BUIHA Varsity"*. **The Elite League is not mentioned anywhere in that document — and its own book settles the question the other way.** The **Motorpoint EIHL Casebook 26-27** writes **no Rule 76 at all**; its contents jump from Rule 69 to Rule 77, and the word "face-off" appears in it five times, none of them about the draw procedure. Its Preamble then says what fills the gap: the Casebook's interpretations *"will replace or in specific cases, supersede, the ruling that is currently within the IIHF Rule Book"*, and *"[f]or all rules not mentioned in the EIHL Casebook, the official 2026 - 2027 IIHF Rulebook and IIHF Situation Handbook will serve as the official ruling."* **So an EIHL draw runs IIHF 2026/27 Rule 76.4 unamended: the defending centre's stick goes down first at the eight spots, and the visiting centre's at centre ice.** ⚠️ **Do not carry the In-House "either centre" permission into an Elite League game, and note which half of it actually bites.** It is the **defending** centre who loses by it: under IIHF 76.4 they must place first and cannot wait for the attacker's blade, where the In-House Rules would let them. **The attacking centre is freer than the order suggests under both**, because the IIHF's own Situation 76.53 tells the linesperson to allow an attacker who chooses to place first. And a centre who simply declines to put the stick down is caught either way — IIHF 76.3(II) lists a centre who refrains from placing their stick on the ice, and the linesperson has the offending centre replaced before dropping the puck. *(The League issues rule updates in season by email and on its website, so ask your league if a Rule 76 section has since been added.)*

### Positioning requirements

```facts
Position: Square to your opponent's end of the rink, roughly one stick length apart (Rule 76.4)
Rule: Feet either side of the restraining lines that run parallel to the side boards — touching the lines is fine (NHL and IIHF Rule 76.7(iv), which defines this; 76.4 does not)
Rule: The toe of your skate blade must not cross the restraining lines perpendicular to the side boards (NHL and IIHF Rule 76.7(iv))
Rule: At least the toe of your stick blade must be in the white of the spot — NHL and IIHF Rule 76.7(iv) and Hockey Canada 6.2(a) — but not under the IHUK In-House Rules and not under CARHA 57(a), which asks for "the full blade of their sticks flat on the ice"
Rule: USA Hockey 613(a) takes the toe too (Casebook 613 Situation 2) but adds a demand no other book writes — at end-zone spots both blades must be "in contact with the nearest white area of the face-off spot and clear of the center red area"; centre ice and the neutral zone get no blade standard
Rule: Under NHL and IIHF Rule 76.7(iv), the blade must remain in the white until the puck is dropped; lifting it early is a faceoff violation
Rule: ⚠️ **Under the IHUK In-House Rules 2026-27 the toe is not enough — put "the whole of their stick blades (not toe or heel) in the white portion of the face-off circle"** (Section 10, Rule 76); falling short replaces your centre except on a draw after an icing; none of this reaches the Elite League
Rule: No other player may enter the circle or come within fifteen feet of the two takers — NHL 76.6, USA Hockey 613(b), Hockey Canada 6.2(a) and CARHA 57(a); IIHF 76.6 writes it as 4.5 m
Rule: At end-zone draws the other eight skaters keep their bodies on their own side of the hash marks — the restraining lines on the outer edge of the circle (NHL and IIHF Rule 76.6, USA Hockey 613(b))
Rule: The attacking team's four other skaters set their positions first, then the defending team counters — NHL and IIHF 76.6 and Hockey Canada's Interpretation 1 to 6.2(a); USA Hockey 613(b) and CARHA 57(a) write no such right
Rule: The goalkeeper may not participate in a faceoff (Rule 76.1)
```

**For the centre** (Rule 76.4 and 76.7):
- Stand **squarely facing your opponent's end of the rink**, roughly **one stick length apart**.
- Place your feet on either side of the restraining lines running parallel to the side boards — touching the lines is fine.
- The **toe of your skate blade must not cross** the restraining lines running perpendicular to the side boards as you approach.
- At least the **toe of your stick blade** must be on the ice in the designated white part of the spot, and **must stay there until the puck is dropped** — NHL and IIHF 76.7(iv), Hockey Canada 6.2(a), and CARHA 57(a) at the end-zone and neutral-zone spots all name the toe. ⚠️ **But CARHA's opening paragraph to the same rule, scoped to no spot, wants *"the full blade of their sticks flat on the ice"*** — more than any other book asks. The two fit together: a flat blade with its toe in the white satisfies both. **So under CARHA, put the whole blade down** — the same instruction a British player gets. ⚠️ **USA Hockey Rule 613(a) is the fifth answer, different rather than stricter.** ⚠️ **It does not demand the whole blade** — its Casebook, Rule 613 Situation 2, says *"[a]s long as any part of the blade touches the ice in the proper place, the player shall be considered ready for the face-off."* But at an end-zone spot both blades must be in the white *"**and clear of the center red area**"*, which no other book writes, so a toe in the white with the blade across the red passes the other four and fails this one. At centre ice and the neutral-zone spots it says nothing about the blade. **So under USA Hockey, get the whole blade into the white at an end-zone draw** — then it can never be across the red.
- 🇬🇧 **Under the IHUK In-House Rules, put the whole blade in the white.** They tell linespersons to ensure both centres have *"the whole of their stick blades (not toe or heel) in the white portion of the face-off circle"* — stricter than the IIHF's toe. Improper placement is a violation under IIHF Rule 76.7(IV), and under the In-House amendment a violation has your centre replaced, except on a draw following an icing; IHUK does not amend 76.7, so a **second** violation on that draw is still a bench minor. A centre who puts only the toe down, exactly as the IIHF book says, is in violation. **Under those rules the stick order is free.** ⚠️ **Neither half reaches the Elite League**, which those rules never name and whose casebook writes no Rule 76: an EIHL draw keeps the IIHF toe and the IIHF order, the defending centre's stick going down first at the eight spots outside centre ice. See the box at the end of "Who puts their stick down first".

**For everyone else** (Rule 76.6):
- No other player may enter the faceoff circle or come within **fifteen feet** of the two players taking the draw. **All five books carry that distance**, two of them metric: the NHL writes *"fifteen feet (15')"*, **USA Hockey Rule 613(b)** *"15 feet"*, **Hockey Canada Rule 6.2(a)** *"at least 4.57 m (15 ft.)"*, **CARHA Rule 57(a)** *"at least 15 feet (4.57 m)"*, and **IIHF Rule 76.6** writes it as *"4.5m"*.
- At end-zone draws, all eight other skaters must keep their bodies **on their own side of the hash marks**. **The NHL and the IIHF use the same words for it** — bodies *"on their own side of the restraining lines marked on the outer edge of the face-off circles"* (Rule 76.6) — and **USA Hockey Rule 613(b)** says *"behind the hash marks on the outer edge of the face-off circle"*. **Hockey Canada draws the line by where a skate is rather than where a body is**: its glossary defines encroachment as standing *"with a skate inside the face-off circle, on the face-off circle, or in the area between the hash marks"*, adding that a player's stick may be inside the circle but not between the hash marks.
- Crucially: **the attacking team's four other skaters must establish their positions first**, and only then may the defending team counter and hold. If an attacker tries to reposition after that, the violation is charged to the *attacking* team — even if the defending centre backs out of the dot in response.

Where that rule applies, it is a genuine tactical asset for the defending team, and it is the reason defensive-zone alignments can be reactive there: you get to see where their net-front threat is going before you decide who takes them.

**That asset is not in every book,** ⚠️ **so find out whether it is yours.** **Hockey Canada writes it in the same terms** — Interpretation 1 to Rule 6.2(a): *"When positioning players for end zone face-offs (other than the players taking the face-off), the attacking team must be first to take their positions; the defending team has the right to set up its defence."* **USA Hockey does not write it at all.** Its Rule 613(b) says only that no other player may *"enter the face-off circle or come within 15 feet of the players facing-off the puck"* and that *"[a]ll other players must position themselves behind the hash marks on the outer edge of the face-off circle or stand approximately one stick length apart from the opponent"* — nothing about who sets up first. So under that book, do not build a defensive-zone alignment on watching their net-front threat commit before you commit: you may not get to.

**The goalkeeper may not participate in a faceoff** (Rule 76.1). This matters when your goalie is pulled — see "Situational Faceoffs".

## Violations, Ejections and the Bench Minor

Get this exactly right, because the consequences are asymmetric and one of them is routinely misunderstood.

**Two different consequences, depending on your book — and, in the IIHF's case, on which edition of it.** The *list* of violations is essentially the same in the NHL and IIHF rulebooks — the same premature movement, the same encroachment, the same bad stick placement. What differs is what it costs you. The NHL throws your centre out of the circle. The **IIHF 2025/26** book issued **a warning to your team** and re-dropped the puck with the same centre still in the dot.

> **That IIHF answer changed with the 2026/27 edition, and this section was written to the older one.** Everything marked "IIHF" below is the **2025/26** position. The **IIHF Official Rule Book 2026/27**, published June 2026, moved to the NHL's ejection: its 76.6 has the encroaching team's centre *"ejected from the face-off circle"*, its 76.7 dropped the warning sentence for premature movement, swapped the warning for pre-drop physical contact for an order that the centre be *"replaced"*, and deleted the first warning its predecessor gave for helmet-to-helmet contact, and its 76.3 and 76.4 replace the offending centre rather than warning him. **⚠️ If you are playing in Britain, read the NHL column, not the IIHF one, for who gets removed — and you can read it without first establishing which IIHF edition your league runs, because the IHUK In-House Rules 2026-27 write the removal in themselves**, on every draw but one that follows an icing. The 2025/26 text is kept below because the rest of it is unchanged — the standard for where a skate may be, the two-violation bench minor, the skate-win violation itself, the stick-placement order, and the once-a-game lateness carve-out, which survives in substance though the newer book rewrote its wording — and because knowing what moved is how you spot it if your league is still administering the old book. **Neither 2026/27 document has been read in full** — each was read for the face-off rules and for every other rule and situation stated here as reading the same in both editions, each of which carries its number where it is used — so nothing here says whether the rest of this document's IIHF layer is current under the newer book.

**If you play anywhere in Britain, an IIHF book is the one you live under** — see [UK and England Rules](../foundation/uk_rules.md) — **with the IHUK In-House Rules on top of it across the Home Countries, and on faceoff violations those In-House Rules are what actually decide the question.** ⚠️ **In the Elite League they do not decide it: the In-House Rules never name that league, and the EIHL Casebook writes no Rule 76 at all**, so an EIHL draw runs IIHF Rule 76 unamended. Read this section with that substitution in mind, and with the edition warning above; both are flagged again at each point where they change what you should do.

### What gets your centre thrown out of the dot

```facts
Rule: A faceoff violation normally ejects the offending team's centre from that draw, replaced "by any teammate then on the ice" (NHL Rules 76.4, 76.6)
Rule: Under the IIHF 2025/26 book a faceoff violation was a warning to the team and a re-drop, with the centre staying in the dot (IIHF 2025/26 Rules 76.6, 76.7)
Rule: The IIHF 2026/27 book ejects the centre from the circle for a violation instead, as the NHL does (IIHF 2026/27 Rule 76.6)
Rule: Under the IHUK In-House Rules 2026-27 — the Home Countries, not the Elite League — the linesperson replaces the centre for every violation except on a draw following an icing (Section 10, Rule 76); IHUK does not amend IIHF 76.7, so a second violation on that draw is still a bench minor
Rule: Premature movement takes nobody out of the dot under the NHL or either IIHF book — the draw "must be conducted again" (76.7; the 2025/26 edition warns the team first) — but the In-House Rules replace the centre except after an icing, and the Elite League, which they never name, keeps the re-drop
Rule: Improper stick placement, or skates in the wrong place relative to the restraining lines, is a violation (NHL and IIHF Rule 76.7, in both IIHF editions)
Rule: A teammate putting a skate inside the circle or across the hash marks is a violation (NHL and IIHF 76.7, both editions) — but merely moving is not, and the premature-movement clause names the centre, not them
Rule: Physical contact before the drop is a violation whoever makes it, but the minor is the taker's — mandatory under USA Hockey 613(e), Hockey Canada 6.2(c) and CARHA 57(b), discretionary under NHL and IIHF 76.7; a teammate's contact ejects your centre instead (Hockey Canada 6.2(a), CARHA 57(a))
Rule: Under the IIHF 2026/27 book that contact costs you the centre as well — 76.7 has the linesperson "order the center of the offending team replaced", where the 2025/26 book only warned him (IIHF 76.7, both editions)
Rule: Lining up off-side for the draw is a violation in all five books this document surveys — NHL and IIHF 76.3(V), Hockey Canada 6.2(a), CARHA 57(a), USA Hockey 613(d) — and in every one but the IIHF 2025/26 edition, which warned, it is your centre who is replaced
```

Under NHL rules **most** faceoff violations **eject the offending team's centre from that draw**, replaced "by any teammate then on the ice" (Rule 76.4). ⚠️ **The exception is the first item below:** a centre who moves early, or a puck dropped unfairly, costs only a re-drop under the NHL and both IIHF editions. 🇬🇧 **The Home Countries are the exception to that.** The **IHUK In-House Rules 2026-27** (Section 10, Rule 76) have the linesperson replace the centre for every violation except on a draw following an icing, and those rules are the *"overriding interpretation of how the IIHF Rule Book is to be applied domestically."* ⚠️ **The Elite League runs the IIHF rule instead, so an early centre there still gets the re-drop** — the In-House Rules never name that league, and its own Casebook writes no Rule 76 and makes the 2026-27 IIHF Rulebook the ruling for everything it does not mention. The violations (Rule 76.7):

- **Moving prematurely** before the drop. ⚠️ **The rule names the centre, not everybody.** NHL Rule 76.7 makes it a violation *"[i]f a center should move prematurely prior to the face-off, or if the Referee or Linesperson shall have dropped the puck unfairly"*, and the remedy is that *"it must be conducted again"* — ⚠️ **the one violation on this list for which the NHL and the IIHF remove nobody.** 🇬🇧 **The Home Countries do remove the centre**, through the In-House Rules, whose linesperson *"shall immediately replace the centre for all violations"* bar post-icing draws. ⚠️ **The Elite League does not: an early centre there still gets the re-drop.** The IIHF prints the NHL sentence word for word in its 2026/27 edition; ⚠️ **the 2025/26 edition — the one the In-House Rules' Introduction names — adds a step:** *"A warning shall be issued to the offending team and the 'face-off' must be conducted again."* A winger who twitches is caught only by the **encroachment** clauses, where the movement puts a skate where it may not be — *"[a] player not taking the face-off may be moving, provided they are outside the circle and on-side"* (USA Hockey Casebook, Rule 613, Situation 7). The centre's blade must *"remain there until the puck is dropped"* (76.7(iv)).
- **Improper stick placement or positioning** — not getting the blade into the white, lifting it before the drop, or skates in the wrong place relative to the restraining lines. **Declining to put your stick down is no way round it in any book here.** Under the NHL and both IIHF editions a centre who will *"refrain from placing their stick on the ice"* is replaced (NHL 76.3(ii), IIHF 76.3(II)); USA Hockey 613(d) and CARHA 57(c) let the official replace a centre who will not take position when directed; and Hockey Canada 6.2(a) sets the position and 6.2(d) has the official eject the centre.
- **Encroachment by a teammate.** A player other than the centre putting a skate inside the circle, or across the hash marks, before the puck is dropped.
- **Physical contact with an opponent** before the drop — a violation whoever makes it. ⚠️ **For the two takers a minor can come with it in every book, and under USA Hockey, Hockey Canada and CARHA it must.** NHL Rule 76.7 (IIHF 76.7 the same in both editions) leaves it to the referee, who *"may, at his discretion impose a minor penalty or penalties"* on whoever caused the contact, and both books count it as a faceoff violation too. The **2026/27** IIHF edition made that half harder: the 2025/26 book had the centre *"warned by the Linesperson"*, the 2026/27 book has the *"Linesperson shall order the center of the offending team replaced."* **So under the current IIHF book, leaning on the other centre costs you the draw outright.** **The other three give no discretion:** USA Hockey 613(e) — *"[a] minor penalty for interference shall be assessed to any player facing-off who makes any physical contact"*; Hockey Canada 6.2(c) — *"the Referee will assess a Minor penalty or penalties"*; CARHA 57(b) — *"the Referee shall assess a Minor penalty"*, with no discretion clause. Wherever you play, leaning on the other centre can cost you two minutes as well as the dot. ⚠️ **Those penalty clauses reach the takers, not the players on the hash marks**, so a winger who leans on the man beside him does not get the faceoff minor — though where body contact itself is barred he can get one anyway: under CARHA in every game (Rule 49(a), a minor or a major), in USA Hockey's Competitive Contact categories (604(c) Note), and in Hockey Canada's U13-and-below, female and Member-approved divisions (7.3(a)). His contact is also a violation in its own right (Hockey Canada 6.2(a), CARHA 57(a)), and it is **your centre** who is ejected for it. [Winger](../positions/winger.md) sets that out for the player it lands on.
- **Lining up in an off-side position** for the draw (Rule 76.3(v)). ⚠️ **All five books agree on this one, and in every one but the IIHF's superseded 2025/26 edition, which warned, your centre is replaced.** NHL 76.3(v) and IIHF 76.3(V) name *"[a]ny player who lines up for the face-off in an off-side position"*; Hockey Canada 6.2(a) and CARHA 57(a) catch a non-taker who moves off-side and eject *"the offending team's player taking the face-off"*; USA Hockey 613(d) reaches it through position. Line up on-side, everywhere.

#### Being late to the dot — the one softening, and who writes it

```facts
Rule: Being late to the faceoff location, and therefore off-side for the draw, is warned once in the game under NHL and IIHF 76.7 — the warning going to the team and its coach — and the rule says the centre "is not ejected from the face-off"
Rule: Timing your arrival is not the same as being late — a centre who "attempts to arrive at the face-off spot just as the five seconds elapses in an attempt to gain an advantage" is removed and replaced under NHL and IIHF 2026/27 Rule 76.4, unless the draw follows an icing
Rule: Once your team has had its one lateness warning, being slow to the dot again is a bench minor for delay of game — "Slow Proceeding to Face-off Location" (NHL and IIHF Rule 76.7)
Rule: USA Hockey 613(d) and CARHA 57(c) write no warning at all — each lets the official order a player facing off who fails to take their proper position when directed "replaced" for that draw, first time, at discretion (CARHA writes "the proper position", USA Hockey "their")
Rule: Hockey Canada reaches the same place in two steps, which is a reading of two rules together and not one sentence — 6.5(a) names "late arrival" a face-off violation and 6.2(d) ejects the taker; 6.5(a)'s warning is Junior divisions after an icing only
Rule: Under the IHUK In-House Rules 2026-27 the two texts point opposite ways — IIHF 76.7 says a late centre is not ejected, the In-House Rules replace the centre for all violations bar post-icing draws — and nothing published resolves it; the Elite League has no such clash
Rule: The carve-out survived the IIHF edition change and changed currency — the 2025/26 book said the late centre "will not receive a warning", the 2026/27 book says the centre "is not ejected from the 'face-off'" (IIHF Rule 76.7, both editions)
Action: Be at the dot on time — the safe play is the same under every book, and it is free
```

**One softening worth knowing, and it belongs to two books rather than to all five:** under the NHL and the IIHF, players who are simply **late to the faceoff location**, and therefore off-side for the draw, get **one warning per game** — given to the offending team and to its coach — and Rule 76.7 is explicit that "in this situation, the offending team's center is not ejected from the face-off." Any subsequent instance is a bench minor, announced as "Delay of Game – Slow Proceeding to Face-off Location." So being slow off the bench is not the same offence as being early on the drop: the first one costs you a warning, the second costs you your centre. ⚠️ **USA Hockey and CARHA write no warning into this at all, and they write it in near-identical sentences.** **USA Hockey Rule 613(d)** provides that where a player facing off *"fails to take their proper position immediately when directed by the Official, the Official may order them replaced for that face-off by any teammate then on the ice"*; **CARHA Rule 57(c)** says the same of the Referee — where a player facing off *"fails to take the proper position immediately when directed by the Referee, the official may order them replaced at that face-off by any player on the ice."* First time, no warning, at the official's discretion. **Under those two books, late is late**, and what it costs is the draw rather than nothing. ⚠️ **Hockey Canada arrives at the same place in two steps rather than one, so treat this as a reading of two rules together rather than a single sentence**: its Rule 6.5(a) names *"late arrival"* among face-off violations, and Rule 6.2(d) has the official eject the offending team's draw-taker for a face-off violation — with the warning in 6.5(a) reserved to Junior divisions on a draw following an icing. The safe play is the same under all three, and it is free: be at the dot on time. 🇬🇧 **⚠️ That is the NHL and IIHF answer; under the IHUK In-House Rules the two texts point opposite ways and nothing published settles which wins — so across the Home Countries do not plan on keeping the dot. The Elite League has no such clash, because those rules never name it. Get there on time either way.**

**That protection is for arriving late, not for timing your arrival** — ⚠️ **and the sentence that takes it away is in a different rule.** **NHL Rule 76.4** deals separately with the centre who plays the clock: *"[i]f the center attempts to arrive at the face-off spot just as the five seconds elapses in an attempt to gain an advantage to win the face-off, he is to be removed from the face-off and replaced, resulting in a face-off violation."* **The IIHF's 2026/27 Rule 76.4 prints the same sentence** in its own pronouns — its 2025/26 edition had the centre *"warned"* there instead, because that book had no removal to apply. **Both books then carve out exactly one draw** — the NHL in these words: *"[i]f the face-off is a result of an icing infraction and the center attempts to arrive at the face-off spot just as the five seconds elapse to gain an advantage to win the face-off, he shall not be removed from the face-off"*, and the IIHF's 2026/27 edition in the same sentence with *"the center"* where the NHL writes *"he"*. Under either, the centre is warned instead. **So the free attempt described under "The icing exception" below is the only one either book gives you.** Try the same thing on any other draw and you lose the dot — the once-a-game warning above does not cover it, because it is written for players who are simply late.

**Under the IIHF 2025/26 book this softening was worth more, not less.** That edition's Rule 76.7 carried the same once-a-game lateness warning, but phrased the carve-out for a book that had no ejections: *"In this situation, the offending team's center will not receive a warning for a 'face-off' violation."* Because a warning was the currency in that book — the thing that put you one twitch from a bench minor — that sentence meant being slow to the dot did **not** burn the warning you might still need on the same draw. Your one free violation stayed intact.

**The 2026/27 edition keeps the carve-out and rewrites it for a book that now does eject.** Its 76.7 reads: *"In this situation, the offending team's center is not ejected from the 'face-off'."* So the protection survives the edition change — being slow to the dot still does not cost you your centre — but what it protects you from is now a removal rather than a warning.

> 🇬🇧 **⚠️ In Britain that protection and the In-House Rules do not obviously agree, and this document cannot tell you which one wins.** IIHF Rule 76.7 says in terms that on a late arrival *"the offending team's center is not ejected from the 'face-off'"*, and the 2026/27 Situation Handbook repeats it at Situation 82.4. But the **IHUK In-House Rules 2026-27** say *"[w]ith the exception of face-offs following icings, the linesperson shall immediately replace the centre for all violations"* — and being late to the location puts you in an off-side position for the draw, which is a violation. On this one case the two texts point opposite ways: the IIHF hands you a once-a-game warning, and the In-House sentence carves out post-icing draws and nothing else. **The Elite League has no such clash**, because the In-House Rules never name it and its own Casebook leaves IIHF 76.7 ruling — there the once-a-game warning stands.
>
> **Nothing published resolves it.** The In-House Introduction cuts both ways in two consecutive sentences: those rules are the *"overriding interpretation of how the IIHF Rule Book is to be applied domestically"*, which points at the centre being replaced — but *"[u]nless otherwise stated, all rules will be enforced in line with the IIHF Rule Book"*, and the IIHF book does state otherwise for exactly this case. **One further hook, offered as a reading and not as a ruling:** the IIHF lateness warning is given by the **Referee**, where the In-House sentence instructs the **linesperson**, so the two may not be addressing the same act at all. ⚠️ **That is a reading, not a ruling — ask your league rather than relying on it.** In the meantime the safe play is the same under either answer, and it is free: be at the dot on time.


**One trap if you go and check this in your own book, and it is edition-specific.** The IIHF rulebook states the same lateness carve-out twice, in two different vocabularies. In the **2025/26** edition, Rule 76.7 used the warning wording quoted above while Rule 82.2(VII) used ejection wording — *"In this situation, the offending team's center is not ejected from the 'face-off'."* In that edition, that was the **only** appearance of the word "ejected" anywhere in the face-off material, and it was a sentence saying an ejection did *not* happen; it was not evidence that 2025/26 IIHF face-off ejections existed somewhere else, because the word appeared nowhere else in that book's Rule 76. **⚠️ Do not carry that reasoning into the 2026/27 book, where it is simply wrong.** There the word appears throughout Rule 76 and describes a real consequence: 76.6 ejects the centre for a teammate's encroachment, and 76.4 speaks of the replacement for *"a Player… ejected from the face-off."* The two rules now agree because both were rewritten, not because neither meant it.

### Getting low without putting your head in it

```facts
Rule: Helmet-to-helmet contact with the other centre before the drop is named in the rulebook as a faceoff violation (IIHF 76.7(III), both editions); the 2025/26 book gave it a first warning, the 2026/27 book deleted that warning
Rule: The IIHF Situation Handbook 2026/27 tells the linesperson to "immediately eject the center that is responsible for the helmet to helmet contact", and to eject both where they cannot tell who caused it — the 2025/26 handbook said warn (Situation 76.43)
Never: Lean in until your head is against the other centre's — heads "almost ear to ear" is IIHF Situation 76.42, and both editions of the handbook say it should not be allowed
Rule: A centre who will not move back when the linesperson breaks up an ear-to-ear stance is warned under the IIHF 2025/26 Situation Handbook and removed from the face-off under the 2026/27 edition (Situation 76.42)
Rule: Under the In-House Rules — the Home Countries bar the Elite League — do not plan on that first warning: their Introduction names the 2025/26 edition, but their own Rule 76 replaces the centre for every violation bar a post-icing draw, and helmet-to-helmet contact is one (Section 10, Rule 76)
Rule: Helmet-to-helmet contact at the dot is physical contact before the drop as well, so the referee's discretionary minor under NHL and IIHF 76.7 sits on top of the violation — and that minor is mandatory under USA Hockey 613(e), Hockey Canada 6.2(c) and CARHA 57(b)
Action: Get your chest over the puck, not your head over the dot — depth comes from the knees and hips, and ear-to-ear is the position the IIHF handbook tells linespersons to break up
```

**"Get low, chest over the puck" is the most repeated instruction in this document, and it is the one instruction here that can put your head somewhere the rulebook does not want it.** Two opposing centres both doing it well finish with their heads close together over the dot, and the IIHF Situation Handbook addresses that position twice in succession.

**Situation 76.42** takes the case where *"[b]oth centers lean in over the face-off dot such that their heads are side by side, almost ear to ear"* and asks whether it should be allowed. Both editions open identically — *"[t]his should not be allowed. The Linesperson should instruct both players to move back"* — and then divide on what follows a refusal: in the **2025/26** handbook *"one or both should be warned"*, and in the **2026/27** handbook *"one or both should be removed from the face-off."*

**Situation 76.43** is the same geometry after contact has actually happened: *"[b]oth centers lean in over the face-off creating helmet to helmet contact."* Again *"[t]his is not permitted"*, and again the consequence moved. The 2025/26 handbook had the linesperson *"immediately warn the center that is responsible for the helmet to helmet contact"* and, where they could not tell, *"warn both centers."* The 2026/27 handbook has them *"immediately eject the center that is responsible for the helmet to helmet contact"* and, failing that, *"eject both centers."*

**The rulebook singles the head out too, and that is the change that matters most.** Rule 76.7(III) covers *"[a]ny physical contact with an opponent prior to the puck being dropped"* and then names this case specifically. Under the **2025/26** edition a centre making helmet-to-helmet contact *"must receive a first 'face-off' violation warning"*, and where the linesperson could not tell who initiated it, *"both Players must receive a first 'face-off' violation warning."* The **2026/27** edition deletes the warning from both limbs: the player *"has committed a 'face-off' violation"*, and both players *"have committed a 'face-off' violation."*

**So the free first head bump is gone, and what replaces it depends on which book you are under.** The 2026/27 handbook says eject; the IHUK In-House Rules 2026-27 say replace the centre for all violations bar a post-icing draw; and the discretionary minor in the same rule sits on top either way — **mandatory rather than discretionary under USA Hockey 613(e), Hockey Canada 6.2(c) and CARHA 57(b)**, which write no exception for the head and catch any pre-drop contact at all.

**What to do about it is small, and it costs you nothing on the draw.** Depth comes from bending your knees and dropping your hips, not from pushing your head out over the dot; the low stance this document recommends is a loaded one, not a leaning one. And if the other centre leans into you, the linesperson's first instruction is to tell **both** of you to move back — so move when told, because the second sentence of both situations is about the centre who does not.

### The rule everybody gets wrong

```facts
Rule: A teammate moving into the circle ejects the centre, not the teammate (NHL Rule 76.6)
Rule: Under the IIHF 2025/26 book a teammate's encroachment cost the team its one warning for that draw, not the centre (IIHF 2025/26 Rule 76.6)
Rule: The IIHF 2026/27 book ejects the centre for a teammate's encroachment, and the IHUK In-House Rules 2026-27 replace the centre for every violation but a post-icing one, leaving the team one mistake from the bench minor of IIHF 76.7 (IIHF 2026/27 Rule 76.6; In-House Section 10, Rule 76)
Rule: Both skates stay outside the circle and outside the hash marks (NHL and IIHF Rule 76.7, in both IIHF editions)
Rule: Skate contact with the line is permitted — crossing it is what does it (NHL and IIHF Rule 76.7, in both IIHF editions)
Rule: Your stick may be inside the circle or the hash marks if it touches no opponent or their stick (NHL and IIHF Rule 76.7, in both IIHF editions)
Rule: USA Hockey allows no reaching stick — its Casebook answers "No" to a stick between the hash marks: "Both players must be completely behind their respective hash marks. This includes all sticks and all skates" (Rule 613, Situation 8)
Rule: Hockey Canada splits it — its glossary calls a stick "inside the face-off circle" acceptable but one "in the area between the hash marks" encroachment, and counts a skate "on the face-off circle" as encroachment too
Action: Tell your linemates — the NHL and IIHF let your stick reach inside the circle and the hash marks, never your skates; USA Hockey and Hockey Canada do not let the stick reach the hash marks
```

**Encroachment by a teammate gets the CENTRE ejected, not the teammate.** **Rule 76.6** supplies the consequence: if a player other than the taker *"moves into the face-off circle prior to the dropping of the puck, then the offending team's player taking the face-off shall be ejected from the face-off circle."* **Rule 76.7** lists what counts — **(i)** a non-taker entering the circle, **(ii)** anyone crossing into the area between the hash marks — and adds the escalation, quoted as printed: *"When a [sic] least two face-off violations have been committed by the same team during the same face-off, this team shall be penalized with a bench minor penalty to the offending team."* The missing word is the rulebook's own; it means *at* least.

So if your winger leans over the hash marks to time a jump, **you lose your best draw-taker for the most important draw of the game**, and the winger stays. It costs nothing to prevent: tell your linemates.

**In Britain this is the sentence that changed with the IIHF's 2026/27 edition,** ⚠️ **and it changed toward the NHL.** The **2025/26** book gave the same offence only a *"warning for a 'face-off' violation"*: your centre stayed, and your winger had spent the team's one free violation on that draw. **IIHF 2026/27 Rule 76.6 replaced that with the NHL's sentence**, and the **IHUK In-House Rules 2026-27** reach the same place directly, on every draw but one following an icing. **So a British winger creeping over the hash marks costs his team its centre, exactly as in the NHL**, and IIHF 76.7 still makes a second violation on that draw a bench minor. Under either IIHF edition you may reach with your stick, and under neither may you step.

**The standard is the same in the NHL and IIHF books, and it is the part people get wrong.** **Both skates** stay outside the circle and outside the hash marks; **skate contact with the line is permitted** — crossing it is the offence. Your **stick** may be inside the circle or between the hash marks, *"provided there is no physical contact with his opponent or his opponent's stick"* (NHL 76.7(i)–(ii); IIHF 76.7(I)–(II) in the same words in both editions). So under those two books you can reach; you cannot step.

**The reaching stick is an NHL and IIHF permission,** ⚠️ **and the other three books do not hand it to you.**

- **USA Hockey: no reach at all.** Rule 613(b) puts every other player *"behind the hash marks on the outer edge of the face-off circle"* — the fifteen-foot standoff beside it is common to all five books — and its Casebook settles the stick: Rule 613, Situation 8 answers *"**No.** Rule Reference 613(b). Both players must be completely behind their respective hash marks. **This includes all sticks and all skates.**"*
- **Hockey Canada: half a reach.** Its Glossary defines the encroachment Rule 6.2(a) punishes as a skate *"inside the face-off circle, on the face-off circle, or in the area between the hash marks. It is acceptable for the player's stick to be inside the face-off circle but not in the area between the hash marks."* A stick between the hash marks ejects your centre like any other encroachment.
- **And that same Hockey Canada sentence reverses the skate half,** ⚠️ **so "touching the line is fine" is an NHL and IIHF answer, not a universal one.** A skate *"on the face-off circle"* is encroachment there, so keep it off the paint. The entry also reaches players *"in motion while a face-off is taking place"*, **whereas USA Hockey expressly permits a moving player** *"provided they are outside the circle and on-side"* (Casebook Rule 613, Situation 7). Drifting toward the paint before the drop is a gamble on which book you are under.
- **CARHA: half a reach, the same as Hockey Canada's.** Rule 57(a) says only that players and their sticks be *"on side,"* but CARHA's Glossary defines encroachment in nearly Hockey Canada's words: a stick may be inside the circle but not between the hash marks, and a skate merely touching the circle line is already encroachment.

**The instruction that survives all five books is the short one: keep your skates and your stick behind your own hash mark, and your skates off the circle line.** That is legal everywhere, it needs no edition and no book named, and the reach it gives up is worth nothing set against what a violation costs your centre.

### The second violation

```facts
Rule: Two faceoff violations by the same team on the same draw is a bench minor under NHL and IIHF Rule 76.7
Rule: USA Hockey routes it to the offender instead — 613(d) makes a team's second violation "of any of the provisions of this subsection" on the same draw "a minor penalty for delay of game being assessed to the player committing the second violation", not a bench minor
Rule: Hockey Canada 6.2(e) makes a team's second face-off violation on the same draw a discretionary minor, "may be assessed" under Rule 10.1 (viii) — mandatory, "will be assessed", if the offender then contacts the linesperson before they can clear
Rule: CARHA 57(d) is discretionary and limb-scoped — a second violation "of any of the provisions of Section (a)" "may be assessed a Minor penalty"; Section (a) names a non-taker who "moves offside, makes physical contact with an opponent, or encroaches on the face-off circle"
Rule: So a winger's pre-drop contact under CARHA does feed 57(d). What sits outside that limb is the taker's own contact, because 57(b) — scoped to "no player facing-off" — already makes that a mandatory minor on the first offence
Rule: USA Hockey 613(d) is limb-scoped too, to "any of the provisions of this subsection" — 613(d)'s own. Both books scope it, but not to the same content: 613(d) reaches a non-taker's contact only through "fails to maintain their proper position"
Rule: ⚠️ **Hockey Canada's discretion vanishes if the second violation runs into the official** — a player who contacts the linesperson before they have "reasonable time to vacate the face-off location" "will be assessed a Minor penalty" (Hockey Canada 6.2(e))
Rule: Any of the four on-ice officials may identify a faceoff violation, not just the linesperson dropping the puck (NHL Rule 76.7)
Action: With a replacement in the dot keep it simple: lift or press their stick with yours, then play the puck. Never a hand on their stick or your stick on their hands, and never hook it or hold it down
```

**Two faceoff violations by the same team on the same draw is a bench minor** for "Delay of Game – Face-off Violation" (Rule 76.7). So the replacement taker has to be immaculate — your team is one twitch from a penalty — and should keep it to a clean tie-up. **Lift or press their stick with yours and then play the puck; never put a hand on their stick or your stick on their hands, and never hook it or hold it down**, because each of those can cost a minor of its own.

**The other three books do not write it that way** — ⚠️ **one moves it onto a different person, and two make it discretionary.** **USA Hockey Rule 613(d)** gives *"a minor penalty for delay of game being assessed to the player committing the second violation"* — the skater who did it sits, not whoever your bench nominates. **Hockey Canada Rule 6.2(e)** says the team *"may be assessed a Minor penalty by the Referee"*. **CARHA Rule 57(d)** is permissive too — *"may be assessed a Minor penalty for 'face-off violation' by the Referee"* — and covers only a second violation *"of any of the provisions of Section (a)"*. ⚠️ **Section (a) is wider than positioning:** it reaches a non-taker who *"moves offside, **makes physical contact with an opponent**, or encroaches on the face-off circle prior to the dropping of the puck"*, so a winger's pre-drop contact counts toward the second-violation minor. What sits outside is the **taker's** own contact, which CARHA 57(b) already makes a mandatory minor on the first offence. ⚠️ **USA Hockey scopes its second-violation minor the same way, and it is easy to miss** — to *"any of the provisions of this subsection"* — so it does not reach the pre-drop contact its 613(e) makes a mandatory minor on the first offence, and it reaches a non-taker's contact only through *"fails to maintain their proper position"*.

**But Hockey Canada's Rule 6.2(e) has a second paragraph,** ⚠️ **and it is not permissive at all.** Where the offending player *"subsequently contacts the Linesperson before the Linesperson has had reasonable time to vacate the face-off location"*, a minor *"will be assessed"* — the referee's discretion is gone. On a re-drop your eyes are on the puck and the linesperson is backing out of the space you are moving into, so **under Hockey Canada, after a first violation, let the official clear before you commit**, and take the re-drop straight.

**The two-violation bench minor is the rule that did all the work in Britain under the older IIHF edition, and it is unchanged in the newer one.** IIHF Rule 76.7, identical in both editions: *"When two"* face-off *"violations have been committed by the same team during the same 'face-off', this team shall be penalized with a bench minor penalty"*. The IIHF Situation Handbook has any skater the team designates serve it (Situations 17.3 and 17.4, citing Rules 17.1 and 76.6). What changed is what sits between the two violations. The **2025/26** handbook had the centre *"warned"*; the **2026/27** handbook has him removed — fifteen situations across five rules swap the warning for a removal, and a new Situation 81.18 asks and answers it: *"Is the center removed or just warned? ANSWER The offending center is removed."* That is the strongest evidence available here that the change is deliberate. **So under the 2026/27 book your first violation removes your centre *and* leaves the team one mistake from the bench minor.** After the first violation: nobody moves early, nobody leans, nobody reaches.

Note that any of the **four on-ice officials** may identify a faceoff violation, not just the linesperson dropping the puck. IIHF Rule 76.7 says the same in the same parenthesis.

### The icing exception — which books grant it, and to whom

```facts
Rule: Under the NHL, the PWHL and the IIHF 2026/27 book, the first faceoff violation after an icing does not remove the centre, on either team (NHL and IIHF 76.4, PWHL 78.4)
Rule: The centre is warned instead, and any subsequent violation by that team is a bench minor (NHL Rule 76.4)
Rule: The IIHF 2025/26 book had no post-icing carve-out because it needed none — every first violation on every draw was already a warning (IIHF 2025/26 Rules 76.6, 76.7)
Rule: The IIHF 2026/27 book adopted the NHL's post-icing carve-out in the NHL's own words, because it also adopted the ejection it exempts you from (IIHF 2026/27 Rule 76.4)
Rule: The In-House Rules have the carve-out too — their 2026-27 Rule 76 replaces the centre for every violation "with the exception of face-offs following icings" (Section 10, Rule 76); it is not a free draw, because IHUK does not amend IIHF 76.7 and a second violation is still a bench minor
Rule: Hockey Canada writes the carve-out but rations it by division — in Junior divisions only, any post-icing faceoff violation is a warning and a subsequent one a minor (Hockey Canada Rules 6.5(a), 6.5(b))
Rule: USA Hockey and CARHA write no post-icing carve-out at all — after an icing the centre comes off exactly as on any other draw (USA Hockey Rule 613, CARHA Rule 57)
Key: The warning is per team, so each side has its own free one
Action: After an icing, use your free attempt to cheat the timing, and expect their centre to use theirs — but find out which book, and under Hockey Canada which division, before you plan on one
```

**Under the NHL book, on a draw that follows an icing, the *first* faceoff violation does not get anybody's centre removed — and that applies to *both* teams, not just the team that iced.** Rule 76.4: *"When an icing infraction occurs, the first face-off violation by either team will not result in the center being removed. The center will be warned by the Linesperson that the team has committed their first face-off violation, and any subsequent violation by that team will result in a bench minor penalty for delay of game - face-off violation being assessed."*

Read the two limits carefully. It is the **first** violation only — not "any" violation — and the warning is per team, so each side has its own free one. Any subsequent violation by that team is a bench minor. But on a defensive-zone draw after your own icing, with a tired line that cannot change, you get one free attempt to cheat the timing, and so does the team that has just pinned you. Good centres use it and expect it.

> **This is the clearest place to see what the IIHF's 2026/27 edition did,** ⚠️ **because the rule and its reason arrived together.** The **2025/26** book had no post-icing carve-out, and did not need one: its default was already a warning on every draw, so a British reader had the NHL's free first violation on all fifty-six draws, not just the post-icing ones.
>
> **Both halves changed in the 2026/27 edition,** ⚠️ **so a British reader must now read this section as written.** Having adopted the NHL's ejection, the IIHF adopted the NHL's exemption: IIHF 2026/27 Rule 76.4 is the NHL sentence quoted above, word for word. The same edition adds that a centre who times his arrival at the dot after an icing *"shall not be removed from the face-off"* and is warned instead.
>
> **And the British answer no longer depends on the edition,** ⚠️ **because IHUK carved out the same draw itself** — face-offs following icings are the one exception to the In-House replacement. What happens instead comes from the IIHF book underneath, and both editions give a warning. **It is not a free pass:** IHUK does not amend Rule 76.7, so a **second** violation by your team on that draw is still a bench minor. The In-House sentence also does not repeat IIHF 76.4's "first violation only" limit, so treat one attempt as what you have.
>
> **So the loophole is now yours, and so is the rationing.** The free first violation comes on a post-icing draw, once per team, exactly as for an NHL centre; on every other draw the first violation costs you your centre. Expect the centre across from you to cheat the drop after an icing and to be careful everywhere else.

**The other three North American books split on this,** ⚠️ **and only one of them gives you the free attempt.** **Hockey Canada** writes it only for Junior: Rule 6.5(a) — *"[i]n Junior divisions, on a face-off following an icing, any face-off violation, including but not limited to late arrival, encroachment, or early movement, will result in a warning"*. Outside Junior Rule 6.2(a) takes your centre off after an icing as on any other draw, and 6.5(b) makes any subsequent violation by the same team on that stoppage a minor. **Neither USA Hockey nor CARHA writes a post-icing carve-out:** USA Hockey Rule 613 has none, and both USA Hockey volumes were searched for one without finding it; CARHA Rule 57 was read in full, and the word *"warn"* appears nowhere in it. **Find out which book — and under Hockey Canada, which division — you are in before you plan on a free attempt.**

### Closing your hand on the puck is a *penalty*, not a face-off violation — but the books differ on when it is one

```facts
Rule: Everyone on the ice may catch a puck out of the air, provided they "immediately place it or knock it down to the ice" — but not the two takers, not until a third player has touched the puck (NHL and IIHF Rules 67.2, 76.5)
Rule: Not the two takers, though — the note to NHL and IIHF Rule 67.2, printed again at Rule 76.5, bars them from playing the puck with a hand "until such time as a third player (from either team) has at least touched the puck"
Rule: The bar on the two faceoff takers is on playing the puck with the hand at all, not merely on batting it or closing on it (NHL and IIHF Rule 76.5)
Rule: Closing your hand on the puck is a minor under the NHL, IIHF and PWHL (67.2, 69.2), and under Hockey Canada 10.2(a)(iii) and CARHA 61(a) if it gains an advantage; USA Hockey 618(a) only stops play, outside the crease — but covering a loose puck with your hand is a minor there (614(a))
Rule: A taker who attempts to win the draw by batting the puck with their hand takes a minor for delay of game (NHL and IIHF Rule 67.2)
Rule: An open hand placed over the puck to conceal it is a minor under NHL and IIHF Rule 67.2(ii), but a penalty shot instead when the puck is inside your own goal crease — or an awarded goal if your net is empty (Rules 67.4, 67.5, 63.6)
Rule: USA Hockey escalates in the crease too — 618(a) and 614(b) make a hand holding the puck there a penalty shot or optional minor, and 614(b), an awarded goal on an empty net where the act "has prevented an obvious and imminent goal"
Rule: With your net empty, covering the puck in your own crease is an awarded goal under PWHL 69.5 and Hockey Canada 10.2(a)(vi) too; CARHA writes it both ways — 58(c) Note 1 flat, 36(b) conditioned — so play to the strict answer: any cover there is the goal
Rule: Deliberately falling on or gathering the puck into your body is a minor in every book, and a penalty shot where the puck is in your own crease — NHL and IIHF 63.6, PWHL 65.6, USA Hockey 614(b), CARHA 58(c), Hockey Canada 4.11(a)(v) — and under its 10.2(a)(v) where you are in the crease too
Never: Put your hands or your body on the puck in the scrum. Tie up their stick with yours — never a hand on it — and win the puck with your stick instead
Rule: An ordinary face-off violation cannot be challenged after a goal, but a draw won with the hand can be, as a missed game stoppage — IIHF Situations 38.20 (new in 2026/27) and 38.11 (identical in both 2025/26 editions, the book British hockey plays under)
Rule: Winning the draw with your skates is legal in the NHL but a face-off violation under the IIHF book British hockey plays at every level — it cost a warning in the 2025/26 edition and gets the centre replaced in the 2026/27 one (IIHF Rule 76.3(VI), both editions)
```

**Start with what you are allowed to do.** NHL and IIHF **Rule 67.2** open with a permission: *"A player shall be permitted to catch the puck out of the air but must immediately place it or knock it down to the ice."*

**At a draw that permission is switched off** — ⚠️ **for the two players taking it, and for nobody else on the ice.** NHL Rule 76.5, repeating the note to 67.2: *"The two players involved in the actual face-off (the centers) are not permitted to play the puck with their hand without incurring a penalty under this rule until such time as a third player (from either team) has at least touched the puck."* The IIHF says the same at its own 67.2 and 76.5. **The bar is on *playing* the puck with your hand at all**, not just batting it or closing on it. Once the face-off is complete, ordinary hand-pass rules (Rule 79) apply.

**Now the price, and it is a different category entirely.** Under NHL and IIHF Rule 67.2 closing your hand on the puck is a **minor penalty** — a **penalty shot** instead if the puck is inside your own goal crease, and an **awarded goal** if your net is empty (Rules 67.4, 67.5) — and a taker who "attempts to win the face-off by batting the puck with their hand" takes a minor for "delay of game – face-off violation". The **PWHL**'s Rule 69.2 is the NHL's, renumbered. **⚠️ USA Hockey alone stops play for a closed hand rather than penalising it** — Rule 618(a) calls for *"a last play face-off"* — and puts its minor on picking the puck up or covering a loose puck instead (614(a) Note: *"the use of hands to make the puck unplayable should be penalized promptly"*). **618(a) and 614(b) still reach a penalty shot or optional minor when the puck is in the crease**, and an awarded goal on an empty net where the act *"has prevented an obvious and imminent goal."* **Hockey Canada 10.2(a)(vi) awards that goal on the substitution alone, and CARHA writes it two ways** — its 58(c) Note 1 is flat, the 36(b) it points to is conditioned like USA Hockey's — so under CARHA play to 58(c): with your net empty, any cover in your own crease is the goal. **Hockey Canada 10.2(a)(iii) and CARHA 61(a) add an advantage test** — a minor for closing the hand and gaining an advantage, sparing a player who catches or stops the puck and immediately drops it — where the NHL, IIHF and PWHL penalise the act whether or not it gained you anything. **In all six books the same act keeps you clear: catch it and drop it straight down.**

The practical line: **in the scrum after the drop, your hands and your body stay off the puck.** For the two takers that is the rule itself; for everybody else it is advice. An *open* hand is not a loophole — **NHL Rule 67.2(ii)** gives a minor for placing a hand over the puck on the ice to conceal it or keep an opponent off it, and in your own crease *"a penalty shot shall be assessed (67.4) or a goal awarded (67.5)"*; the IIHF's 67.2(II) is the same in both editions. Nor is your body — **NHL and IIHF Rule 63.2(v)** give a minor to *"a player other than the goalkeeper who deliberately falls on or gathers the puck into his body"*, escalated to a penalty shot under Rule 63.6 when **the puck** is in the crease, wherever you are. **Hockey Canada adds a second test: its 10.2(a)(v) turns on where the infraction occurs, so under it, being in the crease yourself can cost the shot — and with your net empty, the goal (10.2(a)(vi)).** What **is** legal is tying up your opponent's stick **with your own** — lifting or pressing it, never hooking it, holding it down or putting a hand on it — and winning the puck with your **stick**. **Your skates are a separate problem:** winning the draw with them is legal in the NHL but a face-off violation under IIHF Rule 76.3(VI) in both editions, and under the 2026/27 edition it costs you your centre rather than a warning. See "Using your feet" above and the British box below.

**Under the IIHF's rules a hand at the draw can cost you the goal itself, on review, minutes after nobody on the ice noticed** — ⚠️ **and this is not a new rule you can wait out.** The Situation Handbook's **Situation 38.11** — word for word the same in both 2025/26 versions and the 2026/27 one — has an attacking centre glove the puck from a draw to a teammate who scores, undetected, and allows the challenge for a missed game stoppage (*"glove pass to a teammate in the attacking zone"*). 🇬🇧 **Version 1.1 of the 2025/26 handbook is the edition the In-House Rules' Introduction names**, so this reaches a British reader now. The 2026/27 handbook adds **Situation 38.20**: an ordinary face-off violation *"cannot be challenged as a missed game stoppage event"*, but a centre who wins the draw with his hand can be, as a hand pass. **So every ordinary violation dies with the drop; the hand does not** — Rule 38.2(II) makes a missed stoppage in the attacking zone leading to a goal reviewable, and Rule 38.9 lists Rule 79, Hand Pass, first, in both editions. Rule 79.2 does not stop play for a hand pass in your own defending zone, so it is the attacking-zone draw this reaches. ⚠️ **It does not sit obviously with Rule 76.5, and nothing held here settles that:** 76.5 sends the takers' hand play to Rule 67.2 until a third player touches the puck, while the handbook reaches for Rule 79 — and its own Foreword says *"in case of any contradictions, the valid IIHF Rulebook is always the basis"*, so treat it as how the review is run rather than as the rule. 🇬🇧 **In much of British hockey there is nobody to run it:** the IHUK In-House Rules 2026-27 provide that *"a Coach's Challenge is not permitted in EIH or SIHA competition"*, which names those two competitions and no others, so it does not settle a BUIHA fixture — **and the Elite League keeps the challenge**, its Casebook allowing one *"at any time throughout the game"* (Rule 38.5), so there a hand at the draw can still cost the goal. **None of it changes the instruction: your hands stay off the puck.**

### When your centre gets tossed — and the edition that changed where

```facts
Rule: A teammate on the ice replaces an ejected centre, never the bench — five books bar substitution until the draw is done unless a penalty is assessed: Hockey Canada 6.2(d) and CARHA 57(c) need it to leave a team short-handed, NHL and IIHF 76.8 and USA Hockey 204(a) only to change on-ice strength
Rule: The substitution exception is written two ways — Hockey Canada 6.2(d) and CARHA 57(c) allow the change only where a penalty is assessed "to make a team shorthanded", where NHL and IIHF 76.8 allow it whenever a penalty "affects the on-ice strength of either team", USA Hockey 204(a) likewise
Rule: The replacement centre must come into position quickly or risk the puck being dropped without them set — or being ejected themselves, which counts as a second faceoff violation on the same draw and costs the team a bench minor for delay of game (NHL Rule 76.4)
Rule: No time-out shall be granted following a faceoff violation (Rule 87.1; IIHF Rule 87.1 in the same words, unchanged across both IIHF editions)
Rule: Under the IIHF 2025/26 book a violation was a warning to the team, not an ejection, and the warned centre stayed for the re-drop (IIHF 2025/26 Rules 76.4, 76.6, 76.7)
Rule: The IIHF 2026/27 book removes the centre for a violation instead — ejected under 76.6, replaced under 76.3 and 76.4 — so a British reader should expect the centre to come off, as under NHL rules
Rule: And under the IHUK In-House Rules 2026-27 the IIHF edition no longer needs settling — they replace the centre for all violations bar those on post-icing draws. The Elite League still runs on the IIHF book, which its Casebook names as the 2026/27 edition
Rule: The IHUK In-House Rules govern "in England, Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland" and their Rule 76 amendment names no league, but the Elite League appears nowhere in them — an EIHL centre is removed under the 2026/27 IIHF book's own Rule 76 instead (EIHL Casebook 26-27)
Rule: IIHF counts winning the draw with your skates as a violation in both editions (IIHF Rule 76.3(VI))
Rule: USA Hockey keeps the ejection but makes the second violation a minor on the individual (Rule 613(d))
Rule: Under Hockey Canada the replacement may not be the offender — 6.2(d) has "the player who encroached" identified clearly as the cause and "not eligible to take the face-off", and permits no change of players unless a penalty makes a team shorthanded
Action: Agree the designated replacement in advance — under Hockey Canada it can never be the player who caused the violation — and make the plan a tie-up rather than a set play
```

**Under NHL rules**, when the violation is yours the centre goes and somebody else takes the draw:

- **Any teammate on the ice may step in** (Rule 76.4) — in practice, a winger.
- **They must get set quickly.** Rule 76.4: the replacement "must come into position quickly or risk having the puck dropped by the Linesperson without the player being set," or being ejected themselves, which is the second violation and therefore a bench minor.
- **Agree the backup in advance.** Every winger should know whether they are the designated replacement, and every line should have a default plan for the draw they take without their centre. That plan should almost always be a **tie-up**, not a set play.
- **No timeout can save you.** Rule 87.1: "No time-out shall be granted following a face-off violation." **IIHF Rule 87.1 and PWHL Rule 89.1 say it in the same words.** USA Hockey's time-out must be asked for before the line change ends (636(f)), and Hockey Canada's window closes when the official whistles to start the face-off procedure (6.17(e)) and nothing reopens it, so do not count on one there either. CARHA's rulebook writes no time-out.

**Under Hockey Canada the replacement comes off a shorter list** — ⚠️ **not the ejected centre, not the goaltender, and not the player who caused the violation, so a line whose designated backup is the winger who just encroached has no plan.** **Hockey Canada Rule 6.2(d):** *"The player who encroached must be identified clearly as the player causing the violation and that player is not eligible to take the face-off. No change of players will be permitted, even if the puck has been physically dropped, unless a penalty has been assessed to make a team shorthanded."* So the official **names** the offender and bars him, and you cannot solve it from the bench: the replacement comes from the skaters already out there, minus the named one. **Under that book a line needs two names, not one.** What it costs if the named player steps in anyway is not spelled out; on a reading rather than a ruling, it is a second violation of (d) on the same face-off, which Rule 6.2(e) prices as a delay-of-game minor. Ask your officials rather than finding out on a penalty kill.

**All five books bar a change from the bench for the re-drop** unless a penalty changes the numbers — where it makes a team short-handed under **CARHA 57(c)** and Hockey Canada, and where it affects on-ice strength under **NHL and IIHF Rule 76.8** (both IIHF editions) and **USA Hockey 204(a)**. **Only Hockey Canada bars the player who caused the violation:** the NHL, both IIHF editions and USA Hockey 613(d) allow *"any teammate then on the ice"*, and CARHA names no ineligible player. **So the two-name plan is Hockey Canada's requirement** — but it costs nothing to carry everywhere, because agreeing a second name also settles who is *not* taking the draw.

> **In Britain this section now matters.** An IIHF rule book is the base rule set at every level of UK hockey ([UK and England Rules](../foundation/uk_rules.md)), and until the summer of 2026 **which edition your league ran decided whether your centre could be thrown out of a draw.**
>
> **A British document now answers that.** The **IHUK In-House Rules 2026-27** (Revision 1, published 25 July 2026) add a *Section 10 – Game Flow* amendment to Rule 76: *"With the exception of face-offs following icings, the linesperson shall immediately replace the centre for all violations."* Those rules are the *"overriding interpretation of how the IIHF Rule Book is to be applied domestically"*, so **whichever IIHF edition sits underneath, a violation in a game played under them costs you your centre — on every draw except one that follows an icing.**
>
> **Two limits on that.** **Scope:** the In-House Rules apply *"in England, Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland (the Home Countries)"* and the Rule 76 amendment names no league — where the same document's fighting provision names the leagues it covers — but **the Elite League appears nowhere in them**. Its own Casebook writes no Rule 76 and makes the 2026-27 IIHF Rulebook the ruling for everything it does not mention, so an EIHL draw runs IIHF Rule 76 unamended. **Edition:** the 2026-27 In-House Rules name different IIHF editions on their cover page and in their Introduction, so they are no evidence of which IIHF book British hockey is on generally — but that does not reach the faceoff answer, because IHUK wrote the replacement into its own text. [Rules Primer](../foundation/rules_primer.md) sets the inconsistency out.
>
> **The 2025/26 IIHF edition had no faceoff ejection anywhere in Rule 76.** A violation was a **warning to the offending team**, and the same centre took the re-drop. **⚠️ The 2026/27 edition, published in June 2026, replaced every one of those warnings with a removal**, at 76.4, 76.6 and 76.7: the offending draw-taker is *"ejected from the face-off circle"* and *"replaced for that face-off by any teammate then on the ice."* **So under the current book the NHL paragraphs above are yours.** Two violations by the same team on the same draw is a bench minor under both editions and under the NHL (IIHF Rule 76.7).
>
> **That reverses how you play.** Under the 2025/26 book your specialist took every draw he lined up for; **under the 2026/27 book you need the back-up plan the NHL section describes** — a designated replacement and a **tie-up**, not a set play. ⚠️ **If you learned "my specialist always takes the draw" under the old book, that is the habit to unlearn** — and in Britain the In-House Rules now say so directly. What is still worth asking about is **enforcement**: a coach or an official may still describe the old procedure from memory.
>
> **The IIHF also makes winning the draw with your skates a violation, in both editions.** IIHF Rule 76.3(VI): a violation occurs where **"one of the centers wins the face-off by using the skates"**. Under NHL rules kicking the puck back is legal; under either IIHF book it is not. **What moved is the price:** the 2025/26 book had the Linesperson *"assess a warning for a 'face-off' violation prior to dropping the puck"*; the 2026/27 book has the Linesperson *"have the offending center(s) replaced immediately prior to dropping the puck."* The next violation by anyone in your five is still the bench minor either way. **Across the Home Countries** the In-House Rules leave 76.3 alone and reach "all violations", so a skate win costs you your centre on any draw not following an icing. **The Elite League gets there through the 2026/27 IIHF book's own 76.3(VI)** — bar, on our reading of IIHF 76.4, the first violation on a draw after an icing, which it makes a warning. **Take "kick it back" out of your repertoire** — it is in the technique list above only because this document is written to NHL rules.
>
> **USA Hockey keeps the ejection but changes the escalation** — not your flag if you play in Britain. Rule 613(d) ejects your centre when a teammate *"fails to maintain their proper position"*, as the NHL does, but the second violation is *"a minor penalty for delay of game … to the player committing the second violation"* — a minor on an individual rather than the NHL's bench minor on the team. Same two minutes, different player in the box.
>
> Many rec, beer and youth leagues enforce faceoff violations loosely or not at all, and some simply re-drop with nobody removed. **That *was* the 2025/26 IIHF rule; under the 2026/27 book your centre is out of the circle for the re-drop**, so a re-drop with him still in the dot is the officials being lenient — **with one exception: after an icing, in Britain and under the NHL and the 2026/27 IIHF book alike, leaving the centre in the dot is exactly right.** How strictly the rest is enforced at your level is a matter for your officials. Ask.

## Special Teams Faceoffs

Special-teams draws are about 10 per game (9.6 in 2024-25, 10.1 in 2025-26, from NHL official data) — a small share of the total, and a hugely disproportionate share of the ones that matter. See [Special Teams](special_teams.md) for the formations themselves.

### Power-play draws

```facts
Rule: The team awarded the power play chooses which end-zone dot the draw starts at — NHL and IIHF Rule 76.2; USA Hockey gives no such choice (Rules 612(b), 624(a))
Rule: USA Hockey gives no dot choice here or after an icing (Rules 612(b), 624(a)); the IIHF follows the NHL
Action: Where you have the choice, pick your centre's strong side or the side your one-timer threat shoots from
Key: The entry you avoid is worth more than the draw you win
Priority: Win the draw, or make sure you do not lose it cleanly — a tie-up that keeps the puck in the zone is fine
Never: Fire a fancy set play that ends in a clearance
```

**The team awarded the power play chooses which end-zone dot the draw starts at** (Rule 76.2). Use it: pick your centre's strong side, or the side your one-timer threat shoots from. A surprising number of teams don't think about this at all — coaching observation, not a count.

> **USA Hockey gives you no such choice** — ⚠️ **neither here nor after an icing.** Rule 612(b) sends a penalty draw simply to *"a defending zone face-off spot of the offending team"*, and Rule 624(a) sends an icing draw to *"an end zone face-off spot in the defending zone of the offending team"*. Neither rule offers a selection, and the phrase "choice of" appears nowhere in the 2025-29 book in this context. **The IIHF does follow the NHL and give you the choice, so this right is yours everywhere in Britain** — IIHF Rule 76.2: *"The team awarded the 'Power-Play' will have the choice of which end zone spot the 'face-off' will take place at to start the 'Power-Play'."* So if you play North American rec or youth hockey, everything below about picking your dot is unavailable to you — the official picks it, and your preparation goes into being ready for either side rather than into choosing.

League-wide, power-play units win about **54% of their draws** (53.6% in 2024-25, 54.4% in 2025-26, computed from NHL official data), which reflects both better personnel and the fact that the kill often has a winger in the dot.

**The entry you avoid is worth more than the draw you win.** This is the real argument for offensive-zone power-play draws, and it isn't about the set play at all. If you win the draw, you keep the zone. If you lose it and the puck gets cleared, you have to break out, regroup, and re-enter against a **set** penalty kill standing on its blue line, with four killers already in their structure. Zone-entry research is consistent that controlled entries generate roughly **twice** the shot volume of dump-ins at even strength — [Zone Entries](zone_entries.md) covers those figures, the strength state each was measured at and where each came from, including the separate and narrower power-play comparison — and a power play forced to re-enter against a set kill mostly gets dump-ins. A won draw is worth perhaps one extra possession's shot volume; a lost draw that costs you the zone can cost you a third of your two minutes.

So on the power play: **win the draw, or make sure you don't lose it cleanly.** A tie-up that keeps the puck in the zone is a fine power-play faceoff outcome. Firing a fancy set play that ends in a clearance is not.

### Penalty-kill draws

```facts
Mindset: You are not trying to create — you are trying to end the possession
Risk: A defensive-zone draw shorthanded is where the disadvantages pile up — a man down, in your own end, against a set unit with a rehearsed play
Key: A tie-up is a win here too — anything but their point man shooting into a screen is acceptable
Action: Put your best draw man out even if he is not a natural killer
```

Everything is inverted. You are not trying to create; you are trying to end the possession.

- **A defensive-zone draw shorthanded is where the disadvantages pile up.** You are one man down, in your own end, against a set unit with a rehearsed play and a shooter with his feet planted.
- **Winning it clean is enormous**, because of the icing exemption — which carries enough rule with it to have its own section below.
- **A tie-up is a win here too.** Any outcome that isn't "their point man shoots into a screen" is acceptable.
- **Put your best draw man out even if he isn't a natural killer.** The draw is worth more than the twenty seconds of coverage.

#### The icing exemption at a shorthanded draw — and the one that has nothing to do with being shorthanded

```facts
Rule: Five of the six books let you ice freely below your opponent's on-ice count, USA Hockey only at 15-Only and Girls 16U up, High School and Adult; the NHL and PWHL take it away after a major expires with no substitute in the box (NHL and IIHF 81.6, PWHL 83.6, HC 6.7(b), CARHA 65(d), USAH 624(b)(1))
Rule: USA Hockey grants the shorthanded icing exemption only at Youth 15-Only and above, Girls 16U and above, High School and Adult (Rule 624(b)(1)); below those levels a won shorthanded draw still has to be cleared legally
Rule: The NHL's major-penalty icing exception bites at the expiry, not during the major — where the penalised player was ejected or hurt, Rule 20.3 wants a substitute seated before the major expires, immediately if minors are stacked on it
Rule: Missing the NHL's substitute deadline costs twice — a bench minor for illegal substitution under Rule 68.2, and no clear until the next stoppage (81.6, 20.3)
Rule: IIHF Rule 81.6 carries neither carve-out — no age restriction and no major-penalty exception — but it runs on the on-ice count: a puck released before your penalty expires is never icing, and an expiry ends the exemption only where it leaves you level, so on a 5-on-3 you are 4-on-5 and exempt
Rule: An empty penalty box is priced under the IIHF too — Rule 20.3 wants the substitute seated immediately and Rule 68.2 charges a bench minor; what a British team does not also lose is the icing exemption
Rule: CARHA grants the shorthanded exemption too and rations it no further — Rule 65(d), the count taken "at the instant the puck is shot", with no age classification and no major-penalty exception anywhere in Rule 65
Rule: A puck sent the length of the ice directly off the draw is not icing in any book and at any strength — NHL and IIHF 81.5, PWHL 83.5, Hockey Canada 6.7(c)(iv), USA Hockey 624(b)(3), CARHA 65(e); five say "directly", USA Hockey "as a result of" the taker — treat any other touch as ending it
```

**Winning it clean is enormous — where the shorthanded icing exemption applies.** You may **ice the puck freely while shorthanded** (Rule 81.6), so a won draw while you are below their on-ice numbers is a penalty-free clear and a chance to change tired killers. How much time it burns depends on how fast they retrieve; no published figure puts a number on it, so don't trust the "fifteen seconds" you sometimes hear. **Two carve-outs**, both owned by [Special Teams](special_teams.md). USA Hockey gives the exemption only at Youth 15-Only and above, Girls 16U and above, High School and Adult (Rule 624(b)(1)); below those levels the draw still has to be cleared legally. And the NHL withdraws it in one narrow case, as PWHL Rule 83.6 does word for word, which bites *after* a major has ended rather than during it: where the player serving a major **has been ejected or is injured**, **NHL Rule 20.3** gives his team until *"a stoppage of play prior to the expiration of the major penalty"* to seat a substitute — ⚠️ **or *"immediately"*, if he is also serving minors alongside the major** — and if they fail, from the expiry until the next stoppage they are short-handed but *"not permitted to ice the puck"*. ⚠️ **And Rule 68.2 adds a bench minor for the empty box** — *"calling for a bench minor penalty."*

**Neither carve-out is yours in Britain — the bench minor is.** ⚠️ **IIHF Rule 68.2 carries the NHL's sanction sentence word for word, and IIHF Rule 20.3 wants the substitute in the box *"immediately"*, so an empty box costs two minutes under both books and the clear under only one.** IIHF Rule 81.6 grants the exemption to *"a side below the numerical strength of the opposing team"* and says nothing about majors. ⚠️ **It does carry a timing condition, though, and that is the one that catches killers.** Its second paragraph, the same in both editions: when a penalty is about to expire, icing *"shall be determined at the instant the penalty expires"*, and *"[t]he action of the penalized Player remaining in the penalty box will not alter the ruling."* ⚠️ **That counts the returning player back in, so it ends the exemption only where that leaves you level.** **Kill a lone minor and it does: ice it after your clock has run out and it is icing, even with the player still in the box** — a defensive-zone draw you cannot change lines before. **On a 5-on-3 it does not: after the first expiry you are 4-on-5 and may still ice it.** **And a puck released before your penalty expires is fine** — *"the 'icing' infraction shall not apply"* — even if it expires while the puck is travelling.

**Hockey Canada takes the same head count with no level restriction, but at the shot:** Rule 6.7(b) — no icing if the team is *"below the numerical strength of the opposing team at the instant the puck is shot"*. Its **Note 1** says a team is no longer shorthanded *"once the penalty time expires"*, but ⚠️ **never quote that Note on its own, because read alone it sounds absolute**: **Interpretation 3** counts the player on the ice at that moment *"for the purpose of equal numbers (or not)"*. **USA Hockey** carries both moments in one sentence, 624(b)(1): short-handed *"when the puck is shot"*, the determination *"made at the time the penalty expires"*, and no icing if the puck was shot before expiry. ⚠️ **CARHA grants the exemption with neither carve-out:** Rule 65(d) takes the count *"at the instant the puck is shot"*; **Rule 65 was read in full** and writes no age classification and no major-penalty exception; its Note 2 carries the same box limb. **So the head count is common ground in every book, though not all take it at the same moment; what divides them is the two carve-outs**, and only USA Hockey withholds the exemption from part of its membership, by age. [Rules Primer](../foundation/rules_primer.md) sets it out book by book.

**And there is a second icing carve-out that belongs to this document,** ⚠️ **because it is about the draw itself — every one of the six books writes it, and none makes it conditional on being shorthanded.** A puck that goes the length of the ice **directly off the draw** is not icing. **NHL Rule 81.5**: *"If the puck shall go beyond the goal line in the opposite half of the ice **directly from either of the players while facing-off**, it shall not be considered a violation of this rule."* **IIHF Rule 81.5** (both editions) and **PWHL Rule 83.5** print the same sentence; **Hockey Canada Rule 6.7(c)(iv)**, **USA Hockey Rule 624(b)(3)** and **CARHA Rule 65(e)** say it in their own words. ⚠️ **Five of the six write *directly*; USA Hockey writes *"as a result of"* one of the takers instead.** **Play all six as though the strict word were in every one**: it is the puck your own stroke sends down the ice from the dot — not one you win to a defenceman who clears it, and not one that clips a stick. **The moment anybody else touches it, the ordinary rule applies.** ⚠️ **And it is not a shorthanded rule:** it holds at even strength, for both teams, in every book — the half a killer relying on Rule 81.6 will not think to look for.

### 4-on-4 and 3-on-3

```facts
Convention: 4-on-4 is two forwards and two defencemen, or three forwards and one, depending on the coach
Priority: At 4-on-4 keep both back players honest — a lost draw is a genuine odd-man threat
Key: 3-on-3 draws are close to the highest-leverage moment in the game, because a loss is often a chance within ten seconds
Rule: The five-second setup limit is not enforced in overtime (NHL and IIHF Rule 76.3) — use the time; USA Hockey 613(c) writes no such exception
```

- **4-on-4** — two forwards and two defencemen, or three forwards and one defenceman depending on the coach. The ice is enormous and a lost draw is a genuine odd-man threat, so the alignment is generally more conservative than at even strength: keep both back players honest.
- **3-on-3 overtime** — draws are close to the highest-leverage moment in the game, because a lost draw at 3-on-3 is often a scoring chance within ten seconds and the game ends the moment somebody scores. Note that in overtime **the five-second setup limit is not enforced** (Rule 76.3), so you have time to get this exactly right. Use it. **That relaxation is the NHL's and the IIHF's** — both write it into Rule 76.3 in the same words — **and it is not USA Hockey's**: Rule 613(c) sets the five-second limit with no exception for overtime at all, so under that book the whistle still starts a clock.

## Situational Faceoffs

### After an icing

```facts
Rule: Under the NHL, IIHF and PWHL books the team that iced cannot change lines (NHL and IIHF 81.4, 82.1; PWHL 83.4) or take a time-out (NHL and IIHF 87.1, PWHL 89.1), though an injured player can still be replaced
Rule: The freeze falls on the team that iced, so the home team keeps last change when the visitors ice it (NHL and IIHF Rule 82.1)
Rule: After an icing the IIHF imposes both the no-change rule and the time-out ban in the NHL's words; USA Hockey imposes neither, but bounds both by the same line change procedure — five seconds a side to change, and the time-out requested before that procedure ends (Rules 624, 204(a), 636(f))
Rule: Hockey Canada is a fourth answer — 6.7(d) applies the no-change rule in U18AAA and Junior, and in Senior at the Member's option; 6.7(d)(iii) lets either team take its time-out after an icing, though the team that iced may not substitute during it
Rule: CARHA writes no icing change bar and no time-out, but Rule 19(d) allows each team one change per stoppage, and a late change is sent back the first time and a bench minor after that (19(e), 19(g))
Rule: Under NHL and IIHF rules the attacking team picks the dot (Rule 81.2); USA Hockey gives no choice (Rule 624(a))
Rule: Under the NHL, the PWHL and the IIHF 2026/27 book the first faceoff violation after an icing ejects nobody's centre, on either team (NHL and IIHF 76.4, PWHL 78.4)
Rule: The free first faceoff violation after an icing is Hockey Canada's only in Junior divisions (Rule 6.5(a)), and neither USA Hockey (613) nor CARHA (57) writes one at all
Rule: The IIHF 2025/26 book had no post-icing carve-out and needed none, because its first violation on any draw was already a warning (IIHF 2025/26 Rules 76.6, 76.7)
Rule: The IIHF 2026/27 book adopted the NHL ejection and the NHL post-icing carve-out together, so in Britain the free first violation is now rationed to post-icing draws (IIHF 2026/27 Rule 76.4)
Action: Play fast — their disadvantage is fatigue, and it evaporates the moment they get to breathe
Action: When you iced it, get the puck off the glass and out of your zone at the first opportunity; nothing clever
```

The single most exploitable situation in the game, and it works in both directions.

**When they iced it:**
- **The team that iced the puck cannot change lines** (Rules 81.4 and 82.1), and **cannot take a timeout** (Rule 87.1). Whoever is on the ice for them, stays on the ice — though an injured player can still be replaced — and they are usually tired, because teams ice the puck when they are pinned. **This one travels to the IIHF book intact** — IIHF Rules 81.4 and 87.1 impose both restrictions, 87.1's icing sentence in the NHL's own words and 81.4 in the NHL's own words *plus* one added exception, for which see "With the goalie pulled" below — so it is available to you in British and European league hockey. **USA Hockey imposes neither** (Rule 624 restricts no substitution, Rule 204 gives the normal change windows, Rule 636(f) attaches no icing condition to time-outs), so under that book there is no edge here at all. ⚠️ **Neither permission is open-ended, though, and one procedure closes both.** Rule 204(a) gives the visiting team five seconds to change and then the home team five, after which *"no additional player substitutions shall be permitted until the face-off has been completed legally and play has resumed, except when a penalty or penalties are imposed that affect the on-ice strength of either or both teams"*, with a subsequent *"undue delay by either team in placing their lineup on the ice or changing players"* warned and then a minor for delay of game — and the time-out is bounded by that same window, Rule 636(f) requiring that *"[t]ime-outs must be requested during a stoppage of play prior to the conclusion of the line change procedure."* So a coach under that book genuinely may ice the puck and then take the time-out; what they may not do is ask late. The Glossary closes the procedure when the referee lowers their arm at the end of the home team's five seconds, and *"[a]t this time, no further player changes may be made until play has resumed or a penalty has been assessed prior to face-off."*

  **Hockey Canada is a fourth answer,** ⚠️ **and under most of that book there is no freeze to exploit at all.** Its Rule 6.7(d) applies the *"'no-change on icing' rule"* only *"[i]n categories of U18AAA, Junior, and at the option of the Member in Senior"* — so below those categories the team that iced changes on the ordinary line-change procedure exactly as you do, and the matchup edge this section describes does not exist for you. Being *late* still costs you there, as it does everywhere. Where it does apply, the exceptions are the NHL's three, and **the time-out survives where the NHL, the IIHF and the PWHL take it away**: 6.7(d)(iii) provides that *"[e]ither team is permitted to take their time-out … following an icing, but during that time out, the team in violation of icing will not be permitted to make player substitutions."* [Rules Primer](../foundation/rules_primer.md) owns the full comparison, including what an attempted change costs under that book.

  **CARHA is a fifth, and it has no freeze either.** Rule 65 says nothing about changing after an icing, so the team that iced changes like anyone else, and the book writes no time-out — but Rule 19(d) allows each team *"only one change of player(s)"* at a stoppage, and under 19(g) the referee gives ten seconds and then raises an arm. A change attempted after that is sent back the first time, and any later one in the game is a bench minor under 19(e). **So under CARHA there is no matchup edge to chase; just change once, and promptly.**
- **You can change; they cannot.** The team that iced is frozen — Rule 82.1: *"A team that is in violation of… Rule 81 – Icing shall not be permitted to make any player substitutions prior to the ensuing face-off."* So you get to answer whoever they are stuck with, which is one of the largest matchup edges available to a coach, and it lasts one shift.

  **And the icing team's line-change freeze holds at home as well as on the road.** Rule 82.1's other clause is often quoted short, and quoted short it looks like a problem: the home team may make any desired substitution *"except in cases following an icing"* — whose icing? The full sentence is *"The home team may then make any desired substitution, except in cases following an icing, which does not result in the delay of the game."* That exception is the same bar the rule spells out later — *"A team that is in violation of Rule 63.8 – Delaying the Game or Rule 81 – Icing shall not be permitted to make any player substitutions prior to the ensuing face-off"* — and that Rule 81.4 restates for icing alone, putting *"this rule"* where 82.1 names two of them: it falls on **the team that iced the puck**, not on both benches. So if the visitors ice it, the home team changes normally and keeps last change; if the home team ices it, the home team is frozen and the visitors get the free matchup. **The IIHF Situation Handbook settles this directly rather than by inference**, at the situation it prints twice, as 81.6 and again as 82.1: after Team A ices the puck and coincidental fighting majors leave the strength unchanged, *"[t]he team that iced the puck (Team A) is only permitted to make a substitution for the penalized player. Team B may make a complete line change if they so choose"* — the same words in the 2025/26 v1.1 and 2026/27 handbooks. See [Special Teams](special_teams.md) for the same point from the special-teams side.
- **You choose the dot — under NHL and IIHF rules, so this one is yours in Britain.** Since 2019-20 the attacking team picks which of the two end-zone dots the draw happens at (Rule 81.2), and IIHF Rule 81.2 says the same in one line: *"Following an 'icing', the attacking team will have the choice of which end zone dot the 'face-off' will take place."* Pick your centre's strong side. **Under USA Hockey there is no choice** (Rule 624(a) sends it to a defending-zone spot, full stop).
- **Play fast.** The advantage is their fatigue, and it evaporates the moment the whistle-to-whistle stoppage lets them breathe. Win the draw and attack immediately.

**When you iced it:** you are tired, stuck, in your own end, and cannot rest. Get the puck off the glass and out of your zone at the first opportunity; nothing clever. Remember that **the first faceoff violation after an icing does not eject the centre** (Rule 76.4) — you have one free warning. So does the other team, so do not count on their centre being tossed either. **In Britain this now reads exactly as written**: the IIHF 2026/27 book carries the same post-icing carve-out in the NHL's words, so the free violation is yours on this draw and rationed to it. Under the older 2025/26 edition you had it on every draw instead, because that book had no ejection to make an exception from. ⚠️ **Do not plan on that free violation in a North American game without checking first:** Hockey Canada gives it only in Junior divisions (Rule 6.5(a)), and **neither USA Hockey nor CARHA gives it at all.** See "The icing exception" above.

### In the last minute

```facts
Key: A defensive-zone draw protecting a one-goal lead late is worth more than the previous fifty draws combined
Action: With a lead, accept the tie-up — a scramble that burns time beats a 50-50 clean-win attempt; or put your best taker out with a plan for the win and the loss
Priority: Get the alignment right before you get the play right
Rule: The five-second setup limit is not enforced in the final two minutes of regulation (NHL and IIHF Rule 76.3); USA Hockey 613(c) writes no such exception
Read: Expect more re-drops and more violations called
```

- **Everything is about the location.** A defensive-zone draw protecting a one-goal lead with 40 seconds left is worth more than the previous 50 draws combined, because there is no time to recover from losing it.
- **This is where you accept the tie-up.** With a lead, a scramble that burns time is a better outcome than a 50-50 attempt at a clean win. The other common approach is to put your best faceoff man out and have a clear plan for the loss as well as the win — many coaches do both; ask your bench which yours stresses.
- **Get the alignment right before you get the play right.** Late-game draws are lost to confusion more often than to the other centre.
- **The five-second setup limit is not enforced** in the final two minutes of regulation (Rule 76.3), so both centres will take their time and the linesperson will manage the alignment more carefully. Expect more re-drops and more violations called. **Check your book before you count on that time**: NHL and IIHF Rule 76.3 both suspend the limit in the last two minutes, and USA Hockey Rule 613(c) writes no such exception.

### With seconds left on the clock

```facts
Action: Needing a goal, win it straight back to a shooter whose feet are already set, with a forward at the net front for the screen and the rebound
Options: Some centres shoot straight at the net off the draw instead — it gives the goalie no time to set, at the cost of a weak shot
Action: Defending a draw with seconds left, a tie-up is a win — wingers into the shooting lanes, and a won puck goes off the glass and out of your zone
Key: There is no time for a second pass, so agree the play at the bench, not at the dot
```

**With a few seconds left and a goal needed — at the end of a period or of the game — the common play is the quickest shot you can set up.** Win it straight back to the strong-side defenceman or the inside winger, feet already set, with a forward at the net front for the screen and the rebound; there is no time for a second pass. **A realistic alternative is for the centre to shoot straight off the draw**, pulling or pushing the puck at the net in one motion — it gives the goalie no time to set, at the cost of a weak shot. Ask your bench which your team uses, and settle it at the bench rather than at the dot.

**Defending that draw, all you need is time.** A tie-up is a win, the wingers get into the shooting lanes rather than chasing the puck, and a won puck goes off the glass and out of your zone — every second of a scramble is a second off their last attack.

### After a timeout

```facts
Read: Expect the opponent to have a plan too, and expect it to differ from their default
Rule: No time-out is granted following a faceoff violation (NHL and IIHF Rule 87.1, PWHL Rule 89.1)
Rule: No time-out for the defensive team following an icing, after their goalie stops a shot from beyond the centre red line, or after a defender accidentally dislodges the net (NHL Rule 87.1)
Rule: A net accidentally displaced by a defending skater or goalkeeper costs the line change as well as the time-out — the offending team may not substitute before the draw (NHL and IIHF Rule 63.8)
Rule: USA Hockey attaches no icing condition to its one 60-second time-out, but requires it be requested during the stoppage and before the line change procedure concludes (Rule 636(f))
Rule: IIHF Rule 87.1 carries both the faceoff-violation and the post-icing time-out bans; USA Hockey Rule 636(f) writes neither, but its sixty-second time-out must be asked for before the line change procedure ends, so after a botched draw it is already too late
```

A timeout buys both teams the same thing: a rest and a chance to draw up the alignment. So expect the opponent to have a plan too, and expect it to be different from their default.

Note the restrictions (Rule 87.1): **no timeout after a faceoff violation**, and **no timeout for the defensive team following an icing** or after their goalie causes a stoppage from a shot beyond the centre red line. So a team cannot ice the puck and then buy itself a rest — the two rules work together, which is exactly why icing is punished the way it is. **IIHF Rule 87.1 carries both restrictions in the same words**, so this is the position in British and European league hockey too, and PWHL Rule 89.1 does the same. **USA Hockey Rule 636(f) carries neither**, and gives a sixty-second timeout rather than thirty, so under that book a team *can* ice the puck and then buy itself the rest. (A faceoff violation still leaves no time-out to take there, because the request window has shut before the puck drops.) Hockey Canada closes its window when the official whistles to start the face-off procedure (Rule 6.17(e)) and writes nothing that reopens it after a face-off violation, and CARHA's rulebook writes no time-out. ⚠️ **It has to ask promptly, though.** Rule 636(f) requires that *"[t]ime-outs must be requested during a stoppage of play prior to the conclusion of the line change procedure"*, and the book's Glossary ends that procedure when the referee lowers their arm after the home team's five seconds. There is no icing condition on the USA Hockey time-out; there is a clock on it.

### With the goalie pulled

```facts
Position: The extra attacker goes to the net front or the high slot, with both defencemen at the points
Priority: The centre must win it back cleanly — there is no safe outlet, and a lost draw lets the defence clear it and run down time you may not get back
Key: Defending against a pulled goalie, win the draw, then get it out or deep under control — shoot at the empty net only with a clean lane, because a miss from your own half is icing
Rule: The goalkeeper may not participate in a faceoff (Rule 76.1)
Rule: A team that pulled its goalie may still substitute after an icing to put the goalie back in (NHL and IIHF Rule 81.4)
Rule: Besides putting a pulled goalie back, the NHL's exceptions to the post-icing no-change rule are an injured player and a penalty that changes on-ice strength (NHL Rule 81.4)
Rule: IIHF 81.4 adds a fourth, "a Player with a broken skate", which IIHF 82.1 does not repeat and no Situation names — treat it as one an official may allow, not one to count on: a change the referee refuses costs a warning your team may already have spent, then a bench minor (IIHF 81.4, 82.1, 82.2(V))
```

- **Six skaters changes the alignment entirely.** The extra attacker usually goes to the net front or the high slot, and both defencemen are at the points. The centre is under maximum pressure to win it back cleanly, because there is no safe outlet, and a lost draw lets the defence clear it and run down time you may not get back.
- **Defending against a pulled goalie:** win the draw, then get the puck out or deep and under control. Shoot at the empty net only when the lane is clean — six against six is not shorthanded, so a miss fired from your own half the length of the ice is icing and brings the draw straight back to your end. Win it cleanly and get it out, and you are most of the way to closing the game; it is a draw worth practising.
- **The goalkeeper may not participate in a faceoff** (Rule 76.1) — a non-issue when they're on the bench, but worth knowing.
- **A team that pulled its goalie may still substitute after an icing** (Rule 81.4) — specifically to put the goalie back in. That is one of **three** exceptions to the no-change rule; the other two are replacing an injured player, and any penalty that changes the on-ice strength of either team.
- **The IIHF book writes a fourth exception, and then contradicts itself about it** — ⚠️ **so do not plan a change around it.** Its Rule 81.4 also permits a substitution for *"a Player with a broken skate"*; its Rule 82.1 restates the same ban and lists only the NHL's three. **Both sentences read exactly that way in the 2025/26 and the 2026/27 editions alike, neither says which governs, and the sport has not published an answer.** The IIHF's own Situation Handbook cites the two rules **jointly** rather than as rivals — at Situations 81.6, 81.8 and 82.1, in both editions — which points away from a real contradiction, **but no situation in either Handbook mentions a broken skate at all**, and Situation 81.7 answers a lengthy-delay question by shortening the list further still, to the penalty case alone. So treat the broken skate as a change an official may allow rather than one to count on, and **do not send a player over the boards on it without the official's say-so**. **Nothing British amends this**: neither edition of the IHUK In-House Rules touches Rule 81 or 82 substitution, and where the 2026-27 edition mentions substitution at all it defers — *"Penalties, player eligibility, and substitutions continue per the Playing Rules."*
- **And know what a refused change costs,** ⚠️ **because it is not a warning you can count on having.** Where an attempted change is not permitted, the Referee *“will send the Players who have attempted to change back to their Players' bench”* and *“then issue a warning to the offending team (through the Coach)”* — but **that warning is one per team per game, and it is not reserved for this**. IIHF Rule 82.2(V) warns that *“any subsequent violations during the rest of the game (including overtime), shall result in a bench minor penalty for ‘delaying the game’”* announced as a bench minor penalty for *“Delay of Game” – Improper Line Change*. So if your bench has already been warned for changing after the signal, for putting too many players out, or for any other personnel change, there is nothing left to spend and the next one is the bench minor. **Rule 82.2(VIII) does not save you late**: it disapplies only points (vi) and (vii) — the five-second line-up whistle and the slow-to-the-dot warning — in the last two minutes and in overtime, which is exactly when your goalie is out. And dawdling over it costs more again: **Rule 82.3** requires that *“Players must proceed directly to the location of the face-off to participate in the ensuing face-off”* and makes *“[a]ny attempts to delay the game by stalling or otherwise unnecessary actions by either team”* a bench minor — announced, in the NHL's printing of the same paragraph, as *“Delay of Game – Slow Proceeding to Face-off Location (or, Slow Proceeding to Players' Bench)”*, and with no warning first.
- **Under the NHL,** ⚠️ **a visiting team may get no warning at all.** NHL Rule 82.3 opens *“Any attempt by the visiting team to make a change after the Referee's signal shall result in the assessment of a bench minor penalty for delay of game”* — a flat first-offence bench minor — where that same book's Rule 82.2(v) warns *“either team”* first. **The NHL book says both things and does not say which governs**, and the IIHF's 82.3 has a different first paragraph again, under which a failure or tardiness to comply by either team *“will result first in a warning from the Referee and then a bench minor penalty”*. So do not rely on the warning in a North American game either.

### When the other centre keeps beating you

```facts
Action: Go to the tie-up — lift or press his stick with yours, then play the puck, so his clean win becomes a scramble your four are already leaning into
Action: Tell your wingers before you line up that it is a tie-up draw, so the two nearest bodies go on the drop
Options: Or change the taker — a second centre who shoots the other way, on his strong side, or a winger who reads this centre better; ask your bench which your team prefers
Read: Scout him from the bench — most centres have one preferred draw and one strong side
Never: Cheat back against a centre who cheats the drop — hold your timing and tie him up, because a premature move is a violation on whoever makes it
Never: Answer a lean with a lean — contact before the drop is a violation whoever makes it, and a minor, mandatory under USA Hockey, Hockey Canada and CARHA
```

Some nights one centre beats you clean every time. **The common answer is to stop trying to win it clean and go to the tie-up**: lift or press his stick with yours, over or under the blade, then play the puck — stick on stick only, never a hand on his stick or your stick on his hands. You are not winning the draw; you are making sure he does not, and turning his strength into a scramble your wingers are already leaning into. Tell them before you line up that it is a tie-up draw, so the two nearest bodies go on the drop rather than watching. **The realistic alternative is to change the taker** — a second centre on the line who shoots the other way and takes the dots on his strong side, or a winger who reads this centre better. Coaches differ on which they reach for first, so ask your bench what your team does — and use your bench time to scout him, because most centres have one preferred draw and one strong side.

**When he is cheating the drop — moving early, or leaning in — do not cheat back.** Hold your timing, watch the hand, and press his stick as the puck lands so his early jump wins nothing. A premature move is a violation on whoever makes it, so matching his gamble only puts the risk on you — and under the IHUK In-House Rules, on any draw but one after an icing, it costs the centre his place in the circle. Never answer a lean with a lean either: contact before the drop is a violation whoever makes it, and it brings the taker a minor, mandatory under USA Hockey, Hockey Canada and CARHA and at the referee's discretion under the NHL and the IIHF.

## How to Practise Faceoffs

Here is the case, plainly. **A faceoff comes nearer than anything else you do dozens of times a game to a fixed starting position, a fixed cue, a fixed distance, and a binary outcome.** Everything else in open play — a shot, a pass, a battle, a gap — happens from a different position every time. A penalty shot has a fixed start too, but you may take a handful in a season; it is the *frequency* that makes the draw the one worth drilling. So the draw is hockey's nearest thing to a closed skill in an open-skill sport — nearest, not pure, because the drop is deliberately unpredictable and what you are training is a reaction from a fixed position. That is the combination that repays repetition.

The 22-point spread between the best and worst NHL centres cited at the top of this document is not a talent gradient. Faceoff percentage is one of the few statistics in hockey where the people at the top are, mostly, the people who worked at it.

### What actually to do

```facts
Action: Reps in pairs with a real drop — correct height, unpredictable timing, because you are training reaction
Action: End every practice with ten minutes of draws, both circles, both grips
Priority: Practise the technique you are worst at — your weak-side draws are where the points are
Action: Rehearse alignments with all five skaters: run the win, run the loss, run the tie-up
Action: Have every winger take draws in practice, because every winger will take one in a game
Key: Teach your wingers the encroachment rule — thirty seconds, and it stops a winger's early step costing your centre his place in the draw in every book here, the IIHF's since its 2026/27 edition
Rule: Under the In-House Rules that lesson does not wait on the IIHF edition — they replace the centre for every violation bar one following an icing, so a winger's early step costs you the centre there too; the Elite League reaches it through IIHF 76.6 instead (In-House Section 10, Rule 76)
```

- **Reps, in pairs, with a real drop.** Not a coach handing the puck over — someone dropping it from the correct height with unpredictable timing. Prediction is the enemy; you are training reaction.
- **End every practice with draws.** Ten minutes at the end of the session, both circles, both grips. This is the standard advice from coaching sources and it works because it is repeatable and nobody has to plan it.
- **Practise the technique you're worst at**, rather than the strong-side backhand pull you already trust. Your weak-side draws are where the points are.
- **Rehearse alignments, not just the draw.** Have all five skaters on the ice. Run the win, run the loss, run the tie-up. Most faceoff goals against come from four players not knowing what to do after the puck moves, not from the centre losing it.
- **Practise as the replacement.** Every winger should take draws in practice, because every winger will take one in a game after their centre is tossed — and some of you will be moved to centre permanently, which [Switching Positions](../positions/switching_positions.md) covers.
- **Off-ice: grip and hand speed.** Coaching sources recommend wrist and grip strengthening and hand-eye work (dribbling a golf ball with a stick is the classic). The premise those recommendations rest on is that a draw is decided in a fraction of a second and is a hand-speed event before it is a strength event — **coaching craft; there is no published measurement of faceoff reaction times, so do not quote a figure for it.** See [Conditioning and Recovery](../off-the-ice/conditioning_and_recovery.md).
- **Scout from the bench.** Watch the opposing centres take draws while you're resting. This is free, and almost nobody does it.
- **Teach your wingers the encroachment rule.** Thirty seconds, and it is what stops a winger's early step costing your centre his place in the circle — under NHL Rule 76.6, under IIHF Rule 76.6 as the 2026/27 edition now writes it, and in Britain under the IHUK In-House Rules 2026-27, which replace the centre for every violation bar one following an icing. **Only under the superseded IIHF 2025/26 edition did it cost the team its one free violation instead**, and that is the version a British coach may still be teaching.

See [Practice and Development](../off-the-ice/practice_and_development.md) for how to structure deliberate practice generally.

## Common Mistakes

- **Chasing your faceoff percentage instead of the important draws.** Prepare the draws that decide something — your own end, the kill, a late lead. **Computed for this document** from the NHL's official team statistics for 2015-16 to 2025-26, a team's faceoff win percentage explains only about 6% of the variation in its **points percentage**, and the relationship went negative in 2016-17. That is a **team-level** figure, not a skater-level one.
- **Standing still after the draw.** Move on the drop. In your own end the wingers go to the points on a loss and to the wall **between the hash marks and the goal line** on a win, not thirty feet up it. Most faceoff goals against come from the four players off the dot, not from the centre losing the draw.
- **Waiting to see what happens on a tie-up.** Attack it: the two nearest bodies go — in your own end usually the inside winger and the boards-side defenceman — and one of them calls "Loose!" A tied-up puck belongs to whoever moves first.
- **Chasing the puck to the point after a lost defensive-zone draw.** Hold the net front and the shooting lane; in the low zone collapse assumed here, your winger has the point. **Under man-to-man the assignments differ**, so ask your bench whose man the point is.
- **Winning a defensive-zone draw into the middle of the ice.** Win it to the boards. On your weak side your backhand pull sends it into your slot, so learn the forehand pull, flip your hands, or put a centre who shoots the other way on that dot.
- **Rimming it halfway.** Rim it hard enough to reach your winger, or tight enough that your goalie can legally stop it behind the net. A hard rim is past the forecheckers before they move, because they were standing still at the whistle; one that dies in your corner sends a defenceman to fetch it under pressure.
- **Winning a neutral-zone draw and then dumping it in.** Carry it or pass it in — you had possession and a standing start. One author's tracking of the 2018-19 Swiss National League, not the NHL, found possessions with a dump-in produced far fewer shots and far less expected-goal value; read the direction, not the multiple.
- **Firing a fancy set play on a power-play draw.** Win it, or make sure you do not lose it cleanly — a tie-up that keeps the puck in the zone is fine. A clever play that ends in a clearance is not, because re-entering against a set kill costs more than the draw was worth.
- **Installing six set plays.** Run two or three per zone, called in one word that the whole line acknowledges, each with a failure branch: "if we lose it, X does Y." With no call, or a replacement in the dot, tie up in your own end and take the point shot in theirs.
- **Using the same technique every time.** Pull one, tie up the next, sweep the third. Three straight wins to the same defenceman get the fourth jumped, and even a weaker technique stops the other centre pre-loading against your best.
- **Watching your opponent instead of the linesperson's hand.** Eyes on the hand, and go on the drop. If you're reacting to him, you're second.
- **Cheating back against a centre who cheats the drop.** Hold your timing and tie him up as the puck lands. Moving early is a violation on whoever does it — under the IHUK In-House Rules it costs the centre his place on any draw but one after an icing — and a lean answered with a lean is pre-drop contact: a minor, mandatory under USA Hockey, Hockey Canada and CARHA.
- **Letting them breathe after they ice the puck.** Win the draw and attack at once, taking your choice of dot where your book gives one. Their line cannot change under the NHL, IIHF and PWHL books, and under Hockey Canada's in U18AAA and Junior (and Senior at the Member's option) — an edge that is gone once they rest.
- **Fetching a puck you won to the wall with your back to the checker.** ⚠️ **Never have your back to him, and never duck** — skates parallel to the boards, forearm and hip to the wall, head up and chin off your chest. **The forechecker was standing still at the whistle and arrives at full speed**, and a tucked chin straightens the neck, so a head-first hit into the boards or a post can injure at walking speed. ⚠️ **If you are that forechecker, never hit a player turned toward the wall, and know your game before you hit anyone:** in a non-checking classification and in any CARHA game the check is a penalty, and an injury or a reckless hit can bring a major and an ejection. Some British competitions ban it without saying what a breach costs — **ask your league, and until you know, do not hit anyone.**
- **Getting low by pushing your head out over the dot.** Get low from your knees and hips, chest over the puck. ⚠️ **There is no free head bump:** helmet-to-helmet contact before the drop is a face-off violation under the IIHF book, and the current IIHF handbook has the linesperson eject the centre responsible — both, if they cannot tell who caused it. In Britain it costs the centre the draw under either the In-House Rules or the Elite League's book. The referee can add a minor on top — must, under USA Hockey, Hockey Canada and CARHA.
- **Wingers creeping over the hash marks.** Keep skates and stick behind your own hash mark and skates off the circle line — legal in all five books. Creeping over ejects your **centre**, not you, and a second violation on the same draw can cost a penalty. The looser NHL permissions — a stick that may reach, a skate that may touch the line — are not every book's; see "The rule everybody gets wrong".
- **Putting only the toe of your blade in the white.** Put the whole blade flat in the white and leave it there until the puck lands — that satisfies all five books. ⚠️ **The toe alone is a violation under CARHA and under the IHUK In-House Rules across the Home Countries:** the In-House Rules replace your centre, except after an icing, and CARHA lets the official replace him. The NHL, the IIHF and the Elite League accept the toe. Under USA Hockey keep the whole blade clear of the red at an end-zone draw.
- **Trying something clever with the replacement taker.** Tie it up and take the re-drop straight: your centre is already out, and a second violation can cost two minutes. ⚠️ **Let the linesperson get clear before you commit** — under Hockey Canada a second violation that runs into the official before they have had reasonable time to vacate the dot is a minor with no discretion. Under Hockey Canada the player who encroached is also barred from the dot and nobody may come over the boards, so name two takers per line before the game.
- **Putting a hand or your body on the puck in the scrum.** Win it with your **stick**. If you took the draw, keep your hand off the puck until a third player has touched it. Falling on it is a minor, and so, usually, is covering it with your hand — ⚠️ **inside your own crease it can be a penalty shot, and with your net empty treat it as an awarded goal**; most books decide by where the puck is, Hockey Canada by where you are as well.
- **Not choosing your dot, where you have one.** Ask early, and take your centre's strong side or the side your one-timer shoots from. Under NHL and IIHF rules you choose after an icing and at the start of a power play; USA Hockey gives no choice; Hockey Canada gives it only in Junior and Senior, to a team that names the dot before the line change starts. Check your book and division.
- **Joining a post-whistle scrum in the offensive zone.** Once the whistle goes, stay outside the circle, whoever you are. Cross its outer edge and the draw moves to the neutral zone — you have thrown away the zone.
- **Going through the other centre instead of round them on a "lose it forward".** Skate *around* them — they are now between you and the puck, and going round is legal everywhere. Driving through a player who no longer has the puck is interference in any game, checking or not. In **women's hockey** stepping or gliding into a stationary player is at least a minor, and a major brings an automatic game misconduct (IIHF Rule 101.1; PWHL Rules 52.1 and 52.5).
- **Sending your skate at the man instead of the puck.** The kicking permission every book gives you is to kick the *puck*. ⚠️ **Kicking at an opponent sits at the top of every book's scale:** a match penalty under the NHL, the PWHL, USA Hockey, Hockey Canada and CARHA, and under the IIHF a major at the referee's discretion with an automatic game misconduct. USA Hockey also gives a major and a game misconduct for using your skate to push off an opponent. Under the IIHF book British hockey plays at every level, do not win the draw with a skate at all.
- **Practising only your strong side.** Spend the reps on your weak side and your worst technique — that is where the points are.

## Key Takeaways

1. **Every time you take a draw, watch the linesperson's hand, not the other centre — and own three or four draws, not one.** React to the drop and you can train it; react to him and you are always second. Mix the backhand pull, the forehand pull, the tie-up and the lift, so the fourth win to the same defenceman is not jumped.
2. **When a centre keeps beating you clean, or cheats the drop, tie him up and let your wingers win it.** Stick on stick only — never a hand on his stick, never your stick on his hands — and tell your wingers, so the two nearest go on the drop. Do not cheat back: moving early is a violation. If the tie-up still loses, ask your bench about changing the taker.
3. **Get low from the knees and hips, never by pushing your head over the dot, and win a scrum with your stick, never your hands or body.** ⚠️ **Neither is only bad form:** helmet contact before the drop can lose you the draw and bring a minor, mandatory under USA Hockey, Hockey Canada and CARHA; falling on the puck is a minor, covering it usually is, and in your own crease either can be a penalty shot — with your net empty, an awarded goal.
4. **If your skate goes near the puck at the dot, it goes at the puck; and when you push the puck past the other centre, go round him, not through him.** ⚠️ **A skate at the man can end your game in every book, and is a match penalty in five of the six.** Under the IIHF book British hockey plays, do not win the draw with a skate at all.
5. **On your weak side in your own end, do not pull to your backhand — it sends the puck into your slot.** Learn the forehand pull, flip your hands, or let a centre who shoots the other way take that dot; test which circle is your strong one with ten draws in each. **Then rim it hard enough to reach your winger, or tight enough for your goalie to legally stop it behind the net — never in between.**
6. **When you fetch a puck won to the boards, expect a hit at full speed — so never turn your back to the checker and never duck:** skates parallel to the boards, forearm and hip to the wall, head up, chin off your chest. ⚠️ **If you are the forechecker, never hit a player turned toward the wall, and do not hit at all until you know your game allows it** — in non-checking play and any CARHA game it is a penalty that can become an ejection.
7. **On an own-end draw, the four of you off the dot move on the drop, to fixed places.** In the common default, on a loss the wingers take the points and the slot defenceman holds the net front; on a win the wingers go to the wall **between the hash marks and the goal line**; on a tie-up the two nearest attack it. Under man-to-man each winger follows his man — ask your bench.
8. **On an offensive-zone draw, win it back to a shooter whose feet are already set, and know what you do if you lose it.** Commonly — ask your bench — the strong-side defenceman is loaded at the point and the inside winger drives the net for the screen and the rebound, staying off the goalie. Lose it cleanly and the wingers pressure at once while the defence hold the line. Attack a tie-up.
9. **Keep the playbook small enough that the whole line can run it.** Two or three plays per zone, called in one word and acknowledged by everyone, each with a failure branch. When the call is confused or a replacement is in the dot, tie up in your own end and take the point shot in theirs.
10. **When they ice the puck, win the draw and attack before they breathe.** Under the NHL, IIHF and PWHL books they can neither change nor take a time-out, so send out whoever you like; under other books, check. Where you can pick the dot, ask early and take your centre's strong side. **When you iced it, get the puck out at the first chance — nothing clever.**
11. **Late in a game, the score decides the draw.** Protecting a one-goal lead in your own end, settle for the tie-up, or put your best taker out with a plan for the win and the loss. Chasing a goal with seconds left, win it straight back to a set shooter with a body at the net. Against a pulled goalie, get it out or deep, and shoot at the empty net only with a clean lane — a miss from your own half is icing.
12. **Before every draw, tell your linemates: skates and sticks behind your own hash mark, skates off the circle line.** In all five books a teammate stepping into the circle early gets your **centre** thrown out, not himself. ⚠️ **And once the whistle blows, stay outside the circle** — a scrum that crosses its edge moves the draw to the neutral zone.
13. **If you take a draw for an ejected centre, tie it up and take it straight — and know before the game whether that will be you.** Your team is one violation from a penalty, so nobody moves early, leans or reaches, and let the official clear the dot before you commit. ⚠️ **Pre-drop contact with the other centre is never free:** the referee may add a minor, and under USA Hockey, Hockey Canada and CARHA must.
14. **Put the whole blade flat in the white and leave it there until the puck lands** — that satisfies every book, where the toe alone fails under CARHA and the IHUK In-House Rules. ⚠️ **Never refuse to put your stick down to wait the other centre out** — every book makes that a violation or lets the official replace you. **When you must place first, vary the blade angle you show.**
15. **On the power play, win the draw or at least do not lose it cleanly; on the kill, a tie-up is a win.** A fancy play that ends in a clearance costs a re-entry against a set kill. Killing, the common choice is your best draw man even if he is not a natural killer — ask your bench. ⚠️ **A clean shorthanded win in your own end can go straight down the ice, because a team short-handed by a live penalty may ice the puck — but two of the five books ration that exemption, so check yours.**
16. **Stop chasing your faceoff percentage, but do work at your draws.** Computed for this document, team faceoff percentage explains only about 6% of the variation in NHL team points percentage across 2015-16 to 2025-26, while the 109 centres with at least 500 draws in 2025-26 ran from 62.1% to 39.7%. The two are not comparable, but together they say: attend to the draws that matter, and practise the draw itself.
