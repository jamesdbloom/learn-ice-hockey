## Key focus

**Watch the linesperson's hand, not the other centre, and go on the drop.** Get low from the knees and hips, chest over the puck, bottom hand low, back foot loaded. React to them and you are always second.

**Own three or four draws, and mix them.** The backhand pull, the forehand pull, the tie-up and the lift. Win three straight to the same defenceman and the fourth gets jumped.

**Against a centre who beats you clean, tie them up and let your wingers win it.** Lift or press their stick with yours, then play the puck — stick on stick only, never a hand on their stick or your stick on their hands. Or change the taker; ask your bench.

**The four off the dot move on the drop, to places agreed before it.** In the common own-end default the wingers take the points on a loss and the wall between the hash marks and the goal line on a win, and the slot defenceman holds the net front. Ask your bench what yours is.

**Win a defensive-zone draw to the boards, never into the middle.** On your weak side the backhand pull sends it into your slot, so use the forehand pull, flip your hands, or a centre who shoots the other way. Then rim it hard enough to reach your winger, or tight enough for your goalie to stop it legally — never in between.

**Prepare the draws that change something, not your faceoff percentage.** Your own end, the kill, a late lead, the draw after an icing. A clean own-end loss lets a set opponent run a prepared play while your five stand still.

**Tell your linemates where to stand: skates and sticks behind your own hash mark, skates off the circle line.** That is legal in every book here. A teammate who creeps into the circle early gets your **centre** thrown out, not themselves, and a second violation can cost a penalty.

**Scout the centre opposite from the bench.** Most centres have one favourite draw and one strong side. A rolled-over bottom hand means a backhand pull; hands high mean a sweep or a tie-up; the loaded foot shows which way they will turn.

**Run two or three set plays per zone, each with a failure branch.** One-word call from the centre, acknowledged by everyone, and "if we lose it, X does Y" agreed before you line up. With no call, or a replacement in the dot: tie up in your own end, point shot in theirs.

**When they ice the puck, win the draw and attack at once.** Under the NHL, IIHF and PWHL books they can neither change nor take a time-out. Where your book lets you pick the dot, take your centre's strong side.

## Overview

A **faceoff**, or **draw**, starts and restarts play: an official drops the puck between the sticks of two opponents. There are about **56 in an average NHL game** — 56.6 per game in 2024-25 and 56.0 in 2025-26, computed from the NHL's official statistics. It is the one moment you know where the puck will be and when, so it is the one moment you can plan completely.

**Most draws matter less than people think, and a few matter a great deal.** Across eleven NHL seasons, a team's faceoff win percentage explains only around 6% of the variation in its share of standings points. So prepare the ten or fifteen draws a game that change something (a coaching estimate) — above all in your own end, where a clean loss lets a set opponent run a prepared play while your five face the wrong way.

**Winning the draw is a reaction, not a duel.** Watch the hand, go on the drop from a low, loaded stance, and keep several techniques so the other centre cannot pre-load against one. It repays practice: NHL centres with at least 500 draws in 2025-26 ranged from 62.1% down to 39.7%. That is a skater-level spread, not comparable with the team-level 6%.

**Then the four players off the dot decide what the draw was worth.** Most draws end in a scramble (a coaching estimate), won by whoever moves first. Their jobs are a coaching choice — the defaults here assume zone coverage, and under man-to-man each winger follows their man — so ask your bench. Either way, send a win to the boards in your own end, back to a set shooter in theirs, and never into your own slot.

**The cheapest improvement is telling your linemates where to stand.** If anyone but the two takers enters the circle before the drop, your **centre** is thrown out, and your team is one more mistake from a penalty. ⚠️ **Do not carry the looser NHL version into a North American rec or youth game** — a stick between the hash marks and a skate on the line are NHL, IIHF and PWHL permissions, not every book's.

## Why Faceoffs Matter — Honestly

There is a bad version of this argument and a good one. The bad version says faceoffs win games. They do not, and pretending otherwise makes everything else you say less credible.

### What the evidence actually shows

**In short:** Faceoffs matter far less than they look: a won draw buys a brief possession head start, not a scoring chance.

**Team faceoff percentage barely predicts winning.** Computed from the NHL's official team statistics for the eleven seasons 2015-16 to 2025-26 (344 team-seasons), the correlation between faceoff win percentage and **points percentage** (points earned divided by two per game played) is **r = +0.25** — about **6% of the variation**. It is unstable too: negative in 2016-17 (r = −0.24) and essentially zero in 2025-26 (r = +0.03).

**And teams barely differ.** Across all 32 NHL teams in 2025-26 (all situations, from the NHL's team-summary data), team faceoff percentage ran only from about **45.9% to 54.5%**, around a league mean of **50.0%** by definition — every draw is one team's win and the other's loss.

**Individual centres differ far more.** The team figure blends everyone a club sends to the dot, including wingers (eleven of the 120 skaters with 500+ draws in 2025-26), so it is pulled toward 50%. The centres' range — **62.1% down to 39.7%** across the 109 with 500 or more draws — is about two and a half times as wide. Team percentage describes a roster; skater percentage describes a player.

**It barely predicts possession either.** **Garret Hohl**, writing at Hockey Graphs, found an R² of about 0.20 between team faceoff percentage and Corsi percentage (shot-attempt share) across every 82-game NHL team-season from 2007-08 — real, but four fifths of the variation unexplained.

**The best faceoff man in the league is worth a handful of goals.** Hohl applied **Gabriel Desjardins'** per-zone goal values — *"using data originally brought up by Gabriel Desjardins"* — to the NHL's 100 most frequent draw-takers. The best (Boyd Gordon) came out at **+4.34 goals** and the worst (Ryan Nugent-Hopkins) at **−2.73**, a 7.07-goal spread; his table gives no time base, so read them as season-scale.

His chart puts the gap between top- and bottom-decile possession players at roughly **10–15 goals per 82 games** — *read off the figure, not stated in the text, so treat it as approximate.* His conclusion: faceoffs "matter, but they do not matter on average much more than any other of the many puck battles that occur throughout the game."

**The advantage decays in seconds.** The finding is **Craig Tabita's**, at hockeyprospectus.com: shot volumes after a draw even out about ten seconds later, wherever the draw was. It reaches this document at two removes, through **Alison Lukan's** 2017 summary in *The Athletic*, quoted by Hockey Graphs: *"after 10 seconds of play, regardless of where a face-off occurs … shot volume evens out to be more or less the same"*. A win buys about one possession's head start, not a scoring chance.

### So why bother?

```facts
Priority: Prepare the situational draws, not the league-average one
Risk: A defensive-zone draw lost cleanly puts a set opponent into the most dangerous ice while you are stationary
Action: Fix the free failure modes — a winger creeping over a line is an information problem, not a skill one
Key: A 22-point spread between the best and worst NHL centres in 2025-26 — one season — and coaches treat the draw as learnable
Key: The spread between NHL centres is computed here from NHL official statistics — the 109 who took at least 500 draws in 2025-26, running from 62.1% down to 39.7%, median 51.8%
Never: Chase your faceoff percentage. The draws worth preparing are the ten or fifteen a game that change something; that count is a coaching estimate, not a measurement
```

Because *average* is the wrong frame. The honest case for faceoffs is four narrower claims, and all four survive the evidence.

- **Situational draws are not average draws.** A shorthanded own-end draw with tired killers is not a neutral-zone draw at 3-0 in the first period. The specific ones described later are worth a great deal, and few enough that a team can prepare for all of them.
- **A lost defensive-zone draw is a shot against, quickly.** A clean loss puts a set opponent with a set play into the most dangerous ice while your five skaters stand still, facing the wrong way.
- **The failure modes are free to fix.** A centre ejected because a winger crept over a line is an information problem, not a skill one. See the violations section.
- **It is learnable, and the gap is enormous.** Computed from the NHL's official skater statistics: among the 109 centres with at least 500 draws in 2025-26 the median was **51.8%**, the tenth percentile 45.4%, the ninetieth 57.7%, and the range **62.1% down to 39.7%** — a **22-point spread** on one repeatable action in one season. Coaches read that as a skill that rewards work. It does not show the draw beats everything else you could practise — nobody has computed that spread for any other skill. ([Switching Positions](../positions/switching_positions.md) leans on this figure.)

**The practical version: don't chase your faceoff percentage. Chase the ten or fifteen draws a game where the outcome changes something, and stop giving away the free ones.** *("Ten or fifteen" is a coaching estimate, not a counted figure.)*

## The Nine Faceoff Locations

Every faceoff happens at one of **nine spots** — NHL and IIHF Rule 76.2 and USA Hockey Rule 612(b) all say so, and the NHL forbids a draw anywhere else.

### Where they are, and what's painted on the ice

```facts
Rule: Every faceoff happens at one of nine marked spots, and nowhere else — NHL and IIHF Rule 76.2, USA Hockey Rule 612(b)
Position: Wingers stand at the hash marks, and those marks are a hard boundary
Rule: The centre's restraining lines define where your feet may go (Rule 1.9)
Rule: The four neutral-zone dots have no circles and no hash marks — all players other than the centres stand "uniformly back" (NHL and IIHF Rule 76.7)
Key: Encroachment — a player other than the two centres moving in before the drop — is called loosely in the neutral zone and tightly in the end zones
```

| Spot | Count | Markings (NHL Rule 1.9) |
|---|---|---|
| **Centre ice** | 1 | A 12-inch blue spot, with a circle of 15-foot radius |
| **Neutral-zone dots** | 4 | 2-foot red spots, 5 feet outside each blue line, 44 feet apart. **No circles** |
| **End-zone dots** | 4 | 2-foot red spots with 15-foot-radius circles, 20 feet out from the goal line and 22 feet either side of the middle |

*([Rink Map](../foundation/rink_map.md) carries the same figures alongside the rest of the ice, with the IIHF metric equivalents.)*

Two markings inside the end-zone circles matter enormously and almost nobody can name them:

- **Hash marks** — red lines two inches wide and two feet long, parallel to the goal line. Rule 1.9 puts two "at the outer edge of **both sides** of each face-off circle", **5 feet 7 inches apart** — so **four per circle**. These are where the wingers stand, and they are a hard boundary (see violations).
- **The centre's restraining lines** — the little painted "L" shapes a foot outside the spot: two lines four feet long and eighteen inches apart parallel to the side boards, plus a line 2 feet 10 inches long parallel to the end boards. These define where the centre's feet may go.

**The four neutral-zone dots have no circles and no hash marks at all.** Rule 76.7 tells linespersons to use judgement and requires only that everyone stand "uniformly back". In practice encroachment — anyone but the two centres moving in before the drop — is called loosely in the neutral zone and tightly in the end zones.

### How often each zone comes up

Computed from the NHL's public team-faceoff data for the **2025-26 regular season** (**team-level, all situations**), a team's draws split roughly:

| Zone | Share of a team's draws | Draws per game |
|---|---|---|
| **Offensive zone** | **34.6%** | ~19–20 |
| **Defensive zone** | **34.6%** | ~19–20 |
| **Neutral zone** (including centre ice) | **30.8%** | ~17 |

The per-game counts are those percentages applied to the roughly **56 draws in an average NHL game**, rounded. Three things follow:

- **The offensive and defensive shares are identical, and they have to be.** Every offensive-zone draw for you is a defensive-zone draw for the other team, so league-wide the two are the same number seen from opposite ends.
- **End-zone draws are about 69% of all draws.** You take about twenty defensive-zone draws a game; they are not a rare event.
- **Offensive-zone draws are only about a third of the total**, which matters when you read "sheltered usage" or zone-start percentage. A coach loading a player with offensive-zone starts is redistributing within that third across a whole bench — one reason the measured effect on any one player's numbers is modest. See [How to Watch Hockey](../off-the-ice/how_to_watch_hockey.md).

### What sends the draw to which spot

**In short:** Learn which stoppage sends the draw to which dot, so you are already on your way when the whistle goes.

![](diagram:faceoff-where-the-draw-goes)

```facts
Action: Work out where the next draw will be and start skating there before the whistle finishes
Rule: Centre ice takes period starts, draws after a goal, and an erroneous icing call (Rules 76.2, 81.2)
Rule: After an icing or a penalty the draw is in the offending team's end (NHL and IIHF 81.2, 76.2); CARHA puts a penalty draw at the dot nearest the incident (39(a)). The dot choice is NHL, IIHF and PWHL; Hockey Canada Junior and Senior only (6.4(b)); USA Hockey and CARHA none
Rule: Hockey Canada 6.4(b) gives the choice of end-zone dot to the attacking (non-offending) team, in Junior and Senior divisions only and only after an icing or a time penalty, and that team loses it unless it tells the referee its dot "prior to the start of the line change procedure"
Rule: A stoppage between the end-zone dots and the end boards draws at the spot on the side it happened, "unless otherwise expressly provided by these rules" (NHL and IIHF Rule 76.2) — USA Hockey 612(b) takes the nearest spot, but only where it calls for a last play faceoff
Rule: A stoppage the attacking team caused in its attacking zone is a different draw under USA Hockey — 612(c) sends it to the nearest neutral-zone spot, and 612(g) moves any draw back toward the goal of the team that caused the stoppage
Rule: Offside on a carry-in draws at the neutral-zone dot outside that blue line (Rule 83.6)
Rule: Offside on a pass or shot sends the draw back to the zone the puck came from (Rule 83.6)
Rule: A post-whistle scrum that pulls players inside the circle's outer edge moves the draw to the neutral zone — the attacking team's point men and bench, and any non-offending player, under NHL and IIHF 76.2; any attacking player under USA Hockey 612(d), Hockey Canada 6.3(d)(vi)
Rule: A puck out of play draws at the nearest spot in the zone it was shot from (NHL 85.1); a puck shot into the players' bench draws no penalty, but the IIHF Handbook exempts only one shot straight in, not one clearing the glass into it (63.2(III) NOTE; Situation 63.24)
Never: Go offside by firing the puck over the blue line — carrying it over and being whistled costs you far less
```

**To centre ice:**
- The start of every period, and after every goal.
- A penalty assessed *after* a goal, or at the end or start of a period (Rule 76.2).
- A linesperson's **erroneous** icing call (Rule 81.2).
- A premature-substitution violation when play was stopped beyond the centre red line (Rule 76.2).

**To an end-zone dot in the offending team's own end:**
- **Icing.** Under the NHL, the IIHF and the PWHL **the attacking team chooses which of the two dots** (Rule 81.2; PWHL 83.2), and it will pick its centre's strong side. See "Situational Faceoffs".
- **A penalty.** One of the two dots in the penalised team's end (CARHA differs, below), and under the NHL, IIHF and PWHL **the team awarded the power play chooses which** (Rule 76.2; PWHL 78.2).
- **Intentional offside** — going offside on purpose to force a whistle (Rule 83.7).
- **A goalie freeze, or any stoppage low in the zone.** The draw is at the end-zone dot **on the side where the stoppage occurred** (Rule 76.2), so the side a goalie freezes it on picks the circle. Goalies know this. The rule holds *"unless otherwise expressly provided by these rules"* (IIHF 76.2 the same), so a stoppage another rule speaks for goes where that rule sends it.
- **A defender or goalie accidentally knocking their own net off.** NHL Rule 63.8 sends the draw to the offending team's end, **the attacking team chooses the dot**, and the offending team may not substitute. IIHF Rule 63.8 is the same rule, in both editions, and so is PWHL 65.8.
- **The goalie freezing the puck on the back of the net, or a defender shielding an attacker from playing it there** — Rule 85.2 sends these to the defending zone. ⚠️ **"Just a faceoff" is the NHL's, the IIHF's and the PWHL's price, not every book's** — see "What it costs" below.

**To a neutral-zone dot:**
- **Offside where the puck was carried over the line** — the dot outside that blue line (Rule 83.6).
- **A neutral-zone stoppage that is nobody's fault** — the nearest dot outside the blue line; a tie goes to the spot that favours the **home** team (Rule 76.2).
- **A goal illegally scored by hand, kick or high stick** (Rule 76.2).
- **A scrum after the whistle that pulls players inside the outer edge of the end-zone circle.** Under NHL and IIHF 76.2 that catches the attacking team's point men and anyone off its bench, and *"any of the non-offending players"*. You have just turned an attacking-zone draw into a neutral-zone one by joining a shoving match.

**Offside where the puck was shot or passed in** sends the draw back to the zone the pass came from (Rule 83.6). So a stretch pass from your own end that goes offside brings the draw back into your own zone — carrying it over and being whistled costs far less. [Zone Entries](zone_entries.md) draws the tactical conclusion.

**Puck out of play:** the nearest spot in the zone it was shot from, or from the neutral or defending zone, the spot giving the **offending team the least advantage** (Rules 85.1, 85.5). A puck merely lodged in the netting can go to *"either of the adjacent face-off spots or at the nearest face-off spot in the zone from which the puck was shot"* (85.5). If the puck leaves the rink **directly off a faceoff**, the NHL and IIHF re-drop at the same spot.

**USA Hockey, Hockey Canada and CARHA send some draws elsewhere.**
- **USA Hockey gives no choice of dot** after a penalty (612(b)) or an icing (624(a)) — the official picks the spot. A low stoppage that is nobody's fault goes to the nearest end-zone spot (612(b)); a stoppage the attacking team caused in its attacking zone goes to the nearest neutral-zone spot (612(c)); and the team that caused a stoppage *"shall not gain a territorial advantage"* from the draw (612(g)).
- **Hockey Canada gives a choice of dot only in Junior and Senior**, after an icing or a time penalty, to the **attacking** team — and only if it names the dot *"prior to the start of the line change procedure"* (6.4(b)).
- **CARHA gives no choice of dot either.** A penalty draw goes to the dot nearest the incident, toward the penalised team's end, and never in the power-play team's own zone, so it can start in the neutral zone (39(a)); an icing draws at the offending team's end spot (65(a)).
- **USA Hockey and Hockey Canada both catch any attacking player** who goes beyond the top of the circles in a post-whistle scrum (USA Hockey 612(d), Hockey Canada 6.3(d)(vi)), not just the point men and the bench.

**What it costs.** ⚠️ **Never aim a puck out off the draw:** neither the NHL nor the IIHF says whether the re-drop covers a deliberate one, and 63.2(ii) makes a deliberate shot out a minor *"from anywhere on the ice surface"*. A puck shot into the players' bench draws no delay-of-game penalty (NHL 63.2(iii) Note) — but under the IIHF only a puck shot **directly** into a bench escapes it, so a clearance over the glass that drops into the bench is still two minutes. One into the **opposing** bench *"through an open bench door"* draws in the neutral zone beside it (85.1). [Risk Management](../hockey-iq/risk_management.md) carries the delay-of-game rule.

**Freezing the puck on the back of the net costs more outside the NHL, IIHF and PWHL.** ⚠️ **It is a minor as well as a draw under USA Hockey and Hockey Canada, and under CARHA 58(b) whenever the goalie's body is entirely outside the crease.** Even in the NHL, IIHF and PWHL a goalie who freezes it deliberately outside the crease takes a minor (NHL 63.2(i), IIHF 63.2(I), PWHL 65.2(i)); [Goaltender](../positions/goaltender.md) has the comparison.

*[Rules Primer](../foundation/rules_primer.md) carries the same material as a lookup table, plus a few situations not covered here — a hand pass in the attacking zone, a goal disallowed for goaltender interference, and two violations at once.*

## Faceoff Technique

A draw is hockey's nearest thing to a closed skill: same start position, same cue, same distance, every time. That is exactly the kind of thing people get good at through reps.

### The stance

```facts
Technique: Get low — deep knee bend, chest over the puck, weight on the balls of your feet
Goal: React faster from a loaded position, and be impossible to push off the dot
Position: Feet either side of the restraining lines, staggered rather than square, back foot loaded
Position: Square to the opponent's end, hips already cocked toward where you intend to send the puck
Key: Eyes on the linesperson's hand, not the opponent — watching the opponent means you are always second
Never: Get low by pushing your head out over the dot — depth comes from knees and hips, and the IIHF handbook has linespersons break up centres leaning in "almost ear to ear" (Situation 76.42)
```

- **Get low.** It is the most repeated instruction in coaching sources: you react faster from a loaded position, and you cannot be pushed off the dot. Deep knee bend, chest over the puck, weight on the balls of your feet. ⚠️ **Get the depth from your knees and hips, not by pushing your head out over the dot.** Head over the dot is the position the IIHF Situation Handbook tells linespersons to break up, and under the 2026/27 handbook a centre who will not move back is removed from the draw (Situations 76.42 and 76.43).
- **Feet either side of the restraining lines**, staggered rather than square, back foot loaded — that is the foot you drive off.
- **Square to the opponent's end**, as the rule requires, with your hips already cocked toward where you mean to send the puck.
- **Eyes on the linesperson's hand, not the opponent.** This is the highest-leverage habit in the skill. You react to the puck's release, not to their movement; watch them and you are always second.

### The grip

```facts
Technique: Bottom hand right down near the blade — that is where the leverage, speed and power come from
Options: Top hand halfway down the shaft or up on top of it; the coaching sources disagree, so try both
Technique: For the reverse grip, roll your bottom hand over so your knuckles face down
Read: Take the reverse grip when you have committed to a backhand pull and want raw speed and strength
Risk: The reverse grip telegraphs, and gives you almost nothing on the forehand
Options: Some players roll both hands into a "kayak paddle" grip for maximum backhand snap
```

- **Standard draw grip:** the two How To Hockey pieces cited here disagree on the top hand — one has it *"about halfway down the stick"*, the other, quoting former NHL player Jeff Serowik, *"on top of shaft"*. **Both put the bottom hand right down near the blade**, which is where the leverage, speed and power come from. Try both top-hand positions.
- **The reverse grip (backhand grip):** roll your bottom hand over so your knuckles face down — How To Hockey's cue is to "hold the stick as if you were going to cross-check someone in the teeth." It gives far more pulling power and speed on the backhand.
- **When to use it:** when you have committed to a backhand pull — typically own-end draws you must not lose, and offensive-zone draws set up for a **one-timer** (a shot straight off a pass, without stopping the puck). Its cost: it telegraphs, and gives you almost nothing on the forehand. A centre who lines up in it every time can be read.
- Some players use a **"kayak paddle"** grip — both hands rolled — for maximum backhand snap.

### Faceoff technique: the techniques

**In short:** Own three or four draws, not one; against a better taker, tying up their stick, stick on stick only, is the common answer.

```facts
Key: There is no single correct draw — the good centres own three or four
Technique: Pull the puck back on the backhand, usually with the reverse grip — pulling is a stronger and faster motion than pushing
Convention: How often each technique wins is not something this document can tell you — the NHL faceoff data behind it records zone, strength and winner, not technique
Options: Lift or press their stick with yours, then play the puck — a 1-on-1 becomes a scramble for your wingers. Never a hand on their stick or your stick on their hands; never hook it or hold it down
Risk: The stick lift is the higher-reward version and it fails badly if you miss
```

There is no single correct draw. There are seven or eight, and the good centres own three or four.

- **The clean backward win.** Sweep or yank the puck behind you to a designated teammate. The highest-value outcome, and the lowest-percentage, because you must beat the other centre outright.
- **The backhand pull.** Rotate and pull the puck back on the backhand, usually with the reverse grip — How To Hockey says it *"helps you improve your quickness and power"*. Pulling is stronger and faster than pushing. Whether it wins more often than other techniques this document cannot tell you: NHL faceoff data records zone, strength and winner, not technique.
- **The forehand pull.** The same on the forehand — weaker and slower for most players, which is why handedness matters (below).
- **Tying up the stick.** Lift their stick or press it for an instant with yours — over or under the blade — then play the puck yourself. You make sure **nobody** wins it, turning a 1-on-1 into a scramble your wingers are already leaning into. Against a better draw-taker this is the common answer; the alternative is changing the taker, and your bench decides. **Stick on stick only: never a hand on their stick, never your stick on their hands, never hook it or hold it down** — and play the puck straight after the lift.
- **The stick lift.** The aggressive version: lift their blade clean off the ice and take the puck. Higher reward, and it fails badly if you miss.

**What it costs.** A hand on an opponent's stick is a minor for holding the stick under NHL and IIHF 54.2, PWHL 55.2, USA Hockey 622, Hockey Canada 8.1(a) and CARHA 63(a), whose text may also allow a major. A hold that injures is a mandatory major under Hockey Canada 8.1(b) and CARHA 63(b), and either one puts you out of the game — 8.1(b) adds a game misconduct, and under CARHA a major ejects you with at least a one-game ban (30(a), 32(d)). A stick on their hands is hooking: IIHF 55.1 names a stick *"against the opponent's hands / or near the opponent's hands"*, and Hockey Canada allows the lift only *"for the purpose of making a play on the puck"* (8.2(a) Interpretation 1). Under USA Hockey (623) and Hockey Canada (8.2) a blade that hooks their **stick** is hooking too.

### The techniques that use your body, your feet or your hands

**In short:** Lean in only after the puck lands, while playing it, and where checking is allowed; skate round on a lose-it-forward; check your book on feet.

```facts
Rule: Contact before the drop is a violation under NHL and IIHF 76.7 and can draw a minor on the taker at the referee's discretion as well, where USA Hockey 613(e), Hockey Canada 6.2(c) and CARHA 57(b) make that minor mandatory; afterwards they permit contact only "in the course of playing the puck"
Rule: Only Hockey Canada defines when a faceoff is complete — Rule 6.2, the puck "contacts the ice surface before contacting the stick or any part of the body of the player taking the face-off", so there contact timed to the drop is contact before completion
Never: Lean on the man before the puck lands; no book in this document makes that free
Rule: Where body checking is banned you cannot go through the man — USA Hockey 604(a) at 12 & under, in all girls'/women's and non-check adult classifications; Hockey Canada 7.3 at U13 and below, female and Member-approved divisions; CARHA 49(a) in every game
Read: Try the "lose it forward" play at the neutral-zone dots, where there is space behind the other centre — go round the other centre, not through them
Rule: ⚠️ **Never skate through the other centre to reach a puck pushed past them** — in women's hockey IIHF 101.1 and PWHL 52.1 oblige you to skate around a stationary player — at least a minor, a major brings a game misconduct, and a PWHL match is possible
Never: Use "lose it forward" in your own end
Rule: Batting the drop is legal with the stick; a taker who tries to win the draw by batting it with the hand takes a minor (NHL and IIHF Rule 67.2)
```

The remaining four put your body, feet or hands into the draw, and each has a rule attached. Three are here; your feet have a section of their own after them.

- **Going through the man.** Lean into your opponent once the puck is on the ice, taking the ice and the puck together. **Both halves are the rule:** *lean, not drive* — contact is allowed only *"in the course of playing the puck after the face-off has been completed"* (Rule 76.7) — and *once it is down, not as it falls*. ⚠️ **Wait for the puck to land: under Hockey Canada a body timed to the drop is a body before the faceoff is complete.** In a CARHA game, and wherever body checking is barred, play the puck and avoid the man.
- **The "lose it forward" play.** Push the puck *forward* past the other centre and go after it. Best at the neutral-zone dots, where there is space behind them and little danger if it fails; in the offensive zone it can beat a centre committed to pulling back; in your own end it is a bad idea. ⚠️ **Go round the other centre, not through them** — they are now between you and the puck, and in women's hockey skating round is the rule, not a preference. **Chase the puck, not the ice they are standing on.**
- **Batting it out of the air.** Advanced, and legal with the **stick**: several coaching sources describe attacking the puck mid-drop. Never with the hand. Time it off the linesperson's release, not the ice.

**What it costs.** Contact before the drop is a faceoff violation under NHL and IIHF 76.7, and the NHL and the IIHF 2026/27 edition replace the offending centre for it. The referee may add a minor; under **USA Hockey 613(e), Hockey Canada 6.2(c) and CARHA 57(b) the minor is mandatory.** Hockey Canada alone defines completion — Rule 6.2: the puck *"contacts the ice surface before contacting the stick or any part of the body of the player taking the face-off"*. The NHL and IIHF say only that it *"ends when the puck has been legally dropped"*.

Driving through the centre on a lose-it-forward: in women's hockey IIHF Rule 101.1 (both editions) says that a stationary player *"is entitled to that area of the ice"* and *"the opponent is obliged to skate around"* her. It makes *"[a]ny move by a Player to step or glide into an opposing Player"* at least a minor, up to *"[a] major penalty (5') and automatic game misconduct penalty"*. In Britain the In-House Rules, Section 11, eject any player given a major in women's hockey; PWHL Rule 52.1 says the same as the IIHF, and a major there ejects you too (52.5). In the men's game it is interference: NHL 56.1 gives a player without the puck *"the ice he is standing on"*, and IIHF 56.1 bars obstructing an opponent *"without possession of the puck"*. Leaning on each other while you both play the loose puck is legal where contact is allowed — the IIHF Glossary lets skaters *"push and lean into each other provided that possession of the puck remains the sole object of the contact"*.

Where body checking is barred you cannot go through the man at all. **USA Hockey** bars it at 12 & under and in *"all Girls'/Women's age classifications and all non-check Adult classifications"* (604(a)), and counts *"physically forcing the opponent off the puck and with no effort to legally play the puck"* as an illegal check. **Hockey Canada** bars it at U13 and below, in female hockey and in Member-approved divisions (7.3), sparing incidental contact in pursuit of the puck. **CARHA** bars it in every game: 49(a) penalises contact a player *"does not avert"*, and 49(c) spares accidental contact — except to the head, face or neck, still a minor (50(a)), or from behind into the boards or net, a match penalty (53(b)).

A taker who *"attempts to win the face-off by batting the puck with their hand"* takes a minor for delay of game (NHL and IIHF 67.2), and the note to that rule keeps the taker's hand off the puck until a third player has touched it.

#### Using your feet — the puck, the man, and the goal

```facts
Rule: Kicking the puck back is legal in the NHL and PWHL and a faceoff violation under IIHF 76.3(VI), so it is not available to you in Britain at any level (IIHF Rule 76.3(VI), both editions)
Rule: USA Hockey, Hockey Canada and CARHA write no faceoff bar on kicking the puck, so the technique rests on a general kicking rule rather than a faceoff one — USA Hockey 627(c), Hockey Canada 6.10(a), CARHA 69
Rule: A kick-back that gets tipped in is no goal under USA Hockey 627(c) or Hockey Canada 6.10(b), which disallow a goal off a deflected kick, or under CARHA Rule 60(c) — no goal where "after being kicked, the puck deflects off any player or stick into the net"
Rule: Hockey Canada scopes its own no-goal rule twice — 6.10's preamble exempts a puck that "deflects off a player's skate, without a deliberate kicking action", and 6.10(b) excepts "a defending player kick[ing] the puck into their own goal", which at your own end zone draw is your net
Rule: Kicking or attempting to kick an opponent is a match penalty under NHL and PWHL 49.3, USA Hockey 627(b) (with a likely multi-game ban), Hockey Canada 7.1(c)(iii) and CARHA 48(c); under IIHF 49.3 the referee may assess a major, and the game misconduct is automatic once they do
Rule: Under USA Hockey a skate does not need a kicking motion to be an offence — 627(a) is a major plus a game misconduct for using the skate to "push off" an opponent, which its Note 2 defines as skate contact with an opponent "in a non-kicking motion"
Rule: A USA Hockey game misconduct is not only this game — 404(b) also suspends the player for their team's next game, and for at least two where it follows a second major in the same game (403(b))
Never: Send the skate at the man. Any kicking permission at the dot is for the puck only, and under IIHF 76.3(VI) a centre may not win the draw with a skate at all
Action: Before you build an offensive-zone draw around the kick-back, find out which book your league runs — the tip behind it is a goal in only three of the six
```

**Using your feet.** Kick the puck back to a teammate, or open your skate as a backstop so a tied-up puck squirts the right way. ⚠️ **Send the skate at the puck, never at the man** — the dot puts two opponents' skates inches apart, and every book prices kicking the man at the top of its scale.

**Check your book before you learn it.** It is legal in the NHL, and in the PWHL, whose Rule 78 writes no skate bar. ⚠️ **Under the IIHF it is a violation, so it is not available in British hockey at any level**: Rule 76.3(VI) catches a centre who *"wins the face-off by using the skates"*. USA Hockey, Hockey Canada and CARHA write no faceoff bar — Rules 613, 6.2 and 57 were each read in full — so there it rests **by inference** on each book's general kicking permission (USA Hockey 627(c), Hockey Canada 6.10(a), CARHA 69).

**The tip off a kick is a goal in only three books.** Under the NHL, the IIHF and the PWHL a kicked puck that deflects in off a stick counts (Rule 49.2). USA Hockey (627(c), with Casebook Situations 4 and 5), Hockey Canada (6.10(b)) and CARHA (60(c), not 69) disallow it — so there the kick has to end in a teammate's possession and a clean shot. Find out your book before you plan a tip off a draw. [Passing and Receiving](../technique/passing_and_receiving.md) carries the same split for a loose puck in your feet.

**What it costs.** ⚠️ **Kicking or attempting to kick an opponent is a match penalty** under NHL and PWHL 49.3, USA Hockey 627(b) (with a likely multi-game suspension), Hockey Canada 7.1(c)(iii) and CARHA 48(c), the PWHL adding a five-minute time penalty *"[w]hether or not an injury occurs"*. Under IIHF 49.3 the referee *"may assess a major penalty and an automatic game misconduct penalty"*. ⚠️ **USA Hockey does not even need a kick:** 627(a) gives a major plus a game misconduct for using your skate to *"push off"* an opponent, which its Note 2 defines as contact *"in a non-kicking motion"* — so there the opened skate is the risky half. Any USA Hockey game misconduct also costs your team's next game (404(b)), and at least two if it comes with your second major of the game (403(b)).

Hockey Canada narrows its no-goal rule twice: a puck deflecting off a skate *"without a deliberate kicking action"* is not a kick (6.10), and 6.10(b) excepts *"a defending player kick[ing] the puck into their own goal"* — so at your own-end draw, a puck you kick into your own net counts. Under the IIHF, winning the draw with a skate costs a warning (2025/26) or your centre (2026/27); "When your centre gets tossed" sets it out.

## Reading and Beating Your Opponent

### Everyone has a tell

```facts
Action: Watch other lines' draws from the bench, not just your own
Read: A rolled-over bottom hand means a backhand pull is coming
Read: Hands high on the shaft means a sweep or a tie-up rather than a yank
Read: Which foot is loaded tells you which way they intend to rotate
Read: Stick blade angle tells you where they want the puck to go
Read: A tight grip and low coiled stance means a clean-win attempt; relaxed and upright means a tie-up
```

Most centres have exactly one preferred technique and one strong side. Two shifts of attention is usually all it takes to find them.

- **Watch other lines' draws, not just your own.** You get roughly 56 chances a game to scout the two or three centres you will face, most of them from the bench.
- **Read the grip.** A rolled-over bottom hand means a backhand pull. Hands high on the shaft mean a sweep or a tie-up rather than a yank.
- **Read the stance.** The loaded foot tells you which way they will rotate; the blade angle tells you where they want the puck.
- **Read the tension.** A tight grip and a low, coiled stance usually mean a clean-win attempt; relaxed and upright often means a tie-up.

### Watch the hand, not the man

```facts
Key: Look at the linesperson's hand and the puck, not your opponent's stick
Goal: Turn the draw into a pure reaction-time problem, which you can train
Risk: Reacting to your opponent leaves you a step behind and open to feints
Options: Some coaching sources watch the official's elbow instead, on the grounds that it moves fractionally earlier
Read: Linespersons have tendencies too — height of drop, speed of drop, how long they hold
```

This is the one universal. **Look at the linesperson's hand and the puck, not your opponent's stick.** Reacting to the drop is a reaction-time problem you can train; reacting to them leaves you a step behind and open to feints. Some coaching sources watch the official's **elbow** instead, because it moves fractionally earlier.

Linespersons have tendencies too — height of drop, speed of drop, how long they hold. Notice them.

### Vary your approach

```facts
Action: Change the technique, not just the target — pull one back, tie the next up, sweep the third
Risk: Win three straight draws to the same defenceman and the fourth will be jumped
Key: Even a technique you are worse at has value if it stops an opponent pre-loading against your best one
Technique: A shoulder feint, hip fake or delayed stick approach can draw an opponent into moving early — which, if they move before the drop, is a faceoff violation
Risk: The feint can be charged to you instead — 76.7 names the centre moving prematurely, and a feint that becomes contact is a minor, mandatory under USA Hockey, Hockey Canada and CARHA
Goal: Make them move before the drop — that is a faceoff violation, but the remedy NHL Rule 76.7 writes for it is a re-drop, not a removal
Rule: Neither IIHF edition removes their centre for a premature move — the 2025/26 book warned their team and re-dropped, the 2026/27 book re-drops (IIHF 76.7, both); the ejections live in 76.6 and in 76.7's contact paragraph, not in its opening sentence
Rule: ⚠️ **Under the IHUK In-House Rules 2026-27 the feint actually pays** — the linesperson must "immediately replace the centre for all violations" except after an icing, and a premature move is one. Not in the Elite League — its own Casebook writes no Rule 76, so IIHF 76.7 only re-drops
```

The counter to a scouted centre is unpredictability. Win three straight draws pulling to the same defenceman and the fourth will be jumped. Change the *technique*, not just the target: pull one back, tie the next up, sweep the third. Even a technique you are worse at has value if it stops them pre-loading against your best one. (Kicking it back is a fourth option under the NHL but not the IIHF — see "Using your feet".)

Deception belongs here too: a small feint of the shoulder, a hip fake, or a deliberately delayed stick can draw an opponent into moving early. ⚠️ **Feint with the shoulder, never into them** — you are about one stick length apart, so the shoulder is the movement most likely to become contact before the drop.

**What it pays depends on your book.** Under the NHL and both IIHF editions, drawing the other centre early only gets a re-drop — the faceoff *"must be conducted again"* (76.7), the 2025/26 book warning their team first — and nobody is removed. (NHL 76.3(i) also has a linesperson replace a centre *"not positioned for the face-off"*; whether that reaches a centre who is positioned and then twitches is not written down, so plan on neither.) **Across the Home Countries the feint pays:** the In-House Rules *"immediately replace the centre for all violations"* except after an icing, so there that centre comes out. ⚠️ **Not in the Elite League**, where an early centre takes the re-drop. **Under Hockey Canada it pays too:** 6.5(a) names early movement a violation and 6.2(d) ejects the taker for one, except on a Junior draw after an icing. Under the NHL, PWHL and IIHF books a second violation on the same draw is a bench minor, so bait them once, then take a clean draw.

**What it costs.** The premature-movement clause names *"a center"*, and you are one: a shoulder that goes early is your violation on the same terms — a re-drop, or your place in the circle under the In-House Rules and Hockey Canada — and under the NHL and IIHF it spends one of the two violations that make a bench minor. ⚠️ **A feint that touches the other centre is a penalty, not just a violation.** Under NHL and IIHF 76.7 the referee *"may, at his discretion impose a minor penalty"*; under USA Hockey 613(e) a minor *"**shall** be assessed"*, under Hockey Canada 6.2(c) the referee *"**will** assess a Minor penalty"*, and under CARHA 57(b) the referee *"**shall** assess a Minor penalty"*.

### Handedness and which side you're strong on

**In short:** Send each centre to the circle that suits their shot: match centres to circles, or put a left-shot and a right-shot out together.

```facts
Convention: Coaching sources call the strong-side draw a right-shot centre on the right side, or a left-shot on the left
Key: Your strong side is the circle where your natural backward pull sends the puck toward the boards
Action: Take ten draws in each circle and you will know within a minute
Risk: On your weak side the board-side pull is a forehand — slower and weaker for most players
Never: Solve that by pulling to your backhand in your own end — it sends the puck to the middle, the worst place it can go
Options: Match the circle to the centre, flip your hands, or put a left-shot and a right-shot centre out together
```

A centre's shot hand decides which circle they are better in, and coaching staffs build around it.

**"Strong side" means two things in this document.** Here it is about handedness: the circle where a centre's natural backward pull sends the puck to the boards. Elsewhere — the defenceman "on the draw side", or the corner the puck goes to after a loss — it has the ordinary meaning: the side of the ice the puck is on. The two usually coincide, because a team tries to put its centre on their strong side, but they are not the same claim.

- The consensus in coaching sources (Ice Hockey Systems, Hockey's Arsenal) is that a **strong-side draw** is a **right-shot centre on the right side**, or a **left-shot centre on the left**.
- On your **strong side**, you pull the puck back on your **backhand toward the boards** — your most powerful motion, to the safest part of the ice.
- On your **weak side**, that board-side pull has to be a **forehand**, slower and weaker for most players. Pulling to your backhand instead sends the puck toward the **middle**, which in your own end is the worst place it can go.

> **Flagged:** the sources state the mapping (right shot / right side) but not the reference frame — "right side" as you stand at the dot facing the opponent's end is the standard reading and the one used here. **The self-test that needs no convention: your strong side is the circle where your natural backward pull sends the puck toward the boards.** Take ten draws in each circle and you will know within a minute.

![](diagram:faceoff-strong-side-dot)

**What teams do about it:**
- **Match the circle to the centre.** This is why coaches send a specific centre to a specific dot, and why the rules that let you *choose* the dot are worth more than they look.
- **Flip your hands.** Some NHL centres — Vincent Trocheck is the frequently cited example — take weak-side draws holding the stick the other way round. It is a second, mirror-image skill: worth it if you take 1,000 draws a season, probably not if you take 100.
- **Use two centres.** Put a left-shot and a right-shot centre on the ice together and swap the taker by circle. This is free, and almost no rec team does it.

## Full Five-Player Alignments

> **This entire section describes coaching choices, not rules of hockey.** Every team's alignments differ, and the differences are large. What follows is the most common default at each location, with realistic alternatives named. **Find out what your team runs before your first shift** — and what the *call* is for each draw. An alignment half the line is guessing at is worse than none.
>
> The defaults assume the house system used throughout this guide: **low zone collapse (zone) defensive-zone coverage** and a **2-1-2 forecheck** (see [Defensive Zone Coverage](defensive_zone_coverage.md) and [Forechecking Systems](forechecking_systems.md)).
>
> **A word on "left" and "right".** Which circle is on your left depends on which net you defend and which way you face, so the alignments below use two terms that do not move: **boards-side** — the half of the circle nearer the side boards — and **inside**, the half nearer the middle of the ice; and your centre's **strong side** (see "Handedness" above). Learn those instead of left and right, and the alignment applies unchanged at either circle — it is a mirror image at the other one.

### Defensive-zone draws

**In short:** In your own end, settle for a tie-up over a clean loss, never win it to the middle, and jump every loose puck.

```facts
Priority: Do not lose this cleanly — a tie-up is acceptable, a clean loss to their strong-side point is not
Position: Boards-side winger on the outside hash mark; inside winger on the inside hash mark facing the slot; boards-side defenceman behind and outside that winger; slot defenceman in front of the goalie
Action: On a clean win, pull to the boards-side defenceman to rim it out or hit the boards-side winger
Action: On a clean loss, wingers release to the points on the drop; the centre covers their opponent, boards-side D takes the corner or half-wall, and the slot D holds the net front and blocks the lane
Convention: This whole defensive-zone alignment is the house zone system, including who takes the corner and who holds the net front — under man-to-man each winger follows their man and those assignments change, so ask which your team plays
Action: On a tie-up everyone jumps; the inside winger and the boards-side defenceman are usually closest
Never: Pull the puck to the middle — a draw won into the slot is barely better than a loss
Never: Take the contact with your back to anyone coming at you on a defensive-zone draw retrieval, and never duck. Skates parallel to the boards, forearm and hip to the wall, head up and chin off your chest
```

The highest-stakes alignment in hockey. It has to work on a win, a loss and a tie-up, because you get all three.

**The standard setup:**

- **Centre** — in the dot. Your job is simple: **do not lose this cleanly.** A tie-up is acceptable; a clean loss to their strong-side point is not.
- **Boards-side winger** — on the **outside hash mark**, nearer the boards. Usually the winger whose natural side matches the circle. You are the first breakout outlet on a win and the first man to the opposing point on a loss.
- **Inside winger** — on the **inside hash mark**, facing the **slot** (the lane straight out from the net between the circles; the *high slot* runs from the dots up to the top of the circles, the *goalmouth* is the immediate net front). The harder job: on a loss you must reach the far point, and on a scramble you are the closest body to the most dangerous ice.
- **Boards-side defenceman** — behind and outside the boards-side winger, near the hash marks, ready to retrieve anything pulled back to the wall or to get behind the net.
- **Slot defenceman** — in front of the goalie, responsible for the net front and for blocking a point shot.

**Common variation:** many teams set their defencemen on their **"off" sides** for own-end draws — the right-shot D on the left boards and vice versa — so a puck pulled to the wall arrives on the forehand and the breakout pass is a forehand pass. It costs a little in coverage and gains a lot on the exit. Ask which your team does.

**The alignment does not change from one circle to the other — your centre's strong side does.** A team that gets to choose its dot uses the choice to put your centre on it.

**On a clean win:**
- Pull to the **boards-side defenceman**, who rims it out or hits the boards-side winger for a direct exit. The boards are the safe half of the ice — ⚠️ **for the puck, not automatically for the player fetching it.** That player is facing the wall with a forechecker arriving behind them, which is the geometry every checking-from-behind and boarding rule is written about. ⚠️ **Never turn your back to anyone coming at you, and never duck.** Skates parallel to the boards, forearm and hip to the wall — not the point of the shoulder — feet apart, knees bent, head up and chin off your chest. Go in on an angle; if you are caught facing the wall, turn parallel if you possibly can, and if you cannot, get your stick and gloves up and spread on the glass. See [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md).
- The **wingers release** the moment the puck is secured, to the wall **between the hash marks and the goal line** — the outlet spot [Breakouts](breakouts.md) covers, at or just below the dot line, roughly twenty feet out. Not thirty feet up the wall, which makes the pass long, flat and into your back.
- **Never pull the puck to the middle.** An own-end draw won into the slot is barely better than a loss.

**On a clean loss:**
- **Both wingers go straight to the points.** In the zone system assumed here that is the call, made on the drop rather than after you see the puck — it is all that stands between you and a shot from the top of the circle with your goalie screened. **Under man-to-man** each winger follows the opponent they were assigned, which may mean staying low. A hybrid ("zone with man-on-man in the low slot") sends the wingers to the points while the low defender stays on one body. Ask which your team plays.
- **Centre finds their centre**, who will head for the **high slot** or the **net front**.
- **Slot defenceman holds the net front and blocks the lane.** Do not chase the puck to the point; your winger has it.
- **Boards-side defenceman takes the strong-side corner or half-wall.** The **half-wall** is the boards level with the faceoff dot, about 20 feet out from the goal line — barely a third of the way to the blue line. It is not the **point**, the area just inside the blue line.

**On a tie-up — the most common outcome, though that is a coaching estimate:**
- **Everyone jumps.** A tied-up draw is a loose puck in a two-foot area with eight players around it, and it belongs to whoever moves first. Players wait to see what happens instead — in coaching experience the most under-coached moment in hockey.
- The **inside winger** and the **boards-side defenceman** are usually the two closest bodies, and the two who should attack it.
- **Call it.** "Loose!" from anyone with eyes on the puck. See [On-Ice Communication](../foundation/on_ice_communication.md).


### Offensive-zone draws

```facts
Position: Strong-side defenceman at the point, feet already set and stick already loaded — this is the shooter
Position: Weak-side defenceman at the far point, holding the line as the safety against a counterattack
Action: Centre wins it back rather than forward
Read: The inside winger is the usual target for a quick pull-back, and the net-front driver if the draw is lost — drive to the net, not into the goalie
Priority: On a clean loss the priority flips instantly to not conceding a counterattack
Action: On a clean loss, wingers pressure the puck rather than retreat, the strong-side D recovers to the line, and the weak-side D holds — a 2-1-2 forecheck already in position
Key: An offensive-zone tie-up is a free 50-50 puck in the other team's end — a good outcome, not a failure
Convention: The offensive-zone alignment described here is a common default, not a rule — they differ from team to team, so find out what your team runs
```

The one place in hockey you get to run a genuine set play against a stationary defence from a known start. **What follows is a common default, not a rule**; find out what your team runs.

**The standard setup:**

- **Centre** — in the dot, trying to win it back rather than forward.
- **Boards-side winger** — outside hash mark, ready to attack the wall or spin off it.
- **Inside winger** — inside hash mark, the usual target for a quick pull-back shot from the circle, and the net-front driver if the draw is lost. Get to the front of the net but stay off the goalie and out of their way: contact, or a position that stops them moving freely in their crease, can cost you the goal, and contact can cost a penalty.
- **Strong-side defenceman** — at the point on the draw side, **feet already set, stick already loaded**. This is the shooter.
- **Weak-side defenceman** — at the far point, holding the line as the safety against a counterattack.

Boards-side and inside mirror here as in your own end, and "strong-side" for the point man means the draw side, not necessarily their shooting hand.

![](diagram:faceoff-ozone-alignment)

**On a clean win:** run the set play (see below). The shooter's feet are already planted — one of the very few shots in hockey where you do not have to receive, settle and set. That is worth more than a slightly better angle.

**On a clean loss:** the priority flips instantly to **not conceding a counterattack**. The weak-side defenceman holds; the strong-side defenceman recovers to the line; the wingers pressure the puck rather than retreat, because you have four skaters above it and their exits are limited. A 2-1-2 forecheck is never more effective, because everyone is already in position.

**On a tie-up:** attack the puck with the two nearest bodies and keep both defencemen at the line. An offensive-zone tie-up is a free 50-50 puck in their end — a good outcome, not a failure.

### Neutral-zone draws

```facts
Position: Both wingers wide in their lanes, level with or just behind the dot
Position: Both defencemen back toward your own blue line, one either side of the middle
Action: On a win advance the puck immediately, and look to carry or pass into the zone with control
Risk: A won neutral-zone draw that ends in a dump-in has produced very little — one author's tracking of the 2018-19 Swiss National League, not the NHL, so read the direction rather than the multiple
Action: On a loss, gap up immediately and defend the rush
Mindset: This is where you experiment — a lost neutral-zone draw costs you almost nothing
Convention: The neutral-zone alignment described here is a common default, not a rule — they differ from team to team, so find out what your team runs
```

The lowest-stakes draws, and the ones most worth a risk. **The setup below is a common default, not a rule**; find out what your team runs.

**The standard setup:**

- **Centre** in the dot; **both wingers** wide in their lanes, level with or just behind the dot.
- **Both defencemen** back toward your own blue line, one either side of the middle, ready for a rush if it goes wrong.

**On a win:** advance the puck immediately, and look to carry or pass into the zone with control. **Daniel Weinberger**, writing at Hockey Graphs, found neutral-zone faceoff wins mostly turn into **dump-ins**, and that possessions with a dump-in produced 5.5 shots and 0.17 **expected goals** per 100 possessions against 32 shots and 0.68 expected goals without one. (*Expected goals* estimates how many goals an average shooter would score from those shots — chance quality, not just count.)

**Note the league before you carry those numbers anywhere:** they are InStat tracking of the **2018-19 Swiss National League**, not the NHL, from one author's sample in one season. Part of the gap is selection — a possession that never got a controlled entry is *why* it ended in a dump-in. Read the direction as solid and the multiple as approximate. See [Zone Entries](zone_entries.md).

![](diagram:faceoff-neutral-zone)

**On a loss:** your defencemen are the whole plan. Gap up immediately and defend the rush — see [Defending the Rush](defending_the_rush.md).

**This is where you experiment:** a lost neutral-zone draw costs almost nothing. Try the "lose it forward" play — going **round** the other centre, never through them — the weak-side flipped grip you are learning, or a technique you want them to have in their head for the own-end draw later.

## Set Plays

Set plays off draws are the closest hockey gets to American football. They also fail constantly, usually from complexity rather than execution — a coaching observation; nobody counts why set plays fail.

> Everything in this section is a **coaching choice**. These are the common patterns; your team's playbook will differ.

### Offensive-zone plays

```facts
Options: The D one-timer, the winger walk-out, the tip play, or a board-side variation
Key: The D one-timer works because the shooter's feet are set first — in CARHA a backswing past fifteen inches is a slap shot: a minor, or if it injures, a major that ends this game and at least the next
Rule: In a CARHA league keep the one-timer's follow-through below the shoulders — Rule 62(b) makes high-stick contact above an opponent's shoulders a major even by accident and even if nobody is hurt; deliberate or injuring, it also ejects you (62(b) Note)
Risk: Every defensive alignment expects the D one-timer, and your inside winger stands in its line — on the drop they step out of it, usually to the net front to screen the goalie
Read: Use the winger walk-out against a team that immediately pressures the point
Options: The tip play is the safest, because a point shot into traffic rarely turns into a counterattack
Risk: Board-side loops and swaps work precisely once per game against an attentive opponent
```

**The D one-timer.** The centre pulls the puck straight back to the strong-side defenceman, who shoots first-time. The shooter's feet are set and the traffic is in place before the puck moves. Its weakness: every defensive alignment expects it. And your own inside winger stands in the shot's line, so on the drop they step out of it — usually to the net front to screen the goalie, staying off them.

**The winger walk-out.** The centre pulls to the inside winger in the circle, who takes a stride toward the middle and shoots or feeds the slot. Better than the D one-timer against a team that pressures the point at once, because you attack the space they left.

**The tip play.** The pull goes to the point, but the aim is a deflection — someone screens, someone tips. Lower value per shot, more rebounds and chaos, and the safest play, because a point shot into traffic rarely turns into a counterattack.

**The board-side variations.** The boards-side winger loops **over the top of the circle to the inside hash mark** for a one-timer, or a winger and defenceman **swap positions** before the draw to open an unfamiliar lane. These work about once a game against an attentive opponent.

**What it costs.** In a CARHA league keep the one-timer's backswing short: more than fifteen inches behind the puck makes it a slap shot there — a minor, and a major if it injures an opponent, which ends your game and at least the next (Rules 79(a), 30(a), 32(d)). A follow-through that catches an opponent above the shoulders is a separate foul there: a major even by accident and even if nobody is hurt, and an ejection only if it was deliberate or injures (Rule 62(b)).

### Defensive-zone plays

**In short:** Off an own-end win, rim it hard all the way round or hit the breaking winger, never a soft rim that dies.

```facts
Options: The quick rim, the direct exit, the reverse, or off the glass and out of your zone — every forechecker is stationary at the whistle, so a hard rim or direct exit beats them
Rule: Under NHL and IIHF rules — the KHL reportedly too, unverified — your goalie may only play the puck behind the goal line inside the trapezoid (Rule 1.8; penalty NHL 27.8 and 63.2(viii), IIHF 27.7 and 63.2(VIII)); the sole exception is skate contact with the crease
Rule: The goalkeeper's restricted area is IIHF Rule 27.7, not the NHL's 27.8. Nothing in the Elite League's Casebook switches it off; England Ice Hockey's Rules & Regulations 22.3 call it "not currently enforced", scope and standing unsettled — keep your goalie inside it
Read: Play it as though it binds — your goalie stays inside the trapezoid, so a rim that dies in the corner is yours to fetch, not theirs
Rule: USA Hockey, Hockey Canada and the CARHA rec book write no trapezoid, so there a rim dying in your corner is your goalie's to fetch, though none lets them sit on it; a PWHL goalie plays as if it binds — Rules 1.8 and 27.8 switch it off, but 65.2(viii) keeps the minor
Rule: No book without a trapezoid, nor the PWHL, lets your goalie sit on a dead rim out of the crease — USA Hockey 614(c) behind the goal line or outside the privileged area; CARHA 58(b) behind the goal line and 61(b) anywhere; PWHL 65.2(i); Hockey Canada clause v of Interpretation 3 to 10.1(a)
Key: Closer in, and in front of the goal line, your goalie has considerably more latitude under every book — Goaltender owns that test, not this section
Never: Rim it halfway. Rim it hard enough to reach your winger instead, or tight enough for the goalie to stop it behind the net; in CARHA a backswing past fifteen inches is a slap-shot penalty
Read: Use the reverse against a team that overloads the boards side
Action: Call off the glass and out of your zone when you are tired, pinned, or a man down — off it, not over it
```

**The quick rim.** Win the puck to the boards-side defenceman, who rims it hard around the boards at once. No possession, no risk, and you are out — every forechecker is standing still at the whistle, so a hard rim is past them before anyone moves. **Rim it hard enough to carry all the way round to your winger, or tight enough that your goalie can legally stop it behind the net and leave it — never in between.** In a CARHA league keep the backswing under fifteen inches, or it is a slap-shot penalty. Full treatment in [Breakouts](breakouts.md). ⚠️ **A rim that dies in your corner sends a defenceman to fetch it against a forechecker arriving at full speed** — below.

**Who fetches a rim that dies depends on your book, because of the trapezoid.** Under NHL and IIHF rules your goalie may play a puck behind the goal line only inside the trapezoid, so a rim that dies in your corner is a defenceman's to fetch, with the forecheck coming. ⚠️ **That retrieval is worse than an ordinary one: the forechecker was stationary at the whistle and arrives at full speed.** ⚠️ **So never turn your back to anyone coming at you, and never duck** — skates parallel to the boards, forearm and hip to the wall (not the point of the shoulder), head up and chin off the chest ([Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md)).

**USA Hockey, Hockey Canada and CARHA write no trapezoid**, so there a rim that dies in the corner is your goalie's to fetch — but they have to play it, not cover it. A PWHL goalie should play as if it binds (see "What it costs"). The KHL is commonly said to have one too; no KHL rulebook was available to check.

**🇬🇧 In Britain, keep your goalie inside it.** Nothing in the Elite League's Casebook switches it off; the Casebook adopts the 2026-27 IIHF book. England Ice Hockey's *Rules & Regulations* 22.3 (2024-25) call it *"not currently enforced"*, but that regulation's scope and standing are unsettled, and the In-House Rules never mention it and say *"[u]nless otherwise stated, all rules will be enforced in line with the IIHF Rule Book"*. Play it as though it binds: a rim that dies in the corner is yours to fetch.

**The direct exit.** Win the puck to the boards-side defenceman, who hits the boards-side winger already breaking up the wall. You keep possession, and the play is done before the forecheck is organised.

**The reverse.** Win it behind you and let a defenceman take it behind the net to change the angle. Good against a team that overloads the boards side.

**Off the glass and out.** Less a play than an outcome — but it should be the explicit call when you are tired, pinned or a man down. Nobody has ever been beaten by a puck in the neutral zone.

**What it costs.** The trapezoid is Rule 1.8 in both the NHL and IIHF books, penalised under NHL 27.8 and 63.2(viii) and IIHF 27.7 and 63.2(VIII). The PWHL switches it off in Rules 1.8 and 27.8 but its Rule 65.2(viii) still writes the minor — [Goaltender](../positions/goaltender.md) sets out that contradiction.

**Your goalie cannot cover a dead rim out there either.** ⚠️ **None of the four books without a working trapezoid lets them sit on it** — each makes freezing it away from the net a minor:

- **PWHL** 65.2(i): any player, *"including the goalkeeper"*, who freezes the puck *"in such a manner as to deliberately cause a stoppage of play"* — reaching a goalie outside her crease.
- **USA Hockey** 614(c): a minor on any of four triggers, the first being that *"[t]he puck is behind the goal line and their body is entirely outside of the goal crease."* The others are not playing it with the stick when able before being pressured, being outside the privileged area, and holding it against the goal frame, boards or netting.
- **CARHA** 58(b): a goalie who *"deliberately falls on or gathers the puck into their body, or holds or places the puck against any part of the goal or boards"* with the puck behind the goal line and their body entirely outside the crease. *Deliberately* qualifies falling and gathering, but not plainly holding it against the goal or boards, so do not count on intent excusing those; the book does not say whether its *"when"* clause conditions both limbs. 61(b) reaches anywhere, in front of the goal line too: a minor for a goalie who *"deliberately holds the puck"* *"causing an unnecessary stoppage of play"*. Anywhere on the ice, 74(b) is a minor for *"any player who deliberately holds the puck against the boards or any part of the goal in any manner, unless they are being checked by an opponent"*, and 55(a) makes deliberate delay a bench minor, its Note 2 telling referees to be strict on *"freezing the puck"*.
- **Hockey Canada** 10.1(a) Interpretation 3, clause v: *"[w]hen a goaltender leaves their crease, they will not be allowed to freeze the puck. If they do, they will be assessed a Minor penalty. No warning will be issued."*

Closer to the net a goalie has more latitude in every book — [Goaltender](../positions/goaltender.md) owns that test, and [Rules Primer](../foundation/rules_primer.md) the rules.

#### Who may hit the defenceman fetching it

```facts
Never: Take the contact with your back to anyone coming at you fetching a dead rim in your corner, and never duck. Skates parallel to the boards, forearm and hip to the wall, head up, chin off your chest
Never: Hit a player who has turned toward the wall as the forechecker. Where checking is allowed at all, take the puck or angle them off it instead
Rule: A rim forechecker's hit on you is at least a minor in USA Hockey Competitive Contact (604(c)), Hockey Canada U13 or below, female or Member-approved play (7.3(a)) and any CARHA game, avoidable contact too (49(a)). CARHA excuses an accident, except to the head or from behind into the boards or net
Rule: A minor is the floor for an illegal check on a rim retrieval — Hockey Canada 7.3(b) mandates a major and game misconduct once it injures, and 7.3(c), like CARHA 48(a), makes a deliberate attempt to injure a match penalty. No IHUK regulation prices a breach of its own band
Rule: Under USA Hockey a reckless check on a rim retrieval is a major plus a game misconduct in a Competitive Contact game (604(d)), and in any game Rule 602(a) says a match "shall be assessed" for reckless endangerment — plan on the match and a likely multi-game ban
Rule: In the PWHL a forechecker may angle her off a rim skating the same way, but extending "an arm, shoulder, or hip after angling an opponent", or stepping or gliding into her, is an illegal hit — at least a minor (Rule 52.1)
Rule: In Britain checking is set by your competition's Rules of Competition, not a rule book — IHUK's four: junior U14 up and NIHL 1 and 2 checking, U10, U12 and WNIHL not. Those four settle no other league — not the EIHL, NIHL National, BUIHA, the PNIHL or the SNL. ⚠️ **Ask; until you know, do not hit**
```

**If you are the forechecker,** ⚠️ **never hit a player who has turned toward the wall** — take the puck or angle them off it instead. **And know your game before you hit anyone at all: whether body checking is allowed turns on your classification or league — and never in a CARHA game.** In a non-checking game the hit is a penalty, and one that injures can end your night.

**If you are the defenceman, none of that takes anything off the posture, because the wall still arrives.** Even where checking is barred, Hockey Canada's 7.3 assesses no penalty for *"incidental contact of two opposing players in pursuit of the puck on the ice in the same direction"*, and the Note to USA Hockey 604(c) puts legal competitive contact *"in front of the goal or along the boards."* Take it parallel to the boards, head up.

🇬🇧 **In Britain the answer is in a competition regulation, not a rule book.** The **IHUK Rules of Competition 2026-27** make junior hockey non-checking at U10 and U12 and checking at U14, U16 and U19 — *"Bodychecking is NOT allowed in U10 matches."* NIHL Division 1 and 2 are checking; every WNIHL women's division is non-checking. ⚠️ **Those four settle no other league** — not the EIHL, NIHL National, BUIHA, the PNIHL, the SNL or other Scottish hockey, and the Elite League's Casebook does not mention body checking. ⚠️ **Ask your league before you hit anyone, and until you have an answer, do not.**

In women's hockey, chasing that rim, ⚠️ **do not use the boards on her:** IIHF Rule 101.1 says players *"are not allowed to use the boards to make contact with an opponent to eliminate her from the play, push her into the boards, or pin her along the boards"*. The PWHL lets a forechecker angle her off the puck skating the same way, but not extend *"an arm, shoulder, or hip after angling an opponent"*, or step or glide into her (52.1). [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md) has the league-by-league table, and [UK and England Rules](../foundation/uk_rules.md) quotes the four British regulations.

**What it costs.** ⚠️ **A minor is the floor, not the price.** In a **USA Hockey** Competitive Contact category — 12 & under and below, all Girls'/Women's classifications, all non-check Adult classifications — a body check on that retrieval is a minor (604(c)). **604(d)** makes a major plus a game misconduct mandatory for a player who *"recklessly endangers an opponent with a body check in a Competitive Contact category"*, and 604(e) lets the referee go to a match penalty for reckless endangerment. **602(a)** says a match *"shall be assessed"* to a player who *"recklessly endangers or attempts to injure"* an opponent — so plan on the match for a reckless hit, and a likely multi-game suspension.

Under **Hockey Canada** 7.3(a) a player who *"intentionally body-checks, bumps, shoves, or pushes any opposing player"* takes a minor at U13 and below, in female hockey and in Member-approved divisions. **7.3(b)** allows a major and game misconduct on *"the degree of violence of the impact"*, and makes them mandatory — *"must be assessed"* — where a player is injured by a check that would otherwise be a minor; **7.3(c)** is a match penalty for a player who *"deliberately attempts to or deliberately injures an opponent by body-checking."*

Under **CARHA** the forechecker may not hit that player in any game. 49(a) penalises intentional contact and contact a player *"does not avert"*, and makes it a major when intentional contact injures; 49(c) spares only accidental contact, and not to the head, face or neck (still a minor, 50(a)) or from behind into the boards or net (a match penalty, 53(b)). A major there ends the forechecker's game (30(a)) and brings at least a one-game suspension (32(d)), and a foul that puts the defenceman into the boards becomes a double minor (49(b)) — two of the three penalties that eject a CARHA player (32(a)). A deliberate attempt to injure is a match penalty (48(a)).

The four British regulations price no breach of their own band. The nearest is the U10 procedure, where the official *"can remove a player from the game for the remainder of the shift"* and a repeat offender comes off for the game. The IIHF book has no non-check classification to price it under. **Treat the band as the instruction and the price as unsettled.** The IIHF Glossary, in both editions, still lets players near the puck *"push and lean into each other provided that possession of the puck remains the sole object of the contact."* In women's hockey IIHF 101.1 makes *"[a]ny move by a Player to step or glide into an opposing Player"* at least a minor, and in the PWHL an illegal hit is at least a minor (52.1).

### Keeping it simple enough to work

```facts
Convention: Two or three plays maximum, per zone
Action: One-word calls from the centre at the dot, acknowledged by everyone — a play nobody heard is not a play
Key: Every play needs a defined failure branch: if we lose it, X does Y
Never: Get clever when there is confusion, no call, or a replacement taking the draw. Tie up in your own end instead, point shot in theirs
```

The failure mode is universal: teams install six plays, run each badly, and the players spend the drop trying to remember which one is on.

- **Two or three plays maximum**, per zone. One play run excellently beats six run adequately.
- **One-word calls.** The centre calls it at the dot; everyone acknowledges. A play nobody heard is not a play.
- **Every play needs a failure branch.** "If we lose it, X does Y." Most plays are drawn up only for the win, which is why teams that lose an offensive-zone draw look so disorganised.
- **The default is always the simple one.** Confusion, no call, or a replacement taking the draw: tie up in your own end, point shot in theirs.

## The Procedure and the Rules

**"The six books" are the NHL, the IIHF, the PWHL, USA Hockey, Hockey Canada and CARHA**, with the British amendments flagged separately. The **PWHL**'s faceoff chapter is the NHL's renumbered: Rule 78 for the NHL's 76, 83 for 81, 84 for 82. Those three were read in full against the NHL clause by clause, and **the one divergence is the re-drop line change** (PWHL 78.8, set out below), so wherever the faceoff rules below say "the NHL", a PWHL reader can read along at the other number. **⚠️ Where a sentence says a rule is some books' *only*, the PWHL is named there instead** — reading along cannot repair a word that shuts you out.

🇬🇧 **If you play U10 or U12 in IHUK's junior leagues, much of this section is not your game yet.** At U10 every stoppage restarts with a quick draw at centre ice, refereed by a coach told to *"not wait for teams to be set-up in a face-off formation"* and to *"[c]all the nearest player from each team"*. Penalties are *"the same as with other EIH junior leagues"* but *"no penalties are recorded"* — the official can take the offender off for the rest of the shift, and their own coach should take off one who keeps offending for the rest of the game. U12 has *"[n]o icing calls"*, and offside does not stop play — the attacking team must clear the zone, and *"[f]ailure to retreat quickly"* is a delay-of-game penalty. From U14 up, the draw is the one described here; [UK and England Rules](../foundation/uk_rules.md) sets out the junior formats.

### The sequence, start to finish

**In short:** Head straight to the dot and line up quickly; stalling to give your bench a rest is a penalty.

```facts
Rule: The visiting team gets up to five seconds to change, then the home team up to eight (NHL and IIHF Rule 82.2) — USA Hockey 204(a) and Hockey Canada 6.1(d) give the home team five, not eight
Rule: CARHA has no last change at all — Rule 19(g) gives both teams one shared ten-second window and names neither the home nor the visiting team, so there is no matchup to hunt off it
Rule: After the linesperson's whistle both teams have no more than five seconds to line up — NHL and IIHF Rule 76.3 and USA Hockey 613(c); Hockey Canada and CARHA write no five-second line-up clock at all
Rule: The sticks go down before the drop, and the order they go down in is not the same in every book (Rule 76.4)
Rule: The faceoff ends when the puck has been legally dropped (Rule 76.1)
Rule: The five-second limit is not enforced in the last two minutes of regulation or in overtime (NHL and IIHF Rule 76.3) — USA Hockey 613(c) writes no such exception and Hockey Canada and CARHA set no five-second line-up clock at all
Rule: Hockey Canada has no five-second line-up clock but does have an untimed one — after a referee's warning, failing to commence play or "unnecessarily delay[ing] the resumption of play" is delay of game (Hockey Canada 10.1(vii))
Rule: CARHA has no line-up clock either, and no warning — its Rule 57(c) lets the official replace a centre who is slow into position at that draw, so "no clock" is not "take your time"
Rule: The PWHL renumbers rather than rewrites — its Rule 84.2 gives the visitors five seconds and the home team eight, exactly as NHL and IIHF 82.2 do, and its whole faceoff chapter is the NHL's at Rule 78
Key: Last change is why the away centre so often finds themselves against a specialist — in every book read here but CARHA, which has none
```

1. **Line change.** The referee gives the **visiting team up to five seconds** to change, raises a hand, then gives the **home team up to eight** (Rule 82.2). This is "last change": the home team sees the visitors' personnel first, which is why the away centre so often finds themselves against a specialist.
2. **The referee lowers their hand.** No more changes.
3. **The linesperson blows the whistle.** Both teams have **no more than five seconds** to line up (Rule 76.3).
4. **The sticks go down** (Rule 76.4). Order matters — see below.
5. **The puck is dropped** between the two blades. The faceoff "ends when the puck has been legally dropped" (Rule 76.1).

**Go straight to the dot.** Dawdling to buy your bench a breath is a penalty, not a tactic. **In the last two minutes of regulation and in overtime the five-second line-up limit is not enforced** under the NHL and IIHF, so there is more time to set your alignment — and more time for both centres to fidget.

**Do not carry the eight seconds or the late-game relaxation into a North American game.** **USA Hockey** (204(a)) and **Hockey Canada** (6.1(d)) give the home team five seconds, not eight — last change survives, three seconds shorter. **CARHA has no last change at all**: Rule 19(g) gives both teams one shared ten-second window, so do not plan a matchup off it. **USA Hockey** 613(c) keeps its five-second line-up limit to the final whistle. **Hockey Canada and CARHA have no line-up clock**, but no clock is not "take your time" — an official can still hurry you.

**What it costs.** Rule 82.3: *"Players must proceed directly to the location of the face-off… Any attempts to delay the game by stalling or otherwise unnecessary actions by either team shall result in the assessment of a bench minor penalty for delaying the game"*. The IIHF prints it in the same words, and unlike the slow-to-the-dot warning in 82.2(vii), it comes with no warning first. ⚠️ **Do not read "no five-second limit" as "take as long as you like".** Under **Hockey Canada** 10.1(vii), a team that *"after a warning by the Referee, fails to place the correct number of players on the ice and commence play"*, or a player who *"unnecessarily delays the resumption of play"*, is delay of game. Under **CARHA** 57(c) the official may *"order them replaced at that face-off"* the first time a centre is slow into position, with no warning.

### Who puts their stick down first

**In short:** When you must place first, vary the blade angle you show; when you place second, take your look.

```facts
Rule: At centre ice the visiting player places their stick first (NHL Rule 76.4)
Rule: At the other eight spots the defending player places first, then the attacking player (NHL Rule 76.4)
Rule: The IIHF book — the one every level of British hockey is played under — matches the NHL word for word at all nine spots, in both its 2025/26 and 2026/27 editions (IIHF Rule 76.4)
Rule: ⚠️ **The IHUK In-House Rules 2026-27 override that across the Home Countries — England, Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland, but not the Elite League — saying "Either centre can now put their stick down first for a face-off"** (Section 10, Rule 76)
Rule: The Elite League is the exception: those In-House Rules never name it and the EIHL Casebook writes no Rule 76 at all, so an EIHL draw sits on IIHF 76.4 unamended and the defending centre's stick still goes down first at the eight spots
Rule: ⚠️ **The In-House Rules require the whole blade in the white**, not the toe IIHF 76.7(IV) asks for — "the whole of their stick blades (not toe or heel)" (Section 10, Rule 76); short of it, your centre is replaced unless the draw follows an icing. The EIHL, which they never name, keeps the IIHF toe
Rule: USA Hockey reverses this — the attacking player places first everywhere but centre ice (USA Hockey Rule 613(a))
Rule: Hockey Canada is a third answer — the visiting player places first at every spot, attacking or defending, though a home player who wants to place first "are allowed to do so, if it does not cause a problem" (Rule 6.2(a), Interpretation 1)
Rule: CARHA prescribes no stick-down order at all — its Rule 57 names neither the visiting player nor the defending one, so under that book there is no blade to wait for and none to claim
Rule: The IIHF Situation Handbook 2026/27 reads the IIHF's own stick order as an option — an attacking centre may place first if they choose, and the linesperson allows it (Situation 76.53)
Key: Placing first shows your blade angle first, so under NHL and IIHF rules the advantage of going second is the attacking team's
Action: When you are the one who must show your hand, vary the angle you show
```

Putting your stick down first shows your blade angle first, which you would rather not give away — and who goes first differs by book.

- **NHL and IIHF** (both editions, word for word, Rule 76.4): at centre ice the **visiting** player places first; at the other eight spots the **defending** player places first, *"followed immediately by the attacking player."* So the common shorthand "the visitor goes first" is true at one spot of nine, and **at the other eight the advantage of going second is the attacking team's.** At a neutral-zone dot "defending" means the team defending the goal at that end — the standard reading; the book does not spell it out.
- **The IIHF 2026/27 Situation Handbook reads that order as an option** (Situation 76.53): an attacking centre who chooses to place first is allowed to, and the linesperson drops as soon as the defender's stick is down. It is an interpretation, not rule text, but nothing in 76.4 contradicts it.
- **USA Hockey reverses it** (613(a)): *"The attacking player shall be the first to place their stick on the ice, except for a center ice face-off"*, where the visitor goes first. So in North American rec or youth hockey the advantage of going second is yours when you are *defending*.
- **Hockey Canada** (6.2(a), Interpretation 1): the **visiting** player places first *"for all face-offs… regardless of whether they are the attacking or defending team"*. A first violation is a warning, a second in the same stoppage *"may result in a Minor penalty"*, and a home player who wants to go first *"are allowed to do so, if it does not cause a problem."*
- **CARHA prescribes no order at all** (Rule 57), so there showing your blade first gives nothing away.

> **🇬🇧 If you play in Britain, which order is yours depends on your league — and across the Home Countries it is none of the above.** The **IHUK In-House Rules 2026-27**, *Section 10 – Game Flow*, amend Rule 76: *"**Either centre can now put their stick down first for a face-off.**"* So the order tells you nothing: set your blade when you are ready and watch the hand. ⚠️ **But put the whole blade in the white** — the IIHF asks only for the toe, and a British linesperson replaces your centre for less, except after an icing.
>
> **The Elite League is the exception.** The In-House Rules never name it, and the **Motorpoint EIHL Casebook 26-27** writes no Rule 76, while its Preamble makes *"the official 2026 - 2027 IIHF Rulebook and IIHF Situation Handbook"* the ruling *"[f]or all rules not mentioned"*. So an EIHL draw keeps IIHF 76.4: the defending centre's stick down first at the eight spots, the visitor's at centre ice, and only the toe in the white. **Do not carry the In-House "either centre" permission into an Elite League game** — it is the defending centre who loses by it. *(The League issues rule updates in season, so ask whether a Rule 76 section has since been added.)*

**What it costs.** Declining to put your stick down is no way round any of this. Under the NHL and both IIHF editions a centre who will *"refrain from placing their stick on the ice"* is replaced (NHL 76.3(ii), IIHF 76.3(II)); USA Hockey 613(d) and CARHA 57(c) let the official replace a centre who will not take position when directed; Hockey Canada 6.2(a) and 6.2(d) eject the centre. A second violation by your team on that draw is a bench minor under IIHF 76.7, which IHUK does not amend.

### Positioning requirements

**In short:** Take the draw square, whole blade flat in the white until the drop, and keep everyone else outside the circle and behind the hashes.

```facts
Position: Square to your opponent's end of the rink, roughly one stick length apart (Rule 76.4)
Rule: Feet either side of the restraining lines that run parallel to the side boards — touching the lines is fine (NHL and IIHF 76.7(iv) define this, as does PWHL 78.7(iv); 76.4 does not); Hockey Canada 6.2(a) says "clear of the face-off restraining lines", USA Hockey 613(a) "clear of the ice markings"
Rule: The toe of your skate blade must not cross the restraining lines perpendicular to the side boards (NHL and IIHF Rule 76.7(iv), PWHL 78.7(iv))
Rule: At least the toe of your stick blade must be in the white of the spot — NHL and IIHF Rule 76.7(iv) and Hockey Canada 6.2(a) — but not under the IHUK In-House Rules and not under CARHA 57(a), which asks for "the full blade of their sticks flat on the ice"
Rule: USA Hockey 613(a) takes the toe too (Casebook 613 Situation 2) but adds a demand no other book writes — at end-zone spots both blades must be "in contact with the nearest white area of the face-off spot and clear of the center red area"; centre ice and the neutral zone get no blade standard
Rule: Under NHL and IIHF Rule 76.7(iv), the blade must remain in the white until the puck is dropped; lifting it early is a faceoff violation
Rule: ⚠️ **Under the IHUK In-House Rules 2026-27 the toe is not enough — put "the whole of their stick blades (not toe or heel) in the white portion of the face-off circle"** (Section 10, Rule 76); falling short replaces your centre except on a draw after an icing; none of this reaches the Elite League
Rule: No other player may enter the circle or come within fifteen feet of the two takers — NHL 76.6, USA Hockey 613(b), Hockey Canada 6.2(a) and CARHA 57(a); IIHF 76.6 writes it as 4.5 m
Rule: At end-zone draws the other eight skaters keep their bodies on their own side of the hash marks — the restraining lines on the outer edge of the circle (NHL and IIHF Rule 76.6, USA Hockey 613(b))
Rule: The attacking team's four other skaters set their positions first, then the defending team counters — NHL and IIHF 76.6 and Hockey Canada's Interpretation 1 to 6.2(a); USA Hockey 613(b) and CARHA 57(a) write no such right
Rule: The goalie may not participate in a faceoff (Rule 76.1)
```

**For the centre** (Rules 76.4 and 76.7):
- Stand **squarely facing your opponent's end**, roughly **one stick length apart**.
- Feet either side of the restraining lines that run parallel to the side boards — touching them is fine under the NHL, IIHF and PWHL, but Hockey Canada and USA Hockey want you clear of them.
- The **toe of your skate must not cross** the restraining lines perpendicular to the side boards.
- **Put the whole blade flat in the white, and keep it there until the puck is dropped** — that satisfies every book. The books ask for different amounts:
  - **NHL, IIHF and Hockey Canada:** at least the toe of the blade in the white (76.7(iv); 6.2(a)).
  - **CARHA:** the toe at the end-zone and neutral-zone spots, but its opening paragraph, scoped to no spot, wants *"the full blade of their sticks flat on the ice"* (57(a)).
  - **USA Hockey:** any part of the blade will do (Casebook 613 Situation 2), but at an end-zone spot both blades must be in the white *"**and clear of the center red area**"* (613(a)), which no other book writes. A whole blade in the white can never be across the red.
  - 🇬🇧 **The IHUK In-House Rules:** *"the whole of their stick blades (not toe or heel)"*. ⚠️ **A British centre who puts only the toe down, as the IIHF book says, is in violation** and is replaced, except after an icing. The Elite League keeps the IIHF toe.

**For everyone else** (Rule 76.6):
- No other player may enter the circle or come within **fifteen feet** of the two takers. All six books carry it — IIHF 76.6 writes it as *"4.5m"*, Hockey Canada and CARHA as 4.57 m (15 ft).
- At end-zone draws all eight other skaters keep their bodies **on their own side of the hash marks** (NHL and IIHF 76.6, USA Hockey 613(b)). **Hockey Canada and CARHA** draw the line by skate rather than body: encroachment is a skate *"inside the face-off circle, on the face-off circle, or in the area between the hash marks"*.
- **The attacking team's four other skaters set their positions first**, and only then does the defending team counter. An attacker who repositions after that is charged with the violation — even if the defending centre backs out of the dot in response.

Where that rule applies it is a real asset to the defending team: you see where their net-front threat is going before you decide who takes them. **Hockey Canada writes it too** (6.2(a) Interpretation 1: *"the defending team has the right to set up its defence"*). **USA Hockey and CARHA do not**, so there do not build an own-end alignment on watching their net-front man commit first.

**The goalie may not participate in a faceoff** (Rule 76.1). This matters when your goalie is pulled — see "Situational Faceoffs".

## Violations, Ejections and the Bench Minor

**The list of violations is much the same in every book; what differs is what it costs.** Under the NHL a violation throws your centre out of the circle. The **IIHF 2025/26** book issued **a warning to your team** and re-dropped with the same centre.

> **The IIHF 2026/27 edition moved to the NHL's ejection.** Its 76.6 has the encroaching team's centre *"ejected from the face-off circle"*, its 76.7 dropped the warning for premature movement and replaced the warning for pre-drop contact with an order that the centre be *"replaced"*, and its 76.3 and 76.4 replace the offending centre rather than warning them. The 2025/26 position is kept below because the rest is unchanged — where a skate may be, the two-violation bench minor, the skate-win violation, the stick order — and because knowing what moved is how you spot a league still administering the old book.
>
> **🇬🇧 In Britain, read the NHL column for who gets removed.** Across the Home Countries you need not even settle the IIHF edition: the **IHUK In-House Rules 2026-27** write the removal in themselves, on every draw but one following an icing. ⚠️ **The Elite League does not run those rules** — they never name it, and its Casebook writes no Rule 76 — **so an EIHL draw runs IIHF 2026/27 Rule 76 unamended.** Neither 2026/27 document has been read in full; each was read for the faceoff rules and for the other rules cited here as reading the same in both editions.

### What gets your centre thrown out of the dot

**In short:** Line up onside, blade in the white, wingers behind the hashes: violations can cost your centre the dot, and pre-drop contact a minor.

```facts
Rule: A faceoff violation normally ejects the offending team's centre from that draw, replaced "by any teammate then on the ice" (NHL Rules 76.4, 76.6)
Rule: Under the IIHF 2025/26 book a faceoff violation was a warning to the team and a re-drop, with the centre staying in the dot (IIHF 2025/26 Rules 76.6, 76.7)
Rule: The IIHF 2026/27 book ejects the centre from the circle for a violation instead, as the NHL does (IIHF 2026/27 Rule 76.6)
Rule: Under the IHUK In-House Rules 2026-27 — the Home Countries, not the Elite League — the linesperson replaces the centre for every violation except on a draw following an icing (Section 10, Rule 76); IHUK does not amend IIHF 76.7, so a second violation on that draw is still a bench minor
Rule: Premature movement takes nobody out of the dot under the NHL or either IIHF book — the draw "must be conducted again" (76.7; the 2025/26 edition warns the team first) — but the In-House Rules replace the centre except after an icing, and the Elite League, which they never name, keeps the re-drop
Rule: Hockey Canada names early movement a violation (6.5(a)) and ejects the taker for one (6.2(d)), except on a Junior draw after an icing; USA Hockey and CARHA do not name it
Rule: Improper stick placement, or skates in the wrong place relative to the restraining lines, is a violation (NHL and IIHF Rule 76.7, in both IIHF editions)
Rule: A teammate putting a skate inside the circle or across the hash marks is a violation (NHL and IIHF 76.7, both editions) — but merely moving is not, and the premature-movement clause names the centre, not them
Rule: Physical contact before the drop is a violation whoever makes it, but the minor is the taker's — mandatory under USA Hockey 613(e), Hockey Canada 6.2(c) and CARHA 57(b), discretionary under NHL and IIHF 76.7; a teammate's contact ejects your centre instead (Hockey Canada 6.2(a), CARHA 57(a))
Rule: Under the IIHF 2026/27 book that contact costs you the centre as well — 76.7 has the linesperson "order the center of the offending team replaced", where the 2025/26 book only warned the centre (IIHF 76.7, both editions)
Rule: Lining up offside for the draw is a violation in all six books — NHL and IIHF 76.3(V), PWHL 78.3(v), Hockey Canada 6.2(a), CARHA 57(a), USA Hockey 613(d) — and in every one but the IIHF 2025/26 edition, which warned, it is your centre who is replaced
```

**Under the NHL most violations eject the offending team's centre from that draw**, replaced *"by any teammate then on the ice"* (Rule 76.4). ⚠️ **The exception is a centre who moves early, or a puck dropped unfairly: that costs only a re-drop under the NHL and both IIHF editions.** **The Home Countries are the exception to the exception** — the In-House Rules, the *"overriding interpretation of how the IIHF Rule Book is to be applied domestically"*, replace the centre for every violation except after an icing. ⚠️ **The Elite League runs the IIHF rule, so an early centre there still gets the re-drop.** **Hockey Canada ejects the centre too** — 6.5(a) names early movement a violation and 6.2(d) ejects the taker — except on a Junior draw after an icing; USA Hockey and CARHA do not name it. The violations (Rule 76.7):

- **Moving prematurely** before the drop. ⚠️ **The rule names the centre, not everybody** — *"[i]f a center should move prematurely prior to the face-off, or if the Referee or Linesperson shall have dropped the puck unfairly… it must be conducted again"*. The 2025/26 IIHF edition — the one the In-House Rules' Introduction names — adds *"A warning shall be issued to the offending team"* first. A winger who twitches is caught only by **encroachment**, where the movement puts a skate where it may not be: *"[a] player not taking the face-off may be moving, provided they are outside the circle and on-side"* (USA Hockey Casebook 613 Situation 7).
- **Improper stick placement or positioning** — the blade not in the white, lifted before the drop, or skates in the wrong place relative to the restraining lines. Declining to put your stick down does not get round it in any book (see "Who puts their stick down first").
- **Encroachment by a teammate** — a skate inside the circle, or across the hash marks, before the drop. Under Hockey Canada and CARHA a skate on the circle line counts too, and a stick between the hash marks counts under those two and USA Hockey.
- **Physical contact with an opponent** before the drop — a violation whoever makes it. **Leaning on the other centre can cost you a minor as well as the dot**; see "What it costs" below. ⚠️ **Under USA Hockey, Hockey Canada and CARHA the taker's minor is mandatory.** A winger who leans on the opponent beside them is not given the faceoff minor, but it is still a violation — and it is **your centre** who is ejected for it (Hockey Canada 6.2(a), CARHA 57(a)). [Winger](../positions/winger.md) sets that out for the player it lands on.
- **Lining up offside** for the draw. ⚠️ **All six books make it a violation, and in every one but the superseded IIHF 2025/26 edition your centre is replaced** — NHL 76.3(v), IIHF 76.3(V), PWHL 78.3(v), Hockey Canada 6.2(a), CARHA 57(a), USA Hockey 613(d). Line up onside, everywhere.

**What it costs.** ⚠️ **For the two takers, pre-drop contact can bring a minor in every book, and under USA Hockey, Hockey Canada and CARHA it must.** NHL 76.7 (IIHF 76.7 the same) lets the referee *"at his discretion impose a minor penalty or penalties"*. USA Hockey 613(e): *"[a] minor penalty for interference shall be assessed to any player facing-off who makes any physical contact"*; Hockey Canada 6.2(c): *"the Referee will assess a Minor penalty or penalties"*; CARHA 57(b): *"the Referee shall assess a Minor penalty"*. The NHL, the PWHL and the IIHF 2026/27 edition also have the *"Linesperson… order the center of the offending team replaced"* for it, where IIHF 2025/26 only warned — so under all three, leaning on the other centre loses you the draw outright, except as the first violation after an icing (NHL 76.4, PWHL 78.4, IIHF 2026/27 76.4).

Those penalty clauses reach the takers, not the players on the hash marks. But where body contact itself is barred, a winger's lean can be penalised anyway: under CARHA in every game (49(a), a minor or a major), in USA Hockey's Competitive Contact categories (604(c) Note), and in Hockey Canada's U13-and-below, female and Member-approved divisions (7.3(a)).

#### Being late to the dot — the one softening, and who writes it

```facts
Rule: Being late to the faceoff location, and therefore offside for the draw, is warned once in the game under NHL and IIHF 76.7 — the warning going to the team and its coach — and the rule says the centre "is not ejected from the face-off"
Rule: Timing your arrival is not the same as being late — a centre who "attempts to arrive at the face-off spot just as the five seconds elapses in an attempt to gain an advantage" is removed and replaced under NHL and IIHF 2026/27 Rule 76.4, unless the draw follows an icing
Rule: Once your team has had its one lateness warning, being slow to the dot again is a bench minor for delay of game — "Slow Proceeding to Face-off Location" (NHL and IIHF Rule 76.7)
Rule: USA Hockey 613(d) and CARHA 57(c) let the official order a player facing off who fails to take their proper position when directed "replaced", first time, at discretion; a USA Hockey slow line change to the dot is warned, then a bench minor (Casebook 613 Situation 13)
Rule: Hockey Canada reaches the same place in two steps, which is a reading of two rules together and not one sentence — 6.5(a) names "late arrival" a faceoff violation and 6.2(d) ejects the taker; 6.5(a)'s warning is Junior divisions after an icing only
Rule: Under the IHUK In-House Rules 2026-27 nobody has settled whether teammates' lateness costs your centre — IIHF 76.7 warns the team, the In-House Rules replace the centre "for all violations" bar post-icing draws — so plan on losing the dot. The Elite League keeps the IIHF warning
Rule: The carve-out shields the centre from their teammates' lateness in both IIHF editions — 2025/26's 76.7 says the centre "will not receive a warning", 2026/27's that the centre "is not ejected from the 'face-off'"
Action: Be at the dot on time — the safe play is the same under every book, and it is free
```

**Be at the dot on time — the safe play is the same under every book, and it is free.**

**Under the NHL and the IIHF, players simply late to the faceoff location**, and therefore offside for the draw, get **one warning per game**, to the team and its coach, and Rule 76.7 says *"the offending team's center is not ejected from the face-off."* Any later instance is a bench minor, *"Delay of Game – Slow Proceeding to Face-off Location."* It shields the centre from their teammates' lateness, not their own: if they are *"simply slow getting to the face-off spot"*, the puck is dropped without them (Rule 76.4).

**Timing your arrival to the clock is not the same as being late.** NHL Rule 76.4 removes a centre who *"attempts to arrive at the face-off spot just as the five seconds elapses in an attempt to gain an advantage to win the face-off"*, and the IIHF 2026/27 edition prints the same sentence (2025/26 warned the centre instead). Both carve out one draw: after an icing they are warned rather than removed. So the free attempt under "The icing exception" below is the only one either book gives you. ⚠️ **Time your arrival to the clock on any other draw and you lose the dot.**

**Both IIHF editions protect the centre; the 2025/26 book words it two ways.** Its 76.7 says that when players are late the centre *"will not receive a warning"*, while its 82.2(VII) already says the centre *"is not ejected"*. The 2026/27 book's 76.7 uses the second wording: *"the offending team's center is not ejected from the 'face-off'"*.

**Elsewhere, a slow centre can lose the draw.** ⚠️ **USA Hockey 613(d) and CARHA 57(c) let the official replace that centre the first time:** where a player facing off *"fails to take their proper position immediately when directed"* (CARHA: *"the proper position"*), the official may order them replaced for that draw, at discretion. A team merely slow changing to the dot is different: USA Hockey's Casebook (613 Situation 13) warns the bench, then gives a bench minor, and leaves the centre in. **Hockey Canada** reaches the same place through two rules read together: 6.5(a) names *"late arrival"* a faceoff violation, and 6.2(d) has the official eject the taker; 6.5(a)'s warning is for Junior divisions after an icing only.

> 🇬🇧 **⚠️ Across the Home Countries, do not plan on keeping the dot.** The IIHF rule charges teammates' lateness to the team, and the 2025/26 book the In-House Rules adopt says the centre *"will not receive a warning for a 'face-off' violation"*. But the In-House Rules say *"[w]ith the exception of face-offs following icings, the linesperson shall immediately replace the centre for all violations"*, and a player lined up offside is a violation under IIHF 76.3(V). Whether that reaches a team that is merely late is not written down, and where the texts differ the In-House Rules win: they call themselves the *"overriding interpretation"*, and IHUK's Rules of Competition for NIHL 1 and 2, the WNIHL and junior hockey say the IHUK wording *"will take precedence for domestic competition"*. **The Elite League has none of this**: under the 2026/27 book it plays, the once-a-game warning stands and the centre *"is not ejected"*.

### Getting low without putting your head in it

```facts
Rule: Helmet-to-helmet contact with the other centre before the drop is named in the rulebook as a faceoff violation (IIHF 76.7(III), both editions); the 2025/26 book gave it a first warning, the 2026/27 book deleted that warning
Rule: The IIHF Situation Handbook 2026/27 tells the linesperson to "immediately eject the center that is responsible for the helmet to helmet contact", and to eject both where they cannot tell who caused it — the 2025/26 handbook said warn (Situation 76.43)
Never: Lean in until your head is against the other centre's — heads "almost ear to ear" is IIHF Situation 76.42, and both editions of the handbook say it should not be allowed
Rule: A centre who will not move back when the linesperson breaks up an ear-to-ear stance is warned under the IIHF 2025/26 Situation Handbook and removed from the faceoff under the 2026/27 edition (Situation 76.42)
Rule: Under the In-House Rules — the Home Countries bar the Elite League — do not plan on that first warning: their Introduction names the 2025/26 edition, but their own Rule 76 replaces the centre for every violation bar a post-icing draw, and helmet-to-helmet contact is one (Section 10, Rule 76)
Rule: Helmet-to-helmet contact at the dot is physical contact before the drop as well, so the referee's discretionary minor under NHL and IIHF 76.7 sits on top of the violation — and that minor is mandatory under USA Hockey 613(e), Hockey Canada 6.2(c) and CARHA 57(b)
Action: Get your chest over the puck, not your head over the dot — depth comes from the knees and hips, and ear-to-ear is the position the IIHF handbook tells linespersons to break up
```

**"Get low, chest over the puck" can put your head where the rulebook does not want it.** Two centres both doing it well finish with their heads close together over the dot. **Get the depth from your knees and hips, not by pushing your head out** — the low stance is loaded, not leaning. And if the other centre leans into you, move back when the linesperson tells you both to.

**The IIHF Situation Handbook takes the position twice.** **Situation 76.42** — heads *"side by side, almost ear to ear"* — says *"[t]his should not be allowed. The Linesperson should instruct both players to move back"*; a refusal was *"warned"* in the 2025/26 handbook and is *"removed from the face-off"* in the 2026/27 one. **Situation 76.43** — *"helmet to helmet contact"* — moved from *"immediately warn the center that is responsible"* (and *"warn both centers"* where it cannot be told) to *"immediately eject"* the centre (or *"eject both centers"*).

**What it costs.** The rulebook names the head too. IIHF Rule 76.7(III) makes helmet-to-helmet contact before the drop a faceoff violation; the 2025/26 edition gave a *"first 'face-off' violation warning"*, and the 2026/27 edition deleted it. ⚠️ **So the free first head bump is gone**: the 2026/27 handbook says eject, and the In-House Rules replace the centre for every violation bar a post-icing draw. It is also pre-drop contact, so the referee's discretionary minor under NHL and IIHF 76.7 sits on top — **mandatory under USA Hockey 613(e), Hockey Canada 6.2(c) and CARHA 57(b)**, which write no exception for the head.

### The rule everybody gets wrong

**In short:** Keep skates and stick behind your hash mark and skates off the circle line; your encroachment gets your centre thrown out.

```facts
Rule: A teammate moving into the circle ejects the centre, not the teammate (NHL Rule 76.6)
Rule: Under the IIHF 2025/26 book a teammate's encroachment cost the team its one warning for that draw, not the centre (IIHF 2025/26 Rule 76.6)
Rule: The IIHF 2026/27 book ejects the centre for a teammate's encroachment, and the IHUK In-House Rules 2026-27 replace the centre for every violation but a post-icing one, leaving the team one mistake from the bench minor of IIHF 76.7 (IIHF 2026/27 Rule 76.6; In-House Section 10, Rule 76)
Rule: Both skates stay outside the circle and outside the hash marks (NHL and IIHF Rule 76.7, in both IIHF editions; PWHL 78.7)
Rule: Under the NHL, IIHF and PWHL a skate may touch the circle line or the hash-mark line — crossing it is what does it (76.7, both IIHF editions; PWHL 78.7); under Hockey Canada and CARHA a skate on the circle is already encroachment; USA Hockey wants skates "completely behind" the hash marks
Rule: Under the NHL, IIHF and PWHL your stick may be inside the circle or between the hash marks if it touches no opponent or their stick (76.7, both IIHF editions; PWHL 78.7); USA Hockey, Hockey Canada and CARHA bar it between the hash marks
Rule: USA Hockey allows no reaching stick — its Casebook answers "No" to a stick between the hash marks: "Both players must be completely behind their respective hash marks. This includes all sticks and all skates" (Rule 613, Situation 8)
Rule: Hockey Canada and CARHA split it — both glossaries accept a stick inside the circle but make one "in the area between the hash marks" encroachment, and count a skate "on the face-off circle" as encroachment too
Action: Tell your linemates: skates and stick behind your own hash mark, skates off the circle line — legal in all six books; the reach the NHL, IIHF and PWHL allow is not every book's
```

**Encroachment by a teammate gets the CENTRE ejected, not the teammate.** So if your winger leans over the hash marks to time a jump, **you lose your best draw-taker for the most important draw of the game**, and the winger stays. It costs nothing to prevent: tell your linemates.

**The instruction that survives all six books is the short one: keep your skates and your stick behind your own hash mark, and your skates off the circle line.** That is legal everywhere, needs no book named, and the reach it gives up is worth nothing against what a violation costs your centre.

**The looser permissions are the NHL's, the IIHF's and the PWHL's, not every book's.** Under those three, both skates stay outside the circle and the hash marks but **skate contact with the line is permitted**, and your **stick** may reach inside *"provided there is no physical contact with his opponent or his opponent's stick"* (76.7(i)–(ii); IIHF the same in both editions, PWHL 78.7(i)–(ii)). So there you can reach; you cannot step. ⚠️ **The other three books do not hand you the reach**:

- **USA Hockey: no reach at all.** Its Casebook (613 Situation 8) answers *"**No.** … Both players must be completely behind their respective hash marks. **This includes all sticks and all skates.**"*
- **Hockey Canada: half a reach, and** ⚠️ **no touching the line.** Its Glossary makes it encroachment to have a skate *"inside the face-off circle, on the face-off circle, or in the area between the hash marks. It is acceptable for the player's stick to be inside the face-off circle but not in the area between the hash marks."* It also reaches players *"in motion while a face-off is taking place"*, where USA Hockey expressly permits a moving player *"provided they are outside the circle and on-side"* (Casebook 613 Situation 7).
- **CARHA: the same half reach as Hockey Canada.** Its Glossary lets a stick inside the circle but not between the hash marks, and a skate merely touching the circle line is already encroachment.

**What it costs.** NHL Rule 76.6: if a player other than the taker *"moves into the face-off circle prior to the dropping of the puck, then the offending team's player taking the face-off shall be ejected from the face-off circle."* Rule 76.7 adds the escalation, quoted as printed: *"When a [sic] least two face-off violations have been committed by the same team during the same face-off, this team shall be penalized with a bench minor penalty to the offending team."* (The missing word is the rulebook's own.) ⚠️ **In Britain this is the sentence that changed:** the IIHF 2025/26 book gave only a *"warning for a 'face-off' violation"*, but IIHF 2026/27 Rule 76.6 printed the NHL's ejection, and the In-House Rules reach the same place on every draw but one after an icing. So a British winger creeping over the hash marks costs their team its centre, and a second violation on that draw is still a bench minor.

### The second violation

```facts
Rule: Two faceoff violations by the same team on the same draw is a bench minor under NHL and IIHF Rule 76.7
Rule: USA Hockey routes it to the offender instead — 613(d) makes a team's second violation "of any of the provisions of this subsection" on the same draw "a minor penalty for delay of game being assessed to the player committing the second violation", not a bench minor
Rule: Hockey Canada 6.2(e) makes a team's second faceoff violation on the same draw a discretionary minor, "may be assessed" under Rule 10.1 (viii) — mandatory, "will be assessed", if the offender then contacts the linesperson before they can clear
Rule: CARHA 57(d) is discretionary and limb-scoped — a second violation "of any of the provisions of Section (a)" "may be assessed a Minor penalty"; Section (a) names a non-taker who "moves offside, makes physical contact with an opponent, or encroaches on the face-off circle"
Rule: So a winger's pre-drop contact under CARHA does feed 57(d). What sits outside that limb is the taker's own contact, because 57(b) — scoped to "no player facing-off" — already makes that a mandatory minor on the first offence
Rule: USA Hockey 613(d) is limb-scoped too, to "any of the provisions of this subsection" — 613(d)'s own. Both books scope it, but not to the same content: 613(d) reaches a non-taker's contact only through "fails to maintain their proper position"
Rule: ⚠️ **Hockey Canada's discretion vanishes if the second violation runs into the official** — a player who contacts the linesperson before they have "reasonable time to vacate the face-off location" "will be assessed a Minor penalty" (Hockey Canada 6.2(e))
Rule: Any of the four on-ice officials may identify a faceoff violation, not just the linesperson dropping the puck (NHL Rule 76.7)
Action: With a replacement in the dot keep it simple: lift or press their stick with yours, then play the puck. Never a hand on their stick or your stick on their hands, and never hook it or hold it down
```

**After a first violation, nobody moves early, nobody leans, nobody reaches.** The replacement taker should keep it to a clean tie-up: **lift or press their stick with yours and then play the puck — never a hand on their stick or your stick on their hands, and never hook it or hold it down**, because each of those can cost a minor of its own. ⚠️ **Under Hockey Canada, let the linesperson clear the dot before you commit**, and take the re-drop straight.

**What it costs.** Under NHL and IIHF 76.7 (both editions) two violations by the same team on the same draw is a bench minor for *"Delay of Game – Face-off Violation"*; the IIHF Situation Handbook has any skater the team designates serve it (Situations 17.3, 17.4). Any of the **four on-ice officials** may call a violation, not just the one dropping the puck (76.7). Under the IIHF 2026/27 book a first violation for encroachment, contact, or a taker's stance or stick now removes your centre *and* leaves the team one mistake from the bench minor. Moving early is still a violation, but it only re-drops, and players merely late to the dot are warned once a game. After an icing the first violation is normally a warning, but in a new Situation, 81.18, where both teams changed players for coincidental penalties before that draw, the handbook asks *"Is the center removed or just warned?"* and answers *"The offending center is removed."*

The other three books write it differently. **USA Hockey 613(d)** gives *"a minor penalty for delay of game being assessed to the player committing the second violation"* — the skater who did it sits — and scopes it to *"any of the provisions of this subsection"*, so it reaches a non-taker's contact only through *"fails to maintain their proper position"*. **Hockey Canada 6.2(e)** says the team *"may be assessed a Minor penalty"*. **CARHA 57(d)** is permissive too, for a second violation *"of any of the provisions of Section (a)"* — which includes a non-taker who *"makes physical contact with an opponent"*; the taker's own contact is already a mandatory minor on the first offence under 57(b).

**One case has no discretion at all.** ⚠️ **Under Hockey Canada, a second violation that runs into the official is a minor:** where the offender *"subsequently contacts the Linesperson before the Linesperson has had reasonable time to vacate the face-off location"*, a minor *"will be assessed"* (6.2(e)).

### The icing exception — which books grant it, and to whom

```facts
Rule: Under the NHL, the PWHL and the IIHF 2026/27 book, the first faceoff violation after an icing does not remove the centre, on either team (NHL and IIHF 76.4, PWHL 78.4)
Rule: The centre is warned instead, and any subsequent violation by that team is a bench minor (NHL Rule 76.4)
Rule: The IIHF 2025/26 book had no post-icing carve-out because it needed none — every first violation on every draw was already a warning (IIHF 2025/26 Rules 76.6, 76.7)
Rule: The IIHF 2026/27 book adopted the NHL's post-icing carve-out in the NHL's own words, because it also adopted the ejection it exempts you from (IIHF 2026/27 Rule 76.4)
Rule: The In-House Rules have the carve-out too — their 2026-27 Rule 76 replaces the centre for every violation "[w]ith the exception of face-offs following icings" (Section 10, Rule 76); it is not a free draw, because IHUK does not amend IIHF 76.7 and a second violation is still a bench minor
Rule: Hockey Canada writes the carve-out but rations it by division — in Junior divisions only, any post-icing faceoff violation is a warning and a subsequent one a minor (Hockey Canada Rules 6.5(a), 6.5(b))
Rule: USA Hockey and CARHA write no post-icing carve-out at all — after an icing the centre comes off exactly as on any other draw (USA Hockey Rule 613, CARHA Rule 57)
Key: The warning is per team, so each side has its own free one
Action: After an icing, use your free attempt to cheat the timing, and expect their centre to use theirs — but find out which book, and under Hockey Canada which division, before you plan on one
```

**On a draw after an icing, each team gets one free violation under the NHL book — and so does the other team.** Rule 76.4: *"When an icing infraction occurs, the first face-off violation by either team will not result in the center being removed. The center will be warned by the Linesperson that the team has committed their first face-off violation, and any subsequent violation by that team will result in a bench minor penalty for delay of game - face-off violation being assessed."*

So on an own-end draw after your icing, with a tired line that cannot change, you get one free attempt to cheat the timing — and so does the team that has just pinned you. It is the **first** violation only, per team; the next is a bench minor. Good centres use it and expect it.

> **🇬🇧 In Britain this now reads exactly as written.** The IIHF 2025/26 book had no post-icing carve-out because every first violation was already a warning. Having adopted the NHL's ejection, the **2026/27** edition adopted the NHL's exemption too, word for word at 76.4. **And the British answer no longer depends on the edition**, because the In-House Rules carve out post-icing draws themselves. ⚠️ **It is not a free pass:** IHUK does not amend Rule 76.7, so a **second** violation by your team on that draw is still a bench minor, and the In-House sentence does not repeat the 2026/27 limit to the first face-off violation — treat one attempt as what you have.

**What it costs elsewhere.** ⚠️ **Only one of the other three North American books gives you the free attempt, and only in Junior.** Hockey Canada 6.5(a): *"[i]n Junior divisions, on a face-off following an icing, any face-off violation, including but not limited to late arrival, encroachment, or early movement, will result in a warning"*; outside Junior your centre comes off as on any other draw, and 6.5(b) makes a later violation on that stoppage a minor. **USA Hockey (613) and CARHA (57) write no carve-out**: both USA Hockey volumes were searched, and the word *"warn"* appears nowhere in CARHA's Rule 57. **Find out which book — and under Hockey Canada, which division — before you plan on one.**

### Closing your hand on the puck is a *penalty*, not a faceoff violation — but the books differ on when it is one

**In short:** In the scrum after the drop, your hands and your body stay off the puck — win it with your stick.

```facts
Rule: Everyone on the ice may catch a puck out of the air, provided they "immediately place it or knock it down to the ice" — but not the two takers, not until a third player has touched the puck (NHL and IIHF Rules 67.2, 76.5)
Rule: Not the two takers, though — the note to NHL and IIHF Rule 67.2, printed again at Rule 76.5, bars them from playing the puck with a hand "until such time as a third player (from either team) has at least touched the puck"
Rule: The bar on the two faceoff takers is on playing the puck with the hand at all, not merely on batting it or closing on it (NHL and IIHF Rule 76.5)
Rule: Closing your hand on the puck is a minor under the NHL, IIHF and PWHL (67.2, 69.2), and under Hockey Canada 10.2(a)(iii) and CARHA 61(a) if it gains an advantage; USA Hockey 618(a) only stops play, outside the crease — but covering a loose puck with your hand is a minor there (614(a))
Rule: A taker who attempts to win the draw by batting the puck with their hand takes a minor for delay of game (NHL and IIHF Rule 67.2)
Rule: An open hand placed over the puck to conceal it is a minor under NHL and IIHF Rule 67.2(ii), but a penalty shot instead when the puck is inside your own goal crease — or an awarded goal if your net is empty (Rules 67.4, 67.5, 63.6)
Rule: USA Hockey escalates in the crease too — 618(a) and 614(b) make a hand holding the puck there a penalty shot or optional minor, and 614(b), an awarded goal on an empty net where the act "has prevented an obvious and imminent goal"
Rule: With your net empty, covering the puck in your own crease is an awarded goal under PWHL 69.5 and Hockey Canada 10.2(a)(vi) too; CARHA writes it both ways — 58(c) Note 1 flat, 36(b) conditioned — so play to the strict answer: any cover there is the goal
Rule: Deliberately falling on or gathering the puck into your body is a minor in every book, and a penalty shot where the puck is in your own crease — NHL and IIHF 63.6, PWHL 65.6, USA Hockey 614(b), CARHA 58(c), Hockey Canada 4.11(a)(v) — and under its 10.2(a)(v) where you are in the crease too
Never: Put your hands or your body on the puck in the scrum. Tie up their stick with yours — never a hand on it — and win the puck with your stick instead
Rule: An ordinary faceoff violation cannot be challenged after a goal, but a draw won with the hand can be, as a missed game stoppage — IIHF Situations 38.20 (new in 2026/27) and 38.11 (identical in both 2025/26 editions, the book British hockey plays under)
Rule: Winning the draw with your skates is legal in the NHL but a faceoff violation under the IIHF book British hockey plays at every level — it cost a warning in the 2025/26 edition and gets the centre replaced in the 2026/27 one (IIHF Rule 76.3(VI), both editions)
```

Keeping your hands and body off the puck is the rule itself for the two takers; for everyone else it is advice. What **is** legal is tying up the other centre's stick **with yours** — lifting or pressing it, never hooking it, holding it down or putting a hand on it. **In every book the same act keeps you clear: catch it and drop it straight down.**

**Everyone else may catch the puck — the takers may not.** NHL and IIHF 67.2 permit a player *"to catch the puck out of the air but must immediately place it or knock it down to the ice."* ⚠️ **At a draw that is switched off for the two takers, and for nobody else**: Rule 76.5 bars them from playing the puck with a hand *"until such time as a third player (from either team) has at least touched the puck."* The bar is on playing it with the hand at all.

**What it costs.** Under NHL and IIHF 67.2 (PWHL 69.2) closing your hand on the puck is a **minor** whether or not it gained you anything, and a taker who *"attempts to win the face-off by batting the puck with their hand"* takes a minor for delay of game. **Hockey Canada 10.2(a)(iii) and CARHA 61(a)** penalise it only where it gains an advantage. **USA Hockey 618(a)** only stops play for a closed hand, but puts a minor on covering a loose puck with your hand (614(a) Note: *"the use of hands to make the puck unplayable should be penalized promptly"*).

An open hand is no loophole: placing a hand over the puck to conceal it is a minor (NHL 67.2(ii), IIHF 67.2(II)), and falling on or gathering it into your body is a minor too (NHL and IIHF 63.2(v)). ⚠️ **In your own crease either can be a penalty shot, and with your net empty an awarded goal** (NHL 67.4, 67.5, 63.6; USA Hockey 614(b) and 618(a), where the goal needs the act to have *"prevented an obvious and imminent goal"*). Most books decide by where **the puck** is; **Hockey Canada** 10.2(a)(v) turns on where the infraction occurs too, so being in the crease yourself can cost the shot — and with your net empty, the goal (10.2(a)(vi)). CARHA writes the empty-net goal two ways — 58(c) Note 1 flat, the 36(b) it points to conditioned — so play to 58(c): any cover in your own crease with your net empty is the goal.

**Under the IIHF a hand at the draw can cost you the goal itself, on review, minutes later.** Situation 38.11 — the same in both 2025/26 versions and in 2026/27 — has a centre glove the puck from a draw to a teammate who scores, undetected, and allows a challenge for a missed game stoppage. The 2026/27 Situation 38.20 adds that an ordinary faceoff violation *"cannot be challenged as a missed game stoppage event"* but a hand-won draw can be, as a hand pass — so it reaches attacking-zone draws (Rules 38.2(II), 38.9, 79.2). It sits awkwardly with Rule 76.5, which sends the takers' hand play to 67.2, and the handbook's Foreword makes the rulebook prevail, so treat it as how the review is run rather than as the rule. The In-House Rules say *"a Coach's Challenge is not permitted in EIH or SIHA competition"*, which settles neither a BUIHA fixture nor the **Elite League**, whose Casebook allows a challenge *"at any time throughout the game"* (38.5). **None of it changes the instruction: your hands stay off the puck.**

### When your centre gets tossed — and the edition that changed where

**In short:** Agree in advance who takes the draw if your centre is thrown out, and default to a tie-up, not a set play.

```facts
Rule: A teammate on the ice replaces an ejected centre, never the bench — all six books bar a change before the draw; a penalty lifts it if it leaves a team shorthanded (HC 6.2(d), CARHA 57(c)) or changes on-ice strength (NHL, IIHF 76.8; USAH 204(a); PWHL 84.1, not for the penalized team: 15.5)
Rule: The substitution exception is written two ways — Hockey Canada 6.2(d) and CARHA 57(c) allow the change only where a penalty is assessed "to make a team shorthanded", where NHL and IIHF 76.8 allow it whenever a penalty "affects the on-ice strength of either team", USA Hockey 204(a) likewise
Rule: The replacement centre must come into position quickly or risk the puck being dropped without them set — or being ejected themselves, which counts as a second faceoff violation on the same draw and costs the team a bench minor for delay of game (NHL Rule 76.4)
Rule: No time-out shall be granted following a faceoff violation (Rule 87.1; IIHF Rule 87.1 in the same words, unchanged across both IIHF editions)
Rule: Under the IIHF 2025/26 book a violation was a warning to the team, not an ejection, and the warned centre stayed for the re-drop (IIHF 2025/26 Rules 76.4, 76.6, 76.7)
Rule: The IIHF 2026/27 book removes the centre for a violation instead — ejected under 76.6, replaced under 76.3 and 76.4 — so a British reader should expect the centre to come off, as under NHL rules
Rule: And under the IHUK In-House Rules 2026-27 the IIHF edition no longer needs settling — they replace the centre for all violations bar those on post-icing draws. The Elite League still runs on the IIHF book, which its Casebook names as the 2026/27 edition
Rule: The IHUK In-House Rules govern "in England, Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland" and their Rule 76 amendment names no league, but the Elite League appears nowhere in them — an EIHL centre is removed under the 2026/27 IIHF book's own Rule 76 instead (EIHL Casebook 26-27)
Rule: IIHF counts winning the draw with your skates as a violation in both editions (IIHF Rule 76.3(VI))
Rule: USA Hockey keeps the ejection but makes the second violation a minor on the individual (Rule 613(d))
Rule: Under Hockey Canada the replacement may not be the offender — 6.2(d) has "[t]he player who encroached" identified clearly as the cause and "not eligible to take the face-off", and permits no change of players unless a penalty makes a team shorthanded
Action: Agree the designated replacement in advance — under Hockey Canada it can never be the player who caused the violation — and make the plan a tie-up rather than a set play
```

**Agree the backup in advance.** Every winger should know whether they are the designated replacement, and every line should have a default plan for a draw without its centre — almost always a **tie-up**, not a set play.

- **Any teammate on the ice may step in** (Rule 76.4) — in practice, a winger. The bench cannot help you.
- **Get set quickly.** The replacement *"must come into position quickly or risk having the puck dropped by the Linesperson without the player being set,"* or be ejected themselves — the second violation, and so a bench minor.
- **No timeout can save you.** *"No time-out shall be granted following a face-off violation"* (NHL, IIHF and PWHL, Rules 87.1 and 89.1). USA Hockey's time-out must be asked for before the line change ends (636(f)) and Hockey Canada's window closes when the official whistles to start the faceoff procedure (6.17(e)), so do not count on one there either. CARHA writes no time-out.

**Under Hockey Canada a line needs two names, not one.** ⚠️ **The player who caused the violation may not take the draw, and nobody can come over the boards** — 6.2(d): *"The player who encroached must be identified clearly as the player causing the violation and that player is not eligible to take the face-off. No change of players will be permitted, even if the puck has been physically dropped, unless a penalty has been assessed to make a team shorthanded."* What it costs if the named player steps in anyway is not written down; on a reading, not a ruling, it is a second violation, which 6.2(e) prices as a delay-of-game minor. Ask your officials. The two-name plan costs nothing to carry everywhere.

**All six books bar a change from the bench for the re-drop, and all six lift the bar when a penalty changes the numbers** — one that makes a team shorthanded under Hockey Canada and CARHA 57(c), one that affects on-ice strength under NHL and IIHF 76.8, USA Hockey 204(a) and PWHL 84.1. **The PWHL adds one limit:** its 78.8 applies Rule 15.5, under which a team *"signaled for a penalty with time required to be placed on the penalty clock"* makes no substitution before the next draw — so, on a plain reading, only the other team may change. Books other than Hockey Canada allow *"any teammate then on the ice"*, and CARHA names no ineligible player.

> **🇬🇧 In Britain this section now matters.** The IIHF 2025/26 edition had no faceoff ejection; the 2026/27 edition turned most warnings into removals, at 76.3, 76.4, 76.6 and 76.7. It kept softenings for the centre: one who moves early gets only a re-drop, a team late to the dot is warned once a game (76.7), and the first violation after an icing is a warning (76.4).
>
> **The IHUK In-House Rules 2026-27** settle it across the Home Countries: *"With the exception of face-offs following icings, the linesperson shall immediately replace the centre for all violations."* Their cover and Introduction name different IIHF editions, but IHUK wrote the replacement into its own text, so that does not matter here. ⚠️ **The Elite League appears nowhere in them**; its Casebook makes the 2026-27 IIHF Rulebook the ruling, so an EIHL centre is removed under IIHF Rule 76 itself. [Rules Primer](../foundation/rules_primer.md) sets out the edition inconsistency.
>
> **That reverses how you play.** Under the 2025/26 book your specialist took every draw they lined up for; **now you need the back-up plan above.** ⚠️ **If you learned "my specialist always takes the draw", that is the habit to unlearn.**

> **🇬🇧 Take "kick it back" out of your repertoire.** IIHF 76.3(VI) makes a centre who *"wins the face-off by using the skates"* commit a violation in both editions. The 2025/26 book had the linesperson *"assess a warning"*; the 2026/27 book has the linesperson *"have the offending center(s) replaced immediately prior to dropping the puck."* Across the Home Countries the In-House Rules reach the same; in the Elite League the 2026/27 IIHF book does — bar, on our reading of IIHF 76.4, the first violation on a draw after an icing, which it makes a warning.
>
> Many rec, beer and youth leagues enforce violations loosely and simply re-drop with nobody removed. Under the 2026/27 book that is the officials being lenient — **except on the first violation after an icing**, where leaving the centre in is exactly right, unless penalties made both teams change players first (IIHF Situation 81.18). Ask how strictly your level enforces it.

**USA Hockey keeps the ejection but changes the escalation.** 613(d) ejects your centre when a teammate *"fails to maintain their proper position"*, as the NHL does, but a second violation is *"a minor penalty for delay of game … to the player committing the second violation"* — the same minor, but a different player in the box.

## Special Teams Faceoffs

Special-teams draws are about 10 a game (9.6 in 2024-25, 10.1 in 2025-26, from NHL official data) — a small share of the total, and a hugely disproportionate share of the ones that matter. See [Special Teams](special_teams.md) for the formations.

### Power-play draws

```facts
Rule: The team awarded the power play chooses which end-zone dot the draw starts at — NHL and IIHF Rule 76.2, PWHL 78.2, and Hockey Canada 6.4(b) in Junior and Senior only
Rule: USA Hockey (612(b), 624(a)) and CARHA (39(a), 65(a)) give no dot choice here or after an icing, and a CARHA power-play draw can fall in the neutral zone (39(a) Note)
Action: Where you have the choice, pick your centre's strong side or the side your one-timer threat shoots from
Key: The entry you avoid is worth more than the draw you win
Priority: Win the draw, or make sure you do not lose it cleanly — a tie-up that keeps the puck in the zone is fine
Never: Fire a fancy set play that ends in a clearance
```

**Where your book lets the power-play team pick the dot, pick it**: your centre's strong side, or the side your one-timer shoots from. A surprising number of teams do not think about it — a coaching observation, not a count. The NHL, IIHF and PWHL give the choice (76.2; PWHL 78.2), so it is yours everywhere in Britain; Hockey Canada only in Junior and Senior, if you name the dot before the change (6.4(b)); USA Hockey and CARHA never, so prepare for either side.

**On a delayed penalty against them, keep the puck and don't hand the referee a whistle of your own.** Ice it and, under USA Hockey, Hockey Canada and CARHA, the power-play draw leaves their end for a neutral-zone dot outside your own blue line (612(b)(4); 6.3(c)(iv); 39(b)). Under USA Hockey any whistle you cause costs you their end (612(b)(4)); under CARHA so does a puck you put out of play from your own zone, or an offside pass over the red line.

League-wide, power-play units win about **54% of their draws** (53.6% in 2024-25, 54.4% in 2025-26, computed from NHL official data) — better personnel, and often a winger in the dot for the kill.

**The entry you avoid is worth more than the draw you win.** Win the draw and you keep the zone. Lose it and see it cleared, and you must break out, regroup and re-enter against a **set** kill on its blue line. Zone-entry research consistently finds controlled entries generate roughly **twice** the shot volume of dump-ins at even strength — [Zone Entries](zone_entries.md) has the figures, their strength states and sources — and a power play re-entering against a set kill mostly gets dump-ins. A lost draw that costs you the zone can cost a third of your two minutes.

So on the power play: **win the draw, or make sure you do not lose it cleanly.** A tie-up that keeps the puck in is fine. A fancy set play that ends in a clearance is not.

### Penalty-kill draws

**In short:** On the kill, use your best draw man, count a tie-up as a win, and ice a clean win where your book allows.

```facts
Mindset: You are not trying to create — you are trying to end the possession
Risk: A defensive-zone draw shorthanded is where the disadvantages pile up — a man down, in your own end, against a set unit with a rehearsed play
Key: A tie-up is a win here too — anything but their point man shooting into a screen is acceptable
Action: Put your best draw man out even if they are not a natural killer
```

Everything is inverted. You are not trying to create; you are trying to end the possession.

- **A shorthanded own-end draw is where the disadvantages pile up.** One man down, in your own end, against a set unit with a rehearsed play and a shooter with their feet planted.
- **Winning it clean is enormous**, because of the icing exemption (below).
- **A tie-up is a win here too.** Anything but "their point man shoots into a screen" is acceptable.
- **Put your best draw man out even if they are not a natural killer.** The draw is worth more than twenty seconds of coverage.

#### The icing exemption at a shorthanded draw — and the one that has nothing to do with being shorthanded

```facts
Rule: Five of the six books let you ice freely below your opponent's on-ice count, USA Hockey only at 15-Only and Girls 16U up, High School and Adult; the NHL and PWHL take it away after a major expires with no substitute in the box (NHL and IIHF 81.6, PWHL 83.6, HC 6.7(b), CARHA 65(d), USAH 624(b)(1))
Rule: USA Hockey grants the shorthanded icing exemption only at Youth 15-Only and above, Girls 16U and above, High School and Adult (Rule 624(b)(1)); below those levels a won shorthanded draw still has to be cleared legally
Rule: The NHL's major-penalty icing exception bites at the expiry, not during the major — where the penalised player was ejected or hurt, Rule 20.3 wants a substitute seated before the major expires, immediately if minors are stacked on it
Rule: Missing the NHL's substitute deadline costs twice — a bench minor for illegal substitution under Rule 68.2, and no clear until the next stoppage (81.6, 20.3)
Rule: IIHF Rule 81.6 carries neither carve-out — no age restriction and no major-penalty exception — but it runs on the on-ice count: a puck released before your penalty expires is never icing, and an expiry ends the exemption only where it leaves you level, so on a 5-on-3 you are 4-on-5 and exempt
Rule: An empty penalty box is priced under the IIHF too — Rule 20.3 wants the substitute seated immediately and Rule 68.2 charges a bench minor; what a British team does not also lose is the icing exemption
Rule: CARHA grants the shorthanded exemption too and rations it no further — Rule 65(d), the count taken "at the instant the puck is shot", with no age classification and no major-penalty exception anywhere in Rule 65
Rule: A puck sent the length of the ice directly off the draw is not icing in any book and at any strength — NHL and IIHF 81.5, PWHL 83.5, Hockey Canada 6.7(c)(iv), USA Hockey 624(b)(3), CARHA 65(e); five say "directly", USA Hockey "as a result of" the taker — treat any other touch as ending it
```

**A clean shorthanded win in your own end can go straight down the ice**, because a team below the opponent's on-ice numbers may ice the puck (Rule 81.6). That is a penalty-free clear and a chance to change tired killers. How much time it burns depends on the retrieval; no published figure exists, so do not trust the "fifteen seconds" you sometimes hear.

**And a puck that goes the length of the ice directly off the draw is not icing at all, in any book and at any strength** — for both teams, even strength included. ⚠️ **Play it as the strict word, "directly": the puck your own stroke sends down the ice from the dot** — not one won to a defenceman who clears it, and not one that clips a stick. The moment anybody else touches it, the ordinary rule applies.

**Check your book before you rely on the exemption.**
- **USA Hockey:** ⚠️ **only at Youth 15-Only and above, Girls 16U and above, High School and Adult** (624(b)(1)); below those levels a won shorthanded draw still has to be cleared legally.
- **The NHL and PWHL withdraw it in one narrow case**, after a major rather than during it. Where the player serving a major has been ejected or is injured, that player's team must seat a substitute at a stoppage before the major expires, ⚠️ **or *"immediately"* if that player is also serving minors**. If it does not, then from the expiry until the next stoppage it is shorthanded but *"not permitted to ice the puck"* (NHL 20.3, 81.6; PWHL 83.6).
- **The IIHF writes neither carve-out**, but it times the count: icing *"shall be determined at the instant the penalty expires"*, and *"[t]he action of the penalized Player remaining in the penalty box will not alter the ruling"* (81.6, both editions). ⚠️ **So kill a lone minor and ice it after your clock has run out, and it is icing — even with the player still in the box.** On a 5-on-3, after the first expiry you are 4-on-5 and may still ice it; and a puck released before your penalty expires is never icing.
- **Hockey Canada** counts *"at the instant the puck is shot"* (6.7(b)). Its Note 1 says a team is no longer shorthanded *"once the penalty time expires"* — never quote that alone: Interpretation 3 counts the returning player on the ice *"for the purpose of equal numbers (or not)"*. **CARHA** 65(d) counts at the shot too, with no age or major-penalty carve-out.

**What it costs.** ⚠️ **An empty penalty box is a bench minor under the NHL, the IIHF and the PWHL** — Rule 68.2 (PWHL 70.2), *"calling for a bench minor penalty"*, with IIHF 20.3 wanting the substitute seated *"immediately"*. In Britain you lose the two minutes, not the clear. The faceoff icing carve-out is NHL and IIHF 81.5, PWHL 83.5, Hockey Canada 6.7(c)(iv), USA Hockey 624(b)(3) and CARHA 65(e); five write *"directly"*, USA Hockey *"as a result of"* one of the takers. [Special Teams](special_teams.md) and [Rules Primer](../foundation/rules_primer.md) set the exemption out book by book.

### 4-on-4 and 3-on-3

```facts
Convention: 4-on-4 is two forwards and two defencemen, or three forwards and one, depending on the coach
Priority: At 4-on-4 keep both back players honest — a lost draw is a genuine odd-man threat
Key: 3-on-3 draws are close to the highest-leverage moment in the game, because a loss is often a chance within ten seconds
Rule: The five-second setup limit is not enforced in overtime (NHL and IIHF Rule 76.3) — use the time; USA Hockey 613(c) writes no such exception
```

- **4-on-4** — two forwards and two defencemen, or three and one, depending on the coach. The ice is enormous and a lost draw is a genuine odd-man threat, so keep both back players honest.
- **3-on-3 overtime** — close to the highest-leverage draw in the game: a loss is often a chance within ten seconds, and the game ends when somebody scores. Under the NHL and IIHF the five-second setup limit is not enforced in overtime, so take the time to get it exactly right. **Under USA Hockey (613(c)) the whistle still starts a clock.**

## Situational Faceoffs

### After an icing

**In short:** When they ice it, play fast and attack their tired line; when you ice it, get it off the glass and out.

```facts
Rule: Under the NHL, IIHF and PWHL books the team that iced cannot change lines (NHL and IIHF 81.4, 82.1; PWHL 83.4) or take a time-out (NHL and IIHF 87.1, PWHL 89.1), though an injured player can still be replaced
Rule: The freeze falls on the team that iced, so the home team keeps last change when the visitors ice it (NHL and IIHF Rule 82.1)
Rule: After an icing the IIHF imposes both the no-change rule and the time-out ban in the NHL's words; USA Hockey imposes neither, but bounds both by the same line change procedure — five seconds a side to change, and the time-out requested before that procedure ends (Rules 624, 204(a), 636(f))
Rule: Hockey Canada is a fourth answer — 6.7(d) applies the no-change rule in U18AAA and Junior, and in Senior at the Member's option; 6.7(d)(iii) lets either team take its time-out after an icing, though the team that iced may not substitute during it
Rule: CARHA writes no icing change bar and no time-out, but Rule 19(d) allows each team one change per stoppage, and a late change is sent back the first time and a bench minor after that (19(e), 19(g))
Rule: Under NHL, IIHF and PWHL rules the attacking team picks the dot (81.2, PWHL 83.2), under Hockey Canada only in Junior and Senior (6.4(b)); USA Hockey (624(a)) and CARHA (65(a)) give no choice
Rule: Under the NHL, the PWHL and the IIHF 2026/27 book the first faceoff violation after an icing ejects nobody's centre, on either team (NHL and IIHF 76.4, PWHL 78.4)
Rule: The free first faceoff violation after an icing is Hockey Canada's only in Junior divisions (Rule 6.5(a)), and neither USA Hockey (613) nor CARHA (57) writes one at all
Rule: The IIHF 2025/26 book had no post-icing carve-out and needed none, because its first violation on any draw was already a warning (IIHF 2025/26 Rules 76.6, 76.7)
Rule: The IIHF 2026/27 book adopted the NHL ejection and the NHL post-icing carve-out together, so in Britain a first violation now usually costs the centre, except on a draw after an icing — unless penalties sent both teams to change first (IIHF 2026/27 Rule 76.4, Situation 81.18)
Action: Play fast — their disadvantage is fatigue, and it evaporates the moment they get to breathe
Action: When you iced it, get the puck off the glass and out of your zone at the first opportunity; nothing clever
```

The single most exploitable situation in the game, and it works in both directions.

**When they iced it:**
- **Play fast.** Their disadvantage is fatigue, and it evaporates the moment the stoppage lets them breathe. Win the draw and attack immediately.
- **You can change; they cannot.** Under the NHL, the IIHF and the PWHL the team that iced may not change lines (Rules 81.4, 82.1) or take a timeout (87.1), though an injured player can still be replaced. So you answer whoever they are stuck with — one of the largest matchup edges a coach gets, for one shift. The freeze falls only on **the team that iced**: if the visitors ice it, the home team still changes and keeps last change. The IIHF Situation Handbook says so directly — *"Team B may make a complete line change if they so choose"* (Situations 81.6 and 82.1, both editions).
- **You choose the dot**, under NHL, IIHF and PWHL rules — *"Following an 'icing', the attacking team will have the choice of which end zone dot the 'face-off' will take place"* (81.2; PWHL 83.2). Hockey Canada gives the choice only in Junior and Senior, if you name the dot before the line change (6.4(b)). Pick your centre's strong side.

**Most North American books take some or all of this away.**
- **USA Hockey** imposes neither restriction and gives no choice of dot (624(a)). Both teams change in the ordinary five-second windows (204(a)), and the time-out must be asked for before that procedure ends (636(f)) — so the team that iced may take one, but not late.
- **Hockey Canada** applies the no-change rule only *"[i]n categories of U18AAA, Junior, and at the option of the Member in Senior"* (6.7(d)), so below those there is no freeze to exploit. Where it applies, either team may still take its time-out, but the team that iced may not substitute during it (6.7(d)(iii)). [Rules Primer](../foundation/rules_primer.md) has the full comparison.
- **CARHA** writes no icing freeze and no time-out, and gives no choice of dot (65(a)), but allows each team *"only one change of player(s)"* per stoppage within ten seconds (19(d), 19(g)); a late change is sent back the first time and is a bench minor after that (19(e)). **No matchup edge to chase — change once, promptly.**

**When you iced it:** you are tired, stuck in your own end, and cannot rest. Get the puck off the glass and out at the first chance; nothing clever. **The first faceoff violation after an icing does not eject the centre** (Rule 76.4) — one free warning for each team, so do not count on their centre being tossed either. In Britain the IIHF 2026/27 book and the In-House Rules give the same carve-out. ⚠️ **In a North American game, check first:** Hockey Canada gives it only in Junior (6.5(a)), and USA Hockey and CARHA not at all. See "The icing exception" above.

### In the last minute

```facts
Key: A defensive-zone draw protecting a one-goal lead late is worth more than the previous fifty draws combined
Action: With a lead, accept the tie-up — a scramble that burns time beats a 50-50 clean-win attempt; or put your best taker out with a plan for the win and the loss
Priority: Get the alignment right before you get the play right
Rule: The five-second setup limit is not enforced in the final two minutes of regulation (NHL and IIHF Rule 76.3); USA Hockey 613(c) writes no such exception
Read: Expect more re-drops and more violations called
```

- **Everything is about the location.** An own-end draw protecting a one-goal lead with 40 seconds left is worth more than the previous 50 draws combined, because there is no time to recover from losing it.
- **This is where you accept the tie-up.** With a lead, a scramble that burns time beats a 50-50 attempt at a clean win. The other common approach is your best faceoff man with a clear plan for the loss as well as the win — many coaches do both; ask your bench which yours stresses.
- **Get the alignment right before you get the play right.** Late-game draws are lost to confusion more often than to the other centre.
- **The five-second setup limit is not enforced** in the final two minutes of regulation under NHL and IIHF 76.3, so the linesperson will manage the alignment more carefully — expect more re-drops and more violations called. **USA Hockey (613(c)) writes no such exception.**

### With seconds left on the clock

```facts
Action: Needing a goal, win it straight back to a shooter whose feet are already set, with a forward at the net front for the screen and the rebound
Options: Some centres shoot straight at the net off the draw instead — it gives the goalie no time to set, at the cost of a weak shot
Action: Defending a draw with seconds left, a tie-up is a win — wingers into the shooting lanes, and a won puck goes off the glass and out of your zone
Key: There is no time for a second pass, so agree the play at the bench, not at the dot
```

**With a few seconds left and a goal needed, the common play is the quickest shot you can set up.** Win it straight back to the strong-side defenceman or the inside winger, feet already set, with a forward at the net front for the screen and the rebound; there is no time for a second pass. **A realistic alternative is for the centre to shoot straight off the draw**, pulling or pushing it at the net in one motion — the goalie has no time to set, at the cost of a weak shot. Ask your bench which your team uses, and settle it at the bench, not the dot.

**Defending that draw, all you need is time.** A tie-up is a win, the wingers get into the shooting lanes rather than chasing the puck, and a won puck goes off the glass and out — every second of a scramble is a second off their last attack.

### After a timeout

```facts
Read: Expect the opponent to have a plan too, and expect it to differ from their default
Rule: No time-out is granted following a faceoff violation (NHL and IIHF Rule 87.1, PWHL Rule 89.1)
Rule: No time-out for the defensive team following an icing, after their goalie stops a shot from beyond the centre red line, or after a defender accidentally dislodges the net (NHL Rule 87.1)
Rule: A net accidentally displaced by a defending skater or goalie costs the line change as well as the time-out — the offending team may not substitute before the draw (NHL and IIHF Rule 63.8)
Rule: USA Hockey attaches no icing condition to its one 60-second time-out, but requires it be requested during the stoppage and before the line change procedure concludes (Rule 636(f))
Rule: IIHF Rule 87.1 carries both the faceoff-violation and the post-icing time-out bans; USA Hockey Rule 636(f) writes neither, but its sixty-second time-out must be asked for before the line change procedure ends, so after a botched draw it is already too late
```

A timeout buys both teams a rest and a chance to draw up the alignment. So expect the opponent to have a plan too, and expect it to differ from their default.

**What it costs.** Under NHL and IIHF Rule 87.1 (PWHL 89.1) there is **no timeout after a faceoff violation**, and **none for the defending team after an icing**, after their goalie stops a shot from beyond the centre red line, or after a defender accidentally dislodges the net — so a team cannot ice the puck and then buy a rest. A net knocked off by a defender costs the line change too (63.8). **USA Hockey 636(f)** writes neither restriction and gives sixty seconds rather than thirty, so there a team *can* ice it and then rest — but ⚠️ **it must ask *"during a stoppage of play prior to the conclusion of the line change procedure"*,** which ends when the referee lowers their arm after the home team's five seconds. Hockey Canada closes its window when the official whistles to start the faceoff procedure (6.17(e)); CARHA writes no time-out.

### With the goalie pulled

**In short:** With six skaters your centre must win it back cleanly; defending, shoot at the empty net only with a clean lane.

```facts
Position: The extra attacker goes to the net front or the high slot, with both defencemen at the points
Priority: The centre must win it back cleanly — there is no safe outlet, and a lost draw lets the defence clear it and run down time you may not get back
Key: Defending against a pulled goalie, win the draw and get it out under control; shoot at the empty net only with a clean lane, because a miss from your own half is icing
Rule: The goalie may not participate in a faceoff (Rule 76.1)
Rule: A team that pulled its goalie may still substitute after an icing to put the goalie back in (NHL and IIHF Rule 81.4)
Rule: Besides putting a pulled goalie back, the NHL's exceptions to the post-icing no-change rule are an injured player and a penalty that changes on-ice strength (NHL Rule 81.4)
Rule: IIHF 81.4 adds a fourth, "a Player with a broken skate", which IIHF 82.1 does not repeat and no Situation names — treat it as one an official may allow, not one to count on: a change the referee refuses costs a warning your team may already have spent, then a bench minor (IIHF 81.4, 82.1, 82.2(V))
```

- **Six skaters changes the alignment entirely.** The extra attacker usually goes to the net front or the high slot, with both defencemen at the points. The centre must win it back cleanly: there is no safe outlet, and a lost draw lets the defence clear it and run down time.
- **Defending against a pulled goalie:** win the draw, then get the puck out of your zone under control. Shoot at the empty net only with a clean lane — six against six is not shorthanded, so a miss from your own half is icing and brings the draw straight back to your end. It is a draw worth practising.
- **The goalie may not take a faceoff** (Rule 76.1), and a team that pulled its goalie may still substitute after an icing to put the goalie back (81.4) — one of the NHL's **three** exceptions to the no-change rule, with an injured player and a penalty that changes on-ice strength.
- **Do not send a player over the boards on a broken skate without the official's say-so.** ⚠️ **The IIHF book contradicts itself here:** its 81.4 adds a change for *"a Player with a broken skate"*, its 82.1 lists only the three, both editions read that way, and no Situation mentions a broken skate. Nothing British amends it — the In-House Rules defer substitutions to *"the Playing Rules"*.

**What it costs.** ⚠️ **A refused change is not a warning you can count on having.** IIHF 82.2(V) sends the players back and warns the team through the coach — but that warning is **one per team per game**, and *"any subsequent violations during the rest of the game (including overtime), shall result in a bench minor penalty"*. If your bench has already been warned for any personnel change, the next one is the bench minor, and 82.2(VIII) does not relax that late. ⚠️ **Under the NHL a visiting team may get no warning at all:** NHL 82.3 makes *"[a]ny attempt by the visiting team to make a change after the Referee's signal"* a bench minor, while 82.2(v) warns *"either team"* first, and the book does not say which governs. The IIHF's 82.3 warns first, then a bench minor. Stalling on the way to the dot is a bench minor with no warning in both books (82.3).

### When the other centre keeps beating you

```facts
Action: Go to the tie-up — lift or press their stick with yours, then play the puck, so their clean win becomes a scramble your four are already leaning into
Action: Tell your wingers before you line up that it is a tie-up draw, so the two nearest bodies go on the drop
Options: Or change the taker — a second centre who shoots the other way, on their strong side, or a winger who reads this centre better; ask your bench which your team prefers
Read: Scout them from the bench — most centres have one preferred draw and one strong side
Never: Cheat back against a centre who cheats the drop — hold your timing and tie them up, because a premature move is a violation on whichever centre makes it
Never: Answer a lean with a lean — contact before the drop is a violation whoever makes it, and a minor, mandatory under USA Hockey, Hockey Canada and CARHA
```

Some nights one centre beats you clean every time. **The common answer is to go to the tie-up**: lift or press their stick with yours, over or under the blade, then play the puck — stick on stick only, never a hand on their stick or your stick on their hands. Tell your wingers before you line up that it is a tie-up draw, so the two nearest go on the drop. **The realistic alternative is to change the taker** — a centre who shoots the other way and takes the dots on their strong side, or a winger who reads this centre better. Ask your bench which comes first, and scout them from the bench meanwhile.

**When that centre cheats the drop — moving early, or leaning in — do not cheat back.** Hold your timing, watch the hand, and press their stick as the puck lands so their jump wins nothing. Never answer a lean with a lean.

**What it costs.** A premature move is a violation on whichever centre makes it, so matching their gamble puts the risk on you — and under the In-House Rules, on any draw but one after an icing, it costs the centre their place, as it does under Hockey Canada except on a Junior draw after an icing. Contact before the drop is a violation whoever makes it, and brings the taker a minor, **mandatory under USA Hockey, Hockey Canada and CARHA** and at the referee's discretion under the NHL and IIHF.

## How to Practise Faceoffs

**A faceoff comes nearer than anything else you do dozens of times a game to a fixed start, a fixed cue, a fixed distance and a binary outcome.** A penalty shot has a fixed start too, but you take a handful a season; it is the *frequency* that makes the draw worth drilling. It is not a pure closed skill — the drop is deliberately unpredictable, and what you train is a reaction from a fixed position — but that combination repays repetition.

The 22-point spread between the best and worst NHL centres is not a talent gradient. At the top of faceoff percentage are, mostly, the people who worked at it.

### What actually to do

```facts
Action: Reps in pairs with a real drop — correct height, unpredictable timing, because you are training reaction
Action: End every practice with ten minutes of draws, both circles, both grips
Priority: Practise the technique you are worst at — your weak-side draws are where the points are
Action: Rehearse alignments with all five skaters: run the win, run the loss, run the tie-up
Action: Have every winger take draws in practice, because every winger will take one in a game
Key: Teach your wingers the encroachment rule — thirty seconds, and it stops a winger's early step costing your centre their place in the draw in every book here, the IIHF's since its 2026/27 edition
Rule: Under the In-House Rules that lesson does not wait on the IIHF edition — they replace the centre for every violation bar one following an icing, so a winger's early step costs you the centre there too; the Elite League reaches it through IIHF 76.6 instead (In-House Section 10, Rule 76)
```

- **Reps, in pairs, with a real drop.** Someone dropping it from the correct height with unpredictable timing, not a coach handing it over. You are training reaction.
- **End every practice with draws.** Ten minutes, both circles, both grips. It is the standard coaching advice, and it works because nobody has to plan it.
- **Practise the technique you are worst at**, not the strong-side backhand pull you already trust. Your weak-side draws are where the points are.
- **Rehearse alignments, not just the draw.** All five skaters on the ice: run the win, the loss and the tie-up. Most faceoff goals against come from four players not knowing what to do, not from the centre losing it.
- **Practise as the replacement.** Every winger will take a draw in a game after their centre is tossed — and some will move to centre for good, which [Switching Positions](../positions/switching_positions.md) covers.
- **Off-ice: grip and hand speed.** Coaching sources recommend wrist and grip strengthening and hand-eye work (dribbling a golf ball with a stick is the classic), on the premise that a draw is a hand-speed event before a strength one — **coaching craft; there is no published measurement of faceoff reaction times, so do not quote a figure.** See [Conditioning and Recovery](../off-the-ice/conditioning_and_recovery.md).
- **Scout from the bench.** Watch the opposing centres while you rest. It is free, and almost nobody does it.
- **Teach your wingers the encroachment rule.** Thirty seconds, and it stops a winger's early step costing your centre their place — under the NHL, under IIHF 76.6 since the 2026/27 edition, and in Britain under the In-House Rules on every draw but one after an icing. Only the superseded IIHF 2025/26 edition cost the team a warning instead, and that is the version a British coach may still be teaching.

See [Practice and Development](../off-the-ice/practice_and_development.md) for how to structure deliberate practice generally.

## Common Mistakes

- **Chasing your faceoff percentage instead of the important draws.** Prepare the draws that decide something — your own end, the kill, a late lead. Across 2015-16 to 2025-26, a team's faceoff percentage explains only about 6% of the variation in its points percentage (computed for this document from NHL official statistics; a team-level figure).
- **Standing still after the draw.** Move on the drop. In your own end the wingers go to the points on a loss and to the wall **between the hash marks and the goal line** on a win, not thirty feet up it. Most faceoff goals against come from the four players off the dot, not the centre.
- **Waiting to see what happens on a tie-up.** Attack it: the two nearest bodies go — in your own end usually the inside winger and the boards-side defenceman — and one of them calls "Loose!"
- **Chasing the puck to the point after a lost defensive-zone draw.** Hold the net front and the shooting lane; in the zone system assumed here, your winger has the point. **Under man-to-man the assignments differ**, so ask your bench.
- **Winning a defensive-zone draw into the middle of the ice.** Win it to the boards. On your weak side your backhand pull sends it into your slot, so learn the forehand pull, flip your hands, or put a centre who shoots the other way on that dot.
- **Rimming it halfway.** Rim it hard enough to reach your winger, or tight enough that your goalie can legally stop it behind the net. A hard rim is past the forecheckers before they move; one that dies in your corner sends a defenceman to fetch it under pressure.
- **Winning a neutral-zone draw and then dumping it in.** Carry it or pass it in — you had possession and a standing start. One author's tracking of the 2018-19 Swiss National League, not the NHL, found possessions with a dump-in produced far fewer shots and expected goals; read the direction, not the multiple.
- **Firing a fancy set play on a power-play draw.** Win it, or make sure you do not lose it cleanly — a tie-up that keeps the puck in is fine. A play that ends in a clearance costs a re-entry against a set kill.
- **Installing six set plays.** Run two or three per zone, called in one word the whole line acknowledges, each with a failure branch. With no call, or a replacement in the dot, tie up in your own end and take the point shot in theirs.
- **Using the same technique every time.** Pull one, tie up the next, sweep the third. Three straight wins to the same defenceman get the fourth jumped.
- **Watching your opponent instead of the linesperson's hand.** Eyes on the hand, and go on the drop. If you are reacting to them, you are second.
- **Cheating back against a centre who cheats the drop.** Hold your timing and tie them up as the puck lands. Moving early is a violation. Under the NHL, the PWHL and the IIHF it costs a re-drop, and a second violation on that draw a bench minor; under the In-House Rules and Hockey Canada it costs your centre their place, except after an icing under the In-House Rules and on a Junior draw after an icing under Hockey Canada. USA Hockey and CARHA do not name it. A lean answered with a lean is pre-drop contact: your centre replaced under the NHL, the PWHL and the IIHF 2026/27 book, and a minor the referee may add — must, under USA Hockey, Hockey Canada and CARHA.
- **Letting them breathe after they ice the puck.** Win the draw and attack at once, taking your choice of dot where your book gives one. Their line cannot change under the NHL, IIHF and PWHL books, nor under Hockey Canada's in U18AAA and Junior (and Senior at the Member's option).
- **Fetching a puck you won to the wall with your back to anyone coming at you.** ⚠️ **Never turn your back to anyone coming at you, and never duck** — skates parallel to the boards, forearm and hip to the wall, head up and chin off your chest. The forechecker was standing still at the whistle and arrives at full speed, and a tucked chin straightens the neck, so a head-first hit into the boards or a post can injure at walking speed.
- **Hitting a player turned toward the wall when you are the forechecker.** Take the puck or angle them off instead. ⚠️ **That hit is checking from behind in any game, and while it stays off the head it ejects you in every book but USA Hockey** — a mandatory match under Hockey Canada and CARHA when it drives a player who cannot protect themselves into the boards. Under USA Hockey a forceful hit on a player standing along the boards, or one that sends them head first, is at least a major and an ejection — and if it is reckless, plan on a match and a likely multi-game suspension. ⚠️ **And know your game before you hit anyone:** in a non-checking classification and in any CARHA game any check is a penalty, and one that injures ends your game under Hockey Canada and CARHA, CARHA adding at least a one-game ban; under USA Hockey a reckless one is a major and game misconduct — plan on a match and a likely multi-game suspension. Some British competitions ban it without saying what a breach costs — **ask your league, and until you know, do not hit anyone.** The full price list is in [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md#checking-from-behind-and-what-the-onus-does-and-does-not-mean).
- **Getting low by pushing your head out over the dot.** Get low from your knees and hips, chest over the puck. ⚠️ **There is no free head bump:** helmet-to-helmet contact before the drop is a faceoff violation under the IIHF book, and the current IIHF handbook has the linesperson eject the centre responsible — both, if they cannot tell. In Britain it costs the centre the draw under the In-House Rules or the Elite League's book alike. The referee can add a minor — must, under USA Hockey, Hockey Canada and CARHA.
- **Wingers creeping over the hash marks.** Keep skates and stick behind your own hash mark and skates off the circle line — legal in all six books. Creeping over ejects your **centre**, not you, and a second violation can cost a penalty. The NHL, IIHF and PWHL's looser permissions — a stick between the hash marks, a skate on the line — are not every book's.
- **Putting only the toe of your blade in the white.** Put the whole blade flat in the white and leave it there until the puck lands — that satisfies all six books. ⚠️ **The toe alone is a violation under CARHA and under the In-House Rules across the Home Countries:** the In-House Rules replace your centre, except after an icing, and CARHA lets the official replace them. The NHL, the IIHF and the Elite League accept the toe; under USA Hockey keep the whole blade clear of the red at an end-zone draw.
- **Trying something clever with the replacement taker.** Tie it up and take the re-drop straight: your centre is already out, and a second violation is a bench minor under the NHL, the PWHL, the IIHF and the In-House Rules, a minor under USA Hockey, and a possible minor under Hockey Canada and CARHA. ⚠️ **Let the linesperson get clear before you commit** — under Hockey Canada a second violation that runs into the official is a minor with no discretion. Under Hockey Canada the player who encroached is also barred from the dot and nobody may come over the boards, so name two takers per line before the game.
- **Putting a hand or your body on the puck in the scrum.** Win it with your **stick**. If you took the draw, keep your hand off the puck until a third player has touched it. Falling on it is a minor, and so, usually, is covering it — ⚠️ **inside your own crease it can be a penalty shot, and with your net empty treat it as an awarded goal**; most books decide by where the puck is, Hockey Canada by where you are as well.
- **Not choosing your dot, where you have one.** Ask early, and take your centre's strong side or the side your one-timer shoots from. NHL and IIHF rules give the choice after an icing and at the start of a power play; USA Hockey gives none; Hockey Canada gives it only in Junior and Senior, to a team that names the dot before the line change starts.
- **Joining a post-whistle scrum in the offensive zone.** Once the whistle goes, stay outside the circle. Cross its outer edge and the draw moves to the neutral zone — you have thrown away the zone.
- **Going through the other centre instead of round them on a "lose it forward".** Skate *around* them — they are now between you and the puck, and going round is legal everywhere. Driving through them can be called interference wherever you play, and where checking is barred an illegal check as well. In **women's hockey** stepping or gliding into a stationary player is at least a minor, and a major brings an automatic game misconduct (IIHF Rule 101.1; PWHL Rules 52.1 and 52.5).
- **Sending your skate at the man instead of the puck.** Every book's kicking permission is for the *puck*. ⚠️ **Kicking at an opponent sits at the top of every book's scale:** a match penalty under the NHL, the PWHL, USA Hockey (with a likely multi-game suspension), Hockey Canada and CARHA, and under the IIHF a major at the referee's discretion with an automatic game misconduct. USA Hockey also gives a major and a game misconduct for using your skate to push off an opponent. Under the IIHF book British hockey plays at every level, do not win the draw with a skate at all.
- **Practising only your strong side.** Spend the reps on your weak side and your worst technique — that is where the points are.

Takeaways after them, are where you get it. Where the answer lives in another

   IIHF edition your league is running** — then give the one draw in the game

   it, and to whom)*

   first, and which team therefore gets to see a blade angle before showing its
   own? **This has four different answers across the six rulebooks** — give them,

   routine. **Then give the British answer, which is none of the four**, and say
   how much of your blade has to be in the white there. *(§Who puts their stick
   down first)*

3. Your centre's best move is kicking the puck back with their skate. Where is
   that a legitimate technique, where is it not, and what does using it cost the
   team in the book you play under in Britain? **Then say what the same skate costs

   *(§Using your feet — the puck, the man, and the goal, §When your

4. Which faceoff circle is your strong side? Give the self-test that does not

5. You lose a defensive-zone draw cleanly. Where do your two wingers go? This
   has no single answer — say what the versions are, what decides which is
   yours, and when the decision has to have been made. *(§Defensive-zone draws)*

6. Most draws end in neither a win nor a loss. What is supposed to happen then,
   who are the two players nearest the puck, and what is the most common thing

7. You win a defensive-zone draw back to your boards-side defenceman and the

   anyway? *(§Why Faceoffs Matter — Honestly, §So why bother?)*

## Key Takeaways

1. **Every time you take a draw, watch the linesperson's hand, not the other centre — and own three or four draws, not one.** React to the drop and you can train it; react to them and you are always second. Mix the backhand pull, the forehand pull, the tie-up and the lift.
2. **When a centre keeps beating you clean, or cheats the drop, tie them up and let your wingers win it.** Stick on stick only — never a hand on their stick, never your stick on their hands. Do not cheat back: moving early is a violation. If the tie-up still loses, ask your bench about changing the taker.
3. **Get low from your knees and hips, never by pushing your head over the dot; win scrums with your stick, never hands or body.** ⚠️ **Pre-drop helmet contact can cost the draw and bring a minor**, mandatory under USA Hockey, Hockey Canada and CARHA. In your own crease, a hand or body on the puck can be a penalty shot.
4. **If your skate goes near the puck at the dot, it goes at the puck; and go round the other centre, never through them.** ⚠️ **A skate at the man can end your game in every book, and is a match penalty in five of the six.** Under the IIHF, do not win the draw with a skate at all.
5. **On your weak side in your own end, do not pull to your backhand — it sends the puck into your slot.** Use the forehand pull, flip your hands, or let a centre who shoots the other way take that dot. **Then rim it hard enough to reach your winger, or tight enough for your goalie to stop it legally.**
6. **Fetching a puck won to the boards, never turn your back to anyone coming at you and never duck:** skates parallel to the boards, forearm and hip to the wall, head up, chin off your chest. ⚠️ **As the forechecker, never hit a player turned toward the wall, and hit no one until you know your game allows checking.**
7. **On an own-end draw, the four of you off the dot move on the drop, to fixed places.** Commonly the wingers take the points on a loss and the wall between the hash marks and the goal line on a win, the slot defenceman holds the net front, and the two nearest attack a tie-up. Ask your bench.
8. **On an offensive-zone draw, win it back to a shooter whose feet are already set, and know what you do if you lose it.** Commonly — ask your bench — the strong-side defenceman shoots and the inside winger drives the net, staying off the goalie. Lose it cleanly and the wingers pressure at once.
9. **Keep the playbook small enough that the whole line can run it.** Two or three plays per zone, called in one word and acknowledged by everyone, each with a failure branch. When the call is confused or a replacement is in the dot, tie up in your own end and take the point shot in theirs.
10. **When they ice the puck, win the draw and attack before they breathe.** Under the NHL, IIHF and PWHL books they can neither change nor take a time-out; under other books, check. Where you pick the dot, take your centre's strong side. **When you iced it, get the puck out at the first chance.**
11. **Late on, the score decides the draw.** Protecting a one-goal lead in your end, settle for the tie-up or send your best taker with a plan for a loss. Chasing a goal, win it straight back to a set shooter with a body at the net. Against a pulled goalie, shoot at the empty net only with a clean lane.
12. **Before every draw, tell your linemates: skates and sticks behind your own hash mark, skates off the circle line.** In all six books a teammate stepping in early gets your **centre** thrown out, not themselves. ⚠️ **And once the whistle blows, stay outside the circle** — a scrum that crosses it moves the draw to the neutral zone.
13. **Replacing a centre sent from the dot, take it straight; know beforehand if that is you.** Nobody moves early, leans or reaches; let the official clear the dot first. ⚠️ **A second violation can cost a minor in any book, and in most it will. Pre-drop contact with the other centre is never free:** the referee may add a minor, and under USA Hockey, Hockey Canada and CARHA must.
14. **Put the whole blade flat in the white and leave it there until the puck lands**; the toe alone fails under CARHA and the In-House Rules. ⚠️ **Never refuse to put your stick down to wait the other centre out:** every book can take you out of the dot for it. **When you must place first, vary the angle.**
15. **On the power play, do not lose the draw cleanly; on the kill, a tie-up is a win.** ⚠️ **A clean shorthanded win can go straight down the ice — but USA Hockey allows that only at Youth 15-Only and above, Girls 16U and above, High School and Adult, and the NHL and PWHL withdraw it after an unfilled major.**
16. **Stop chasing your faceoff percentage, but do work at your draws.** Team faceoff percentage explains only about 6% of the variation in NHL team points, while centres with 500+ draws in 2025-26 ran from 62.1% to 39.7%. Attend to the draws that matter, and practise the draw itself.
