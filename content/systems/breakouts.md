## Overview

```facts
Goal: Turn defending into attacking — the organised exit from your own defensive zone
Key: The breakout runs several times a shift, every shift, all game — coaching material calls it the most-run system in hockey, though no published count ranks plays by frequency
Risk: It falls apart because five players ran five different plays, not because anyone lacked skill
Convention: Almost everything here is a coaching choice, not a law of hockey — teams call the same play different names, and some call different plays the same name
Action: Learn the framework here, then find out what your team actually calls and actually runs
```

A **breakout** — your team's organised exit from your own end — is run several times a shift, every shift, all game; coaching material calls it the most-run play in hockey, though no published count ranks plays by frequency. At amateur level it fails for want of agreement, not skill: five players running five different plays.

**Leaving with the puck is worth far more than flinging it clear.** An analysis of roughly 230,000 tracked NHL exits — Corey Sznajder's manual tracking, covering roughly half the games of 2016-17 and 2017-18 rather than both seasons complete — found carrying or passing the puck out led to an attack the other way about 89% of the time, against about 20% for a clear nobody recovered — though the study's own conclusion is that **possession is the explanation, not the method**, and a clear your own team collects performs about as well. So keep the puck by default, and clear it because you have read the situation — outnumbered with nobody to pass to, or a turnover in front of your own net as the alternative — rather than by reflex.

**Do not move the puck across the ice in front of your own goal** — not with a pass, not by skating it — **unless the read is unmistakable.** The route to the far side is behind your own net, where an interception means very little; the same pass in front of the net is a point-blank chance against. The one exception is the cross-ice "over" in section 3, which accepts exactly that risk deliberately: it needs a forecheck genuinely over-loaded to one side with the middle empty, and where the behind-the-net route exists you take that instead.

**Under pressure on the side wall, where you send the puck depends on the chaser.** Sealed against the wall, send it onward around the curve of the rink to a teammate further round — hard and low, because a soft one dies in the corner and is a gift. Over-skated by someone who cannot stop, send it back the way you came into the space they have left, and whoever arrives there must call for it, because you are looking the other way.

**Almost everything else is a coaching choice, not a law of hockey.** Where the wingers stand, and whether the centre stays back or leaves early, differ from team to team; teams call the same play different names, and some call different plays the same name. Learn the shapes, then ask which your team runs — starting with what they mean by "over", a word that names both one of the safest plays here and the riskiest.

## A Note on Language

Terms used throughout, defined once here:

- **Strong side** — the side of the ice the puck is currently on. **Weak side** — the opposite side.
- **Low** — closer to your own goal line. **High** — closer to the blue line you're facing.
- **The wall** — the boards along the side of the rink. "On the wall" means playing tight to the boards.
- **The hash marks** — the short red lines marking the outer edges of each faceoff circle, two feet long and 5'7" apart, **four per circle** (NHL Rule 1.9): a pair on the boards side and a pair on the middle-of-the-ice side. They sit level with the faceoff dot, which in your own end is **twenty feet out from the goal line** — a long way above the crease, not beside it. They're the standard landmark for where a winger stands on the breakout, so getting the distance right matters. **"On the wall at the hash marks" means the band on the boards between the hash-mark line and the goal line — at or just *below* the dot line, not above it.** That is roughly the bottom third of the distance from the goal line to the blue line (which sits 64 feet out, Rule 1.5). Throughout this document, "between the hash marks and the goal line" is that same spot.
- **Forecheck** — pressure applied by the attacking team in *your* defensive zone, trying to stop your breakout. The opposite of a **backcheck** (racing back to defend your own end). Full treatment in [Forechecking Systems](forechecking_systems.md).
- **F1 / F2 / F3** — the forecheckers, numbered by **order of arrival**, not by position. F1 is whoever gets there first. These are roles, not people; your centre is often F2 or F3.
- **Outlet** — a teammate available to receive the first pass out of the zone.
- **The point** — the position at the blue line, at the top of the offensive zone. When your opponents "have the point", that is where their defencemen are standing. Not to be confused with the **half-wall**, which is the boards level with the faceoff dot, far below it.
- **The top of the circle** — the edge of a faceoff circle furthest from the goal line, roughly 35 feet out. A common landmark for where a shot becomes dangerous.
- **The slot** — the ice directly in front of the net between the two faceoff circles, from the goalmouth out to the tops of the circles. The **high slot** is its upper half, dots to tops of circles; the **goalmouth** is the immediate net front. **The danger zone** (sometimes "the home-plate area") is the slot plus the ice between the faceoff dots down to the goal line — the most dangerous ice on the rink, where a shot is worth several times the same shot from outside it ([Shooting](../technique/shooting.md) covers the values and their qualifications; [Rink Map and Glossary](../foundation/rink_map_and_glossary.md) covers the shape, which is an analytics convention rather than an official marking). It is the ice you do not move the puck through on a breakout.

## 1. Controlled Exits vs Clears

### The two ways out

```facts
Options: Only two categories of zone exit exist — a controlled exit or a clear
Goal: Controlled exit — leave the zone with the puck on a teammate's stick, carried out or passed out; possession is retained
Action: Clear — put the puck out of the zone with no particular teammate in mind: off the glass, off the boards, high down the ice
Risk: A clear surrenders possession, and whether you get it back is a coin flip at best
```

There are only two categories of zone exit, and they are not the same thing:

- **A controlled exit** — you leave the zone with the puck on a teammate's stick, either carried out or passed out. Possession is retained.
- **A clear** (or **dump-out**) — you put the puck out of the zone with no particular teammate in mind: off the glass, off the boards, high down the ice. Possession is surrendered, and whether you get it back is a coin flip at best.

### Why controlled exits matter

```facts
Key: An analysis of roughly 230,000 tracked NHL zone exits found controlled exits led to a subsequent zone entry about 89% of the time, unrecovered dump-outs about 20%
Key: The tracking underneath it is Corey Sznajder's manual dataset — roughly half the games of 2016-17 and 2017-18, not two complete seasons
Key: The study's own conclusion is that possession is the explanation, not the method — a dump-out your team recovers performs about as well as a controlled exit
Goal: A clean breakout is what gives you a carried entry; a panicked clear is what gives you a dump-in against a set defence
```

The tracking research is unusually one-sided on this. An analysis of roughly 230,000 tracked NHL zone exits, published on Hockey Graphs — the tracking underneath it is Corey Sznajder's manual dataset, **roughly half the games of the 2016-17 and 2017-18 seasons** rather than both seasons complete, and Hockey Graphs is the analysis rather than the tracker — found that **controlled exits (carry-outs and pass-outs) led to a subsequent zone entry about 89% of the time, while unrecovered dump-outs did so about 20% of the time** — controlled exits were close to four times more likely to produce the next attack. Crucially, the study's own conclusion is that *possession* is the explanation, not the method: a dump-out your team recovers performs about as well as a controlled exit. It is losing the puck that hurts, not the shape of the play.

The breakout connects to the other half of the transition chain. The reference work on zone *entries* is Eric Tulsky, Geoffrey Detweiler, Robert Spencer and Corey Sznajder, *Using Zone Entry Data To Separate Offensive, Neutral, And Defensive Zone Performance* (2013 MIT Sloan Sports Analytics Conference), which hand-tracked 330 NHL games from 2011-12. Their headline finding, in their own words: *"carrying the puck across the blue line generates more than twice as many shots, scoring chances, and goals as dumping the puck in."* At 5-on-5 their tracked rates were **0.53–0.62 shots per carry-in against 0.22–0.28 per dump-in** — and "shots" there means **unblocked shot attempts** (shots on goal plus misses), not shots on goal, so do not set those figures against a shots-on-goal rate. [Zone Entries](zone_entries.md) covers the numbers and that qualification. A clean breakout is what gives you a carried entry; a panicked clear is what gives you a dump-in against a set defence. Full treatment, including the break-even maths on when a dump is genuinely correct, in [Zone Entries](zone_entries.md).

### When a clear is the right play

```facts
Mindset: Clearing is a decision, not a reflex
Read: Clear when you are outnumbered low with no outlet, when the alternative is a turnover in the slot, when you need a line change, or late in a game you are leading
Priority: A clear costs you possession; a slot turnover costs you a goal — that trade is never close
Rule: A clear taken for a line change is only a rest if it does not ice — under NHL and IIHF Rule 81.4 the team that ices may substitute only for injury, a returning pulled goalie or a strength-changing penalty, and Rule 87.1 denies it a time-out; USA Hockey imposes neither
Rule: A shorthanded team may ice the puck freely — NHL Rule 81.1, "equal or superior in numerical strength"
Rule: NHL Rule 81.6 withdraws that icing exemption from a team short by a major with an empty penalty bench
Rule: IIHF Rule 81.6 has no such clause, so in Britain — which plays the IIHF book — the shorthanded exemption is not withdrawn; see UK and England Rules
Rule: USA Hockey grants the shorthanded icing exemption only from Youth 15-Only, Girls 16U, High School and Adult upward (Rule 624(b)(1))
Action: Default to the controlled exit; take the clear when the read says take it
Never: Clear by default and attempt control only when the ice is already empty — most amateur teams have this backwards
```

None of that makes clearing wrong. It makes clearing a **decision** rather than a reflex. Get the puck out without possession when:

- **You are outnumbered low** and no outlet exists — three forecheckers, two of you, nobody free.
- **You are beaten and the alternative is a turnover in the slot.** A clear costs you possession; a slot turnover costs you a goal. That trade is never close.
- **You are on the penalty kill** — in most rule sets. A shorthanded team may ice the puck freely (NHL Rule 81.1 applies icing only to a team "equal or superior in numerical strength"), and where that holds, clearing is the entire strategy. **It does not hold everywhere:** USA Hockey grants the exemption only at Youth 15-Only and above, Girls 16U and above, High School and Adult (Rule 624(b)(1)), and NHL Rule 81.6 withdraws it from a team shorthanded by a major with an empty penalty bench. **The IIHF's 81.6 — the rule British hockey plays under — carries neither restriction.** Its two paragraphs cover only numerical strength and the timing of an expiring penalty; there is no major-penalty clause, and IIHF Rule 20.3 attaches no icing sanction to the requirement to keep a substitute in the penalty box. [Special Teams](special_teams.md) covers the rule; check yours before the kill's whole plan rests on it, and see [UK and England Rules](../foundation/uk_rules.md) for which book is yours.
- **You need a line change.** Getting the puck the length of the ice away — the rink is 200 feet long (Rule 1.2) — buys you time to get tired legs off. ⚠️ **Only if it does not ice, though, and that is the trap in this one.** Under **NHL and IIHF Rule 81.4** a team that ices the puck may substitute before the ensuing draw only *"to replace a goalkeeper who had been substituted for an extra attacker, to replace an injured player, or when a penalty has been assessed which affects the on-ice strength of either team"*, and **Rule 87.1** denies it a time-out as well — so a clear taken *for* the change buys you a defensive-zone faceoff with the same tired legs still out there. Get it out off the glass or off the boards short of the centre red line rather than the length of the ice — *over* the glass is a delay-of-game minor, not a clear. **USA Hockey imposes neither restriction.** (*How much time depends entirely on how quickly they retrieve and regroup; there is no measured figure for it, so this document does not offer one.*) See [Game Management](game_management.md).
- **Late in a game you're leading.** Risk tolerance drops; simple gets you the two points. See [Risk Management](../hockey-iq/risk_management.md).

**The habit to build:** default to the controlled exit, take the clear when the read says take it. Most amateur teams have this backwards — they clear by default and attempt control only when the ice is already empty.

## 2. The Five Roles in Every Breakout

Whatever play is called, five jobs exist. Knowing which one is yours *before* the puck is retrieved is most of the battle.

![](diagram:breakout-five-roles)

### 1. The puck-retriever D (D1)

```facts
Position: The defenceman who goes back for the puck — your job starts before you touch it
Action: Look over your inside shoulder on the way back, while you still have time to change your plan
Technique: Arrive at an angle, not straight on — curl in so your first touch already has your body opening toward the ice
Action: Take the puck to a decision point — behind the net or up the wall — rather than stopping on it
Never: Through the slot — not with a pass, not with your feet; the cross-ice over in section 3 is the only exception, and it is the highest-risk of the nine breakout options
Risk: A stationary defenceman is the one who gets hit, and at the puck with your back turned you are guessing
```

The defenceman who goes back for the puck. Your job starts before you touch it:

- **Look over your inside shoulder on the way back.** You need to know where the forecheck is coming from while you still have time to change your plan. Once you're at the puck with your back turned, you're guessing. See [Scanning and Anticipation](../hockey-iq/scanning_and_anticipation.md).
- **Arrive at an angle, not straight on.** Approaching the puck square to the end boards leaves you facing the wall with a forechecker arriving in your back. Curl in so your first touch already has your body opening toward the ice.
- **Take the puck to a decision point** — behind the net or up the wall — rather than stopping on it. A stationary defenceman is the one who gets hit.
- **Never through the slot.** Not with a pass, not with your feet. See [Defender](../positions/defender.md). The single exception is the cross-ice "over" in section 3, the highest-risk of the nine breakout options, which needs a read most shifts never give you.

### 2. The support D (D2)

```facts
Position: Usually the opposite side of the net from your partner, level with or slightly above the goal line, so a pass behind the net reaches you cleanly
Action: Be an option, not a spectator — and be the second wave, because if the first option fails the puck comes back to you
Key: You are usually the one who makes the call — "up", "over", "wheel", "reverse", "rim", "time", "man on" — because you can see the forecheck arriving behind your partner
Action: Occupy the net front for the wheel by getting to your spot early and holding it; already standing there lengthens a forechecker's route across the slot
Rule: NHL Rule 56.1 — "a player is always entitled to use his body position to lengthen an opponent's path to the puck", but only "provided his stick is not utilized… his free hand is not used and he does not take advantage of his body position to deliver an otherwise illegal check"
Rule: NHL Rule 56.1 — "moving laterally and without establishing body position, then making contact with the non-puck carrier is not permitted and will be penalized as interference"
Rule: Moving into an opponent's path without initially having body position is a pick, a minor — and not capped there: NHL 56.4 allows a major on violence and 56.5 a game misconduct where that major injures, IIHF 56.5 a major plus game misconduct where it recklessly endangers, with nobody hurt
Rule: USA Hockey caps Rule 625 itself at a minor, but that is not the ceiling on the act — a check delivered to a vulnerable or defenceless opponent with "no effort to play the puck" must be penalised under the boarding, charging, checking-from-behind, head-contact or roughing rules instead
Rule: Where a pick goes to roughing, USA Hockey 640(g) is a major plus a game misconduct for recklessly endangering an opponent and 640(h) a match, both only for acts under 640(b–f) — and 602(a)'s match penalty for a player who "recklessly endangers or attempts to injure" sits outside 625 and 640 both
Technique: Stick in tight and vertical, hands to yourself, no bump as he goes past
Never: Drift across to meet him — a stick stuck out sideways across the slot is interference even though your feet never moved
```

The partner. This is the least-understood job on the ice and the one that makes breakouts work.

- **Be an option, not a spectator.** Position yourself so a pass behind the net reaches you cleanly — usually on the opposite side of the net, level with or slightly above the goal line.
- **You are usually the one who makes the call.** You can see the forecheck arriving behind your partner. "Up", "over", "wheel", "reverse", "rim", "time", "man on" — those calls are mostly yours. More in section 9.
- **Occupy the net front for the wheel — and understand exactly what that is and is not.** If your partner is carrying it around the net, being *already standing* in front of the net lengthens a forechecker's route across the slot. Rule 56.1 is explicit that this is legal: "A player is allowed the ice he is standing on (body position) and is not required to move in order to let an opponent proceed," and "a player is always entitled to use his body position to lengthen an opponent's path to the puck." **Read the rest of that sentence, because at the net front it is the part that gets called** — the entitlement holds only *"provided his stick is not utilized (to make himself 'bigger' and therefore considerably lengthening the distance his opponent must travel to get where he is going); his free hand is not used and he does not take advantage of his body position to deliver an otherwise illegal check."* So: stick in tight and vertical, hands to yourself, and no bump as he goes past. A stick stuck out sideways across the slot is interference even though your feet never moved. **And what is not legal is moving into him.** The same rule: "Moving laterally and without establishing body position, then making contact with the non-puck carrier is not permitted and will be penalized as interference" — and a player who "moves into an opponent's path without initially having body position" is delivering a **pick**, which is a minor — and not capped there: 56.4 allows a major on the degree of violence, and **the NHL's own game misconduct sits one step further on: 56.5 imposes one *"when a major penalty is imposed under this rule for an infraction resulting in an injury of an opponent"***, so under the NHL book it takes an injury. **Under the IIHF book it does not** — 56.5 there reaches a major plus a game misconduct where it recklessly endangers, with nobody hurt. ⚠️ **USA Hockey is the one to know if you play in the States, and it is built differently.** Its Rule 625 defines interference in the words of this exact play — using the body, *"('pick' or 'block')"*, *"to impede the progress of an opponent (non-puck carrier) with no effort to play the puck, maintain normal foot speed or maintain an established skating lane"* — and its *Standard of Play and Rule Emphasis – Restraining Fouls* section lists as an example of interference *"Using the body to establish a 'pick' or 'block' that prevents an opponent from being able to chase a puck carrier."* **Rule 625 itself writes nothing above the minor. That is not the ceiling on the act.** USA Hockey's *Declaration of Player Safety, Fair Play & Respect* directs that infractions arising from *"a body check delivered to a vulnerable or defenseless player **must** be penalized under the boarding, charging, checking from behind, head contact or roughing rules"*, and that where it is *"done in a dangerous, careless or reckless (unacceptable) manner where the player delivering the check has made **no effort to play the puck**, the major plus game misconduct or match penalty provisions of these rules **must** be assessed."* Roughing is Rule 640, and **640(g)** assesses *"a major plus a game misconduct penalty ... to any player who recklessly endangers an opponent as a result of any actions falling under Rule 640(b, c, d, e or f)"* — the sub-sections covering an avoidable check on a player no longer in control of the puck, a check delivered *"with no effort to gain possession of the puck"* with the stick blade above the knees, and a check on an opponent already physically engaged for the puck. **640(h)** adds *"a match penalty for reckless endangerment"* under the same sub-sections. ⚠️ **Note the scope of those sub-sections before you rely on them**: 640(b), the avoidable check on a player no longer in control of the puck, is written *"(except Adult Male Classifications)"*, while (d) and (e) carry no such limit — so in adult men's hockey the escalation route for this play runs through (d) and (e), not (b). **And Rule 602(a) sits outside 625 and 640 both** — a match penalty for any player who *"recklessly endangers or attempts to injure any opposing player"*, tied to no named foul at all. ⚠️ **The trigger is the state of the player you hit.** USA Hockey counts a skater as vulnerable or defenceless when *"unaware, unprepared, or unsuspecting of an impending hit"* — which describes the forechecker whose eyes are on the puck when you step sideways into him. Whether a referee grades it that way is their judgement; the point is that the minor is a floor and not a ceiling under this book too. So: **get to your spot early and hold it. Do not drift across to meet him.** The difference between a free two seconds for your partner and a penalty that starts at two minutes is whether your feet were already planted.
- **Be the second wave.** If the first option fails, the puck comes back to you. Don't be somewhere it can't.

### 3. The strong-side winger

```facts
Position: The winger on the same side as the puck — the primary outlet
Position: You start high, up on the opposing point man, while the other team still has the puck
Action: Come down the wall as the breakout starts, to the boards between the hash marks and the goal line on the puck side
Key: Those two spots are 44 to 64 ft apart and belong to two different moments — the trip between them, and its timing, is the job
Action: Present a target and time your movement into the pass
Technique: Receive with your body open to the ice, not with your nose against the glass
```

The winger on the same side as the puck — the primary outlet, and the most commonly botched job in hockey. Full detail in section 4.

- **You start high, not low.** While the other team still has the puck you are up on the opposing point man, covering him — that is your defensive-zone job, and [Defensive Zone Coverage](defensive_zone_coverage.md) is the authority on it.
- **You come down the wall as the breakout starts.** The moment your team wins the puck — or your defenceman gets to it with time — you leave the point and drop to the wall **between the hash marks and the goal line**, on the same side as the puck. Those two spots are **44 to 64 feet apart** — the point is at the blue line, 64 ft out from the goal line, and the outlet band runs from level with the faceoff dot, 20 ft out from the goal line, down to the goal line itself ([Rink Map and Glossary](../foundation/rink_map_and_glossary.md) covers both figures) — and they belong to two different moments. The trip between them, and its timing, *is* the job; neither end of it is a place to live.
- **Present a target and time your movement into the pass.**
- **Receive with your body open to the ice**, not with your nose against the glass.

### 4. The weak-side winger

```facts
Position: The far winger — what you do here is genuinely system-dependent
Convention: Under the standard swing breakout, come across or up the middle as the second option and the outlet for a cross-ice or over play, then get out ahead of the puck
Convention: Under a "slash" or flying-winger structure, leave early through the middle toward the strong side or up ice, stretching the opposing defence vertically
Convention: Under a rim-heavy structure, you are the rim's target and you stay low on your own wall to collect it
Action: Find out which your team runs
Risk: Leaving early in a system that expects you low is the reason the breakout has no second option
```

The far winger. What you do here is genuinely system-dependent:

- **Under the standard swing breakout** (assumed throughout this document), you come across or up the middle as the second option and the outlet for a cross-ice or over play, then get out ahead of the puck.
- **Under a "slash" or flying-winger structure**, you leave early through the middle toward the strong side or up ice, stretching the opposing defence vertically and creating space behind you. This is deliberately more aggressive and leaves you less available defensively.
- **Under a rim-heavy structure**, you are the rim's target and you're staying low on your own wall to collect it.

**Find out which your team runs.** A weak-side winger who leaves early in a system that expects you low is the reason the breakout has no second option.

### 5. The centre

```facts
Position: Low support, underneath the puck — the pivot the whole structure turns on
Convention: The five-role structure is itself a coaching choice — the conventional swing breakout, taught by most development programmes and assumed throughout
Options: The centre fly — the centre leaves the zone early up the middle to stretch the ice, and the weak-side winger drops in to take the low support job instead
Options: Flat or spread breakouts — both wingers hold higher near the blue line and the exit is made with longer passes rather than short support
Action: Ask your coach which one you play
Risk: A centre flying in a system where the wingers hold high leaves nobody underneath the puck at all — the failure that produces most weekend breakdowns
```

Low support, underneath the puck. This is the pivot the whole structure turns on, and it is covered in full in section 5.

> **This five-role structure is itself a coaching choice.** What's described above is the conventional **swing breakout** taught by most development programmes and assumed throughout. The two alternatives you will actually meet:
> - **The centre fly** (sometimes "centre high", or the "weak-side-wing-support" breakout): the centre deliberately leaves the zone early up the middle to stretch the ice, and the **weak-side winger drops in** to take the low support job instead.
> - **Flat or spread breakouts:** both wingers hold higher, near the blue line, and the exit is made with longer passes rather than short support.
>
> Ask your coach which one you play. The failure that produces most weekend breakdowns is a centre flying in a system where the wingers hold high near the blue line — the flat/spread structure above — leaving nobody underneath the puck at all. (Note this is a different sense of "high" from the defensive-zone instruction to a winger to *stay high on the point*: that one expires as soon as your team wins the puck, and in the swing breakout the strong-side winger comes down the wall.)

## 3. The Named Breakout Options

Each option below gets the same treatment: **setup**, **the read** that calls for it, **execution**, **the risk**, and **how the opposition defeats it**.

Two warnings on names before you start. First, teams disagree about these names — badly. Second, one collision is so common it deserves its own heading, and it is covered under "Over" below.

---

### Up

```facts
Action: Direct pass from the puck-carrying defenceman to the strong-side winger on the wall — the fastest of the nine breakout options when the lane is open, though no count ranks them by speed
Position: D1 below the goal line or on the wall, the winger on the boards between the hash marks and the goal line, the centre low in the middle as the bail-out
Read: The forecheck has committed behind the net or to the middle, leaving the wall lane open — or F1 is arriving fast on the wrong side, so pass before F1 can influence the lane
Technique: Pass firmly and to the winger's stick, not to the boards behind them; head up first, and aim where the winger will be, not where they were
Risk: The wall pass travels the lane a forecheck posts its wall-sealer in — F2 in a 2-1-2 — so intercepted at the hash marks it is a scoring chance from the top of the circle
Countered by: Any forecheck with a designated wall-sealer — F2 in a 2-1-2, the strong-side half-wall player in a 1-2-2 — or a weak-side defenceman stepping down on the winger the instant the pass leaves
```

The direct pass from the puck-carrying defenceman to the strong-side winger on the wall. The fastest of the nine breakout options when the lane is open — no count ranks breakout plays by speed — and, when it's there, the right one.

- **Setup:** D1 has the puck below the goal line or on the wall. The strong-side winger is on the boards between the hash marks and the goal line. The centre is low in the middle as the bail-out.
- **The read:** Take it when the forecheck has committed *behind* the net or to the middle, leaving the wall lane open. It is also the right call when F1 is arriving fast on the wrong side — pass before F1 can influence the lane. D2 typically makes this call, because D2 can see what D1 cannot.
- **Execution:** Pass firmly and *to the winger's stick*, not to the boards behind them — a rim to a stationary winger is a different play and a worse one. Head up first: aim the pass where the winger will be, not where they were.
- **The risk:** The wall pass travels the lane a forecheck posts its wall-sealer in — F2 in a 2-1-2, the strong-side half-wall player in a 1-2-2. Intercepted at the hash marks, it's a scoring chance from the top of the circle.
- **How it's defeated:** Any forecheck with a designated wall-sealer beats it — the second forechecker in a 2-1-2 whose job is exactly this, or the strong-side half-wall player in a 1-2-2. Teams also beat it by having their weak-side defenceman step down on the winger the instant the pass leaves, so the winger has no time even after a good pass.

### Wheel

```facts
Action: The defenceman carries the puck himself, skating behind the net and up the far side
Position: D1 retrieves in one corner and keeps skating; D2 takes the net front early, and the far-side winger and the centre become outlets
Read: You need two things — speed and space: a step on the first forechecker, and no second forechecker waiting on the far side
Technique: Cut tight to the net so the net itself screens you, and come out of the turn with your head up and speed already built
Risk: You carry the puck through the middle-ish of your own zone with your back briefly to the play; if you are caught, you are caught below your own goal line with the puck
Rule: A partner already standing at the net front is legal, but sliding across into the forechecker's path is an interference minor (NHL 56.1) — not capped there: NHL 56.4 allows a major on violence, 56.5 a game misconduct where that major injures, IIHF 56.5 a major plus game misconduct with nobody hurt
Rule: USA Hockey writes no tier above 625's minor, but a slide into a forechecker delivered as a check leaves 625 for the boarding, charging, checking-from-behind, head-contact or roughing rules
Rule: USA Hockey's roughing tiers are 640(g), a major plus a game misconduct, and 640(h), a match — both reaching only acts under 640(b–f) — with 602(a) a match penalty sitting outside 625 and 640 both
Countered by: A second forechecker holding the far side — precisely what F2 does in a 2-1-2 — or a forechecker who takes the back-door route around the net rather than chasing you
```

The defenceman carries the puck himself, skating behind the net and up the far side.

- **Setup:** D1 retrieves in one corner and keeps skating around the back of the net. D2 takes up position in front of the net early — see the interference note in section 2 — and the far-side winger and the centre become outlets.
- **The read:** You need two things: **speed** and **space**. Wheel when you have a step on the first forechecker and there is no second forechecker waiting on the far side. It's excellent against a slow or single-man forecheck, and against a forecheck that has just committed hard to one corner.
- **Execution:** Cut **tight** to the net — the net itself is your screen, and cutting wide gives the forechecker a straight line to intercept you. Come out of the turn with your head up and speed already built. Your partner, **already standing** at the net front, makes a cross-slot pursuit slower: the forechecker has to go around a player who is entitled to the ice he is on. That is legal. It does not make the pursuit *illegal*, and if your partner slides across into the forechecker's path to make it happen, that is an interference minor against your team (Rule 56.1) — and not capped there: 56.4 allows a major on the degree of violence, and NHL 56.5 adds a game misconduct, though only *"when a major penalty is imposed under this rule for an infraction resulting in an injury of an opponent"*. IIHF 56.5 reaches a major plus a game misconduct where it recklessly endangers, with nobody hurt, and Hockey Canada 8.3(b) reaches the same tier on *"the degree of violence of the impact"* with nobody hurt, and makes it mandatory where it injures. ⚠️ **And USA Hockey, the book you are under if you play in the United States, gets there by a different road:** its Rule 625 writes no tier above the minor, but a slide into a forechecker delivered as a body check does not stay in 625 — the *Declaration of Player Safety* directs it to the boarding, charging, checking-from-behind, head-contact or roughing rules, where **640(g)** is a major plus a game misconduct for recklessly endangering an opponent and **640(h)** a match — both reaching only *"actions falling under Rule 640(b, c, d, e or f)"*, not roughing at large — and **602(a)** reaches a match penalty from outside both. Set out in full in section 2.
- **The risk:** You are carrying the puck through the middle-ish of your own zone with your back briefly to the play. If you're caught, you're caught below your own goal line with the puck.
- **How it's defeated:** A second forechecker holding the far side — precisely what F2 does in a 2-1-2 — turns a wheel into a trap. A forechecker who takes the "back door" route around the net rather than chasing you also kills it. The counter to being caught mid-wheel is the reverse, below.

### Reverse

```facts
Action: Send the puck back against the flow of the forecheck, usually a hard backhand off the boards behind you
Position: Moving one direction with the puck — often mid-wheel or up the wall — with a forechecker chasing your hip and D2 or the centre arriving behind you
Read: The forechecker has over-committed and skated past you; their momentum is the whole play, because they cannot stop
Technique: Bank it off the boards firmly and low behind you — soft reverses die in the corner and become turnovers
Key: The receiving teammate must call for it — "reverse!" — because D1 is looking the wrong way
Risk: You are putting the puck into a space you cannot see, below your own goal line; if nobody is there, the opposition has it behind your net with your defence facing the wrong way
Countered by: A containing F1 who steers rather than chases and is never past you to begin with, or an aggressive F2 who reads the reverse and arrives in the vacated space
Key: A rim goes onward around the boards in the direction the puck was already travelling; a reverse goes back against it
```

Sending the puck **back against the flow** of the forecheck, usually a hard backhand off the boards behind you, to a teammate arriving in the space the forechecker just vacated.

- **Setup:** You are moving one direction with the puck — often mid-wheel or working up the wall — with a forechecker chasing your hip. D2 or the centre is arriving behind you.
- **The read:** Reverse beats a forechecker who has **over-committed and skated past you**. The whole play depends on their momentum: they cannot stop, so the puck goes where they've just been. As [Defender](../positions/defender.md) puts it: rim to beat a forechecker who is sealing you against the boards; reverse to beat one who has over-committed. Reading which of the two you're facing is a core defenceman skill.
- **Execution:** Bank it off the boards firmly and low behind you. Soft reverses die in the corner and become turnovers. The receiving teammate must call for it — **"reverse!"** — because D1 is looking the wrong way.
- **The risk:** You are putting the puck into a space you cannot see, below your own goal line. If nobody is there, the opposition has the puck behind your net with your defence facing the wrong direction.
- **How it's defeated:** Disciplined forechecks defeat reverses by simply not over-committing — the containing F1 of a 1-2-2 steers rather than chases and is never past you to begin with. Aggressive forechecks defeat it with F2 reading the reverse and arriving in the vacated space instead of your teammate.

**Rim and reverse are different plays. Keep them straight:** a **rim** goes *onward* around the boards in the direction the puck was already travelling; a **reverse** goes *back* against it.

### D-to-D

```facts
Action: Pass across to your partner, changing the side the attack comes from and forcing the entire forecheck to reset and shift
Position: D1 with the puck low on one side, D2 available low on the other
Read: Your side is flooded and the other is not — its real value is that it costs the opposition time, and everything they had covered comes uncovered while they travel
Never: Through the slot. Go behind your own net instead; intercepted below the goal line it means nothing, the same pass in front of the net is a point-blank chance against
Action: Pass it quickly rather than skating it across — carrying the puck toward your partner drags the pressure with you
Risk: Executed correctly, one of the lowest-risk plays in hockey; executed through the slot, one of the highest, with very little middle ground
Countered by: A 2-1-2 with two forecheckers arriving on separate sides, or a 1-2-2 whose F1 takes away the D-to-D lane as their first responsibility
```

The pass across to your partner, changing the side of the ice the attack comes from and forcing the entire forecheck to reset and shift.

- **Setup:** D1 has the puck low on one side; D2 is available low on the other.
- **The read:** Use it when your side is flooded and the other is not. Its real value is that it costs the opposition time: a forecheck built to pressure one side has to travel across the ice, and everything they had covered comes uncovered while they do.
- **Execution — and the rule that does not bend:** **Never through the slot.** Go behind your own net. ⚠️ **"Behind the net" is the default, not the only route** — some teams instead exchange high in the zone, above the tops of the circles, reasoning that an interception there is not a slot chance. That is a coaching convention and nothing in the rulebook touches it ([Risk Management](../hockey-iq/risk_management.md) labels it as one); ask which your team plays. **What does not vary is the slot itself.** A D-to-D pass below the goal line that gets intercepted means nothing — the puck is behind the net with no shot available. The same pass in front of the net that gets intercepted is a point-blank chance against, and it is one of the most punished mistakes in the game — coaching emphasis, not a counted ranking. Pass it quickly rather than skating it across; carrying the puck toward your partner just drags the pressure with you and hands them your problem.
- **The risk:** Executed correctly, this is one of the lowest-risk plays in hockey. Executed through the slot, it is one of the highest. There is very little middle ground.
- **How it's defeated:** A 2-1-2 with two forecheckers arriving on separate sides beats D-to-D because there's already someone on your partner. A 1-2-2 defeats it by having F1 take away the D-to-D lane as their *first* responsibility — that is often exactly what the containing forechecker is told to do — forcing the puck up the wall into the two waiting forwards.

### Rim

```facts
Action: A hard shot along the boards that follows the curve of the rink around to a teammate further along — usually the far-side winger
Position: D1 in the corner or behind the net under real pressure, with a winger positioned around the boards to collect it
Read: A forechecker has sealed you against the boards and there is no passing lane through them; it is also the standard release valve when you simply have no time
Technique: Height and pace — low and flat enough to stay on the boards, hard enough to carry all the way around; a soft rim dies in the corner and is a gift
Technique: Aim it to arrive where a teammate can meet it, not simply "around"
Risk: A rim concedes possession more often than any other option except an outright clear — a coaching judgement, not a tracked rate; a 50-50 dressed as a play
Countered by: The play a defenceman sealed on the boards is most expected to make — coaching emphasis, not a counted ranking; any forecheck can post a player at the far wall, and good ones pre-position
```

A hard shot along the boards that follows the curve of the rink around to a teammate further along — usually the far-side winger.

- **Setup:** D1 has the puck in the corner or behind the net under real pressure. A winger is positioned around the boards to collect it.
- **The read:** Rim when a forechecker has **sealed you against the boards** and there is no passing lane through them. It is also the standard release valve when you simply have no time.
- **Execution:** Two things decide whether a rim works. **Height and pace:** keep it low and flat enough to stay on the boards and hard enough to carry all the way around — a soft rim dies in the corner and is a gift. **Target:** aim it to arrive *where a teammate can meet it*, not simply "around". See the trapezoid section (10) — this changes rim targeting fundamentally.
- **The risk:** A rim concedes possession more often than any other option except an outright clear, because the receiver is collecting a moving puck on the wall with a defenceman closing. **That ranking is a coaching judgement, not a tracked rate** — the exit tracking in section 1 measures controlled exits against clears, not one named breakout option against another. It's a 50-50 dressed as a play.
- **How it's defeated:** The rim is the play a forecheck most expects from a defenceman sealed on the boards — coaching emphasis, not a counted ranking. Any forecheck can post a player at the far wall to intercept it, and most do — the weak-side defenceman stepping down to the far wall to kill rims is standard. Skilled teams beat rims without even moving: they read the pressure you're under and pre-position.

### Over / cross-ice

```facts
Never: Use "over" without agreeing which sense — Sense A is this cross-ice pass, the highest-risk option here; Sense B is the D-to-D behind your own net, one of the lowest-risk
Action: Pass from the puck-carrying defenceman across the width of the ice to the far winger, skipping the strong-side structure entirely
Position: D1 with the puck usually a little higher than the goal line; the far-side winger or the weak-side defenceman open across the ice
Read: Only when the forecheck has genuinely over-loaded one side and the middle is empty
Technique: Firm and flat — a cross-ice pass that has to be handled at the receiver's feet has taken so long that the play is over
Risk: The puck travels laterally in front of your own net at the height of the opposing forwards — intercepted, it is picked off in the middle facing your goal
Priority: Where the behind-the-net D-to-D route exists, take it instead — it achieves a similar side-switch with essentially no downside
Countered by: Any forecheck with a middle presence — the three-across middle of a 1-3-1, F3 in a 2-1-2 — and it is defeated by the shape existing at all, not by anyone reacting to it
```

**Read this naming warning first.** The word "over" is used for two completely different plays with completely different risk profiles:

- **Sense A — cross-ice "over":** a pass across the width of the ice to the far winger, in front of your own net. **This is the play described in this section**, and it is the highest-risk of the nine breakout options.
- **Sense B — D-to-D "over":** the pass behind your own net to your partner. Many playbooks and most **goalie call vocabularies** use "over" to mean this, and it is one of the *lowest*-risk plays in hockey.

**Find out which one your team means before you use the word**, because the two carry opposite instructions. This document labels the sense every time it uses the word: **the option below is Sense A; the goalie's call list in section 9 is Sense B.**

The cross-ice over is a pass from the puck-carrying defenceman across the width of the ice to the far winger or far side, skipping the strong-side structure entirely.

- **Setup:** D1 has the puck, usually a little higher than the goal line. The far-side winger or the weak-side defenceman is open across the ice.
- **The read:** Only when the forecheck has genuinely over-loaded one side and the middle is empty. The reward is real — one pass and you've beaten three forecheckers, arriving with the far winger already in stride.
- **Execution:** Firm and flat. A cross-ice pass that has to be handled at the receiver's feet has taken so long that the play is over.
- **The risk — and why this is the highest-risk of the nine breakout options:** the puck travels laterally across the width of the ice, in front of your own net, at the height where the opposing forwards are. If it's intercepted, the interception happens in the middle of the ice facing your goal, with your defence flat-footed and out of position. Compare it to the D-to-D behind the net, which achieves a similar side-switch with essentially no downside. Where the behind-the-net route exists, take it instead.
- **How it's defeated:** Any forecheck with a middle presence — the three-across middle of a 1-3-1, F3 in a 2-1-2 — is standing in this lane by design. It is defeated not by reacting to it but by the shape existing at all.

### Quick-up / counter

```facts
Action: The immediate transition off a turnover, before the forecheck has any chance to set up
Position: Your team has just won the puck — a blocked shot, a stripped puck at the blue line, a rebound — and the opposition still has bodies below the puck
Read: Not really a called play but a recognised moment — the two or three seconds immediately after a turnover are the least organised the opposition will ever be
Technique: First look up ice, not down, and move the puck on the first touch if you can — speed of decision matters more than quality of pass here
Risk: The habit version is the real risk — playing quick-up when the moment isn't there, forcing a pass into a formed defence
Countered by: Counter-pressing — teams that re-attack the puck within a couple of seconds of losing it shorten the fuse rather than removing the play
```

The immediate transition off a turnover, before the forecheck has any chance to set up.

- **Setup:** Your team has just won the puck — a blocked shot, a stripped puck at the blue line, a rebound your goalie has left. The opposition still has bodies below the puck and their structure is nowhere.
- **The read:** This isn't really a called play; it's a recognised moment. **The two or three seconds immediately after a turnover are the least organised the opposition will ever be.** If a teammate is available up the ice, that is the moment to move it — before your own structure has formed, before theirs has re-formed.
- **Execution:** First look up ice, not down. Move the puck on the first touch if you can. Speed of decision matters more than quality of pass here; a five-second wait to make a perfect play has given away the entire advantage.
- **The risk:** The lowest-risk high-reward play available — the opposition has fewer defenders back and less shape than at any other time. The real risk is the *habit* version: playing quick-up when the moment isn't there, forcing a pass into a formed defence.
- **How it's defeated:** Counter-pressing. Teams that immediately re-attack the puck within a couple of seconds of losing it are specifically trying to remove your quick-up window. Against those teams the quick-up is still available — it just has a shorter fuse.

### Stretch pass

```facts
Action: A long pass from your defensive zone to a forward who has left early and is waiting up ice
Position: One forward — usually the weak-side winger — leaves the defensive zone early and stretches high; the retrieving defenceman looks up and hits them
Read: The opposition has committed all three forwards deep on the forecheck and their defencemen have stepped up — that shape leaves a lane and leaves your winger behind coverage
Technique: Head up before you handle the puck, and lead the receiver so they collect it in stride
Rule: NHL Rule 83.1 and IIHF Rule 83.1 are materially identical, differing in pronoun — one skate in contact with the blue line, or behind it, keeps your winger onside, and a skate in the air over the neutral zone still counts, because the plane has not been broken
Rule: The airborne skate is the NHL and IIHF position, so it is the British one; under USA Hockey 630(a) the skate must have contact with the neutral zone or the blue line, and Hockey Canada 6.11 counts only skates in contact with the ice, so in two books of the four an airborne skate is offside
Rule: Neither the NHL nor the IIHF rulebook contains a centre-red-line ("two-line") pass restriction, and neither do USA Hockey's or Hockey Canada's — CARHA Rule 73(d) abolishes it in terms, so at every level with a published book the long pass is legal
Never: Rebuild your attack around a local two-line pass rule unless somebody shows it to you in writing — no published rulebook held here confirms that any league still runs one
Risk: An intercepted stretch is a full-speed rush the other way with your winger a full zone out of the play — genuinely high-risk/high-reward, and in most amateur hockey not a good default
Countered by: A 1-3-1, which exists to kill it; a conservative 1-2-2 with two forwards holding high; or defencemen simply backing off rather than stepping up
```

A long pass from your defensive zone to a forward who has left early and is waiting up ice, typically near the neutral zone or the far blue line.

- **Setup:** One forward — usually the weak-side winger — leaves the defensive zone early and stretches high. The retrieving defenceman looks up and hits them.
- **The read:** Take the stretch when the opposition has committed all three forwards deep on the forecheck and their defencemen have stepped up. That is the shape that leaves a lane and leaves your winger behind coverage.
- **Execution:** Head up before you handle the puck, and lead the receiver so they collect it in stride. **Your winger must stay onside at the far blue line, and the rule is worth stating the right way round.** Rule 83.1: *"A player is on-side when either of his skates are in contact with the blue line, or on his own side of the line, at the instant the puck completely crosses the leading edge of the blue line."* So **one skate touching the line, or behind it, keeps your winger onside.** They are only **offside** if **both** skates are completely over the leading edge before the puck completely crosses it. Read plainly: the puck goes first, or at worst you and the puck arrive together — and a skate in the air over the neutral zone still counts as onside, because the plane has not been broken. **The IIHF's Rule 83.1 says this in materially identical words** — it writes *"either of **their** skates"* and *"their 'own side'"* where the NHL writes *"his"* — down to the "plane" of the blue line extending upwards and the skate that "has yet to break the plane" — so if you play anywhere in Britain, the airborne trail skate is legal in your book too. **Under USA Hockey 630(a) and Hockey Canada 6.11 it is not**, though they get there by different words: USA Hockey requires *contact* with the neutral zone or the blue line, and Hockey Canada counts only skates *"in physical contact with the ice surface"*. Either way an airborne skate is offside. **Neither the NHL nor the IIHF rulebook contains a centre-red-line ("two-line") pass restriction** — the long pass is legal, and it is absent from USA Hockey's and Hockey Canada's books too. That now includes the rec level: the **CARHA Hockey Official Rule Book**, the standalone book a large number of Canadian adult leagues play under, abolishes it in as many words at **Rule 73(d)** — *"For the purpose of 2-line passes, the red line will not be in play. It will still be in effect for icing."* ⚠️ **Flagged as unverified:** it is often said that a few older recreational-association rulebooks still run a two-line pass rule locally, but there is no published rulebook held here to confirm it. So do not rebuild your attack around one unless somebody shows it to you in writing. [Neutral Zone Systems](neutral_zone_systems.md) carries the same flag.
- **The risk profile — the honest version:** a completed stretch pass is close to a breakaway. An intercepted one is a full-speed rush the other way with your winger a full zone out of the play — call it **sixty to eighty feet behind the puck** — because they left before the exit was secure. This is genuinely a high-risk/high-reward play, and its expected value depends almost entirely on your league's ability to complete a pass of that length: the blue lines sit **64 feet** out from each goal line (Rule 1.5) and the rink is **200 feet** long (Rule 1.2 — Dimensions; note that 1.5 covers the lines and 1.6 only names the three zones), so from your own goal line to the far blue line is **114 feet**, and even a stretch to the centre red line is **89 feet**. Very few amateur players can put a flat, receivable pass through either. In most amateur hockey it is not a good default. **Those are NHL-sheet distances.** The IIHF book specifies a different sheet, and the current In-House Rules authorise rinks smaller again *"for all levels of hockey"*, in *"all fixtures"*, right across the Home Countries — *"England, Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland"*; [UK and England Rules](../foundation/uk_rules.md) has the amendment and [Rink Map and Glossary](../foundation/rink_map_and_glossary.md) covers the dimensions. Pace the pass to the sheet you are standing on.
- **How it's defeated:** A 1-3-1 exists to kill it — three players stretched across the neutral zone erase the lane. A conservative 1-2-2 with two forwards holding high does much the same. Teams also defeat it simply by having their defencemen back off rather than step up, which makes the receiving winger easy to cover.

### Off the glass and out

```facts
Read: When the alternatives are a turnover in a dangerous area or a play you are not confident in — and when you desperately need a change
Technique: Aim high off the glass in the corner or up the wall, not flat up the middle; a flat clear up the middle is both the classic interception and the classic icing
Rule: Hybrid icing turns on "which player would first touch the puck, not which player would first reach the end zone face-off dots" (NHL Rule 81.1; IIHF Rule 81.1 materially identical) — the dot is the deadline, and a race too close to call by then goes to icing
Rule: After your own icing your line cannot change (NHL and IIHF Rule 81.4) and cannot take a time-out (Rule 87.1: "No time-out shall be granted to the defensive team following an icing…") — 81.4's exceptions are an injury, a strength-changing penalty, and a pulled goalkeeper, never a tired line
Rule: IIHF 87.1 matches the NHL on icing; IIHF 81.4 adds one substitution exception ("a Player with a broken skate") its own 82.1 omits, and a change refused on it is a warning or a bench minor if your bench has had one (82.2(V)); both bans bind you in Britain, USA Hockey imposes neither (624, 636(f))
Rule: Shorthanded you may ice it freely (NHL Rule 81.6), subject to two carve-outs — a major with an empty penalty bench, and USA Hockey's classification limit — owned by Special Teams and Rules Primer; IIHF 81.6 carries neither
Rule: Putting the puck directly out of play from your defending zone is a two-minute delay-of-game minor (NHL 63.2(iii), IIHF 63.2(III)); carve-outs are boards with no glass, a puck off a faceoff, one straight into the bench — the NHL penalises glass "behind" the bench, the IIHF "surrounding" it, wider
Rule: USA Hockey writes no such minor for putting the puck out of play — Rule 610(c) penalises only a deliberate shoot-out, and 631(d) makes an accidental one a last-play faceoff; Hockey Canada 10.1(ii) is deliberate-only too, its direct limb 10.1(v) naming the goaltender alone
Key: A clear that hits the glass and stays in the rink is fine, and so is a puck deflected out of play
Key: Glass clears rarely get intercepted and rarely ice, because they slow the puck and keep it wide — which is why the play is called "off the glass", not "over the glass"
Countered by: A defenceman holding the offensive blue line and stopping the puck getting out — a different play from a pinch, which is stepping down from the line into the zone
```

No possession, no goal against. Always available and never wrong when you're in trouble.

- **Setup:** You have the puck, you have pressure, and you have no play.
- **The read:** When the alternatives are a turnover in a dangerous area or a play you're not confident in. It is also the correct play when you desperately need a change.
- **Execution:** Aim **high off the glass** in the corner or up the wall, not flat up the middle. Glass clears rarely get intercepted and rarely ice, because they slow the puck and keep it wide. A flat clear up the middle is both the classic interception and the classic icing.
- **Two rules you must know:**
  - **Icing — and it is hybrid icing, which is not quite what most players think.** If you are at even strength or up a skater, and you shoot the puck **from your own half of the ice** (the centre red line divides the halves, Rule 81.1) across the far goal line, the linespersons do not wait for anyone to touch it. **The race is to the puck, and the end-zone faceoff dot is only the deadline for the decision:** Rule 81.1 states that *"the determining factor is which player would first touch the puck, not which player would first reach the end zone face-off dots"*, and the call is made no later than the instant the first player's skate reaches that dot. If it is too close to call by then, icing is called. So a clear from your own blue line with a defender two strides ahead of your winger is icing whether or not the puck is ever touched. Play stops, the faceoff comes back to your zone, and your line **cannot change** (Rule 81.4 — whose only exceptions are a substitution for an injured player, one made when a penalty has changed the on-ice strength, and one to replace a goalkeeper who had been pulled for an extra attacker; a tired line is not among them) and **cannot take a timeout** (Rule 87.1: *"No time-out shall be granted to the defensive team following an icing…"*) — that last part is the real cost, because the tired players who just iced it are stuck out there for a defensive-zone draw. **All of that holds under the IIHF book, and therefore in Britain:** IIHF Rule 81.1 carries the same "which Player would first touch the puck, not which Player would first reach the end zone 'face-off' dots" sentence and the same "too close to determine… 'icing' shall be called" instruction, IIHF 81.4 bans the substitution, and IIHF 87.1 says in terms *"No 'time-out' shall be granted to the defensive team following an icing…"*. Do not expect your own book to punish an icing more lightly. **IIHF 81.4 does list one exception the NHL's does not** — you may substitute for *"a Player with a broken skate"* — ⚠️ **but the same book's 82.1 states the identical ban for an icing and omits it, and neither rule says which governs.** Try the change and be refused and IIHF **82.2(V)** has the Referee send those players back to the bench and warn the team through its Coach that *"any subsequent violations during the rest of the game (including overtime), shall result in a bench minor penalty"* for delaying the game — **one warning per team per game**, so a bench that has already had one takes the penalty at once. **The IIHF's 81.1 is materially identical to the NHL's rather than word-for-word**, and it is the longer of the two: it adds a sentence the NHL's book has no equivalent of — *"If there is no 'race' for the puck, icing should not be called until a defending skater crosses its defending blue line and the puck crosses the goal line (not between the goal posts)."* So where nobody is chasing, the IIHF book defers the call until a defender has come back for it. **Shorthanded, you can ice it freely** (Rule 81.6) — subject to two carve-outs set out in [Special Teams](special_teams.md) and [Rules Primer](../foundation/rules_primer.md): a team shorthanded by a *major* that has left nobody on the penalty bench loses the exemption, and USA Hockey only grants it from Youth 15-Only / Girls 16U / High School / Adult upward. **Neither carve-out is in the IIHF's 81.6** — see [UK and England Rules](../foundation/uk_rules.md). Note the two halves detail: a clear released from *their* side of centre can never be icing no matter how far it travels.
  - **Over the glass is a penalty.** Under NHL Rule 63.2(iii), shooting or batting the puck **directly (non-deflected) out of the playing surface from your defending zone** is a two-minute minor for delay of game. Off the glass and staying in is fine; a deflection out is fine; a clean flip over the glass from your own end is a penalty. The rule's own exceptions: it does not apply "where there is no glass", it does not apply to a puck shot **into** the players' bench (though over the glass *behind* the bench does count), and no penalty is assessed when the puck leaves the playing area **directly off a faceoff**. This is why the play is called "off the glass", not "over the glass". ⚠️ **🇬🇧 And this one is not the NHL's alone — the IIHF writes the same provision in words of its own**, so it is the rule a British player is actually under. **IIHF Rule 63.2(III)** assesses the minor *"on any Player who shoots or bats (using their hand or stick) the puck directly (non-deflected) out of the playing surface from their defending zone, except where there is no protective glass"*, with the same determining factor and the same two Note carve-outs, unchanged between the 2025/26 and 2026/27 editions. **The two limbs are materially the same rule, and they are not word for word — the difference runs against you:** where the NHL penalises a puck *"shot over the glass 'behind' the players' bench"*, the IIHF penalises one *"shot over the protective glass **surrounding** the Players' bench"* — a wider word, which appears to reach the glass at the **sides** of the bench as well. **The other two books do not write this limb at all:** USA Hockey **610(c)** reaches only a deliberate shoot-out, with **631(d)** sending an accidental one to a last-play faceoff, and **Hockey Canada 10.1(ii)** likewise reaches only a puck *"deliberately"* shot, batted or thrown out. ⚠️ **Do not read that limb as skaters-only, though.** It says *"a player"*, and Hockey Canada's Glossary provides that *"the goaltender is considered a player, except where explicitly stated because special rules or privileges apply to them"* — the same page showing what an express exclusion looks like, at *"POSSESSION: The state of a player, **except the goaltender**"*. Rule 10.1 names a goaltender at **10.1(v)**, at **10.1(vi)** and in its unnumbered Players' Bench paragraph — and not at 10.1(ii). Its direct limb that writes no deliberateness word, **10.1(v)**, is confined to *"a goaltender who shoots or bats the puck directly out of the playing surface, **with their stick**"* — so a goalie who deliberately glove-throws the puck out is caught by Rule 10.1(ii) rather than Rule 10.1(v). **That a glove-thrown puck falls under Hockey Canada's 10.1(ii) rather than 10.1(v) is this guide's reading of the text, not a ruling the book has published:** Rule 10.1(a)'s twelve Interpretations do not address it, and Interpretation 5 — the one about goaltenders putting the puck out — carves out only the no-glass, glass-touch and deflection cases. ⚠️ **And do not read the bench as a target.** The IIHF Situation Handbook — **Situation 63.24**, put in the same words in the 2025/26 (v1.1) and 2026/27 editions — puts a clear that goes **over** the glass and *lands* in the Players' Bench, and still calls the minor: *"the determining factor… is where the puck leaves the playing surface"*, judged on *"an imaginary plane that continues upwards to the roof from the top of the glass"*, and only a puck *"shot **directly** into the Players' Bench (where there is no glass)"* escapes. Its Foreword ranks it below the book — *"in case of any contradictions, the valid IIHF Rulebook is always the basis"* — so that is how it is officiated rather than the rule itself. [Risk Management](../hockey-iq/risk_management.md) carries all four books in full.
- **How it's defeated:** By a defenceman **holding the offensive blue line** — staying at the line and stopping the puck getting out. That is a different play from a **pinch**, which is stepping *down* from the line into the zone to attack a puck below you. Holding beats a glass clear; pinching beats a puck already dying in the corner, and costs far more when it misses. Your own defencemen face the same choice at the other end — see [Defender](../positions/defender.md).

## 4. Winger Timing and Positioning on the Wall

This is one of the most common amateur failures in hockey — coaching emphasis rather than a counted ranking — and it is not a skill problem.

### The failure

```facts
Never: Stand still, flat against the boards at the hash marks, waiting
Risk: You are uncoverable but useless — your check cannot take the puck off you before you have it, so it feels like you are doing your job
Risk: You have no speed — the pass arrives and you start from zero with a defenceman closing at speed
Risk: You have nowhere to go — pinned flat against the wall, your only escape routes are along the boards, the exact lane the opposition is defending
Key: A moving target with an opening lane is far easier to hit than a still one in a closed one
```

You stand still, flat against the boards, at the hash marks, waiting. And here's the thing — **you are technically uncoverable there.** Your check can't really take the puck off you before you have it, and you feel available. So it feels like you're doing your job.

You are not. You are **uncoverable but useless**, for three connected reasons:

1. **You have no speed.** The pass arrives and you're starting from zero, with a defenceman closing at speed. You will lose that race every time.
2. **You have nowhere to go.** Pinned flat against the wall, your only escape routes are along the boards — the exact lane the opposition is defending.
3. **You are not actually available.** Standing still against the boards means the passer has to hit a stationary target in a lane an opponent can simply stand in. A moving target with an opening lane is far easier to hit than a still one in a closed one.

### Where to stand

```facts
Position: On the wall, between the hash marks and the goal line, on the strong side — low enough that the pass is short and safe, high enough that you are moving up ice when you get it
Key: This is where you arrive, not where you have been standing
Action: Under the house-default low zone collapse you spend the defensive shift high, level with the opposing point man, and come down the wall to this spot as your team wins the puck
Never: Sit above the hash marks once the breakout is under way — it makes the pass longer, flatter and more interceptable, and the pass comes into your back
Key: Being high before that, while they still have possession, is not an error at all — the error is failing to make the trip, not starting from the top
Convention: Wall position is system-dependent — a flat/spread structure asks wingers to hold higher toward the blue line, and a stretch or flying-winger structure sends one winger up ice entirely
```

The default landmark is **on the wall, between the hash marks and the goal line, on the strong side** — low enough that the pass from behind or beside the net is short and safe, high enough that you're moving up ice when you get it.

**This is where you arrive, not where you have been standing.** Under the house-default low zone collapse you spend the defensive shift *high*, level with the opposing point man on your side, and you come down the wall to this spot as your team wins the puck — the one time in that system a winger leaves the point voluntarily. See [Defensive Zone Coverage](defensive_zone_coverage.md) and [Winger](../positions/winger.md). Read anything below about "where the strong-side winger stands" as *where the strong-side winger stands once the breakout has started*.

![](diagram:breakout-winger-wall)

Being *above* the hash marks **once the breakout is under way** is the more common error: it makes the pass longer, flatter and more interceptable, and it means the pass comes into your back. Being high *before* that, while they still have possession, is not an error at all — it is your coverage. The error is failing to make the trip, not starting from the top.

> **Wall position is system-dependent.** The hash-mark wall position described here belongs to the conventional swing breakout. Teams running a **flat/spread** structure ask wingers to hold higher toward the blue line; teams running an aggressive **stretch or flying-winger** structure send one winger up ice entirely. Find out which one you're in — the failure mode of a winger standing at the hash marks in a stretch system is exactly as bad as the reverse.

### When to move

```facts
Mindset: Time your arrival, don't camp — better a little late than a little early, because it is easy to speed up and very hard to slow down and re-accelerate
Read: If the puck is contested, come back for it — shorten the pass and skate toward the puck to receive
Read: If the puck is clean, stay in stride — take a small route away from the puck, then arrive back into the lane with speed
Action: Either way, keep your feet moving; the player standing still is the player who gets hit
```

**Time your arrival, don't camp.** The principle borrowed from centre-support coaching applies just as hard to wingers: **it is better to be a little late than a little early**, because it is easy to speed up and very hard to slow down and re-accelerate.

- **Come back for it if the puck is contested.** If your defenceman is in a battle or under real pressure, shorten the pass. Skate *toward* the puck to receive. A short pass to a supporting winger beats a long pass to a distant one every time.
- **Stay in stride if the puck is clean.** If your defenceman has the puck and time, don't stop and wait — take a small route away from the puck and then arrive back into the lane with speed, so you receive the pass already moving up ice. This is what "timing" means in practice.
- **Either way, feet moving.** Even a small shuffle keeps you able to react. The player standing still is the player who gets hit.

### Presenting a target, and receiving open to the ice

```facts
Technique: Stick on the ice, blade flat, angled toward the passer — your blade is the target, so put it where you want the puck
Action: Check your shoulder before the puck arrives, so you already know whether you are skating, passing or eating it
Never: Receive the puck with your chest to the boards — facing the glass you have surrendered every option except a blind chip
Technique: Open your hips to the ice and take a step or two off the boards, so you can skate, pass to the centre, pass back to your defenceman or attack the middle
Technique: If the puck arrives hard on the boards, angle a skate to kill it off the wall onto your stick rather than reaching and missing
Never: Shield with your back to the boards, or duck. Get your skates parallel to the boards instead, forearm and hip to the wall, head up and chin off your chest
Convention: Whether you also call for it is a team preference — some prefer silence on the wall to avoid tipping the play; the stick is not optional
```

**Stick on the ice, blade flat, angled toward the passer** — not held up, not dragging behind you. Your blade is the target; put it where you want the puck. Whether you also *call* for it is a team preference (some prefer silence on the wall to avoid tipping the play — see [On-Ice Communication](../foundation/on_ice_communication.md)); the stick is not optional. And **check your shoulder before the puck arrives**, so you already know whether you are skating, passing or eating it.

**Do not receive the puck with your chest to the boards.** This is the technical heart of the whole job.

- **Open your hips to the ice** as the puck arrives, so you can see the full rink and the puck at once. Facing the glass, you have surrendered every option except a blind chip.
- **Get separation from the wall.** A step or two off the boards means you can skate, pass to the centre, pass back to your defenceman *or* attack the middle. Flush against the wall you can do one of those.
- **Use your skates when you must** — if the puck arrives hard on the boards, angle a skate to kill it off the wall onto your stick rather than reaching and missing.
- **Protect it if you have to.** With a defenceman on your back and no option, "eat it" — shield the puck with your body until support arrives — or chip it off the glass. Both beat a blind turnover. **Safety override:** shield with your **skates parallel to the boards** and your **forearm and hip** to the wall, head up and chin off your chest — **never with your back to the boards, and never duck.** Puck protection never justifies that orientation; see [Body Contact and Battles → Receiving a Hit Safely](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md), which covers it.

### The winger's four options — and the order is a coaching choice

```facts
Options: Four things you can do — skate up ice, pass to the centre, pass back to your defenceman, or eat it / chip it out
Convention: Which comes first is a system decision, not a law, and the two common answers point in opposite directions
Convention: Skate-first — taught to young wingers who freeze on the wall, and the safer default when your team's passing is unreliable
Convention: Pass-first (quick-up) — what a quick-transition team wants: first look up ice before your feet commit, and skating is what you do when the look is not there
Action: Find out which one your coach wants
Key: Both versions agree on one thing — check your shoulder before the puck arrives; a winger who scans after receiving is left with whichever option has not closed
```

Once you have it you have four things you can do: **skate** (feet moving up ice, away from your own net), **pass to the centre** (they should be low and close, at or below your height), **pass back to your defenceman** (if the pressure came from above), and **eat it or chip it out** (last resort, and never a failure).

**Which comes first is a system decision, not a law, and the two common answers point in opposite directions.**

- **Skate-first** is the version taught to young wingers who freeze on the wall, and the safer default when your team's passing is unreliable — a winger moving up ice with the puck cannot throw it away.
- **Pass-first (quick-up)** is what a quick-transition team wants: **first look up ice** before your feet commit, on the reasoning that the two seconds after a retrieval are the least organised the opposition will ever be. This document's own "Quick-up / counter" option and its reading-pressure section lean that way, and [Winger](../positions/winger.md) states it flatly — *your first look is always up ice*. Under this version, skating is what you do when the look is not there.

**Find out which one your coach wants.** Both versions agree on one thing: **check your shoulder before the puck arrives**, so you already know which of the four you are choosing. A winger who scans after receiving is left with whichever option has not closed.

## 5. The Centre's Swing

If the winger's job is the most botched, the centre's is the most misunderstood of the three forward jobs — and it does more damage when it goes wrong, because the centre being in the wrong place removes the *option* the whole structure relies on.

### The swing, and where exactly it goes

```facts
Goal: A low support loop — swing back down into your own zone, stay beneath the puck, and offer a short, safe, middle-lane pass
Position: Inside the faceoff dots and below the hash marks — the strong-side circle, not the wall
Key: Below the hash marks the lane is open and the pass is short, and you receive facing up ice; above them you are level with their forwards and the puck arrives into your back
Never: Drift wide out to the boards — that is the strong-side winger's ice, one opponent then covers you both, and it kills the middle-lane option the structure depends on
Key: If you can touch the glass, you are in the wrong job
Key: You are the bail-out — when the wall is sealed and the wheel is closed, the low centre is what keeps the breakout alive
```

Your job on a breakout is a **low support loop**: you swing back down into your own zone, staying beneath the puck, and offer a short, safe, middle-lane pass. You come low, curl, and leave with speed as the play exits.

**Where exactly: inside the faceoff dots and below the hash marks — the strong-side circle, not the wall.** Two boundaries, for two different reasons:

- **Below the hash marks.** Above them you are level with the opposition's forwards and the passing lane closes; below them the lane is open and the pass is short. Short passes complete and long ones do not — a centre ten feet from the defenceman is a pass that always works, one forty feet away is a hope. Coming from below and beside, you also receive **facing up ice**; coming from above, the puck arrives into your back.
- **Inside the dots, not out on the boards.** Drifting wide puts you in the **strong-side winger's** ice, where one opposing player covers you both, and it kills the middle-lane option the structure depends on. Your swing may arc toward the strong side — it should, that is how you gather speed — but it arcs into the *circle*. If you can touch the glass, you are in the wrong job.

![](diagram:breakout-centre-swing)

You are **the bail-out**: when the wall is sealed and the wheel is closed, the low centre is what keeps the breakout alive. Without you, your defenceman is down to a rim, a reverse to nobody, or a turnover.

> **Note for readers of [Center](../positions/center.md):** the centre's breakout spot is the strong-side circle, **not** the strong-side boards — the boards are the winger's. **Take breakout positioning from here.**

### How a high centre kills the breakout

```facts
Never: Leave early anticipating the rush and end up high in the zone or already in the neutral zone
Risk: The defenceman is down to two options — the wall or a clear — and the opposition knows it
Risk: The pass angle is wrong even if it is attempted, because it has to travel up through the middle of your own zone
Risk: You receive with your back to the play, and every option you gave the play was a decoy, so the forecheck can commit to the wall and the wheel at 100%
Mindset: Better a little late than a little early — late, you accelerate into the puck; early, you have to stop and restart, and you have already killed the play
```

The near-universal amateur failure: **the centre leaves early**, anticipating the rush, and ends up high in the zone or already in the neutral zone. The cost is fourfold. **The defenceman is down to two options** — the wall or a clear — and the opposition knows it. **The pass angle is wrong** even if it is attempted, because it now has to travel up through the middle of your own zone, the one place you never want a puck lost. **You receive with your back to the play**, if you receive it at all. And **every option you gave the play was a decoy**, so the forecheck can commit to the wall and the wheel at 100%.

Coaching resources put the failure rate on this at the overwhelming majority of young players — an impression rather than a measured figure. The correction is the wingers': **better a little late than a little early.** Late, you accelerate into the puck. Early, you have to stop and restart, and you have already killed the play.

### Timing the exit

```facts
Goal: The point of swinging low is not just to be a target — it is to leave with speed
Technique: Take a route that has you moving away from the puck briefly, then turning back into it, so you are already at pace as you cross your own blue line
Risk: A centre who receives a pass standing still has technically executed the play and gained nothing
Convention: Centre-low is a coaching choice — the alternative is the centre fly, and both are legitimate
Never: Fly in a flat/spread system where the wingers hold high near the blue line — then nobody has low support at all
```

The point of swinging low is not just to be a target — it's to **leave with speed**. Take a route that has you moving *away* from the puck briefly, then turning back into it, so that when you receive it or when the play goes up the wall, you're already at pace as you cross your own blue line. A centre who receives a pass standing still has technically executed the play and gained nothing.

> **Centre-low is a coaching choice too** — the alternative is the **centre fly**, described in section 2. Both are legitimate. What is never legitimate is a centre flying in a flat/spread system where the wingers hold high near the blue line: then nobody has low support, and that is the breakdown you see most weekends. See [Center](../positions/center.md).

## 6. Breaking Out Against Each Forecheck

The correct breakout is not chosen from a list of favourites; it's chosen by reading the shape in front of you. **What each of these forechecks actually is, and how it is coached, belongs to [Forechecking Systems](forechecking_systems.md) — one line each here, then straight to how you beat it.**

> **A note on "most common".** Coaching material widely describes the **1-2-2** as the most-used forecheck in hockey ([forecheck systems guide](https://hoopsking.com/blogs/ice-hockey/hockey-forecheck-systems)), but **no league-wide count exists, so treat that as an impression, not a measured figure.** It does not conflict with the **2-1-2** being the **house default** throughout: that is a documentation convention — the 2-1-2 is the easiest to teach and the most common at youth and rec level, so it is the shape assumed here when one has to be assumed.

### Against a 1-2-2

```facts
Read: F1 pressures and steers rather than attacks, two forwards hold the middle-and-wall band behind, two defencemen hold the line — containment, not chaos
Key: It takes away the middle, and usually the D-to-D lane; the two F2/F3 forwards sit exactly where a wall pass wants to go
Mindset: You will usually have time — use it; this is the forecheck that punishes rushed decisions most and pressured decisions least
Action: Make F1 commit with a wheel, or simply by skating at F1 — once they pick a side, the other side is genuinely open
Key: The low centre is your key, because the 1-2-2's two middle forwards are positioned relatively high and the space beneath them is real
Never: Rim into a set 1-2-2 — it hands the puck to two waiting forwards. Beat it with numbers up the middle instead
Action: Against the extreme version, the 1-2-2 trap, take controlled possession and patience, including regrouping in your own zone rather than skating into the wall of players
```

**One line:** F1 pressures, two forwards hold the middle-and-wall band behind, two defencemen hold the line. Containment, not chaos — F1 steers rather than attacks.

**What it takes away:** the middle, and usually the D-to-D lane, which F1 is often specifically tasked with closing. The two F2/F3 forwards sit exactly where a wall pass wants to go.

**How to break it:**
- **You will usually have time.** Use it. Take the extra second to look — this is the forecheck that punishes rushed decisions most and pressured decisions least.
- **Make F1 commit.** A wheel, or simply skating at F1, forces the containing forechecker to pick a side. Once they pick, the other side is genuinely open.
- **The low centre is your key.** The 1-2-2's two middle forwards are positioned relatively high; the space beneath them is real.
- **Beat it with numbers up the middle**, not with a rim. Rimming into a set 1-2-2 hands the puck to two waiting forwards.
- **Against the extreme version (the 1-2-2 trap)**, the answer is usually controlled possession, patience, and forcing them to come to you — including regrouping in your own zone rather than skating into the wall of players. See [Neutral Zone Systems](neutral_zone_systems.md).

### Against a 2-1-2

```facts
Read: Two forwards pressure deep taking a side each, one supports in the middle, two defencemen hold — the most aggressive standard forecheck, and the house default throughout
Key: It takes away time, and both corners
Action: Play fast and simple — you do not have time to be clever, and the rushed cross-ice pass here is exactly the goal-against this forecheck is designed to produce
Key: Reverse and rim come into their own, because two forwards attacking deep are two forwards with momentum, and momentum is what the reverse punishes
Risk: The wheel is dangerous here — there is a second forechecker on the far side by design
Action: Beat them behind their pressure — when both their forwards are below your goal line, F3 is alone in the middle and their defencemen are exposed
Options: Glass and out is a legitimate answer — against sustained hard forechecking, refusing to turn the puck over in the danger zone is itself a strategy
```

**One line:** two forwards pressure deep taking a side each, one supports in the middle, two defencemen hold. The most aggressive standard forecheck, and the house default throughout.

**What it takes away:** time, and both corners.

**How to break it:**
- **Play fast and simple.** You do not have time to be clever. The rushed cross-ice pass here is exactly the goal-against this forecheck is designed to produce.
- **Reverse and rim come into their own.** Two forwards attacking deep are two forwards with momentum, and momentum is what the reverse punishes.
- **The wheel is dangerous** — there is a second forechecker on the far side by design.
- **Beat them behind their pressure.** When both their forwards are below your goal line, F3 is alone in the middle and their defencemen are exposed. A completed quick-up or a well-timed stretch is devastating against a 2-1-2 for exactly this reason.
- **Glass and out is a legitimate answer.** Against sustained hard forechecking, refusing to turn the puck over in the danger zone is itself a strategy.

### Against a 1-3-1

```facts
Read: One forward token-pressures, three stretch across the neutral zone, one defenceman anchors behind — less a forecheck than a wall
Key: It takes away every long pass — the stretch is dead, the cross-ice over is dead, and anything through the middle of the neutral zone is dead
Mindset: Lots of time and nowhere to go — the opposite problem to the 2-1-2, and it requires the opposite temperament
Action: Skate it out — one token forechecker cannot stop a defenceman carrying with speed, and making the puck-carrier a threat is what forces the wall to break shape
Action: Use short passes up the wall and along the boards, not through the middle — the wall lanes are the softest part of the shape
Options: Chip-and-chase is not a defeat — against a genuine trap it is frequently the highest-value option, because forcing a pass into three players turns into an odd-man rush the other way
Action: Attack the width — the 1-3-1 sacrifices lateral coverage to hold the middle, so quick side-to-side movement makes them slide, and sliding creates seams
```

**One line:** one forward token-pressures, three stretch across the neutral zone, one defenceman anchors behind. Less a forecheck than a wall.

**What it takes away:** every long pass. The stretch is dead, the cross-ice over is dead, and anything through the middle of the neutral zone is dead.

**How to break it:**
- **You will have lots of time and nowhere to go.** This is the opposite problem to the 2-1-2 and requires the opposite temperament.
- **Skate it out.** A 1-3-1 with one token forechecker cannot stop a defenceman carrying the puck with speed. Making the puck-carrier a threat is what forces the wall to break shape.
- **Use short passes up the wall and along the boards**, not through the middle. The wall lanes are the softest part of the shape.
- **Chip-and-chase is not a defeat.** Against a genuine trap, putting the puck behind them and racing for it is frequently the highest-value option available, because the alternative — forcing a pass into three players — turns into an odd-man rush the other way.
- **Attack the width.** The 1-3-1 sacrifices lateral coverage to hold the middle. Quick side-to-side movement makes them slide, and sliding creates seams.

### Against a full-ice press

```facts
Read: All-out pressure, all five opponents pushing up, usually late and chasing a goal
Key: A press by definition leaves space behind it — one clean pass through the first layer and you are attacking an outnumbered defence
Risk: A turnover under a press is an immediate high-danger chance
Priority: First layer first — don't hunt the killer stretch pass; beat the first forechecker with a short, safe play, and the space behind will still be there two seconds later
Options: Glass and out is completely defensible — take the icing if you have to, because an icing costs you a defensive-zone draw and a press turnover costs you a goal
Rule: After your own icing the NHL and IIHF bar the change (Rule 81.4) — but the bar lifts to replace an injured player, to put a pulled goalkeeper back, or when a penalty changes on-ice strength; never for a tired line
Rule: The post-icing no-change bar is not universal: USA Hockey's Rule 624 attaches no substitution restriction to an icing at all, and Hockey Canada's 6.7(d) applies its 'no-change on icing' rule only in U18AAA, Junior, and in Senior at the Member's option
```

**One line:** all-out pressure, all five opponents pushing up, usually late and chasing a goal.

**How to break it:**
- **The reward is enormous.** A press by definition leaves space behind it. One clean pass through the first layer and you are attacking an outnumbered defence.
- **The risk is equally enormous.** A turnover under a press is an immediate high-danger chance.
- **First layer first.** Don't hunt the killer stretch pass. Beat the first forechecker with a short, safe play, and the space behind will still be there two seconds later.
- **Glass and out is completely defensible.** Under a press, getting the puck the length of the ice away and forcing them to reset is a win. Take the icing if you have to — a press turnover costs you a goal, and an icing costs you a defensive-zone draw and, **under the NHL and the IIHF, your line change** (Rule 81.4). ⚠️ **That bar is neither universal nor absolute.** USA Hockey's **Rule 624** attaches no substitution restriction to an icing at all, and Hockey Canada's **6.7(d)** applies its *"'no-change on icing' rule"* only *"[i]n categories of U18AAA, Junior, and at the option of the Member in Senior"*. And where it does bind you, 81.4's own exceptions let you substitute *"to replace an injured player"*, to put back a goalkeeper who had been pulled for an extra attacker, or when a penalty has changed on-ice strength — so a hurt teammate comes off; a tired one does not. Section 3 sets the rule out in full.

### When they are already set — the control breakout

```facts
Key: A control breakout is a five-player pattern run from full possession behind your own net, against a trap that is already set and waiting
Action: The four players without the puck all move, with speed, and the carrier picks whichever route the trap has not taken away
Options: Blue-to-blue stretch — the centre swings behind the net with speed while wingers wait at each blue line, giving the puck-carrying D four options
Options: Strong-side slant — the centre and strong-side winger swing together, one slanting inside and one staying wide, with the far winger high
Options: Three high — all three forwards stay up in the neutral zone and the D holds the puck a beat before choosing; not the power-play umbrella of the same name
Convention: Coaching material presents all three as workable and advises drilling one rather than carrying all three — no published count says which teams run which, so expect to be taught one and ask which
Never: Stand still waiting for the pass, unless your pattern posts a winger up on purpose — otherwise a control breakout with stationary receivers is a slow breakout into a set trap
Technique: Posted up still means body open to the ice, head up and never your back to the boards. Get your skates parallel to the wall instead, forearm and hip into contact if it comes, chin off your chest
```

Everything above answers a forecheck that is **coming at you**. This answers the opposite problem: you have the puck behind your own net with full control, nobody is pressuring you, and the reason nobody is pressuring you is that all five of them are standing in the neutral zone waiting. Chipping it out gives them the puck. Skating into them gives them the puck.

**A control breakout is a coordinated five-player pattern designed for exactly that.** It works on two conditions: **the four players without the puck all move, with speed** — bar a receiver your pattern deliberately posts up — and **the puck carrier knows where every one of them will be** and picks whichever the trap has not taken away. The carrier is not choosing between two safe outlets; they are picking whichever of four or five running teammates the trap has failed to cover. Run properly these do not merely escape the zone — they often arrive with speed and produce a chance.

Three are named in coaching material, and there is no published count of which teams run which:

- **Blue-to-blue stretch.** The puck-carrying defenceman waits behind the net for the centre to come back with speed; the centre swings through behind the net, the partner swings to the opposite corner, and the two wingers wait at the corners of the near and far blue lines. That gives the carrier four options — hand it to the centre to weave through, or move it to either winger, or back to the partner and up the far side.
- **Strong-side slant.** The centre swings behind the net or into the far corner and the strong-side winger swings *with* them; as the pair move up ice, **one slants inside and one stays wide**. The far winger stations themselves in the middle of the far blue line ready to come back into the play, and the partner waits deep in the corner. The pass goes D-to-D first, and the second defenceman then chooses between the slanting centre through mid-ice, the wide winger, or the far winger off the boards. The two swinging players are the ones who break the trap, and they only do it with speed.
- **Three high** — not the power-play umbrella of the same name, which [Special Teams](special_teams.md) covers. Here the carrier holds the puck behind the net for a beat while **all three forwards stay high in the neutral zone** and the partner supports wide into a corner. The carrier then steps out and picks: the centre curling through mid-ice, or a winger either moving or **posted up** — standing still along the boards at a line. **Posted up still means body open to the ice and head up, and never your back to the boards. Get your skates parallel to the wall instead, forearm and hip into contact if it comes, and chin off your chest**; a stationary receiver on the wall is the one who gets hit, and [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md) covers the override. If the centre takes it under pressure, they can chip it past and turn it into a footrace.

**Expect to be taught one of them, not three.** Coaching material treats all three as workable and warns that a team trying to hold all three executes none of them well, so the usual advice is to pick one and drill it until nobody has to think. **Find out which one is yours, and learn where you are standing in it** — every one of these depends on four players being somewhere specific at the same moment.

## 7. Breakouts from a Defensive-Zone Faceoff

```facts
Key: The moment after a defensive-zone draw is the most structured situation in hockey and the best chance for a genuine set play — coaching emphasis, not a counted ranking
Options: Off a won draw — the wheel off the faceoff, or its three variations: win-and-up, win-and-reverse, and the modified wheel
Technique: Win-and-reverse works because the centre swings low behind the play after a draw rather than sprinting up ice
Action: Decide the play before the puck drops — look at your winger, look at your partner, know what happens next
Priority: Off a lost draw your first job is defensive, not offensive. Get to your coverage assignment immediately
Convention: Under the house-default low zone collapse, wingers get to the points, because a lost defensive-zone draw usually goes back for a point shot
Never: Chase as a group — that is exactly how the goal gets scored
Read: Once you win it back, treat it as a quick-up — they have just committed to offence and their structure is at its weakest
```

The moment after a defensive-zone draw is the most structured situation in hockey (coaching emphasis, not a counted ranking): everyone's feet are set, everyone knows where everyone is, and nothing is moving yet. It is the single best opportunity for a genuine **set play** — and the point at which your team's lack of one is most obvious.

**Alignment, draw technique and faceoff violations are in [Faceoffs](faceoffs.md).** What follows is only the breakout that comes out of the draw. (The common defensive-zone setup: centre on the dot, one defenceman on the boards side and one in the slot, wingers on the hash marks — though which winger stands where varies by team.)

**Off a won draw**, the classic set play is a **wheel off the faceoff**: the centre pulls the puck back to the defenceman behind and to the side, who collects it and wheels behind the net while the far winger releases up the wall as the primary target and the centre curls low to support. Three variations worth knowing:

- **Win-and-up:** the centre wins it directly to the boards-side winger or defenceman for an immediate wall exit. Fastest, most contested.
- **Win-and-reverse:** wheel is called, the forecheck arrives instantly, and the defenceman reverses to the centre swinging low behind the play. This is why the centre swings low after a draw rather than sprinting up ice.
- **The modified wheel:** the weak-side winger drives across the middle instead of up their own wall, dragging an opposing defenceman with them and opening the middle for the centre. Depends entirely on all five knowing it is on.

**The one thing that makes any of them work:** the play is decided *before* the puck drops. Look at your winger, look at your partner, know what happens next.

**Off a lost draw**, the opposition has possession in your zone with everyone set — the most dangerous routine situation in hockey, which is coaching emphasis and not a counted ranking. Your first job is **defensive, not offensive**: get to your coverage assignment immediately (man or zone depends on your system — see [Defensive Zone Coverage](defensive_zone_coverage.md)). **Wingers get to the points**, because a lost defensive-zone draw usually goes back for a point shot and under the house-default low zone collapse closing it down is your job. **Do not chase as a group** — that is exactly how the goal gets scored. And once you win it back, treat it as a **quick-up**: they have just committed to offence and their structure is at its weakest.

## 8. Delayed Calls and the 6-on-5 Breakout

### Breaking out under a delayed penalty

```facts
Rule: NHL Rule 15.1 — when a penalty is committed by a player of the team not in control of the puck, play stops when the team to be penalised gains control of the puck
Key: Control, not touch — control is "the act of propelling the puck with the stick, hand or feet"
Key: Control is not lost when the puck is contacted by an opponent, the boards or the net
Rule: The offending team cannot score unless the non-offending team shoots the puck into their own net (Rule 78.5(xi)); if you score, a minor penalty is washed out (Rule 15.2), but majors and match penalties are still assessed
Rule: A goal on a delayed call does not wash out everything the offender owes — with two or more minors called on the play the offending captain picks which single minor is washed out (NHL 15.2), and a double minor under Rules 47, 58, 60 or 62, high-sticking among them, still costs two minutes (15.3)
Convention: Pulling the goalie here is a coaching choice, not an automatic consequence of the arm going up — plenty of rec, beer-league and youth teams do not, and often should not
Rule: A goalie removed for an extra attacker in regular-season overtime must wait for the next stoppage and cannot change on the fly, or it is a bench minor for an ineligible player (NHL Rule 84.2)
Rule: That restriction is NHL-only — IIHF Rule 84.2 is one sentence permitting the pull — and across England, Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland overtime is played only in NIHL, and SNL where its regulations provide (In-House Rules, Rule 84); see UK and England Rules
Never: Clear the puck — a clear surrenders control and ends the advantage for nothing, and coaches name it the commonest delayed-penalty error in amateur hockey, though no count measures it
Action: Take the safe possession play every time — D-to-D behind the net, back to the point, hold it in the corner — and buy time for the goalie to reach the bench
Risk: The own-goal is the only way you can lose from here, so no blind passes back through your own crease with an empty net behind it
```

**The rule, verified:** under NHL Rule 15.1, when a penalty is committed by a player of the team **not in control of the puck**, the referee raises an arm to signal a delayed penalty; play stops **when the team to be penalised gains control of the puck**. Two crucial refinements:

- **Control, not touch.** The rulebook's glossary defines control of the puck as "the act of propelling the puck with the stick, hand or feet," and specifies that control is **not** lost when the puck is contacted by an opponent, the boards or the net, provided the player in control keeps propelling it. A deflection off the offending team does not end the delayed penalty; possession does.
- **You cannot be scored on, with one exception.** Rule 78.5(xi): during the delayed calling of a penalty, the offending team cannot score **unless the non-offending team shoots the puck into their own net**. And Rule 15.2: if you *do* score, the minor penalty *"shall not be imposed"* — while *"major and match penalties shall be imposed in the normal manner regardless of whether or not a goal is scored."* ⚠️ **What the goal washes out is one minor, not every minor on the play, and two clauses say so.** Rule 15.2's second paragraph: where *"two or more minor penalties were to be imposed and a goal is scored on the play by the non-offending side, the Captain of the offending team shall designate to the Referee which minor penalty(ies) will be assessed and which minor penalty will be washed out as a result of the scoring of the goal."* So against two infractions you get one of them cancelled and the other team chooses which. And **Rule 15.3** takes the double minor out of the bargain altogether: where the penalty is under Rule 47 head-butting, Rule 58 butt-ending, **Rule 60 high-sticking** or Rule 62 spearing *"and a goal is scored, two minutes of the appropriate penalty will be assessed to the offending player."* High-sticking is much the commonest of those four, so the ordinary blood-drawing double minor still costs two minutes however well you converted the 6-on-5.

**What that means for your breakout:** if your goalie comes off, you have a few seconds of free 6-on-5 with essentially zero defensive risk.

> **Pulling the goalie here is a coaching choice, not an automatic consequence of the arm going up.** It is standard at NHL level and in good junior and senior hockey. **Plenty of rec, beer-league and youth teams do not do it**, and often should not: a goalie who is slow to the bench, a bench that is at the wrong end, or a team that cannot reliably keep the puck for four seconds all turn a free attacker into a live own-goal risk. If your team does not have a called procedure for this, the safe version is simply to keep the puck and let the whistle come.
>
> **One rule trap if it is regular-season overtime.** NHL Rule 84.2: once the goalie has been removed for an extra attacker in regular-season overtime, "he must wait for the next stoppage of play before returning to his position. He cannot change 'on the fly.' If he does, a bench minor penalty shall be assessed for having an ineligible player." So a goalie who comes off on a delayed penalty in overtime and then tries to slip back in when the advantage evaporates has handed the other team a power play in sudden death.
>
> **This trap is NHL-only, and in Britain it mostly does not arise.** The IIHF's own Rule 84.2 is a single sentence — *"A team shall be allowed to pull its goalkeeper in favor of an additional skater in the overtime period"* — with no restriction on the goalie's return. And the In-House Rules amend Rule 84 so that overtime *"is only played in NIHL fixtures and in SNL fixtures where specifically provided for in competition regulations"*, so most games across the Home Countries — *"England, Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland"*, which is where the current In-House Rules say they apply — simply end level. See [UK and England Rules](../foundation/uk_rules.md).

With the goalie off:

- **Do not clear the puck.** Ever. A clear surrenders control and ends the advantage for nothing. Coaching material names it the commonest delayed-penalty error in amateur hockey, though no count measures that.
- **Take the safe possession play every time.** D-to-D behind the net, back to the point, hold it in the corner. The goal is to keep control until your sixth attacker is on the ice.
- **Buy time for the change.** Playing the puck back and even out of the attacking zone to let the goalie reach the bench and the extra attacker join is standard and correct.
- **Then attack.** Once you're set, you have a genuine 6-on-5 that cannot be punished. Get a shot on net; the worst outcome is a whistle and a power play.
- **The own-goal is real.** It is the only way you can lose from here — so no blind passes back through your own crease with an empty net behind it. NHL Rule 84.2 spells out the extreme case: in regular-season overtime, if your goalie is off for a delayed penalty and you shoot it into your own net, the game is over and the team that was about to be penalised wins.

### The 6-on-5 breakout with your goalie pulled

```facts
Mindset: A completely different problem to the delayed penalty, because now a mistake ends the game
Rule: Icing still applies — at six skaters against five you are not shorthanded, so a clear from your own half that reaches the far goal line is iced in all four books (NHL and IIHF 81.1; USA Hockey 624; Hockey Canada 6.7(a))
Rule: Only two of the four books then freeze your bench — NHL and IIHF 81.4 stop the team that iced changing; USA Hockey 624 writes no bar and 204(a) gives both benches their change at every stoppage; Hockey Canada 6.7(d) applies it in U18AAA and Junior, and in Senior at the Member's option
Rule: That freeze does not strand your goaltender — NHL and IIHF 81.4 both except "a player substitution to replace a goalkeeper who had been substituted for an extra attacker", and Hockey Canada 6.7(d)(i) writes the same exception first; the tired skaters are what you cannot change
Key: Icing removes the long clear, not the glass clear — a glass clear is deliberately slow and wide, so it dies in the neutral zone and never reaches the far goal line
Action: Aim the glass clear to land in the neutral zone with a teammate skating onto it
Priority: Possession over speed — an empty net behind you means any turnover is a goal against, so the controlled exit is now the only correct exit
Action: Use the sixth attacker as a low outlet giving you a short, certain pass, not standing in front of their net
Goal: Enter with control — after the effort of a 6-on-5 exit, a dump-in against a five-man box gives you nothing
```

Late in a game, chasing a goal, goalie on the bench. This is a completely different problem to the delayed penalty, because now a mistake ends the game.

- **Icing still applies — but read 81.4 to the end, because it has an exception written for exactly this situation.** Rule 81.1 applies icing to a team "equal or superior in numerical strength" — with six skaters against five you are not shorthanded, so a clear from your own half that reaches the far goal line is icing and brings the puck back to your own end. It also **prevents your line changing** (Rule 81.4) — and in a 6-on-5 that is close to fatal. ⚠️ **What it does not do is strand your goaltender on the bench.** NHL Rule 81.4 bars substitutions after your own icing, *"However, a team shall be permitted to make a player substitution to replace a goalkeeper who had been substituted for an extra attacker, to replace an injured player, or when a penalty has been assessed which affects the on-ice strength of either team."* **IIHF 81.4 carries that same goalkeeper exception word for word**, ahead of the broken-skate one noted in section 3. So you can put the goalie back in; what you cannot do is get the tired skaters off. ⚠️ **And the bench freeze itself is not universal.** Icing is — Hockey Canada **6.7(a)** uses the same *"equal or superior in numerical strength to the opposing team"* test and USA Hockey **624(a)** reaches any team that shoots it down from its own half — but **USA Hockey attaches no substitution restriction to icing at all** (Rule 624 read whole has none, and Rule 204(a) gives each team its change at every stoppage), and **Hockey Canada rations it by division**: **6.7(d)** applies its *"'no-change on icing' rule"* only *"[i]n categories of U18AAA, Junior, and at the option of the Member in Senior"* — and even there **6.7(d)(i)** lists the same exception first, *"[t]o replace a Goaltender that has been substituted for an extra attacker."* Check which book you are under before you price the clear.
- **What icing removes is the long clear, not the glass clear.** A hard, high, length-of-ice dump is off the table. But **off the glass and out is still available**, exactly as described in section 3 — a glass clear is deliberately slow and wide, so it dies in the neutral zone and never reaches the far goal line, which is precisely why it rarely ices. The distinction is worth keeping straight: what you cannot afford at 6-on-5 is *distance*, not *the play*. Aim it to land in the neutral zone with a teammate skating onto it.
- **Prioritise possession over speed.** You must not turn it over — an empty net behind you means any turnover is a goal against. The controlled exit is now the *only* correct exit.
- **Use the extra body as a low outlet.** The sixth attacker is most useful giving you a short, certain pass, not standing in front of their net. Numbers below the puck are what let you exit cleanly against a team that has nothing to lose by pressing.
- **Enter with control.** After the effort of a 6-on-5 exit, a dump-in against a five-man box gives you nothing. See [Zone Entries](zone_entries.md) and [Game Management](game_management.md).

### Breaking out on a delayed offside

```facts
Key: Keep your head up and expect to be hit — in all four books nothing in the rule covers you once the puck has left your stick
Key: The linesperson's raised arm is not a stoppage, and a whistle for an imminent hit stops the play rather than a body already committed at speed
Rule: On a delayed offside NHL and IIHF 83.3 stop play if an attacker forces you deeper or is about to make contact — but both triggers name the defending puck carrier, so both lapse the instant you pass
Rule: Hockey Canada 6.11(b)(ii) (the stop-play clause) writes no pressure limb and no contact limb at all, so nothing in that rule stops a forechecker closing on you
Rule: USA Hockey 630(d)'s only limb reaching a forechecker is "continues to apply pressure to the defending puck carrier", which lapses on your pass too — and it is granted only at Youth 15-Only and above, Girls 16U and above, High School and Adult
Action: Play it as an ordinary breakout under pressure — take the extra look, and take it with your head up
Never: Put your back to the boards and wait because the arm is up. Get your skates parallel to the wall instead, forearm and hip, head up and chin off your chest
```

**Keep your head up and expect to be hit — in all four books.** An attacker has beaten the puck over your blue line, the linesperson's arm is up, and you have the puck in your own end with what feels like free time to make a clean outlet. **The raised arm is not a stoppage.** You are still playing the puck, the forecheck is still coming, and **in none of the four books does the rule still cover you once the puck has left your stick.**

Under the **NHL and the IIHF** the linesperson stops play if, during the delayed offside, an attacker *"forces the defending puck carrier further back into his own zone, or who is about to make physical contact with the defending puck carrier"* (NHL Rule 83.3; IIHF Rule 83.3 is the same sentence with "their"). **Both of those triggers name the defending *puck carrier*** — and that is you only while you have the puck. Make the outlet pass and the protection travels with the puck to your teammate, not with you. ⚠️ Under USA Hockey Rule 630(d) **the only limb that reaches a forechecker at all names the carrier too, and lapses on the same pass** — it is *"continues to apply pressure to the defending puck carrier"*. That rule is also granted only at Youth 15-Only and above, Girls 16U and above, High School and Adult; below those levels the whistle comes immediately and the situation never arises. ⚠️ Under Hockey Canada Rule 6.11(b)(ii) **nothing in the rule stops a forechecker closing on you at all** — its stop-play clause has no pressure limb and no contact limb, and play stops only *"if any attacking player touches the puck or attempts to gain possession of a loose puck while the puck is still in the attacking zone"*. And a whistle for an imminent hit stops the *play*; it does not stop a body already committed at speed.

[Rules Primer](../foundation/rules_primer.md) works the four books through in full, including what the attacking player in the zone has to do.

**So play it as an ordinary breakout under pressure.** Under the NHL and the IIHF you do have a beat more than usual, because a forechecker who drives you deeper ends the play — **but that beat ends the moment the puck leaves your stick, so take the extra look with your head up.** Do not use it to put your back to the boards and wait for a whistle. **Get your skates parallel to the wall instead, forearm and hip, head up and chin off your chest** ([Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md)). Everything in sections 3 and 9 still applies — read the pressure, pick the option, move the puck.

## 9. Reading Pressure

Every choice in this document depends on one question: **how much time do I have?** Here is how to answer it.

### Reading it yourself

```facts
Priority: Every choice depends on one question — how much time do I have?
Action: Look before you have the puck, not after; the shoulder check on the way back to a retrieval is a defenceman's highest-value habit — coaching emphasis, not a counted ranking
Read: Count the forecheckers, not the pressure — one means options and probably time to wheel, two means the corners are gone and you are playing wall/reverse/rim, three deep means somebody up ice is free
Read: Watch their feet, not their sticks — a forechecker gliding is containing you, a forechecker striding is committing
Read: Know which way they are steering you; if you can feel yourself being funnelled, go the other way while the option exists
Key: Time is a two-second window, not a state — by the time you feel pressure the pass you wanted has usually already closed, so decide early
```

- **Look before you have the puck, not after.** The check over your inside shoulder on the way back to a retrieval is a defenceman's highest-value habit — coaching emphasis, not a counted ranking. You cannot buy that information back once you're facing the boards.
- **Count the forecheckers, not the pressure.** One forechecker means you have options and probably time to wheel. Two means the corners are gone and you're playing wall/reverse/rim. Three deep means somebody up ice is free — and also that you may have nothing low.
- **Watch their feet, not their sticks.** A forechecker gliding is containing you; a forechecker striding is committing. The first calls for a play up the wall or a carry; the second is what a reverse eats alive.
- **Know which way they're steering you.** Most forechecks want you on one specific side. If you can feel yourself being funnelled, that's the read — go the other way while the option exists.
- **Time is a two-second window, not a state.** By the time you feel pressure, the pass you wanted has usually already closed. Decide early.

### The goalie's role

```facts
Key: Your goaltender is standing still, facing the play, with the clearest view of everything behind your defenceman's back — worth nothing unless the words are agreed and the calls come early
Convention: Goalie vocabulary varies by team — agree yours in advance rather than assuming
Key: "Time" means nobody near you, turn and make a play; "man on" means pressure arriving now, do not turn into it
Key: "Up" means the wall lane is open; "wheel" means you have a step; "leave it" or "set" means a rim stopped and left stationary for you
Never: Use "over" without agreeing the sense — this list uses Sense B, the D-to-D behind your own net; if your team means cross-ice, your goalie must say "D" or "partner" instead
Never: Take a "man on" call with your back to the boards. Get your skates parallel to the wall instead, forearm and hip, head up and chin off your chest
Action: Calls must be early and loud — a call arriving as the forechecker does is worse than no call, because it makes the defenceman hesitate
Key: This is not only the goalie's job — D2, and the winger who can see the weak-side defenceman stepping down, both know things the puck-carrier does not
```

Your goaltender is standing still, facing the play, with the clearest view on the ice of everything happening behind your defenceman's back. Under pressure, a defenceman is physically and mentally loaded; the goalie is calm. **Whether any of that reaches your defenceman depends entirely on the vocabulary being agreed in advance and the calls coming early.**

The standard goalie vocabulary — your team may vary it, and should agree it in advance. **General call discipline and the full communication treatment are in [On-Ice Communication](../foundation/on_ice_communication.md); what follows is only the breakout set.**

- **"Time"** — nobody near you, turn and make a play.
- **"Man on"** — pressure arriving now; do not turn into it. Some teams add a side: "man on, left". **And never take it with your back to the boards.** Get your skates parallel to the wall instead, forearm and hip, head up and chin off your chest ([Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md)).
- **"Reverse"** — the forechecker has over-committed past you; the space behind is open.
- **"Up"** — the wall lane is open, hit the winger.
- **"Over"** — **this list uses "over" in Sense B: go behind the net to your partner (D-to-D).** That is the goalie-call convention and it is the *opposite* risk profile to the cross-ice "over" in section 3. If your team uses "over" to mean cross-ice, your goalie must use a different word here — "D" or "partner" — or someone will eventually throw a puck across their own slot because the goalie shouted it.
- **"Wheel"** — you have a step, take it around.
- **"Leave it"** or **"set"** — the goalie has stopped a rim behind the net and left it stationary for you. Some coaches prefer "leave" because its sound is harder to confuse with "up" and "over"; two-syllable calls survive a noisy rink better.

**Calls must be early and loud.** A call arriving as the forechecker does is worse than no call, because it makes the defenceman hesitate. And this is not only the goalie's job: D2, and the winger who can see the weak-side defenceman stepping down, both know things the puck-carrier does not.

## 10. The Trapezoid and Retrieval

```facts
Rule: The goaltender may play the puck behind the goal line only inside the marked trapezoid; outside it is a two-minute minor for delay of game, and "the determining factor shall be the position of the puck" (NHL Rules 27.8 and 63.2(viii)) (sole exception: skate contact with the crease)
Rule: Exactly one exception, the NOTE to 63.2(viii) — no minor is assessed "when a goalkeeper plays the puck while maintaining skate contact with his goal crease"; there is no teammate-based exception
Never: Take the geometry from Rule 27.8 — its six-foot figure is stale, describing the pre-2014 trapezoid; Rule 1.8 describes the lines actually painted
Rule: Applies in the NHL and under the IIHF book (IIHF Rule 27.7), the KHL reportedly too but unverified; in England and Wales the status is unsettled — England Ice Hockey's Rules & Regulations 22.3 say it is "not currently enforced", so assume it binds and ask your league
Key: Neither USA Hockey nor Hockey Canada has a trapezoid — under those books your goalie may fetch a rim from anywhere behind the net, so find out which book your league runs
Rule: "No trapezoid" is not "no restriction" — USA Hockey 614(c) makes it a minor for a goalkeeper who freezes the puck outside the "goalkeeper's privileged" area, and equally when "the puck is behind the goal line and their body is entirely outside of the goal crease"
Rule: Hockey Canada goes further than USA Hockey 614(c) — once a goaltender leaves the crease "they will not be allowed to freeze the puck. If they do, they will be assessed a Minor penalty. No warning will be issued." (Interpretation 3 to Rule 10.1(a), clause v)
Rule: Interpretation 3 to Hockey Canada's Rule 10.1(a), clause ii, lets a goaltender come out to cut down the angle and, after stopping the shot, cover the puck. That clause ii is the exception to clause v is a reading here, not a Hockey Canada ruling; the NHL and IIHF 63.2(vi) Notes write it in
Action: Look behind your own net before your first shift and see whether the trapezoid lines are painted — a hint about which book you are under, not a ruling, since the rule turns on where the puck is
```

This rule reshapes the entire retrieval game, and it is the reason rim targeting is a skill rather than a shrug.

**The rule, verified.** The goaltender may play the puck behind the goal line only inside the marked **trapezoid**, and doing it outside that area is a **two-minute minor for delay of game** (**Rules 27.8 and 63.2(viii)** — 63.2(viii) is the one that assesses it: *"On a goalkeeper who plays the puck outside of the designated area behind the net. The determining factor shall be the position of the puck."*). **Use Rule 1.8 for the geometry, never 27.8**: the lines begin **eight feet from each goal post** at the goal line and widen to **eleven feet from each post — 28 feet apart — at the end boards.** There is exactly **one** exception, and it is the NOTE to 63.2(viii): *"The minor penalty will not be assessed when a goalkeeper plays the puck while maintaining skate contact with his goal crease."* There is no teammate-based exception.

*(The two rules give different near dimensions, and 27.8 is simply the older one — it is stale, not self-contradictory. Rule 27.8, which defines the penalty, still reads "six feet from either goal post" — wording left over from before the trapezoid was widened by two feet each side in 2014. Rule 1.8 describes the lines actually painted on the ice, and its eleven-feet-from-post figure is what produces the 28-foot span both rules agree on. Trust Rule 1.8. Handled the same way in [Defender](../positions/defender.md) and [Forechecking Systems](forechecking_systems.md).)*

**Where it applies:** the **NHL** and **IIHF play**. **The KHL is commonly said to have one as well, though that one is unverified here: no KHL rulebook was available to check it against.** The IIHF's own 2021 announcement of its unified rulebook dates the IIHF's adoption to the **2021-22 season**; **the NHL's commonly given 2005-06 date is in no rulebook held here.** **And IIHF play is not only international hockey** — **IIHF Rule 27.7** carries the same rule in the same terms (*"A goalkeeper shall not play the puck outside of the designated area behind the net… The determining factor shall be the position of the puck"*, with the same skate-contact-with-the-crease exception), and the IIHF book is the book British hockey runs on — the In-House Rules adopt it for IHUK and BUIHA fixtures *"in England, Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland (the Home Countries)"*, and the Elite League is understood to run it the same way, though its Casebook is unread here. Neither the 2025-26 nor the 2026-27 In-House Rules amend the IIHF book on this point. **In England and Wales, though, treat the status as unsettled rather than settled:** England Ice Hockey's own *Rules & Regulations* 22.3 say the restricted area is *"not currently enforced to allow ice rinks the necessary time to make the changes to implement this rule"* — that edition is 2024-25, and both the 2025-26 and the 2026-27 In-House Rules are silent on it, so the published documents do not resolve whether the suspension still stands. Assume it binds, and ask your league. **Neither North American governing body's book has one.** The *USA Hockey Playing Rules 2025-29* and the *Hockey Canada Playing Rules 2026-2028* mark no goalkeeper's restricted area anywhere in their rink sections, and neither limits *where* a goalkeeper may play the puck — USA Hockey's delay-of-game rule (610) and Hockey Canada's (10.1) both leave that alone. **Neither leaves the goalkeeper unrestricted back there, though**, and that matters to a rim you intend your goalie to stop and hold. USA Hockey **Rule 614(c)** assesses a minor for delay of game to a goalkeeper *"who falls on or gathers the puck into their body and causes a stoppage of play"* on any of **four** triggers: **(1)** *"[t]he puck is behind the goal line and their body is entirely outside of the goal crease"*; **(2)** they *"fail to play the puck with their stick when provided the opportunity to do so prior to being pressured by an attacking player"*; **(3)** *"[t]he puck is outside the boundaries of the 'goalkeeper's privileged' area"*; **(4)** they *"hold or place the puck against any part of the goal frame or boards"*, or intentionally drop it on the back of the netting. ⚠️ **For a rim behind the net it is trigger (1) that bites, not (3)** — the privileged area is bigger than it sounds. The rule's own Note draws it *"by connecting the end zone face-off spots with an imaginary line and imaginary lines from each face-off spot running perpendicular to the end boards"*, and USA Hockey's Casebook answers the question directly: *"Does the goalkeeper's Privileged Area include the area behind the goal line? Yes… The goalkeeper's Privileged Area extends to the closer end boards"* (**Rule 614, Situation 14**). So a goalie who sits on a rim directly behind your net **is** inside the privileged area and is penalised anyway, because the puck is behind the goal line — the Casebook says so in terms, ruling that *"if the puck is behind the goal line or outside of the privileged area, a minor penalty for delaying the game must be assessed"* (**Situation 12**). What trigger (3) reaches is the corner. And trigger (2) is the general principle behind both: a goalkeeper who beats an attacker to a loose puck **inside** the privileged area and falls on it still takes the minor, because *"[t]he goalkeeper has an obligation to play the puck with their stick, just like any other player"* (**Situation 6**). ⚠️ **Two Casebook rulings pull the other way and are worth knowing before you shout at your goalie:** no penalty where the goalkeeper had *"some part of"* their body *"in contact with the goal crease when initial contact with the puck is made"* (**Situation 7**), and none where the attacking team *"is in a position to pressure the goalkeeper"* (**Situation 11**) — the same pressure test trigger (2) writes. **Hockey Canada goes further still.** Its Interpretation 3 to Rule 10.1(a) provides that once a goaltender leaves the crease *"they will not be allowed to freeze the puck. If they do, they will be assessed a Minor penalty. **No warning will be issued.**"* ⚠️ **That clause is half of a five-clause Interpretation, though, and a goaltender told only that half has been told the rule wrong.** Clause **ii** of the same Interpretation provides that *"[t]he goaltender may come out of their crease to cut down the angle and catch the shot or, after stopping the shot, cover the puck"* — an express permission to cover a puck the goalie was outside the crease to stop. **Hockey Canada publishes no sentence saying which of the two clauses governs**, and no Hockey Canada casebook, situation handbook or bulletin was available here to settle it, so what follows is a reading of the text rather than a ruling. Three things support it. The Interpretation's own stated intent, one line above clause i, is *"to eliminate unnecessary stoppages caused by the goaltender"*, which is not what a save-then-cover is. An absolute clause v would leave the second half of clause ii with nothing left to permit, and a reading that makes an express permission a dead letter is the weaker one. And Hockey Canada's five clauses map onto **NHL Rule 63.2**, where the other two books print the same pair with the exception written in: the **NOTE to NHL 63.2(vi)** reads *"If a goalkeeper comes out of his crease to 'cut down the angle' on a shot and after making the save covers the puck, this shall be legal"*, and **IIHF 63.2(VI)** carries it in the plural. So read clause v as reaching the goaltender who leaves the crease to **retrieve** a puck — the rim in this section — and not the one who covers a save they came out to make. How much rec, beer-league and youth hockey runs on those two books is a question about leagues rather than about books, and this document has no count for it. In Britain the practical question may be the rink rather than the rule, so check whether the lines are actually painted behind your net. [UK and England Rules](../foundation/uk_rules.md) records the same uncertainty.

### What it does to your breakout

```facts
Never: Aim a rim straight into the corner — your goalie legally cannot go and get it, so a defenceman has to, under pressure, with his back to the forecheck
Options: Hard and all the way around, arriving where your winger can meet it in stride — good
Options: Tight and short, staying close enough behind the net that your goalie can legally stop it inside the trapezoid and leave it for you — good
Risk: Medium, dying in the corner outside the trapezoid — bad, because nobody can get it safely and the forecheck arrives first
Action: Agree in advance which of the two good ones you want
Key: With no trapezoid your goalie is a third puck-handler — they can play a rim anywhere behind the net and make the D-to-D for you, but neither USA Hockey nor Hockey Canada lets them freeze it out there
```

The retrieval technique itself is spread across several documents rather than held in one place: [Defender](../positions/defender.md) covers planning the retrieval route and which way to pivot, [Scanning and Anticipation](../hockey-iq/scanning_and_anticipation.md) covers the shoulder checks that have to happen before the puck arrives, [Skating](../technique/skating.md) covers the cutback and lateral escapes off the wall, and [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md) covers what happens when a forechecker gets there with you. The goalie's side is in [Goaltender](../positions/goaltender.md). What the rule changes is **where you aim a rim**:

- **Your goalie legally cannot go and get a rim that dies in the corner.** A defenceman has to, under pressure, back to the forecheck — the exact situation that produces turnovers behind your own net. So a rim aimed straight into the corner is a gift to the forecheck, not because the play is bad but because of who has to retrieve it.
- **Two acceptable rims, one bad one.** **Hard and all the way around**, arriving where your winger can meet it in stride — good. **Tight and short**, staying close enough behind the net that your goalie can legally stop it inside the trapezoid and leave it for you — good. **Medium, dying in the corner outside the trapezoid** — bad, because nobody can get it safely and the forecheck arrives first. *Agree in advance which of the two good ones you want.*
- **If your league has no trapezoid, your game changes completely.** Your goalie becomes a genuine third puck-handler: they can play a rim anywhere behind the net, make the D-to-D for you, and act as an extra breakout option. **They still cannot sit on it, so do not aim a rim expecting it to be held until you arrive** — USA Hockey Rule 614(c) makes it a minor for a goalkeeper to freeze the puck outside the *"goalkeeper's privileged"* area, and, for a rim behind your net, equally when *"the puck is behind the goal line and their body is entirely outside of the goal crease"*; Hockey Canada's Interpretation 3 to Rule 10.1(a), clause v, bars freezing a puck once they have left the crease, *"No warning will be issued"* — and a rim they went out to retrieve is exactly the case it reaches, unlike the save-then-cover that clause ii of the same Interpretation expressly permits. Find out which rules you are under — it changes what a rim is *for*.

## Common Mistakes

Errors the body above does not cover — the ones that survive knowing the system.

- **Sliding across to "screen" for the wheel.** Standing in front of the net and holding the ice you already occupy is legal and useful. **Moving laterally into a forechecker's path is interference** (Rule 56.1), and it costs you at least two minutes at the exact moment your partner had beaten the forecheck — **two minutes is the floor, not the ceiling**: NHL 56.4 allows a major on the degree of violence, and NHL 56.5 a game misconduct on top of it — though only *"when a major penalty is imposed under this rule for an infraction resulting in an injury of an opponent"*, so under the NHL book that ejection waits on somebody being hurt. **The other three books, set out next, do not make it wait.** IIHF 56.5 reaches a major plus a game misconduct where it recklessly endangers, with nobody hurt, and Hockey Canada 8.3(b) reaches the same tier on *"the degree of violence of the impact"* with nobody hurt, and makes it mandatory where it injures. ⚠️ **USA Hockey gets there differently, and it is the book you are under if you play in the United States:** Rule 625 writes no tier above the minor, but a pick delivered as a body check to a player who did not see it coming leaves 625 — the *Declaration of Player Safety* sends it to the boarding, charging, checking-from-behind, head-contact or roughing rules, and **640(g)** is a major plus a game misconduct where it recklessly endangers an opponent, **640(h)** a match — both reaching only *"actions falling under Rule 640(b, c, d, e or f)"*, not roughing at large — with **602(a)**'s match penalty sitting outside both. Get there early or do not do it.
- **Running a control breakout with nobody moving.** The pattern is the easy part; the speed of the four players without the puck is what beats a set trap. And where your pattern does post a winger up on the wall on purpose, posted up still means body open to the ice, and never your back to the boards. Get your skates parallel to the boards instead, forearm and hip to the wall, head up and chin off your chest.
- **Saying "onside means both skates over the line."** That is the definition of *offside*, and players repeat it back inverted every season. **One skate touching the line or behind it keeps you onside.** Say it that way round or you will hesitate on a stretch pass you were entitled to take.
- **Using the word "over" without knowing which "over" your team means.** Cross-ice over is the riskiest of the nine breakout options; D-to-D over is one of the safest, and goalie call sheets usually mean the second. Two players with different definitions of one word is a puck through your own slot.
- **Assuming a glass clear is unavailable at 6-on-5.** It is the *length-of-ice* clear that is off the table, because icing applies. A glass clear that dies in the neutral zone cannot be icing and remains a legitimate safety valve.
- **Waiting for a defender to touch the puck before believing it is icing.** Hybrid icing is decided on who would have reached **the puck** first, and the linesperson must call it by the instant the first player's skate reaches the **end-zone faceoff dot** — the dot is the deadline, not the finish line. A race too close to call at that moment goes to icing. Your clear was iced long before anyone reached the puck.
- **Assuming the goalie automatically comes off on a delayed penalty.** It is a coaching choice, and in rec and youth hockey often the wrong one. Know your team's procedure; if there isn't one, just keep the puck.
- **Pulling the goalie on a delayed penalty in regular-season overtime and then sneaking them back on the fly.** NHL Rule 84.2 makes that a bench minor in sudden death. **This one is NHL-only** — the IIHF's 84.2 imposes no such restriction, and across England, Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland overtime is played only in NIHL and in SNL where its regulations provide for it.
- **Confusing holding the line with pinching** when talking about how your glass clear got killed. Holding is staying at the blue line; pinching is stepping down inside it. They carry very different consequences when they fail.
- **Quoting Rule 27.8's six feet for the trapezoid.** The painted lines are Rule 1.8's — eight feet from each post at the goal line. Rule 27.8's six feet is not an error in arithmetic; it is an accurate description of the *pre-2014* trapezoid that was never updated.
- **Five players running five different plays.** Not a skill failure. A communication failure, and the root cause of most of the above.

## Key Takeaways

1. **Do not move the puck through your own slot** — not with a pass, not with your feet — **unless the read is unmistakable.** Behind your own net is the route across: intercepted there it costs nothing, intercepted in front it is a point-blank chance against. The cross-ice "over" in section 3 is the one option that asks you to do it, and it is the highest-risk of the nine breakout options — the forecheck has to have genuinely over-loaded one side with the middle empty, and the behind-the-net route has to be unavailable.
2. **Default to leaving with the puck, and clear because you have read the situation rather than by reflex.** In roughly 230,000 hand-tracked NHL zone exits — Corey Sznajder's manual dataset, roughly half the games of 2016-17 and 2017-18 rather than both seasons complete — published on Hockey Graphs, controlled exits led to a follow-on zone entry about 89% of the time against about 20% for dump-outs nobody recovered — though the study's own conclusion is that possession, not method, is the explanation: a dump-out your own team collects performs about as well.
3. **Every swing breakout has five jobs** — retriever D, support D, strong-side winger, weak-side winger, centre — and knowing which is yours before the puck is retrieved is most of the battle. The five-role structure is itself a coaching choice.
4. **Wingers, in the conventional swing breakout:** cover the opposing point while they still have the puck, then come down the wall to between the hash marks and the goal line as your team wins it. Time the arrival — better a little late than a little early — and receive with your body open to the ice. **This is a system, not a law:** a flat or spread structure holds you higher toward the blue line, and a stretch or flying-winger structure sends one winger up ice entirely. Ask which you play. **One safety override outranks the play on the wall: never your back to the boards, and never duck. Get your skates parallel to the boards instead, forearm and hip to the wall, head up and chin off your chest.**
5. **Centres, in that same swing structure:** stay low, inside the dots and below the hash marks, in the strong-side circle rather than on the wall, and leave with speed. **The centre fly is an equally legitimate alternative.** What never works is flying while your wingers hold high near the blue line, because then nobody is underneath the puck at all. Ask your coach which one you run.
6. **Rim to beat a forechecker sealing you against the boards; reverse to beat one who has over-committed past you.** Both go hard and low, because a soft one dies in the corner and is a gift — and the teammate arriving behind you must call "reverse", because you are looking the other way.
7. **Read the forecheck before you pick the play** — a 1-2-2 gives you time and takes the middle, a 2-1-2 takes your time and leaves space behind, a 1-3-1 kills the long pass. **And when they are not coming at you at all but standing set and waiting, the answer is a control breakout** — a coordinated five-player pattern, of which blue-to-blue stretch, strong-side slant and three high — the last being a breakout shape, not the power-play umbrella of that name — are the three this source sets out. It works only if the four players without the puck move — with speed, unless your pattern posts one of them up on purpose — and you should expect to be drilled in one of them rather than all three.
8. **Agree what your calls mean before the game, starting with "over".** A cross-ice over is the highest-risk play in a breakout; the D-to-D behind your own net — which most goalie vocabularies mean by the word — is one of the lowest.
9. **Where the trapezoid applies — the NHL and IIHF play (IIHF Rule 27.7), and the KHL reportedly too though that one is unverified here — your goalie may not go and get a rim that dies in the corner.** So rim hard all the way around to a winger, or tight enough behind the net for your goalie to stop it legally — never the medium rim dying outside the trapezoid. Neither USA Hockey nor Hockey Canada has one, and in England and Wales England Ice Hockey's *Rules & Regulations* 22.3 say the restricted area is "not currently enforced" — so check whether the lines are painted, assume it binds, and ask your league. Where there is no trapezoid, your goalie is a third puck-handler — though neither North American book lets them freeze the puck out there, so they fetch a rim rather than sit on it.
10. **With a delayed penalty signalled against the other team you cannot be scored on except by your own hand (NHL Rule 78.5(xi)) — so keep the puck rather than clearing it.** Pulling your goalie for the extra attacker there is a coaching choice, not automatic. In NHL regular-season overtime a pulled goalie may not return on the fly (Rule 84.2), a restriction the IIHF book does not carry — and under the In-House Rules most games across England, Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland play no overtime at all.
11. **On a delayed offside, keep your head up and expect to be hit.** The linesperson's raised arm is not a stoppage. Under the NHL and the IIHF (Rule 83.3) an attacker who forces you deeper or is about to make contact ends the play — but both of those triggers name the defending *puck carrier*, so both lapse the instant you pass, as does USA Hockey 630(d)'s pressure limb. Hockey Canada 6.11(b)(ii) (the stop-play clause) writes no pressure limb and no contact limb at all.
