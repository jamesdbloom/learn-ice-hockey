## Key focus

**Make one killer cover two of you.** The extra skater is worth nothing until it does. Create two-on-ones anywhere, and cash them in near the net — one at the net front is a near-certain chance, one at the top of the zone is a shot from distance.

**Move the puck with passes, and look for the second one.** A kill can cover one option, not three in two seconds: the first pass moves them, the second arrives where they no longer are. If the entry is denied, turn back and come again rather than force it.

**Be ready to shoot or pass the instant the puck arrives.** Feet set, blade on the ice, eyes inside before you look up. A player who needs two touches is not an option, and the kill ignores them.

**At the net front, screen from just outside the paint and hunt the rebound.** Tip point shots, and arrive at the back post as the shot goes rather than camping there. **Keep both feet clear of the red crease line, and never touch the goalie.**

**Know your team's shape and your spot in it before your first shift.** A man up: 1-3-1, umbrella, overload, spread or 2-3. A man short: box, diamond or wedge+1. **Each is a coaching choice, not a law** — ask your coach which you run.

**When they clear it, one player chases and the other four re-form.** The chaser arrives before the puck stops; if two go, nobody holds the zone. Whoever holds the line keeps the puck in first and shoots second.

**On the kill, hold your own job and concede only the shot you chose.** Kill it at your blue line if you can. Chase only a puck you can win — a bobble, a turned back, an unsupported puck on the boards — and **go for the puck, not through the man**.

**Clear, then change, and call the man coming out.** Change when you can, not when you must. The player leaving the box is a fresh attacker nobody is marking. Agree the defensive-zone draw, and where the clear goes, before you line up.

**A man short, fire the long clear the moment you have it — if your book allows it.** A shorthanded team may usually ice it, but USA Hockey withholds that below its older levels, and the NHL and PWHL withdraw it in one major-penalty case. Aim it away from their goalie, and release it before the penalty ends.

**On the kill, take away lanes: stick on the seam, body in the shooting lane.** Concede the perimeter pass. Get into the lane before the shot, never in front of your goalie's eyes, and block with your body, on your feet, head out of the lane. How much to block is your coach's call.

## Overview

**Special teams** means the two teams have different numbers of skaters. A **power play** is when you have the extra one; a **penalty kill** is when you are one short — written "5-on-4", counting skaters only.

**Both halves are the same problem from opposite sides.** A power play tries to make one killer responsible for two attackers; a kill makes sure no killer ever is. So the power-play half is built around the plays that create a two-on-one — the seam pass, the one-timer, the royal-road pass, the point shot for a tip, the backdoor arrival, the walk-down from the half-wall. The kill half is built around meeting the entry, which shot each shape concedes, and not chasing. Learn the plays first; the shape is just where you stand to make them.

**On the power play, the puck moves and everyone is ready for it.** The pass that matters is usually the second, and it works only if the receiver can shoot or pass on the first touch. The net-front player screens from just outside the paint and arrives at the back post as the shot goes. When the kill clears it, one player chases and four re-form. When the entry is denied, turn back and come again.

**On the kill, hold your job and take away lanes.** Kill it before it reaches your zone by standing up at the line. Once they are set, sticks take the seam first and the perimeter pass is conceded on purpose. Chase only a puck you can win, and go for the puck, not through the man. Clear first, change second, call the man coming out, and block from the lane on your feet — never from in front of your goalie's eyes.

**The shapes are coaching choices, not rules.** The five power plays below — 1-3-1, umbrella, overload, spread/box and 2-3 — and the three kills — box, diamond and wedge+1 — are systems a coach picks. So is preferring the pass to the shot, so is how much your team blocks, and so is the idea under all of them, that danger depends on location. **Find out which system your team plays, and your job in it, before you are on the ice.**

**Two rules are the tactic here, and both differ by book — so find out which book your league runs.** **A man short, clear the length of the ice whenever you can**: a shorthanded team may usually do it without icing being called. The NHL and the PWHL withdraw that from a kill whose penalty box is empty when a major expires, and **under USA Hockey it does not exist below Youth 15-Only, Girls 16U, High School and Adult**. **And keep every clear hard, flat and in the rink:** a puck sent directly out of play from your own end is a minor under the NHL, the IIHF and the PWHL. **A deliberate one is a minor from anywhere on the ice in all six books**, so never flip it out to buy a whistle. Both are set out in **"You may ice the puck freely — where your rule set allows it"** and **"The two penalties a kill talks itself into"** below.

## POWER PLAY

### The one idea behind every power play

```facts
Goal: Create a 2-on-1 — the extra man only pays when the kill has two attackers to worry about and one body to do it with
Never: Stand still — a static 5-on-4 is a 4-on-4 with a spare man standing somewhere useless
Action: Manufacture 2-on-1s wherever the kill lets you; every one pulls the kill further out of shape and sets up the next
Priority: Cash them in as close to the net as you can — a net-front 2-on-1 is near-certain, one at the top of the zone is a shot from distance
Convention: Danger is a function of location — a coaching judgement, not a rule, and a coaching consensus rather than a counted one
```

Five attackers against four defenders is not "one extra shooter". It is **one extra decision the penalty kill cannot make**.

Four killers can cover four attackers. That is the whole problem. A static 5-on-4 where nobody moves is a 4-on-4 with a spare man standing somewhere useless. The advantage only becomes a goal when the kill has **two attackers to worry about and one body to do it with** — a 2-on-1 somewhere on the ice.

**A 2-on-1 anywhere is worth creating**, because each one pulls the kill further out of shape and sets up the next. But they are **not worth the same**: danger depends on location. A 2-on-1 at the net front — net-front player plus a backdoor arriver against one defenceman — is a near-certain chance. One at the top of the zone is usually a shot from distance into traffic. *(That ranking is coaching consensus, not a rule or a measurement.)* Manufacture them wherever the kill lets you, and cash them in as close to the net as you can.

#### Why puck movement, not shooting, creates it

```facts
Key: A kill can cover any single option; it cannot cover three options in two seconds
Action: Move the kill with passes, not with skating — two crisp passes pull a box further out of shape than thirty seconds of stickhandling
Never: Shoot into a set kill — a point shot into four square bodies is a coin-flip on possession and a re-entry against a set kill
Convention: This document's default is "shoot when the kill is out of shape" — a coaching philosophy, not a law; shot-volume power plays are coached and do work
Key: The pass that beats a kill is usually the second one — the first makes them move, the second arrives where they no longer are
Position: Wherever you stand, be an option the kill must physically account for, able to shoot or pass immediately
```

A penalty kill is a shape, and a shape has to travel to cover the puck. Every pass makes at least one killer skate. Killers skating are killers arriving late, and a killer arriving late is a 2-on-1 for the two seconds before they get there.

- **A kill can cover any single option. It cannot cover three options in two seconds.** That is the entire mechanism.
- **Passing moves the kill faster than skating does.** The puck travels far quicker than a person. Two crisp passes will pull a box further out of shape than thirty seconds of one player stickhandling on the half-wall.
- **Shooting into a set kill is how you end your power play.** A point shot into four square bodies is a coin-flip on possession — they block it, clear it, and you re-enter against a set kill. **This document's default is "shoot when the kill is out of shape"**, a coaching philosophy, not a law. The real alternative is a **shot-volume power play**: shoot from the moment you are set, and let tips, rebounds and a strong retrieval unit do the work. Volume suits a heavy point shot, a fearless net front and good retrievers; patience suits a distributor and a one-timer. **Ask your coach which one you play.**
- **Corollary: the pass that beats a kill is usually the second one.** The first pass makes them move; the second one arrives where they no longer are. Power plays that make one pass and shoot are just slow even-strength hockey.

**What this means for you personally:** whatever spot you occupy, your job is to be an option the kill must physically account for, in a body position where you could shoot or pass *immediately*. A player who has to take two touches to become dangerous is not an option — the kill can ignore them and cover the other three.

### Power play formations

```facts
Convention: Which formation your team plays is a coaching choice, not a rule of hockey — and so is who stands where inside it
Key: Every shape below wins against some kills and loses to others, and every one requires personnel it suits
Action: Find out which formation your team runs, and which spot is yours, before your first power play shift
Never: Guess — if you are guessing, you are the reason the seam is covered
```

> **Read this first.** Which formation your team plays is a **coaching choice**, not a rule of hockey. So is who stands where inside it. Every shape below wins against some kills and loses to others, and every one of them requires personnel it suits. **Find out which formation your team runs, and which spot is yours, before your first power play shift.** If you are guessing, you are the reason the seam is covered.

#### 1-3-1 — widely described as the modern default

```facts
Position: One at the point, two on the half-walls, a bumper in the middle of the slot, one at the net front — one point player, not two
Action: The point walks the line laterally — sliding sideways changes the angle of every passing lane in the zone; keep going until a killer turns their head or shifts across to follow
Technique: Half-walls play the off-wing so the puck is on the inside hand; look inside before you look up
Action: The bumper keeps the blade on the ice for a one-touch shot and holds the kill's high players down
Position: The net front lives just outside the blue paint, with both feet off the red crease line. Screen, tip and hunt rebounds from there
Rule: Keep both feet outside the red crease line. Every book that settles it counts the line in — IIHF 1.7, USA Hockey 625(b)'s Note and Casebook, and the Hockey Canada and CARHA glossaries. NHL and PWHL 1.7 do not settle it, and their Table 14 and the IIHF's allow a goal off a screen on the line
Rule: Under USA Hockey a skate in the crease costs a power play the zone, not just the goal — 625(b) sends the faceoff to the nearest neutral zone spot when an attacker stands in or skates through it, the puck is in the attacking zone, your team has possession and the goalie touches the crease
Convention: The bumper is most often the centre, but that is team-dependent, not a law. Ask your coach who has it
Countered by: A diamond — this is the most-scouted shape in hockey, so kills arrive with a plan for it
Risk: With only one player back, a lost puck at the blue line is an immediate odd-man rush the other way
```

> **Three words used constantly below, defined here.** The **slot** is the area directly in front of the net between the two faceoff circles, running from the goalmouth out to the tops of the circles — the most dangerous ice on the rink. Within it, the **high slot** is the upper half, from the faceoff dots out to the tops of the circles; the **goalmouth** is the immediate net front, within a stick's length of the goal line. The **point** is the area just inside the blue line in the offensive zone — an area, not the line itself; the **half-wall** is the boards level with the faceoff dot, well below it. Point and half-wall are not the same place and are never interchangeable.

**The shape:** **one** player at the point (the area just inside the blue line), **three** across the middle of the zone — two on the **half-walls** (the boards roughly level with the faceoff dot, between the corner and the point) with a **bumper** between them in the middle of the slot — and **one** at the net front.

![](diagram:pp-131)

That is one point player, not two. First units are widely **described as** running the 1-3-1 with **four forwards and one defenceman**, who plays the point. The personnel half of that has been measured: Matt Cane (Hockey Graphs, March 2017) found four-forward units took *"roughly 56% of the 5-on-4 ice-time this season"*, a share that was rising in 2017. That is a league-wide share of ice time, not a count of teams or of first units. **Which *shapes* teams run has not been counted, as far as this document has found**, so any "most teams use X" claim about the shape is an impression.

**The jobs:**

- **Point (the "quarterback").** Usually the lone defenceman. You are a distributor first and a shooter second. Walk the line laterally — sliding along the blue line changes the angle of every passing lane and turns the killers' heads. Keep sliding until a killer turns their head or shifts across to follow you. (Getting a shot past one blocker is a different job — see [the point shot for tips and rebounds](#the-point-shot-for-tips-and-rebounds).) Your shot is mainly a threat: once the kill stops respecting it, they stop stepping out to you and the half-walls lose their space. Shoot for tips and rebounds, not to beat the goalie clean.
- **Half-walls (two players, usually forwards).** The primary decision-makers. From here you can reach the point, the bumper, the net front and — through the seam — the far half-wall. Ideally you play your **off-wing** (a left-hander on the right side, or vice versa) so the puck is on your inside hand: that opens your body to the middle of the ice, makes the one-timer available, and lets you shoot or pass without turning. Your first move should almost always be to look inside before you look up.
- **Bumper.** The middle of the three, sitting in the soft spot between the point and the net-front player, roughly the middle of the slot. You are the reason a box fails: a box has nobody assigned to the middle. Your job is to keep your stick free and your blade on the ice for a one-touch shot or redirect, to constantly adjust your angle so a passing lane exists from at least one half-wall, and to hold the kill's high players down so they cannot step out and pressure the point.
- **Net front.** Screen the goalie, tip point shots, hunt rebounds, and sometimes slide below the goal line to open a high-to-low pass. Live **just outside** the **blue paint** — the goal crease, the goalie's protected ice — with both feet clear of the red line that marks it. ⚠️ **Never touch the goalie to make room.** **The line is a rule, not a preference:** the IIHF, USA Hockey, Hockey Canada and CARHA count it as part of the crease, and the NHL does not say. Under USA Hockey, standing in the crease can cost a power play the zone as well as the goal. [Against their goalie](#against-their-goalie-where-the-net-front-line-is) sets it out; see also the goaltender-interference note under [Centre](../positions/center.md).

**Who plays the bumper?** **Most often the centre**, because the position is a centre's whole skill set: soft hands in traffic, back to the play, one-touch passing. But this is genuinely **team-dependent, not a law** — plenty of real NHL units put a **winger** in the bumper. The spot is chosen for the ability to receive and release in a crowd, not for what is printed on the lineup card. Ask your coach who has it; do not assume.

**Strengths:** occupies all five dangerous areas at once; creates four passing triangles, so there is always a short outlet; the bumper attacks the one place a box cannot cover; the off-wing half-walls make the cross-seam one-timer live at all times.

**Weaknesses:** every player must be comfortable making plays under pressure — a weak link in any of the five spots kills it. With only one player back, a lost puck at the blue line is an immediate odd-man rush the other way. It is also the most-scouted shape in hockey, so kills arrive with a plan (usually a diamond) specifically for it.

**Suits:** skilled units with at least one high-end distributor on a half-wall, one genuine one-timer threat, and a centre or winger brave enough to stand in the slot.

#### Umbrella — three high

```facts
Position: Three high in an arc — one apex highest in the middle at the blue line, two flanks lower either side — and two low at the net
Key: There is no bumper in a true umbrella; the middle of the slot is deliberately vacated so the high players have clean lanes into it
Options: Classic — a forward apex with both defencemen out on the flanks; modern — the lone defenceman at the apex with two forwards down at the tops of the circles
Never: String three players level across the blue line and call it an umbrella — with no depth every pass is short and lateral
Action: The three high move the puck side to side for a shot with a lane; the two low screen, tip and collect rebounds
Countered by: A diamond or an aggressive box, which will happily let you pass it around the top all night
Risk: The umbrella works the puck far from the net, and goalies and killers are most comfortable when the puck is far from the net
```

**The shape:** **three players high**, arranged as an **arc** rather than a flat line — that curve is what the name describes. **One apex** sits highest, in the middle at or just inside the blue line, and the **two flanks sit lower than the apex** on either side. Below the arc sit **two low players**, typically one at the net front and one at the side of the net or in a circle.

![](diagram:pp-umbrella)

**How far the flanks drop is the one real variation.** In the **classic** version the apex is a forward in the middle of the blue line with **both defencemen out on the flanks**, still close to the line — the arc is shallow. In the **modern** version the lone defenceman takes the apex and two forwards drop right down to the **tops of the faceoff circles**, where the one-timer lives — a much deeper arc. Both are umbrellas. What neither is, is three players strung out **level** across the blue line: with no depth between them every pass is short and lateral and the kill barely has to move.

**There is no bumper in a true umbrella.** The middle of the slot is deliberately vacated so the high players have clean shooting lanes into it.

**The jobs:** the three high move the puck side to side looking to get it to the middle for a shot with a lane; the two low screen, tip and collect rebounds. Everything is aimed at getting a puck through to the net with a body in front of the goalie.

**Strengths:** enormous space and time up high; hard to pressure, because pressuring one high player opens a pass to another; three shooting angles from the top; very difficult for the kill to get a shorthanded break, because you always have players above the puck.

**Weaknesses:** it is far from the net, and goalies and killers are most comfortable when the puck is far from the net. Used as a permanent shape it becomes predictable, and a diamond or an aggressive box will happily let you pass it around the top all night. It concedes the middle of the ice by design.

**Suits:** teams with heavy, accurate point shots and a genuinely fearless net front; also a good change-up shape when a kill has settled comfortably against your 1-3-1.

#### Overload — four to one side

```facts
Position: Four on one side — below the goal line, in the corner or low circle, on the half-wall and at that point — with the fifth weak side as a release valve
Action: Cycle the puck relentlessly in a small area, creating repeated 3-on-2s along the strong-side wall
Read: When a killer over-commits, attack the seam or swing it cross-ice to the lone weak-side player with the goalie moving
Read: Excellent against a passive box that will not come out to the wall
Countered by: A kill that reads the pattern — the passes are short and easy to intercept, and a strong-side interception is a shorthanded break
Risk: It produces fewer shots than other shapes; cycling without ever pulling the trigger just tires your own unit
```

**The shape:** **four players loaded onto one side of the ice** — typically a player below the goal line, one in the corner or low circle, one on the half-wall and one at the point on that side — with the fifth player weak side as a release valve and safety.

![](diagram:pp-overload)

**The jobs:** cycle the puck relentlessly in a small area, creating repeated 3-on-2s along the strong-side wall until a killer over-commits, then either attack the resulting seam or swing the puck cross-ice to the lone weak-side player for a shot with the goalie moving.

**Strengths:** outnumbers the kill locally; generates chances from deception and short passes rather than long ones; excellent against a passive box that will not come out to the wall.

**Weaknesses:** demands deceptive, patient passers — if the kill reads the pattern, the passes are short and easy to intercept, and interceptions on the strong side lead straight to shorthanded breaks. It also produces fewer shots than other shapes; a unit that cycles without ever pulling the trigger achieves nothing but a tired power play.

**Suits:** skilled, patient units, and teams whose best player is a below-the-goal-line playmaker. Note that at rec and youth level overload is often run out of a **2-3 base** (below), with both defencemen staying at the points. **That is an impression of how those levels play rather than a measured share** — this document has no count of rec or youth power-play deployments, and none was found in the sources searched for it, so treat it as unverified like the other prevalence claims here.

#### Spread / box — pulling the kill apart

```facts
Position: Two low just outside the posts, two high and wide at the tops of the circles or points, one in the middle of the slot
Key: The point is the spacing, not the shape. Stand far enough apart that one killer cannot cover two of you
Action: Occupy space, stay still enough to be a genuine passing target, and make everything a one-touch play
Read: Use it at 5-on-4 against a kill that has collapsed so tightly it has conceded the outside entirely; it is also the standard 5-on-3 shape
Countered by: Long passes — at 5-on-4 they are interceptable and can be blocked by a well-placed stick
Risk: The spread surrenders the possession game — you are not cycling, you are waiting
Key: "Box" names both this power-play spacing and the standard penalty-kill shape; say which one you mean
```

**The shape:** five spots, deliberately stretched to the corners of the danger area — **two low**, one on each side of the net just outside the posts; **two high and wide**, at the tops of the circles or the points; and **one in the middle of the slot**. The point is not the shape itself but the spacing: **stand far enough apart that one killer cannot cover two of you.**

> **Terminology warning:** "box" names both this power-play spacing *and* the standard penalty-kill shape. They are unrelated. Say which one you mean.

**The jobs:** occupy space, stay still enough to be a genuine passing target, and force the kill to make full-length skates to close anyone down. Everything is a one-touch play — with this much space the kill's only defence is anticipation, and one-touch passing beats anticipation.

**Strengths:** the maximum geometric stress you can put on a small defensive unit. Creates backdoor plays almost automatically, because the weak-side low player is simply too far away for the kill's low defender to reach.

**Weaknesses:** at 5-on-4 the passes are long, and long passes are interceptable and can be blocked by a well-placed stick. It surrenders the possession game — you are not cycling, you are waiting.

**Suits:** it is the standard **5-on-3** shape (see below), and it is worth using at 5-on-4 against a kill that has collapsed so tightly it has conceded the outside entirely.

#### 2-3 — the older structure

```facts
Position: Two defencemen at the points and three forwards low — one at the net front, one in each circle or corner
Action: The points shoot and keep pucks in; the three forwards screen, tip and hunt rebounds, with the puck going low-to-high and back
Key: It is safe — two defencemen at the line means very few shorthanded breaks and good retrieval on missed shots
Countered by: A competent box, which is built to defend the two areas this attacks
Risk: It ignores the middle of the slot entirely and generates lots of low-quality shots
Read: Choose the 2-3 with no puck-moving defenceman, when protecting a lead, or where the group has not yet learned to hold the blue line under pressure
```

**The shape:** **two defencemen at the points** and **three forwards low** — one at the net front, one in each circle or corner. This was the default power play for decades and remains common in rec, beer-league and youth hockey. **Both halves of that are impressions rather than measured shares, and this document has no count for either** — the same limit that applies to the prevalence claims elsewhere here.

![](diagram:pp-2-3)

**The jobs:** the points shoot and keep pucks in; the three forwards work the low area, screen, tip and hunt rebounds; the puck generally goes low-to-high and back.

**Strengths:** it is safe. Two defencemen at the line means very few shorthanded breaks and very good puck retrieval on missed shots. It is also the easiest shape to teach and the least dependent on individual skill — everyone knows where to stand.

**Weaknesses:** it attacks the two areas a box is built to defend (the points and the goalmouth) and ignores the middle of the slot entirely. It generates lots of low-quality shots. Against a competent box it is close to harmless.

**Suits:** teams without a puck-moving defenceman, teams who are protecting a lead, and any group that has not yet learned to hold the blue line under pressure. It is a perfectly respectable place to start.

### The key power-play plays

These are the plays the shapes exist to produce. Learn the plays first; the shape is just where you stand to make them available.

#### The seam pass

```facts
Key: A seam runs through or across the middle of the defensive shape rather than around it — half-wall to half-wall, or half-wall to bumper
Goal: Any puck that crosses the middle forces every killer and the goalie to re-orient at once
Technique: Flat, hard, on the ice, to the receiver's stick, with a look or a fake sold first — Passing and Receiving covers the mechanics
Never: Force a seam that is not there — intercepted in the middle it is a shorthanded breakaway, and you have four teammates above the puck
```

A **seam** is a passing lane that runs through or across the middle of the defensive shape rather than around it — most often half-wall to half-wall, or half-wall to bumper.

- It works because a kill's shape is built to defend the *perimeter*. Any puck that crosses the middle forces every killer and the goalie to re-orient at once.
- **How to throw one — flat, hard, on the ice, to the receiver's stick, with a look or a fake sold first** — is standard passing technique under pressure, covered in [Passing and Receiving](../technique/passing_and_receiving.md). What is special-teams-specific is the judgement: **if the seam is not there, do not force it.** A seam pass intercepted in the middle is a shorthanded breakaway, and you have four teammates above the puck.

#### The one-timer from the off-wing

```facts
Technique: A one-timer is a shot taken directly off an incoming pass without stopping the puck
Position: Play your off-wing — left-hander on the right side — so the puck is on your inside hand and your body turns toward the net
Technique: Weight loaded on the back foot, body still and square before the pass leaves — Shooting covers the mechanics
Technique: Keep the backswing short — in a CARHA league a stick brought back more than fifteen inches is a slap shot: a minor; if it injures, ejection and at least a one-game ban
Rule: In a CARHA league keep the one-timer's follow-through below the shoulders — Rule 62(b) makes high-stick contact above an opponent's shoulders a major even by accident and even if nobody is hurt; deliberate or injuring, it also ejects you (62(b) Note)
Key: The threat is worth as much as the shot — a live weak-side one-timer forces one killer to stay wide
Goal: That wide killer is precisely how the bumper and the net front get free, before the puck ever comes to you
```

A **one-timer** is a shot taken directly off an incoming pass without stopping the puck. Playing your **off-wing** (left-hander on the right side, right-hander on the left) puts the puck on your inside hand and turns your whole body toward the net, which is what makes it possible. The shooting mechanics — weight loaded on the back foot, body still and square before the pass leaves — are in [Shooting](../technique/shooting.md). Keep the backswing short: in a CARHA league a stick brought back more than fifteen inches is a slap shot, and CARHA Rule 79(a) makes that a minor, or a major if it injures an opponent — which ends your game and brings at least a one-game suspension (32(d)). A follow-through that catches an opponent above the shoulders is a separate foul there: a major even by accident and even if nobody is hurt, and an ejection only if it was deliberate or injures (Rule 62(b)).

The power-play point is a different one: **the threat is worth as much as the shot.** A live one-timer option on the weak side forces one killer to stay wide, and that is precisely how the bumper and the net front get free. You are being useful in the two seconds *before* the puck ever comes to you.

#### The royal road / slot-line pass

```facts
Key: The royal road runs down the middle of the ice from net to net; a pass crossing it just before a shot is the highest-value pass type measured, roughly double the next one
Key: Ranking the royal road the highest-value pass type rests on volunteer-tracked Passing Project data — 51,308 shots at 5-on-5, all attempts, not shots on goal — so it is not a power-play number
Goal: A goalie tracking the puck across the middle cannot stay square — they are off their angle and depth when the shot arrives
Action: Before you shoot from a half-wall, check the far side — a cross-seam pass beats a shot from the same spot almost every time
Key: Offensive Zone Play covers the measured finishing rate and its qualifications — take the number from there, with those words attached
Risk: The 20-30% range quoted in coaching material has no published primary source behind it; treat it as illustrative only
```

The **royal road** (also called the slot line) is an imaginary line running straight down the middle of the ice from net to net. A pass that **crosses** it immediately before a shot is the highest-value pass type measured, roughly double the next one. *(Published descriptions of exactly where the line runs disagree — some put it net-centre to net-centre as here, others stop it at the top of the circles. [Shooting](../technique/shooting.md) sets out the disagreement; nothing here turns on which is right.)*

- **Why it works:** a goalie who has to track the puck across the middle cannot stay square. They must break from small shuffles into a hard lateral push, and while pushing they are opening up, off their angle and off their depth. The shot arrives before they are set. See [Goaltender](../positions/goaltender.md) for the save mechanics from the other side.
- The concept was popularised by former NHL goalie **Steve Valiquette**, whose shot tracking classified chances by whether the puck crossed the line. That "76% of goals are green shots" figure traces to Valiquette ([NHL.com, December 2014](https://web.archive.org/web/20150924092445/http://www.nhl.com/ice/news.htm?id=744483)), but the tracking behind it is proprietary and has never been replicated — [Shooting](../technique/shooting.md) sets out its limits.
- **The best-sourced figure is 15.50%** — the finishing rate on shots after a royal-road pass, roughly double the next pass type (Alan Wells' analysis of Ryan Stimson's Passing Project, [via NHL.com](https://www.nhl.com/kraken/news/analytics-with-alison-dangerous-passes-327627898)). Carry its limits with it: the data was **volunteer-tracked**, it is *"51,308 shots at 5v5"* (Wells, Hockey Graphs, September 2016), it counts **all attempts, not shots on goal**, and it is **not a power-play measurement**. [Offensive Zone Play](offensive_zone_play.md) covers it. **The 20–30% range quoted in coaching material has no published primary source**; treat it as illustrative. The direction of the effect is not in doubt, and it is why modern power plays are built around cross-ice passing.
- **Practical version:** a cross-seam pass beats a shot from the same spot almost every time. Before you shoot from a half-wall, check the far side.

#### The point shot for tips and rebounds

```facts
Key: The point shot is not an attempt to score from the point — it is a delivery system for a tip or a rebound
Technique: Judge it on whether it arrives low, hard and on net with a body in front
Read: Shoot when the net-front player has established position and the goalie's view is obstructed, not before
Action: Change the angle before you shoot — step sideways along the line until the shot-blocker has to move their feet, and the lane the kill set is no longer the lane you are in
Risk: A missed net is a free clear off the end boards and twenty seconds gone
Rule: In a CARHA league shoot wrist or snap — Rule 79(a) makes a slap shot, a backswing over fifteen inches, a minor, and a major if it injures, which 30(a) and 32(d) make an ejection and at least a one-game ban
```

**The point shot is not an attempt to score from the point.** It is a delivery system for a tip or a rebound, and should be judged on whether it arrives low, hard and on net with a body in front. Release, weight transfer and getting a shot through a blocking lane are in [Shooting](../technique/shooting.md). In a CARHA league make it a wrist or snap shot, because a slap shot there is a minor and, if it injures, an ejection and at least a one-game ban (Rules 79(a), 30(a), 32(d)).

The two special-teams reads: **shoot when the net-front player has established position and the goalie's view is obstructed, not before**, and **change the angle first**, because stepping sideways along the line takes the shot out of the lane the kill has set. Screening a goalie from outside the crease is legal, provided you do not touch them or crowd them so they cannot move. How far is feel rather than a measurement: go until the shot-blocker has to move their feet, which is usually a step or two. A missed net on a power play is a free clear off the end boards and twenty seconds gone.

#### The backdoor play

```facts
Position: The backdoor is the weak-side post — the far side of the net, away from the puck
Read: Whenever the kill's low defender commits to the strong side or to the net-front player, it is a 2-on-1 with nobody defending it
Action: Arrive late — time it, do not camp it; a player standing at the back post all shift is marked
Key: The pass comes low-to-high-to-backdoor or straight across from below the goal line — either way it crosses the royal road, which is why it converts
```

The **backdoor** is the weak-side post — the far side of the net, away from the puck. Whenever the kill's low defender commits to the strong side or to the net-front player, it is a 2-on-1 with nobody defending it.

![](diagram:pp-backdoor)

**Arrive late.** A player standing at the back post all shift is marked; a player arriving as the shot or pass is released is not. Time it, do not camp it. The pass comes low-to-high-to-backdoor or straight across from below the goal line — either way it crosses the royal road, which is why it converts.

#### The walk-down from the half-wall

```facts
Action: Skate the puck down from the half-wall toward the goal line and inside rather than passing
Read: If the kill's low defender steps out to you, the net front is free; if they stay, you have a shot from the top of the circle with a moving goalie
Never: Walk to the corner — below the goal line and wide you are harmless; the threat comes from heading toward the dot
Technique: Head up, feet moving toward the net — the moment your feet stop, the kill resets
Never: Turn your back to anyone coming at you on a walk-down off the half-wall, and never duck — take contact with skates parallel to the boards, forearm and hip to the wall, head up and chin off your chest
Action: If it is denied, reset to the point and start over
Risk: Losing the puck below the goal line is a wasted twenty seconds and usually a clear
```

The **walk-down** is the half-wall player skating the puck down toward the goal line and inside rather than passing. It forces a decision: if the kill's low defender steps out to you, the net front is free; if they stay, you have a shot from the top of the circle with a moving goalie.

![](diagram:pp-walk-down)

**Walk to the middle, not to the corner** — below the goal line and wide you are harmless, and the threat comes from heading toward the dot. Head up, feet moving toward the net; the moment your feet stop the kill resets. If it is denied, **reset to the point and start over.** Losing the puck below the goal line on a power play is a wasted twenty seconds and usually a clear.

**The walk-down starts on the boards with a killer stepping out at you, so the "head up" above is only one limb of the posture and not the whole of it.** ⚠️ **Never turn your back to anyone coming at you, and never duck.** Get your **skates parallel to the boards**, forearm and hip to the wall, head up and chin off your chest — **not the point of your shoulder, and not the whole shoulder if you can help it.** **Being a man up is not protection here**, and neither is a non-checking league: the wall does not need an opponent to hurt you, and a killer closing legally can still arrive with you against it — even where pushing or pinning you into the boards is a penalty, as it is in women's hockey under the IIHF book. [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md) owns the full rule.

### Power play zone entries

**In short:** Enter with control rather than speed, and when the kill has the line, regroup and come again rather than force it.

```facts
Key: This is where most amateur power plays die — at the blue line, not in the zone
Priority: A clean, controlled entry is worth far more than a fast one; enter with possession and you have ninety seconds of pressure
Key: Zone Entries covers the entry toolkit and the tracked values — read the figures there
Action: Use the drop pass — it changes the carrier without changing the speed of the attack and makes the defending line commit to the wrong player
Action: Swing one or two players back to gather speed and receive already moving forward; the single swing is less predictable than the double
Action: Dump to a spot, not a player, and rim away from the goalie's trapezoid so a defender retrieves under pressure
Rule: The goalkeeper's restricted area is IIHF Rule 27.7, but England Ice Hockey's Rules & Regulations 22.3 call it "not currently enforced", scope and standing unsettled — so in any British competition, plan for their goalie to come out for a dumped puck
Rule: USA Hockey, Hockey Canada and CARHA (adult leagues only) write no trapezoid at all, so their goalie may fetch a rimmed puck from any corner — not freeze it out there: USA Hockey 614(c) and Casebook Situation 6; CARHA 58(b) and 61(b); Hockey Canada Interpretation 3 to Rule 10.1(a), clause v
Rule: Against a PWHL goalie, plan for her to come out for a dump too — the PWHL switches the trapezoid off in Rules 1.8 and 27.8, though its 65.2(viii) still writes the goalie's minor
Read: Regroup rather than force — you can afford it and the kill cannot, but the cost is clock, so do not regroup out of habit
Risk: A puck turned over at the offensive blue line with four teammates ahead of it is the way coaches most often name for conceding a shorthanded goal
```

**Before any of this applies, you have to have the puck out of your own end — and a power play spends more time there than it expects.** A kill may clear the length of the ice without being whistled, so the puck comes straight back to you behind your own net; and if you ice it yourself, the next draw is in your own zone. **The exit a man up is in [Breakouts](breakouts.md#breaking-out-a-man-up--the-power-play-breakout)**: the same five jobs and named options as at even strength, with two differences. On a four-forward unit one of the two defence jobs is a forward's. And **icing applies to you**, so the length-of-ice clear is the one exit the rules price differently for you than for them.

**This is where most amateur power plays die** — not in the zone. A kill defending its blue line is compact, has four players between you and the net, and has nothing to lose by conceding the neutral zone, while you have to bring a five-player structure through a narrow gate without a turnover. Units drill the in-zone setup for hours and the entry for minutes, then wonder why they never get set up.

**The entry toolkit itself — carry, chip, dump, delay, the drive-and-drop, and the data on what each is worth — is in [Zone Entries](zone_entries.md), and everything there applies here.** What changes with the extra man is only this:

1. **A clean, controlled entry is worth far more than a fast one.** Enter with possession and set up, and you have ninety seconds of pressure. Dump and lose the race and you have ten, then you do it all again. (The tracked data still favours the carry-in on the power play — roughly two and a half times the shots of a dump-in — but by a **narrower** margin than at even strength, because the extra man also makes dump-in retrievals easier. **Note where that particular figure comes from:** the power-play numbers are **PWHPA / Sportlogiq tracking of women's professional hockey**, not the NHL zone-entry study behind the even-strength figures, and the source does not state which season it covers. [Zone Entries](zone_entries.md) covers them and carries the full qualification. The advantage is real; it just is not larger than usual.)
2. **You do not get many attempts.** A failed entry — dump, lose the race, regroup, come again — burns a serious slice of a 120-second advantage. Two or three of those and the penalty is over. *(There is no measured figure for the cost of a failed entry; treat this as calibration, not data.)*
3. **The drop pass is the signature power-play entry**, because it changes the carrier without changing the speed of the attack and makes the defending line commit to the wrong player. **Arik Parnass's** tracking for the Special Teams Project across six NHL teams in 2015-16 found drop-pass entries produced **0.84 shot attempts per entry versus 0.70** for other entries — but took **15.5 seconds to execute versus 12.6**, which cancels most of the gain. (*Parnass's three categories run drop 15.49 s, regular 12.56 s, stretch pass 10.37 s. 12.6 is the figure that pairs with the 0.70, because Parnass removed stretch-pass results from the non-drop bucket; comparing the drop against the stretch's 10.4 s instead is not like for like.*) Parnass's conclusion is the sensible one, and it is about your own team rather than theirs: keep **both** a polished regular scheme and a drop scheme, and **go to the drop when your primary scheme is in a slump.** *(Decade-old, small-sample study — indicative, not settled.)* Every kill now expects it, and a drop stolen at the red line is a shorthanded breakaway.
4. **The swing** — one or two players arcing back to gather speed and receive already moving forward — solves the entry's hardest problem: arriving at the line with speed and the puck at once. The single swing (one forward swings, one stays wide) is less predictable than the double.
5. **Dump to a spot, not a player.** With the extra man you will usually win the loose-puck race if you time the arrival. Rim it away from the goalie's **trapezoid** — the marked area behind the net where an NHL or IIHF goalie may play the puck — so a defender has to retrieve under pressure. **Under USA Hockey, Hockey Canada and CARHA there is no trapezoid**, so their goalie may fetch a rim from any corner, though not freeze it there; against a PWHL goalie, plan for her to come out too. ⚠️ **On your rim, forecheck with your stick, never your body** — a goalie out in that corner is still not fair game. **🇬🇧 In any British competition, plan for their goalie to come out:** England Ice Hockey's *Rules & Regulations* 22.3 call the IIHF restricted area *"not currently enforced"*, its scope and standing unsettled, so plan for the goalie to come out anyway and look behind the net for painted lines. See [Defender](../positions/defender.md) and [Goaltender](../positions/goaltender.md).
6. **Regroup rather than force.** Curl back out, reset to the point defenceman or the goalie, re-form the swing, attack with new speed and a different pattern. **You can afford this and the kill cannot** — they are tiring while you are not. The cost is clock, so do not regroup out of habit. **A puck turned over at the offensive blue line with four teammates ahead of it is the way coaches most often name for a power play conceding a shorthanded goal** — coaching consensus rather than a counted breakdown. Nobody publishes shorthanded goals classified by the play that produced them.

### Retrievals and keeping possession

Most of a power play is not the set play. It is the twenty seconds of scrambling after the kill clears it or blocks a shot. Units that win here get three or four setups per power play; units that lose here get one.

#### When the kill clears it

```facts
Action: Decide in advance who chases — commonly the net-front player or the weak-side half-wall, with the point held at the line, but that is a coaching choice: ask your coach
Never: Send two chasers — if two players chase, nobody is holding the zone
Goal: The chaser's job is not to win the puck but to arrive before the puck stops, so it can be moved on the move
Action: Everyone else re-forms while the chase is happening, not after — possession with no options is nothing
Read: If the clear leaves the zone entirely, regroup as a unit and re-enter properly rather than chasing in ones and twos
```

- **Decide in advance who chases.** It is commonly the net-front player or the weak-side half-wall, with the point held at the line — **but that is a coaching choice and this document has no count behind it, the same limit that applies to the prevalence claims elsewhere here, so ask your coach which it is on your unit.** What is not optional is that only one player goes: if two chase, nobody is holding the zone.
- **The chaser's job is not to win the puck — it is to arrive before the puck stops.** A rolling puck retrieved on the move can be moved immediately; a dead puck against the boards is a battle you might lose.
- **Everyone else re-forms while the chase is happening**, not after. If the retrieval succeeds and the shape is not rebuilt, you have possession and no options.
- **If the clear leaves the zone entirely, do not chase it in ones and twos.** Regroup as a unit and re-enter properly — see the entries section above. A disorganised re-entry loses more time than a calm one.

#### The defenceman's job holding the line

```facts
Priority: As the lone point player, keeping the puck in the zone is your first responsibility and shooting is your second
Position: Stay inside the blue line — outside it you are not a passing option, and a puck reaching you there has cleared the zone
Technique: Take the puck off the wall with your feet already moving laterally, body on the wall, stick low and flat
Never: Turn your back to anyone coming at you holding the line, and never duck — skates parallel to the boards, forearm and hip to the wall, head up and chin off your chest, never the point of your shoulder
Never: Lunge at a clear that has beaten you cleanly. Turn and skate instead; a missed hold with one defenceman back is a 2-on-0
Key: Holding is staying at the line to stop the puck leaving; pinching is stepping down the wall into the zone, and with one defenceman back it is almost never worth it
Convention: If your team runs two defencemen, only one holds aggressively at a time — the other backs up as the safety
Rule: Drifting into the neutral zone is not offside; offside is judged only at the instant the puck crosses the line into the zone (NHL Rule 83.1)
```

If you are the lone point player in a 1-3-1, **keeping the puck in the zone is your first responsibility and shooting is your second.** A power play that gets cleared has to re-enter against four killers already set in their structure, which is a far harder problem than holding a zone you are already in.

- **Stay inside the blue line.** Drifting out into the neutral zone is not offside — offside is only ever judged at the instant the puck crosses the line into the zone (Rule 83.1), and a puck already in the zone cannot make you offside. The real cost is simpler: with your skates outside the line you are no longer an option for a pass, and if the puck reaches you there the zone is cleared and the whole unit has to re-enter against a set kill.
- **Take the puck off the wall with your feet already moving laterally**, not standing square to the boards — that is how clears go past you.
- **Body on the wall, stick low and flat.** Most clears on the power play are along the boards at ankle height. ⚠️ **"Body on the wall" is where you are, not how you meet it** — a killer chasing that clear arrives with you already against the boards. **Never turn your back to anyone coming at you, and never duck.** **Skates parallel to the boards**, forearm and hip to the wall, head up and chin off your chest — not the point of your shoulder, and not the whole shoulder if you can help it. **A wall does not need an opponent to hurt you, so this holds in a non-checking league too.** See [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md).
- **A hold you miss is a 2-on-0.** With one defenceman back, "keep it in at all costs" is wrong. If the clear beats you cleanly, turn and skate; do not lunge. See [Risk Management](../hockey-iq/risk_management.md).
- **Holding the line is not pinching, and the difference matters here.** *Holding* means staying at the offensive blue line and stopping the puck leaving. (Do not call it "stepping up" — a **step up** is moving *forward* to challenge an attacker early, in the neutral zone or at your **own** blue line. Different end of the rink, different play. See [Rink Map](../foundation/rink_map.md).) *Pinching* means leaving the line and stepping **down** the wall into the zone to attack a puck below you. Holding is the point player's standing job on a power play. Pinching is an extra risk you take on top of it, and with one defenceman back it is almost never worth taking — a missed pinch puts you two strides deeper than a missed hold does.
- If your team runs two defencemen (umbrella, 2-3), **only one holds aggressively at a time** — the other backs up as the safety. Same partnership discipline as an even-strength pinch, described in [Defender](../positions/defender.md).

### 5-on-3

**In short:** Spread out, move the puck across the middle until the goalie is moving, and have someone at the net for the rebound.

```facts
Key: A two-man advantage is a different game, not a better power play — it fails when a unit plays it like a 5-on-4
Read: Three killers sit in a tight triangle around the slot and concede everything outside it — nobody is coming to hunt you on the perimeter
Action: Spread out into the spread/box shape and make the triangle try to cover the whole zone
Action: Move the puck side to side across the royal road, not around the perimeter, until the goalie cannot get set
Never: Shoot from the outside into three shot-blockers. Wait for the goalie to be moving instead, but do not pass for two minutes without shooting
Goal: The goal usually comes from a one-timer at the far post or the second-wave rebound, so have someone at the net for both
Rule: A team never plays fewer than three skaters — a third penalty's clock waits and a substitute keeps them at three (NHL Rule 26.1)
Rule: A goal against a shorthanded team terminates only the minor with the least time remaining, so a 5-on-3 becomes a 5-on-4 (NHL Rule 16.2)
Rule: A goal releases only a minor or bench minor (NHL and IIHF Rule 16.2), so a major is served in full however many you score (NHL and IIHF 20.2) — and a goal scored on a penalty shot releases nothing at all (16.2 in terms, and NHL 24.6)
Rule: Under the In-House Rules a fighting major in NIHL, NIHL1 or NIHL2 in the last five minutes of regulation or in overtime also carries an automatic game misconduct (Section 6, sitting on top of the IIHF book); in women's hockey any major also ejects (Rule 101)
```

A two-man advantage is a different game, not a better power play. It should almost always produce a goal, and when it does not it is nearly always because the unit played it like a 5-on-4.

**Why it is different:** three killers can defend a triangle. They will sit in a tight triangle around the slot — usually two low in front of the net and one high — concede everything outside it, and block shots. **Sustained pressure on the puck is not available to them**, because every killer who leaves the triangle opens a hole nobody can fill. That does not mean you will never be touched: a three-player kill will still jump a bobbled reception, close hard on anyone who walks into the slot, and throw a stick or a body at a shooter. What it means is that **nobody is coming to hunt you on the perimeter** — so you have time out there that does not exist at 5-on-4, and the only way to lose it is to hurry.

**How to play it:**

- **Spread out** into the spread/box shape above, and make the triangle try to cover the whole zone.
- **Move the puck side to side across the royal road, not around the perimeter.** Every cross-ice pass forces the goalie into a full lateral push; three or four in a row and they will not be set. **Do not shoot from the outside into three shot-blockers** — with nobody hunting you there is no reason to rush, so wait for the goalie to be moving.
- **The goal usually comes from a one-timer at the far post or the second-wave rebound.** Have someone at the net for both. Be patient but not passive: two minutes of pretty passing with no shot is a failed 5-on-3.

![](diagram:pp-spread-5on3)

**Rules that matter here:**

> **Terminology warning — "delayed penalty" means two different things in the rulebook, and both appear in this document.**
> - **Sense 1 — the signalled penalty (Rule 15.1).** The referee has seen an infraction by the team *without* the puck, raises an arm, and play continues until the offending team gains control. This is the one where you pull your goalie for an extra attacker.
> - **Sense 2 — the delayed *clock* (Rule 26).** A third penalty assessed to a team that already has two players serving. The third penalty's time does not begin until one of the first two expires. Nobody's arm is raised and nobody pulls a goalie.
>
> They are unrelated. Where this document says "delayed penalty" it says which sense it means.

- **A team never plays with fewer than three skaters.** If a third player is penalised while two are already serving, the third penalty's clock does not start until one of the first two expires (a **delayed penalty in the Rule 26 sense**), and the team may put a substitute on the ice to stay at three (NHL Rule 26.1). So a 5-on-2 does not exist.
- **A goal only releases one penalty.** When you score against a shorthanded team, the minor penalty **with the least time remaining** terminates (Rule 16.2). A 5-on-3 therefore becomes a 5-on-4, not a 5-on-5 — and if you were about to get another chance, take it immediately while the kill is reorganising.
- **This does not apply to major penalties**, which are served in full however many you score, **or to goals on a penalty shot**, which release nobody. The IIHF reaches the same three-skater floor with one difference: the substitute for a third penalised player **must** go on, where the NHL says **may**. The floor is three either way.
- **Do not fight, and never be the one who starts it.** A fighting major is killed in full whoever serves it, and what it really costs is a body off your bench for the rest of the night.

**What it costs in Britain.** Under the In-House Rules, fighting in NIHL, the SNL and BUIHA Varsity is a five-minute major; in **NIHL, NIHL1 and NIHL2**, one in the last five minutes of regulation or in overtime also carries an automatic game misconduct. **In women's hockey, plan on any fighting major ending your game** (In-House Rule 101). The In-House major softens only the plain fight: an instigator still takes the IIHF's *"instigating minor penalty, a major penalty for fighting and an automatic game misconduct"* at any point. **In the Elite League** the instigator takes an instigating minor, a fighting major and a ten-minute misconduct; a game misconduct if they are also the aggressor or it is their second instigation of the game; and in the final five minutes or overtime a game misconduct and a one-game suspension, pending review. Every instigated fight is reviewed for further suspension. Either way, the instigator minor can leave your team killing a penalty on top of the fight. See [UK and England Rules](../foundation/uk_rules.md).

## PENALTY KILL

### The one idea behind every penalty kill

```facts
Key: You cannot cover five players with four, so stop trying
Goal: Decide which shot you are willing to give up, then spend two minutes making sure it is the only shot you give up
Key: Every kill concedes something — a box the middle of the slot, a diamond the outside and the backdoor, a wedge+1 the perimeter
Never: Cover something that was never yours — that is how the thing that was yours becomes a goal
Mindset: Discipline beats effort on a penalty kill, every time
Key: Time is on your side and nothing else is — three point shots and no break beats forty clean seconds and then a break
```

You cannot cover five players with four. **So stop trying.** A penalty kill is a decision about **which shot you are willing to give up** — and then a two-minute exercise in making sure that is the only shot you give up.

Every kill concedes something. A box concedes the middle of the slot. A diamond concedes the outside and the backdoor. A wedge+1 concedes the perimeter. The failure mode is always the same: a killer decides to cover something that was never theirs, and the thing that *was* theirs becomes a goal. **Discipline beats effort on a penalty kill, every time.**

The second idea: **time is on your side and nothing else is.** Two minutes is not long. A kill that concedes zero clean looks for 40 seconds and then breaks is worse than one that concedes three shots from the point and never breaks.

### Penalty kill formations

```facts
Convention: Which shape you run is a coaching choice — the same four players can run all three in one game
Action: Know which one is called before the faceoff
Key: These are the in-zone shapes, a separate decision from the neutral-zone pressure scheme below, not a competing one
Key: The box is the shape almost everyone is taught first and the baseline the other two are described against
Risk: No public count of how many NHL teams run each as a base shape has been found. Treat any "most teams use X" claim as an impression, not a measurement
```

> Again: which shape you run is a **coaching choice**. The same four players can run all three of these in one game. **Know which one is called before the faceoff.**
>
> **On "which one is most common" — an honest answer.** These three are the shapes you defend the *zone* with, once the power play is set up. They are a separate decision from the neutral-zone pressure scheme further below (the 1-3, the 1-1-2 and so on), which is how you meet the entry; a team runs one of each in the same kill and there is no contradiction. Within the in-zone group, the **box is the shape almost everyone is taught first** and the baseline the other two are described against. Coaching material commonly describes the **wedge+1** as widely used at NHL level, and the diamond as the standard counter to a 1-3-1. **No public count of how many NHL teams run each as their base shape has been found**, so treat any "most teams use X" claim — including the ones here — as an impression from coaching sources rather than a measured figure.

#### Box — the shape everyone learns first

```facts
Position: A rectangle around the slot — two forwards high, one on each point, and two defencemen low covering the corners and the goalmouth
Key: A box has two players high and no single "top" — if you are describing "the top of the box", you are describing a diamond
Action: High forwards deny the point time and space, stay in shooting lanes, and never both drift to the same side
Action: The strong-side D pressures the puck below the goal line or in the low circle; the weak-side D owns the goalmouth and the backdoor
Technique: The box slides with the puck rather than rotating — all four shift together, keeping the shape intact
Read: Very good against an overload, which it can out-wait, and against a 2-3, whose attack aims where a box is strongest
Countered by: A 1-3-1 — nobody in a box is assigned to the middle of the slot, so the bumper stands there uncovered by design
```

**The shape:** a rectangle around the slot. **Two players high** — both forwards, one on each side, each responsible for one point — and **two players low**, both defencemen, covering the two low corners and the goalmouth.

![](diagram:pk-box)

**A box has no single "top".** Two forwards, side by side, is the whole point. If you find yourself describing "the top of the box", you are describing a diamond.

**The jobs:**

- **High forwards:** deny the point players time and space, stay in shooting lanes, and never both drift to the same side. Your inside shoulder should be turned so you can see your point man and the middle at once.
- **Low defencemen:** the strong-side D pressures the puck when it comes below the goal line or to the low circle; the weak-side D owns the goalmouth and the backdoor. Same partnership logic as even-strength [Defensive Zone Coverage](defensive_zone_coverage.md).
- **Everyone:** the box **slides** with the puck rather than rotating. When the puck moves across, all four shift together, keeping the shape intact.

**Strengths:** simple, teachable, hard to break down, and excellent at protecting the goalmouth and the low slot. Very good against an **overload**, which it can simply out-wait, and against a **2-3**, whose entire attack aims at the two areas a box defends best.

**Weaknesses:** **nobody in a box is assigned to the middle of the slot.** Against a 1-3-1 the bumper stands in the hole in the middle of your rectangle and is uncovered by design. It is also passive by nature: a box that never pressures gives a skilled unit unlimited time on the perimeter to find the seam.

#### Diamond — a single apex

```facts
Position: One player high at the apex, two in the middle either side, one low — exactly one player at the top
Goal: The two middles squeeze the slot from either side so a 1-3-1's bumper can be accounted for
Action: The apex pressures the point and takes away the middle shooting lane — you are the only player up there, so do not get walked around
Action: Each middle takes a half-wall and both collapse onto the bumper when the puck goes to the middle
Position: The low player owns the goalmouth, alone
Read: Use it against a 1-3-1, or against an umbrella where the apex can pressure the middle point man
Countered by: A quick cross-seam pass to the far half-wall — the shape is narrow, and the far-side one-timer is on if the middles are slow
Risk: The single low player faces a net-front presence and a backdoor arriver at once — a 2-on-1 you are choosing to concede
```

**The shape:** one player **high** at the apex, **two in the middle** on either side, and **one low**. A diamond, not a rectangle: it has exactly one player at the top.

![](diagram:pk-diamond)

**Why it exists:** because a box leaves the bumper unmarked. The diamond's two middle players squeeze the middle of the slot from either side, so the 1-3-1's bumper can be accounted for. Coaches also use it against a three-high **umbrella**, where the apex can pressure the middle point man who would otherwise have a free shot.

**The jobs:**

- **Apex** (often the centre, but this varies by team): pressure the point player, take away the middle shooting lane, and do not get walked around. You are the only player up there — if you overcommit, the point is uncovered.
- **Two middles:** each takes a half-wall, and both collapse onto the bumper when the puck goes to the middle. You are working two jobs at once; the timing of when you leave the wall for the middle is the whole art of playing this position.
- **Low:** own the goalmouth. You are alone down there.

**Strengths:** the only common shape that starts with an answer to the bumper; puts real pressure on the point; encourages turnovers high in the zone, which is where shorthanded chances come from.

**Weaknesses:** **narrow.** A quick cross-seam pass to the far half-wall pulls it apart, and the far-side one-timer is available if the middles are slow. The single low player is alone against a net-front presence *and* a backdoor arriver — a genuine 2-on-1 that you are choosing to concede. Also more mentally demanding than a box; a confused diamond is worse than a competent box.

#### Wedge+1 (also called triangle-and-one)

```facts
Position: Three killers in a tight triangle around the net — two defencemen just off the crease edges, one forward at the mid-to-high slot; the players rotate through the shape, the shape holds its ground
Action: The wedge stays tight, protects the slot and goalmouth, and does not chase; it blocks shots from in the lane and on its feet, never from a screening position
Action: The +1 pressures whoever has the puck — make every possession uncomfortable, do not try to win it, and hand the pressure to the nearest wedge player rather than trail the puck round the outside
Technique: When your pressure forces the puck to move, the nearest wedge player becomes the new +1 and you drop into the triangle
Key: The exchange is the hard part — everyone must know who is out and who is in, every second
Countered by: A +1 who chases without purpose; you are then killing with three players
Risk: High cognitive load makes it a poor choice for a young or ad-hoc unit
Convention: Coaching material describes it as popular at NHL level, but no league-wide count is published, so treat prevalence as unverified; published descriptions also differ on the +1's route — straight out from the goal, sweeping across the top of the zone, or roaming — so ask your coach
```

**The shape:** **three killers hold a tight triangle** around the net — two defencemen just off the crease edges, one forward at the mid-to-high slot — while the **fourth player, the "+1", pressures whoever has the puck**. **Published descriptions part company on exactly how the +1 does that, so ask your coach which one you are running.** Some describe them activating in a straight line out from the goal towards the puck carrier. Some have them sweeping laterally across the top of the zone, above the circles, to keep the power play on one side of the ice. Others have them roaming more freely, challenging on the wall or at the point. What every description shares is the handover: when the puck moves, the nearest wedge player steps out as the new +1 and the old one drops into the triangle. Whatever licence your version gives the +1, a +1 who chases the puck round the perimeter without that handover is the counter to this system, not the system, and leaves you killing with three players.

![](diagram:pk-wedge-plus-1)

**The jobs:**

- **The wedge (three players):** stay tight, protect the slot and the goalmouth, and rotate as a connected unit. You do not chase. The players rotate through the shape; the shape itself holds its ground. **Block shots properly or not at all:** be in the lane before the shot rather than sliding into it, stay on your feet until the puck is actually released, never lift a leg, turn your back or go sideways, and never block from a screening position. [Shot blocking](#shot-blocking) below sets out what is specific to the kill, and [Defender](../positions/defender.md) and [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md) own the technique itself.
- **The +1:** pressure whoever has the puck, by whichever route your coach teaches — see above; the sources differ. Your job is to make every possession uncomfortable, not to win the puck, and never to trail it round the outside instead of handing the pressure to the nearest wedge player.
- **The exchange:** when your pressure forces the puck to move, the nearest wedge player becomes the new +1 and you drop into the triangle. This handover is the hard part and the reason the system needs practice — everyone must know who is out and who is in, every second.

**Strengths:** it protects the highest-danger ice absolutely while still applying pressure, which is why it is **commonly used at NHL level** — [The Coaches Site](https://members.thecoachessite.com/article/explained-wedge-penalty-kill-formation) describes it as popular with most NHL clubs, though **no league-wide count of who runs what is published, so treat the prevalence claim as unverified.** It is very hard for a 1-3-1 to get a clean look at the middle. It creates turnovers and therefore shorthanded chances.

**Weaknesses:** high cognitive load — it demands constant awareness of a rotating assignment, which is why it is a poor choice for a young or ad-hoc unit. If the +1 chases without purpose, you are effectively killing with three players.

#### Aggressive versus passive

```facts
Key: The pressure setting is a separate decision from the shape itself
Read: Go passive — hold structure, concede the perimeter, block lanes — when protecting a lead, when tired, when outgunned, or late in a kill
Options: Go aggressive to force errors; the best-known version is the Czech press, where the high forward genuinely attacks the point player
Risk: Aggression creates chaos, and chaos with four players against five is not usually your friend
Convention: That aggression pushes shots outside while passivity concedes closer ones is a coaching claim — nothing found separates shot location by pressure setting. The one published measurement nearby counts shorthanded attacking rather than pressure, and it favours aggression
Never: Pressure alone. Pressure as a unit or not at all; one player pressuring while three sit is how seams open
```

Every shape has a pressure setting, and it is a separate decision from the shape itself.

- **Passive:** hold the structure, concede the perimeter, block lanes, wait for a clear. Lowest variance. Right when you are protecting a lead, when you are tired, when the opposition power play is much better than you, or late in a kill.
- **Aggressive:** attack the puck carrier and try to force errors. The best-known version is the **Czech press**, where the high forward genuinely attacks the point player rather than just threatening to. Higher variance: it forces mistakes and creates shorthanded chances, but it also creates chaos, and chaos with four players against five is not usually your friend.
- **The honest trade-off:** coaching consensus says aggression pushes shots outside and to the wings, while passivity concedes fewer shots but more of them from close range. **That is a coaching claim — nothing found measures shot location by pressure setting.** The nearest measurement is Matt Cane's 2016 *"PK Aggressiveness"* study on Hockey Graphs, built on one season of tracking (2013-14): kills that attacked more did better. But it counts **attacking while shorthanded, not pressuring the puck in your own end**, and the author calls one season *"too little to draw sweeping conclusions from"* — so it says nothing about the Czech press. What is not in doubt: pressure spends a killer to buy a mistake, and if the mistake does not come you are defending five with three.
- **The one rule:** **pressure as a unit or not at all.** One player pressuring while the other three sit is how seams open.

### The PK forecheck and neutral-zone pressure schemes

```facts
Key: A forecheck is pressure applied in the opponent's end; a neutral-zone scheme is how you meet them between the blue lines
Goal: On the kill these are one conversation — deciding where to make the power play uncomfortable
Key: F1 and F2 are roles, not people — defined by who is first and second to the puck, not by position
```

A **forecheck** is pressure applied in the opponent's end; a neutral-zone scheme is how you meet them between the blue lines. On the kill these are the same conversation, because the whole point is deciding **where** to make the power play uncomfortable.

**F1 and F2 are roles, not people** — defined by who is first and second to the puck, not by position.

#### The four you will meet

```facts
Options: The 1-3 — one forward on the puck, the other forward and both defencemen in a line across the ice behind that forward
Read: Passive 1-3 gives token pressure to make them commit to a side; the same-side press has F1 angle the carrier and F2 close to force a dump or turnover
Risk: With F1 beaten or the three-player line spread, the 1-3 has a lot of ice behind it
Options: The 1-1-2 — F1 on the puck, F2 layered behind on the same side, two defencemen abreast near the line
Countered by: Quick direction changes and drop passes at the red line, which slide underneath F1 and F2 at once
Options: The retreating box (2-2) — all four backing up together; safe and easy, and vulnerable to drop passes and direction changes
Options: Tandem pressure — two forwards take turns attacking the carrier; if both are beaten at once the kill is a 2-on-2 with no support
Convention: Some coaching material names the 1-1-2 as a common NHL choice for neutral-zone pressure; no count of NHL teams was found to confirm it — ask which your team plays
```

- **The 1-3** — one forward up on the puck, the other forward and both defencemen in a line across the ice behind that forward. In the **passive** version F1 gives token pressure to make the power play commit to a side while the three behind back up in unison, refusing to be split; the goal is not to win the puck, it is to **make them dump it in**. In the **same-side press** version F1 angles the carrier to one side and F2 closes from that side to force a dump or a turnover — more pressure, but it needs killers who can read and accelerate. **Weakness:** with F1 beaten or the three-line spread, there is a lot of ice behind it.
- **The 1-1-2** — F1 up on the puck, F2 layered behind on the *same* side forming a wall parallel to the boards, two defencemen abreast near the line. Aggressive without over-committing; it bleeds clock and forces contested entries. Some coaching material calls it a common NHL choice **for neutral-zone pressure** — a different decision from the in-zone shape above, not a competing one. No count of NHL teams was found to confirm it. **Weakness:** quick direction changes and drop passes at the red line slide underneath F1 and F2 at once.
- **The retreating box (2-2)** — two forwards abreast, two defencemen abreast, all four backing up together. Purely reactive, safe, easy, and vulnerable to drop passes and direction changes.
- **Tandem pressure** — two forwards take turns attacking the carrier, one peeling off as the other arrives. Exhausting to play against and exhausting to play; if both get beaten at once the kill is a 2-on-2 with no support.

![](diagram:pk-nz-1-1-2)

#### Killing the entry — what all of this is for

```facts
Goal: The most effective place to kill a penalty is before the puck enters your zone — a power play that never gets set up never uses its extra man
Action: Take away the middle and force them to one side; angle with your body, not your stick
Action: Stand up at the blue line as a pair — both defencemen step or both retreat; one of each is a hole
Action: Take the receiver's passing lane with your stick and play the puck, not the man — contact before the puck arrives is interference
Rule: Where body checking is barred, going into the receiver when the puck arrives is a penalty too; in IIHF or PWHL women's play, so is any step or glide into a player (IIHF 101.1, PWHL 52.1)
Goal: Make them dump it; force three failed entries and the penalty is over without you ever defending your own zone
Never: Lunge or dive at the blue line — a missed poke check at your own line is a clean entry with you out of the play
Read: Pressure only if you can actually get there, the carrier is looking down or facing the boards, you are fresh, and the score allows it
Rule: Angle to the puck, not the back. From behind, NHL, IIHF and PWHL 43.2 write no minor; on the head, NHL and PWHL the harsher of 43 and 48; IIHF: plan on 43.3's major and game misconduct; USA Hockey 608(a) adds a misconduct to its minor, CARHA 53(a) a game misconduct
Rule: Hockey Canada from behind, off the head or head first into boards: a minor and game misconduct (7.5(a)), a major and game misconduct if violent, must if hurt (7.5(b)), a match into boards if defenceless (7.5(c)); on the head, 7.6 head contact (7.7 Junior/Senior), up to a match, a must if defenceless
Rule: USA Hockey makes a check from behind a mandatory major and game misconduct if reckless or head first into the boards or goal frame (608(b)) — plan on a match if reckless (602(a), 608(c)); its Casebook requires that pair, or a match, for a forceful one on a player along the boards (608, Situation 1)
Rule: Checking from behind can reach a match in five books, none needing the boards — mandatory for a deliberate attempt to injure: Hockey Canada 7.5(c) (7.6/7.7(c) on the head), NHL and PWHL 21.1, CARHA 48(a) and USA Hockey 602(a), which reaches a reckless hit, so plan on one; CARHA 53(a) also allows one
Rule: From behind into boards on a defenceless player: a mandatory match in CARHA 53(b) and Hockey Canada (7.5(c) off the head, 7.6/7.7(c) on it); plan on one in USA Hockey if reckless (602(a), 608(c)); IIHF: no minor (43.2), no match in the book — plan on a major and automatic game misconduct (43.3)
Rule: A USA Hockey ejection is not only this game — a game misconduct also suspends you for your team's next game (404(b)), and a second major in one game at least two (403(b))
```

**The most effective place to kill a penalty is before the puck enters your zone**, and every scheme above serves that one sentence. The reason is arithmetic: a power play that never gets set up never gets to use its extra man. Once they are established in a shape you are managing damage; in the neutral zone you are 4-on-5 in a space where you can dictate.

- **Take away the middle and force them to one side.** A power play made to enter along one boards has removed half its own options. Angle with your body, not your stick.
- **"Looking down" and "facing the boards" are reasons the puck is winnable, not licence to arrive harder.** ⚠️ **Angle to the puck, not through the back.** A carrier facing the boards is exactly the picture the checking-from-behind rule is written about. **It ends your game in most books, and every book but the IIHF can make it a match penalty** — the full price is under **What it costs** at the end of this section.
- **Stand up at the blue line as a pair.** Both defencemen step, or both retreat. One of each is a hole.
- **Attack the receiver, not just the carrier.** The dangerous player on entry is usually the one arriving with speed off a drop or a swing. Taking away the target is easier than taking away the puck. Take it with your stick in the receiver's passing lane, and when the puck reaches them go for the puck, not the man: contact before it arrives is interference. Where body checking is barred, or in women's hockey under the IIHF or the PWHL, which penalise any step or glide into a player, going into the receiver when it arrives is a penalty too.
- **Make them dump it.** A dump-in with your defencemen already turned is a win. You do not have to force turnovers to kill a penalty; you just have to keep them out. Force three failed entries and the penalty is over without you ever defending your own zone.
- **Do not lunge or dive at the blue line.** A missed poke check at your own line is a clean entry with you out of the play and your team down two.

**Pressure or retreat? Judge it in this order:** (1) **Can you actually get there** — pressure arriving a stride late is worse than none, because you have spent a player for nothing. (2) **Where is the puck** — attack a carrier looking down, receiving badly, or facing the boards; never one with head up and support both sides. (3) **What is the clock** — pressure early while you are fresh; in the last 30 seconds or when gassed, retreat and make them beat structure. (4) **What is the score** — protecting a one-goal lead late is not the time for the Czech press; see [Game Management](game_management.md). (5) **Who is on** — a unit with one elite carrier is often worth attacking; five good passers will punish you for it.

**What it costs — checking from behind.**

- **NHL, PWHL and IIHF:** there is no minor for it. The NHL and PWHL make it a major and game misconduct; the IIHF writes no minor either, so plan on a major and automatic game misconduct. If it reaches the head, the NHL and PWHL take the harsher of this rule and the head-contact rule (Rule 48), so still no minor; under the IIHF, plan on the same major and game misconduct.
- **Hockey Canada:** while the hit stays off the head, at least a minor and game misconduct; a major and game misconduct on a violent hit, mandatory if the player is injured; a **match** for a hit from behind into the boards or goal frame on a player who cannot protect themselves. If it reaches the head it is priced as head contact instead — up to a match, with a mandatory major and game misconduct for a cross-check above the shoulders, for an injury in minor and female hockey, and in Junior and Senior for a high degree of violence or a head injury off the glass, ice or boards. A hit that drives a player head first into the boards or goal frame gets the harsher of the two prices.
- **CARHA (adult leagues only):** a minor or major, each with a game misconduct, and a major if the player is injured; a **match** into the boards or goal net on a player who cannot protect themselves, and a match is available anywhere on the ice.
- **USA Hockey:** at least a minor and a misconduct. ⚠️ **A major and game misconduct is mandatory for a forceful check on a player standing along the boards, for one thrown head first into the boards or goal frame, and for any reckless hit, boards or not.** A match is mandatory for a reckless hit with excessive force on a defenceless player — and since USA Hockey gives a match to anyone who *"recklessly endangers"* an opponent, plan on a match for any reckless hit from behind. *"The onus is on the player delivering the check to not hit from behind."*
- **A deliberate attempt to injure** is a mandatory match in the NHL, the PWHL, Hockey Canada, CARHA and USA Hockey, boards or not. The IIHF writes no match penalty at all.
- **The night is not the whole price.** Every book reports a game misconduct to an authority that can suspend you further; your team's next game is automatic under USA Hockey, under CARHA when it comes with a major, and under Hockey Canada in the last ten minutes or overtime. [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md) owns the rule and the injury mechanism behind it.

### Clearing, blocking, sticks and discipline

#### You may ice the puck freely — where your rule set allows it

```facts
Rule: A team below the opposing team's on-ice numerical strength cannot be called for icing — play continues and no violation is called (NHL 81.6, PWHL 83.6) — but not unconditionally: the NHL and PWHL withdraw it in one major-penalty case, and USA Hockey withholds it by classification (Rule 624(b)(1))
Rule: IIHF Rule 81.6 grants the shorthanded icing exemption in the same words and carries neither carve-out, so a British kill loses it to neither — but it runs on the on-ice count: a penalty expiring counts its player back in, and ends the exemption only where that leaves you level
Rule: NHL 81.6 and PWHL 83.6 withdraw the exemption only where a major expires with the penalty box empty ("Icing will be called") — not while it is being served, and only until the next stoppage; seating the substitute is a separate duty (NHL and PWHL 20.3)
Rule: Seat a substitute for a player out on a major: missing it is a bench minor under IIHF 68.2 ("immediately") and under NHL 68.2 and PWHL 70.2 ("any violation"), and NHL and PWHL 20.3 add that you play a player short to the next stoppage
Rule: USA Hockey grants the nullification only at Youth 15-Only and above, Girls 16U and above, High School and Adult (Rule 624(b)(1)); below those levels a shorthanded team can be called for icing
Rule: Hockey Canada 6.7(b) and CARHA 65(d) (adult leagues only) grant the shorthanded icing exemption with no age limit and no major-penalty clause — both rules read end to end — and take the count "at the instant the puck is shot" rather than at the penalty's expiry
Rule: Release the puck before the penalty expires and no icing applies even if it ends while the puck is travelling (NHL and IIHF 81.6, PWHL 83.6) — and on a 5-on-3 the first expiry leaves you 4-on-5, below their strength and still exempt
Rule: Not a shorthanded rule at all: a puck sent the length of the ice directly off the draw is not icing in any of the six books (NHL and IIHF 81.5, PWHL 83.5, Hockey Canada 6.7(c)(iv), USA Hockey 624(b)(3), CARHA 65(e), adult leagues only) — USA Hockey's age limit sits on 624(b)(1), not on this limb
Action: Where the exemption applies, fire it the length of the ice the moment you have it — no faceoff, no line-change restriction, and a chance to change
Action: You do not need a teammate — 200 feet away buys clock and a change; make the retrieval awkward, into the corner away from their goalie's trapezoid or hard around the boards
Never: Stretch "numerical strength" beyond penalties — five skaters defending a pulled goalie are not below it and can be called for icing
```

**A shorthanded team cannot be called for icing.** NHL Rule 81.6: *"If the puck was so shot by a player of a side below the numerical strength of the opposing team, play shall continue and the icing violation shall not be called."* The test is whether you are **below the opponent's strength**, not who is serving a penalty — so at 4-on-4 both teams can be iced, and at 6-on-4 with the power play's goalie pulled the four-skater team still cannot be.

**How to use it:**

- **Where the exemption applies, fire it the length of the ice the moment you have it.** No penalty, no faceoff, no line-change restriction. It is your single biggest structural advantage on a kill.
- **You do not need to look for a teammate.** Getting the puck 200 feet away buys a few seconds of clock and, crucially, a chance to change tired players. (*How many seconds depends on how fast they retrieve; there is no measured figure.*)
- **Aim to make the retrieval awkward** — into the corner away from their goalie's trapezoid, or hard around the boards — so they cannot regroup cleanly.
- **With two seconds left on the kill, fire it.** NHL 81.6: *"[s]hould the puck be released from the stick of the player shooting the puck down the ice prior to the penalty expiring, the icing infraction shall not apply."*

**Do not stretch "numerical strength" beyond penalties.** ⚠️ **Five skaters defending a pulled goalie are not below six, and they can be iced.** The IIHF says so outright — strength is affected only when a team is short *"due to one or more penalties that they are serving"* — and the NHL reaches the same place by counting the goalie inside its strength. Defending a 6-on-5 is in [Game Management](game_management.md).

**An expiry ends the exemption only where it leaves you level.** The returning player counts back in whether or not they have left the box — so killing a lone minor, the exemption goes when it expires. **On a 5-on-3 the first expiry leaves you 4-on-5, still below their strength, and you may still ice it.** Every book shares that head count; they differ only on the instant they judge it — the NHL, IIHF and PWHL at the penalty's expiry, Hockey Canada and CARHA (adult leagues only) *"at the instant the puck is shot"*, USA Hockey both. Do not quote Hockey Canada's note that *"[a] team will no longer be considered shorthanded once the penalty time expires"* without that test: a teammate still serving another penalty keeps you short.

**There are two carve-outs, and neither is the IIHF's** — the British position is at the end of this section.

**Carve-out 1 — the NHL's and the PWHL's empty penalty box when a major expires.** A team shorthanded by a major that has not put a player in the box *"to exit upon the expiry of the penalty"* loses the exemption: *"Icing will be called."* It bites **at the moment the major runs out, not while it is being served**, and it lasts only until the next stoppage, when they may put the player back. So four minutes into a five-minute major, keep clearing.

**Keep clearing — but seat somebody.** ⚠️ **That is not a licence to leave the box empty.** When the substitute is due is a separate question from when the icing sanction lands, and the books price it separately:

- **NHL and PWHL:** the substitute is due before the major expires. Miss it and it is a bench minor: NHL 68.2 and PWHL 70.2 treat *"any violation"* as an illegal substitution. You also lose the clear, 20.3 adds that you play a player short until the next stoppage, and a replacement who comes on from anywhere but the box is a bench minor. Where minors sit on the clock beside the major, the substitute is due **immediately**.
- **IIHF:** the substitute is due **immediately**, and failing it is a bench minor — so it can land while the major is still running, and on a kill that is a 5-on-3.
- **USA Hockey:** youth, high-school and girls' teams must seat a substitute immediately on a major plus game misconduct, and an injured player's substitute is due immediately with a bench minor behind it. **Adult teams are not asked to seat anyone** on a major plus game misconduct or a match penalty — there an empty box is the expected state.
- **Hockey Canada:** the coach names a server, or the referee does, so the seat always gets filled.
- **CARHA (CARHA-affiliated leagues only):** a substitute is required, but no moment is named and no sanction written. Treat it as unsettled and seat somebody anyway.

**One case empties the box legitimately everywhere: coincidental majors carrying game misconducts.** Substitutes go straight onto the ice, neither team is shorthanded, and there is no kill. ⚠️ **Check that the game misconducts went with the majors** — without them the players serve their own time and the box is not empty.

**Carve-out 2 — USA Hockey limits the exemption by classification, and for most North American youth readers this is the one that matters.** Rule 624(b)(1) grants it *"(For all Youth 15-Only and above, Girls 16U and above, High School and Adult classifications only)"*. **Below those levels a shorthanded team is called for icing like anyone else**, and a kill built on the long clear just feeds the power play defensive-zone draws.

**One length-of-ice shot is never icing, at any strength, in all six books: a puck sent down directly off the draw.** ⚠️ **Play it the strict way:** it is the puck your own stroke sends from the dot, not one you win to a defenceman who then clears it. USA Hockey's Casebook rules that a centre who wins the draw and shoots an instant later *has* iced it, because *"the puck must receive its impetus from the initial stick contact on the face-off."* USA Hockey's age limit sits on the shorthanded limb, not this one, so a youth team without the shorthanded exemption still has this. [Faceoffs](faceoffs.md) owns it.

> **If you play in Britain — neither carve-out applies.** IIHF 81.6 gives the exemption and the release-before-expiry rule in the NHL's words and stops there, and the In-House Rules do not amend it. The head count still applies: an expiry ends it only where it leaves you level, and on a 5-on-3 the first expiry leaves you still exempt.
>
> **The substitute is still due, and sooner.** Under the IIHF you keep your clear if the box is empty, but ⚠️ **failing to seat a substitute immediately is a bench minor — on a kill, a 5-on-3.** At U12 and U10, IHUK's junior formats change the game itself, icing included. See [UK and England Rules](../foundation/uk_rules.md).
>
> **Rec and youth note:** under USA Hockey the exemption exists only from **Youth 15-Only, Girls 16U, High School and Adult** upward, and many youth and rec leagues use **no-touch (automatic) icing**. Check your league before building a kill around it.

#### The two penalties a kill talks itself into

```facts
Rule: Shooting the puck directly out of the playing surface from your defending zone is a delay-of-game minor even while shorthanded, under NHL 63.2(iii), IIHF 63.2(III) and PWHL 65.2(iii) alike — so it binds a British kill too, and the IIHF's bench clause is wider than the NHL's
Rule: Never flip the puck out deliberately to buy a whistle — a deliberate shoot-out of the playing area is a minor from anywhere on the ice, no zone limit, in six books (NHL and IIHF 63.2(ii), PWHL 65.2(ii), USA Hockey 610(c), Hockey Canada 10.1(ii), CARHA 75(b))
Rule: The "no glass" carve-out means boards without glass, not the benches — over the glass behind the bench is penalised, only a puck straight into it is exempt (NHL 63.2(iii), PWHL 65.2(iii) and Notes; IIHF 63.2(III) says "no protective glass" and reaches glass "surrounding" the bench, wider)
Rule: A draw straight out of play is no penalty under the NHL, IIHF and PWHL "regardless as to which player may have last contacted the puck" (85.1, PWHL 87.1), yet their deliberate minor (63.2(ii), PWHL 65.2(ii)) reaches a shot out from anywhere, as in USA Hockey, Hockey Canada and CARHA — never aim it
Rule: USA Hockey writes no defending-zone limb for putting the puck out of play at all — Rule 610(c) penalises only a deliberate shoot-out, and writes it against "any player or goalkeeper" alike, while 631(d) sends an accidental one to a last-play faceoff
Rule: Hockey Canada 10.1(ii) is deliberate-only and reaches a goalie as well as a skater — but 10.1(v) is a separate minor on a goalie who "shoots or bats the puck directly out of the playing surface, with their stick" — no penalty where there is no glass, or if it touches glass or deflects
Rule: Hockey Canada 6.4(a), Junior and Senior only, where the puck was last touched in the end zone: an unintentional one is no penalty, "but the defending team may not make a line change" — most of what a kill clears for — and the goalie is excluded expressly
Rule: CARHA (adult leagues only) writes the same goalie limb as Hockey Canada — Rule 55(a), a minor on "any goalkeeper who shoots or bats the puck out of the playing surface with their stick", no deliberateness word, same glass exemptions; 75(b) is the deliberate minor on a player
Rule: Smothering the puck to force a whistle costs a kill the same two minutes — a minor on a skater other than the goalie who deliberately falls on or gathers it in (NHL 63.2(v), IIHF 63.2(V), PWHL 65.2(v), USA Hockey 614(a), Hockey Canada 10.2(a)(iv)), and a penalty shot inside your own crease
Rule: The smothering minor excludes the goalie, but that is no permission for them — NHL 63.2(vii) makes it a minor on a goalie freezing the puck in their own crease "unless he is actually being checked by an opponent", IIHF 63.2(VII) writes "they", and USA Hockey 610(b) needs no warning first
Action: Aim clears hard and flat, not over the glass — clearing the glass costs the minor, not the direction; a clear played hard off the glass and staying in the rink is exactly what you want
```

**Both cost you two minutes rather than a faceoff, and both are mistakes a tired kill makes trying to get the puck out.**

- **The first is putting it over the glass.** Aim your clears **hard and flat**, not lofted. A clear played hard **off** the glass that stays in the rink is exactly what you want, and a deflection out is fine. ⚠️ **A clean clear straight over the glass from your own zone is a delay-of-game minor even while shorthanded** under the NHL, the IIHF and the PWHL, and a second penalty is a 5-on-3. 🇬🇧 Because the IIHF writes it, it binds a British kill too. ⚠️ **Never flip it out on purpose to buy a whistle: a deliberate shoot-out is a minor from anywhere on the ice, in all six books.**
- **The second is the more expensive: smothering the puck to beg for a whistle** — set out in [Smothering the puck](#smothering-the-puck-to-force-a-whistle) below.

**What it costs — the exceptions and where the books differ.**

- **No glass, no penalty:** where a stretch of boards has no glass above it, the zone minor does not apply.
- **The benches:** a puck shot straight into the players' bench is no penalty; one shot **over** the glass behind the bench is — the IIHF says glass *"surrounding"* the bench, which reaches further. ⚠️ **Do not aim for the bench:** the IIHF Situation Handbook gives the minor for a puck that clears the glass and then drops into the bench, because what counts is where it leaves the playing surface.
- **Off a faceoff:** a draw that goes straight out of play is no penalty under the NHL, IIHF and PWHL, but their deliberate minor reaches a puck shot out from anywhere, and none says which rule wins for a draw **aimed** out. Never aim it.
- **USA Hockey and Hockey Canada write no zone limb**, so an *accidental* flip over the glass by a skater costs only possession — but a deliberate one is the minor, and it reaches a goalie as much as a skater. In Hockey Canada's Junior and Senior divisions an accidental one also costs the defending team its **line change**, which on a kill is most of what you were clearing for.
- **Goalies under Hockey Canada and CARHA have a limb of their own with no *deliberately* in it:** a goalie who shoots or bats the puck directly out of the playing surface with their stick takes a minor, unless there is no glass there or it touches the glass or deflects off a player or official first. Neither book says whether it needs intent, so ⚠️ **plan for the stricter reading and keep your goalie's clears under the glass.** [Risk Management](../hockey-iq/risk_management.md) carries the books in full.

#### Smothering the puck to force a whistle

```facts
Rule: A skater other than the goalie who deliberately falls on or gathers the puck into their body takes a minor in six books (NHL 63.2(v), IIHF 63.2(V), PWHL 65.2(v) and USA Hockey 614(a) for delay of game; Hockey Canada 10.2(a)(iv) for handling the puck; CARHA 58(a) for falling on it)
Rule: Three of the six books condition that minor — USA Hockey 614(a) reaches the act only where it is "deeming the puck unplayable and causing a stoppage of play", Hockey Canada 10.2(a)(iv) only "while standing or laying on the ice" and CARHA 58(a) only "while standing or lying on the ice"
Rule: Fall on or gather a puck in your own crease as a skater and it is a penalty shot (NHL 67.4, NHL and IIHF 63.6, PWHL 65.6, CARHA 58(c), Hockey Canada 4.11(a)(v)), and Hockey Canada 10.2(a)(v) adds where "the infraction occurs": a cover from inside the crease is the shot there, puck outside or not
Rule: USA Hockey writes that escalation as "a penalty shot/optional minor penalty" (614(b)), and 406(a) gives the election to the NON-OFFENDING team — so it never buys you the cheaper one, and electing the minor does put a second man off
Rule: The puck decides in five books, and Hockey Canada adds where you are — NHL 63.6: "If the puck is in the crease, penalty shot. If the puck is outside the crease and gathered into the body of a player (other than the goalkeeper) who is inside the crease, minor penalty"; IIHF 63.6 and PWHL 65.6 alike
Rule: With your own goalie pulled it is an awarded goal, not a shot — NHL and IIHF 67.5, PWHL 69.5, CARHA 58(c) Note 1, Hockey Canada 10.2(a)(vi) where the infraction is in the crease; USA Hockey 614(b) only where the act "has prevented an obvious and imminent goal"
Rule: Play to CARHA 58(c): with your net empty, any cover in your own crease is the goal. Its Note 1 reads flat — "If the goalkeeper has been removed from the ice, then a goal is awarded" — though the Rule 36(b) it points to needs a shot and an "obvious and imminent goal" prevented
Rule: The PWHL is a sixth book, running all three tiers at the strict end — 65.2(v) is the NHL's minor word for word, 65.6 the penalty shot on the same puck-location test, and 69.5 awards the goal flat, as NHL and IIHF 67.5 do and as USA Hockey and CARHA's Rule 36(b) do not
Rule: A goalie freezing the puck in the crease is a minor unless they are actually being checked (NHL and IIHF 63.2(vii), PWHL 65.2(vii)) or pressured (USA Hockey 610(b); Hockey Canada Interpretation 3 to 10.1(a), after a warning); in CARHA, a minor if the referee judges it an unnecessary stoppage (61(b))
Rule: CARHA 61(b) is a minor on a goalie who "deliberately holds the puck and in the opinion of the Referee, is causing an unnecessary stoppage of play"; Hockey Canada's 10.2 covers one outside their crease
Rule: The Notes to NHL and IIHF 63.2(v), PWHL 65.2(v) and USA Hockey 614(a) excuse a puck shot under a shot-blocker or lodged in their equipment, never a hand used to make the puck unplayable; NHL and IIHF 63.2(vi) and PWHL 65.2(vi) let a goalie who came out cover the save (straight off, IIHF)
Never: Smother a loose puck to buy a whistle on the kill — the best it buys is a defensive-zone draw the tired unit that wanted it must then win a man short, and the worst is a goal
Risk: The worst case is not the penalty shot — with your own net empty it is an awarded goal, and under USA Hockey the other team may take a second man off you instead of the shot
Action: Play the puck, or knock it down and play it — NHL and IIHF 67.2 let you catch it out of the air but require you to "immediately place it or knock it down to the ice"
```

**Play the puck, or knock it down and play it — never fall on it or gather it in.** A tired unit wants the whistle, and the whistle it buys is a defensive-zone draw it then has to win a man short. Every book lets you knock a puck down: the NHL and IIHF let you catch it out of the air, but you *"must immediately place it or knock it down to the ice"*, and USA Hockey stops play only if you close your hand on it and do not drop it at once. ⚠️ **The permission ends the moment you hold it.** Knocking it down in your own zone costs nothing on the hand-pass rule either: a hand pass inside your own defending zone is allowed in the NHL, USA Hockey, Hockey Canada and CARHA.

**What the rule protects is the shot-blocker, not the smother.** A puck shot under you as you block is nothing; *"any use of the hands to make the puck unplayable should be penalized promptly"*. Under USA Hockey's Casebook, a player lying in the crease with the puck shot under them draws no penalty — but *"if the player makes any action to cover the puck by gathering it into their body"*, the penalty shot or awarded goal applies.

**Do not hand the job to your goalie when nobody is near them either.** A goalie who freezes the puck in their crease to stop play takes a minor unless they are actually being checked (NHL, IIHF, PWHL) or pressured (USA Hockey; Hockey Canada after one warning), and so does one who races out to jump on a loose puck. Covering a shot they came out to stop, in front of the net, is allowed if they cover it straight off the save rather than dropping it and covering again — in the NHL, IIHF and PWHL, and in USA Hockey's Casebook when attackers are close enough to pressure them. A goalie told otherwise will let a savable rebound sit. Hockey Canada allows it in one clause and bars freezing outside the crease in another, without saying which wins — so under Hockey Canada, play it out.

**What it costs.** Three tiers, and on a kill you live a stride from the worst of them:

- **A minor**, in all six books, for a skater who deliberately falls on or gathers the puck into their body. USA Hockey reaches it only where it makes the puck unplayable and stops play; Hockey Canada and CARHA only while you are standing or lying on the ice.
- **A penalty shot if the puck is in your own crease.** In five books the puck's location decides; ⚠️ **Hockey Canada also gives the shot where the infraction is in the crease**, so play a cover from inside your crease as the shot even with the puck outside it. In five books the shot is the whole price. **Under USA Hockey the other team chooses between the shot and a minor**, so a kill already a man short can end up at 5-on-3.
- **An awarded goal if your own goalie is pulled** — flat in the NHL, IIHF and PWHL; in Hockey Canada where the infraction is in the crease; under USA Hockey only where the smother *"has prevented an obvious and imminent goal"*. CARHA writes it both ways, so ⚠️ **play to its flat answer: with your net empty, any cover in your own crease is the goal.**

#### Shot blocking

```facts
Key: Blocking shots is a technique, not an act of courage
Position: Be in the lane before the shot, not sliding into it — sliding gets you a puck off the ankle and a shooter who has already changed their angle
Never: Block from a screening position — half-deflecting a shot your goalie could not see is worse than letting it through cleanly
Action: If you cannot get properly into the lane, get out of it
Convention: How much your team blocks at all is a coaching choice, not a law — some kills want defenders collapsing and blocking aggressively, others want the lane taken away on your feet with the goalie seeing everything; find out which yours plays and ask your goalie
Key: Block from close and on your feet, head out of the lane, hands behind you with the backs of the gloves out — the full technique is in Body Contact and Battles and Defender
Never: Lift a leg, turn your back or go sideways, or lunge. Stay on your feet until the puck is actually released
Never: Lead with your stick — a puck off an outstretched blade tends to go upward, often into your face (coaching craft); keep the stick flat on the ice beside you
```

Blocking shots is a **technique**, not an act of courage. **The technique — how close to get, how to present the biggest surface, why to stay on your feet — is in [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md) and [Defender](../positions/defender.md), and this document does not set it out in full.** One part of the technique travels with everything below, because all of it puts you in a shooting lane: where your head, hands and stick go. **Block from close, head out of the lane, hands behind your body with the backs of the gloves out.** Tucking them behind your shin pads is the form for a block taken on the ice, not a standing one. **Never lift a leg, never turn your back or go sideways, and never lunge** — stay on your feet until the puck is actually released. **And block with your body, not your stick:** a puck deflected off an outstretched blade tends to go upward, often into your own face — widely taught coaching craft rather than anything measured — so keep the stick flat on the ice beside you, or out of the lane. Your stick's job on a kill is the *passing* lane, set out in [Stick lanes](#stick-lanes).

Two things are specific to the kill:

- **You are blocking on purpose, not by accident**, which means you should be **in the lane before the shot**, not sliding into it. Sliding gets you a puck off the ankle and a shooter who has already changed their angle.
- **Never block from a screening position.** Half-deflecting a shot your goalie could not see is worse than letting it through cleanly. If you cannot get properly into the lane, get out of it.

**How much your team blocks at all is a coaching choice, not a law.** Some kills want defenders collapsing and blocking aggressively in front of the goalie; others want the lane taken away on your feet, with the goalie left seeing everything. Neither is right in the abstract, and a kill that blocks less is not a kill that is being soft. Find out which yours plays, ask your coach which they want, and ask your goalie what they want — [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md) sets out the choice and the full list of when *not* to block.

#### Stick lanes

```facts
Position: Blade flat on the ice, out in front of your body, in the passing lane — not on the puck carrier
Key: Sticks in lanes deter far more passes than they intercept
Priority: Take away the seam first, and point the stick at the most dangerous option rather than at the puck
Action: Concede the perimeter pass — the puck is being handled by someone your teammate already has
Technique: Stay in a low stance with your knees bent; you cannot make a quick lateral adjustment from standing
```

The most valuable and least visible thing you do on a kill.

- **Blade flat on the ice, out in front of your body, in the passing lane** — not on the puck carrier. Sticks in lanes deter far more passes than they intercept.
- **Take away the seam first**, and point the stick at the most dangerous option rather than at the puck. The puck is being handled by someone your teammate already has; the perimeter pass is the one you are happy to concede.
- **Stay in a low stance with your knees bent.** You cannot make a quick lateral adjustment from standing.

#### The discipline not to chase

```facts
Mindset: The power play wants you to chase — perimeter possession is not a threat, taking the bait is
Read: Chase only when the puck can actually be won — a bobbled reception, a player with their back turned, a puck on the boards with no support
Never: Finish a check on a turned back — if you can see their numbers, pull up and play the puck; every book but the IIHF can make it a match penalty without the boards
Risk: If you chase and miss, you have created the seam yourself
Action: Talk constantly — call switches, call the bumper, call the backdoor
Action: Clear, then change, in that order; change when you can, not when you must, because a tired killer chases
```

**The power play wants you to chase.** Every pass around the perimeter is an invitation, and perimeter possession is not a threat — taking the bait is. **If you chase and miss, you have created the seam yourself**: four players cannot cover a five-player shape with one of them out of position.

Chase only when the puck can **actually be won** — a bobbled reception, a player with their back turned, a puck on the boards with no support. ⚠️ **A turned back tells you the puck is winnable and nothing else.** **Go for the puck, not through the man** — if you cannot get the puck without going through the player's back, you were never winning it. A hit from behind ends your game in most books and can be a match penalty in every book but the IIHF; the full price is under [Killing the entry](#killing-the-entry--what-all-of-this-is-for).

Talk constantly while you do it: most kill breakdowns are two players making different assumptions, so call switches, call the bumper, call the backdoor ([On-Ice Communication](../foundation/on_ice_communication.md)). And **change when you can, not when you must** — a tired killer chases. Clear, then change, in that order.

### Shorthanded offence

```facts
Read: Take it when the turnover handed you a genuine 2-on-1 or breakaway, not one you have to manufacture with speed
Read: Take it when the turnover happened above the puck, so their players are behind you, and you can get there before their point player recovers
Never: Take it on a 2-on-2 or worse, when your partner has not seen it, when protecting a lead late, or when you are the last player back
Priority: The default is always get the puck out, then change — the rush is the exception you take when it is free
Key: If it is a footrace you will lose it is not a rush; if you have to think about whether it is on, it is not on
```

A shorthanded rush is one of the highest-value plays in hockey and one of the easiest ways to lose a game. The difference is entirely the read.

**Take it** when all three of these hold. The turnover *handed* you a genuine odd-man rush — a 2-on-1 or a breakaway — rather than one you would have to manufacture with speed. It happened **above** the puck, so their players are behind you (a power play with four players below the puck is defenceless). And you can get there before their point player recovers. If it is a footrace you will lose, it is not a rush.

**Do not take it** if any one of these is true. It is a 2-on-2 or worse: lose the puck and you are 4-on-5 again, with two players out of the play and them entering with speed. Your partner has not seen it, so the "rush" is you alone turning it over at their line and skating 180 feet back. You are protecting a lead late (clear it and change — see [Game Management](game_management.md)). Or you are the last player back. That last one is obvious and it still happens every week.

**The default is always: get the puck out, then change.** The rush is the exception you take when it is free. If you have to think about whether it is on, it is not on. Fuller treatment of the trade-off in [Risk Management](../hockey-iq/risk_management.md).

## BOTH

### 4-on-4 and 3-on-3

#### 4-on-4

```facts
Key: Each player has a quarter more ice, and the extra room is mostly in the middle, so skill and skating matter more and structure matters less
Risk: With only two forwards back you have one fewer support layer, and a 2-on-1 arrives faster and with more room
Never: Activate as a defenceman more than you would at even strength — being caught is an immediate odd-man rush the other way
Action: Change on possession in the offensive zone or after a clear; a change with the puck live hands them a rush against three
Action: Support the puck with short options and quick movement — do not spread out, exactly as at 5-on-5
```

Coincidental penalties (below) put both teams at four skaters **under NHL rules**; under the IIHF book they bring immediate substitution and the teams stay five a side, so a British 4-on-4 comes from a minor against a major instead. Two fewer bodies on the same sheet changes the game more than the numbers suggest: each player has **a quarter more ice**, and the extra room is mostly in the middle, so skill and skating matter more and structure matters less.

- **You have one fewer support layer defensively.** With only two forwards back, the backchecking centre's middle-lane job (see [Centre](../positions/center.md)) becomes everyone's problem, and a 2-on-1 arrives faster and with more room.
- **Defencemen should activate less, not more.** The open ice tempts you into the rush; the punishment for being caught is an immediate odd-man rush the other way.
- **Change with extreme care** — a change with the puck live at 4-on-4 hands them a rush against three. Change on possession in the offensive zone, or after a clear.
- **Support the puck, do not spread out.** The instinct in open ice is to stretch; the right play is short support options and quick movement, exactly as at 5-on-5.

#### 3-on-3 overtime

```facts
Rule: NHL regular-season overtime is five minutes of 3-on-3 sudden death then a shootout; playoff overtime is 20-minute periods at 5-on-5 (Rules 84.1, 84.4, 84.5)
Priority: Possession is the whole game — you cannot be scored on while you have the puck
Action: Carry the puck back out to the neutral zone and reset rather than forcing a low-percentage attack; the regroup is legal and standard practice
Read: Attack only with a numerical advantage — a 2-on-1 is nearly a goal, a 1-on-1 is nothing
Action: Isolate a defender in space and go at them, but do not chase the puck as a group
Never: Change on the fly with the puck loose. Change by holding possession instead, often passing back to the goalie, who retains and replays it rather than freezing it
Rule: A goalie freezing the puck in the crease is a minor unless they are actually being checked (NHL and IIHF 63.2(vii), PWHL 65.2(vii)) or pressured (USA Hockey 610(b); Hockey Canada Interpretation 3 to 10.1(a), after a warning); in CARHA, a minor if the referee judges it an unnecessary stoppage (61(b))
Rule: A penalty in overtime makes it 4-on-3, not 3-on-2 — the non-offending team adds a skater (NHL Rule 84.3, matched by IIHF 84.3)
Rule: A goalie pulled for an extra attacker in regular-season overtime must wait for a stoppage to return and cannot change on the fly (NHL Rule 84.2, PWHL 86.2); IIHF 84.2 sets no such restriction
Rule: IIHF overtime is 3-on-3 throughout — five minutes in preliminary games and ten in playoff and bronze medal games, with no 5-on-5 playoff format (IIHF Rules 84.1, 84.5)
Rule: Under the In-House Rules overtime is played only in NIHL, and in SNL where its regulations provide; NIHL National is five minutes of 3-on-3 then three shooters a side (Rule 84)
```

NHL regular-season overtime is **five minutes of 3-on-3, sudden death**, followed by a shootout if nobody scores (Rule 84.1, 84.4). **Playoff overtime is full 20-minute periods at 5-on-5** — the 3-on-3 format does not apply (Rule 84.5).

**How the game changes:** there is no forecheck worth the name, no cycle, and nowhere to hide. Everything is possession and odd-man rushes.

**Strategy that actually works:**

- **Possession is the whole game.** You cannot be scored on while you have the puck. The format has shifted from run-and-gun to a deliberate, possession-first approach precisely because of this.
- **The regroup is a weapon.** Carrying the puck back out to the neutral zone to reset, rather than forcing a low-percentage attack, is standard practice at NHL level. The League's general managers discussed limiting it in November 2023 (Dan Rosen, NHL.com, summarising Colin Campbell), but discussion is not adoption: the NHL's 2025-2026 rules contain no restriction, so the regroup is legal.
- **Attack only with a numerical advantage.** A 2-on-1 in 3-on-3 is nearly a goal. A 1-on-1 is nothing. Wait for the extra man rather than driving into a set defender.
- **Never change on the fly with the puck loose.** A bad change at 3-on-3 is a breakaway. Teams change by holding possession — often passing back to the goalie, who **retains and replays** it: settles it, keeps it live, and moves it back out once the change is made. ⚠️ **Not freezes it.** A goalie who covers the puck to force a stoppage when they are not being checked (or, under the IIHF and USA Hockey, pressured) takes a minor without warning under the NHL (63.2(vii), 67.3), the IIHF (63.2(VII), 67.3) and USA Hockey (610(b)), and under Hockey Canada after one warning (Interpretation 3 to Rule 10.1(a), clause iii). The stoppage hands the other team the draw and the reset anyway. **Pass it back to your goalie to be replayed, not to be caught.**
- **Isolate and attack.** With one defender to beat, the point is to get a defender alone in space and go at them. Support arrives too slowly to help them.
- **Do not chase the puck as a group.** Three players chasing is nobody home.

**Penalties in overtime** (Rule 84.3), which are worth knowing because they decide games:

- A penalty in 3-on-3 overtime makes it **4-on-3**, not 3-on-2 — the non-offending team adds a skater. The three-skater floor from the 5-on-3 section above is what forces this.
- If **both** teams take a minor at the same stoppage with nothing else on the clock, play **continues 3-on-3**.
- A two-man advantage in overtime is played **5-on-3**: the offending team stays at three and the other team goes to five.
- Carry-over: 5-on-3 at the end of regulation starts overtime 5-on-3; 4-on-4 at the end of regulation starts overtime **3-on-3**.
- **Pulled goalies in regular-season overtime cannot change on the fly.** Rule 84.2: once removed for an extra attacker, the goalie "must wait for the next stoppage of play before returning to his position. He cannot change 'on the fly.' If he does, a bench minor penalty shall be assessed for having an ineligible player." **Only the NHL and the PWHL (86.2) write it.** IIHF Rule 84.2 is a single sentence — *"A team shall be allowed to pull its goalkeeper in favor of an additional skater in the overtime period"* — and attaches no return restriction and no bench minor.

#### Overtime in Britain

**Check whether you get overtime at all, and then which one.**

**The five-on-the-clock rules above are yours.** IIHF Rule 84.3 is materially identical to NHL 84.3: a penalty makes it **4-on-3**, coincidental minors with nothing else on the clock continue **3-on-3**, a two-man advantage is played **5-on-3**, and the carry-over ladder comes to the same thing. Both books write in the three-skater floor: the IIHF's *"[a]t no time will a team have less than three"* skaters on the ice, the NHL's *"[a]t no time will a team have less than three players on the ice."*

**The format is not.** IIHF overtime is 3-on-3 all the way up: **five minutes** in a round-robin or preliminary-round game (84.1) and **ten minutes** in a playoff, relegation, classification or bronze medal game (84.5), each *"with each team at a numerical strength of three"* skaters and one goalie. **There is no IIHF equivalent of the NHL's 20-minute 5-on-5 playoff overtime — but the Elite League writes its own.** Its *Motorpoint EIHL Casebook 26-27* keeps five minutes of 3-on-3 for the regular season and the Challenge Cup group stage. In a Challenge Cup elimination game, a playoff semi-final, and the second leg of a two-legged playoff quarter-final or Challenge Cup semi-final when the aggregate score is tied, overtime opens with a 20-minute period at 5-on-5, then 3-on-3 periods until somebody scores. The first leg has no overtime. Both finals play on in 20-minute sudden-death periods, and the bronze-medal game has no overtime at all and is decided in a shootout (clause (5)). So in an Elite League overtime that opens at 5-on-5, a penalty in that first period is an ordinary 5-on-4, not a 4-on-3.

**Below the Elite League, the IHUK In-House Rules amend IIHF Rule 84 — overtime — on top of that.** Overtime *"is only played in NIHL fixtures and in SNL fixtures where specifically provided for in competition regulations"* — in most English and Scottish hockey a tied game simply ends tied, and none of this section applies. Where it is played, NIHL National league and group-stage playoff games use **five minutes of 3-on-3**, then a game-winning-shots competition with **three shooters per team**; the NIHL National playoff semi-finals use **20 minutes of 5-on-5** and the Final successive 20-minute 5-on-5 sudden-death periods with no shootout. Two details that catch people out in the rest period: teams **do not change ends** for overtime, and **penalised players must stay in the box** while goalies may go to the bench. The full amendment list is in [UK and England Rules](../foundation/uk_rules.md); your league's own competition regulations are above both.

### Coincidental penalties, expiring penalties and 6-on-4

#### Coincidental penalties

```facts
Rule: One minor to each team at the same stoppage, with nothing else on the clocks, is served without substitution and both teams play 4-on-4 (NHL Rule 19.1)
Rule: Under IIHF Rule 19.1 coincidental minors bring immediate substitution and the teams stay five a side — Britain reaches 4-on-4 by a minor against a major at the same stoppage (Appendix IV Table 13, Table 15 in 2025/26), just not by this one
Rule: But not late — a minor against a major at the same stoppage is 5-on-4 under IIHF 19.3 in the last five minutes of regulation and at any time in overtime, the differential served as a major. NHL 19.4 is the same rule
Rule: If at least one team was already shorthanded, substitution is immediate and the on-ice strength does not change (NHL Rule 19.1)
Rule: Coincidental minors make neither team shorthanded, so a goal scored during 4-on-4 releases nobody from the box (NHL Rule 16.2)
Rule: Where several penalties are assessed at once, equal majors cancel first, then equal minors, and any remaining differential is served normally (NHL Rule 19.5)
Convention: Rec and minor-hockey leagues frequently differ — several substitute immediately so both teams stay at five; check your league
```

Under **NHL Rule 19.1**, when one minor is assessed to a player from each team at the same stoppage, **with nothing else on the penalty clocks**, the penalties are served **without substitution** and both teams play **4-on-4** for two minutes. 🇬🇧 **The *coincidental-minors* 4-on-4 is the NHL's and the PWHL's (19.1), not the IIHF's.** IIHF Rule 19.1 requires *"immediate substitution"* for an equal number of minors. The IIHF **Situation Handbook**, its book of worked interpretations, confirms it at **Situation 84.10**, on two coincidental roughing minors late in the third: *"Since the penalties are coincidental, the penalties are not served on the clock and the teams are playing 5 – 5."* Britain still reaches 4-on-4 by other routes at the same stoppage — a minor to one team against a major to the other does it (IIHF Appendix IV Table 13, Table 15 in 2025/26, examples 18-20 and 32 word for word in both) — so the tactical material below still applies. It is only this trigger that differs.

**The minor-against-major route to 4-on-4 closes in the last five minutes of regulation and at any point in overtime.** **IIHF Rule 19.3** is headed *"Last Five Minutes and Overtime"*, and it takes exactly that pair — a minor or double minor to one team, a major to the other, at the same stoppage — and directs that the three-minute (or one-minute) differential *"shall be served immediately as a major penalty."* The Appendix IV table's own example 19, the same number in both editions, answers the pair twice: normally the teams play 4-on-4, but late *"Team B will play short-handed for three minutes and it will be served in the same manner as a major penalty."* ⚠️ So the strength is **5-on-4 for three minutes**, not 4-on-4, and the team that took the major must place *"the penalized Player or replacement Player in the penalty box immediately."* **NHL Rule 19.4 is the same rule in materially the same words, so this is not a British departure.** Two details in the NHL's do differ: it reaches a **match** penalty as well as a major, and for a major it wants the replacement in the box *"prior to expiration of the penalty"* rather than immediately.

- **If at least one team was already shorthanded**, the rule works differently: immediate substitution is made for the matching penalties, so the on-ice strength does not change, and those players wait for the **first stoppage after their time expires** to come out.
- **Coincidental minors do not make either team "shorthanded"** (Rule 16.2). This matters: a goal scored during 4-on-4 does **not** release anybody from the box.
- Where multiple penalties are assessed at once, referees cancel out equal numbers of majors first and then equal numbers of minors, and any remaining time differential is served normally (Rule 19.5).
- **Rec and minor-hockey leagues frequently differ here** — several play coincidental minors with immediate substitution so both teams stay at five. Check your league.

#### Penalties expiring

```facts
Rule: A goal against a shorthanded team ends the minor with the least time remaining — minors and bench minors only, not majors, and not on a penalty shot (NHL Rule 16.2)
Action: On the power play, know the clock — the last ten seconds are your best chance, because the kill's concentration dips
Read: On the kill, the player coming out of the box is a fresh attacker arriving from the side at full speed with nobody assigned to them
Action: Whoever is nearest calls "man coming out!" and picks them up
Action: If you are the player leaving the box, sprint — you are the most dangerous player on the ice for about three seconds
Technique: While you are in the box, watch the play rather than the clock, and come out facing the ice
```

The seconds around a penalty ending are among the most dangerous in hockey, in both directions.

- **A goal against a shorthanded team ends the minor with the least time remaining** (Rule 16.2). Minors and bench minors only — **not majors**, and **not when the goal comes on a penalty shot**.
- **On the power play, know the clock.** The last ten seconds are your best chance: the kill is watching the clock too, and their concentration dips.
- **On the kill, the player coming out of the box is a fresh attacker** arriving from the side of the rink at full speed with nobody assigned to them. Whoever is nearest must call it — "man coming out!" is one of the most useful things anyone says on a rink — and pick them up. If *you* are the player leaving the box, sprint; you are the most dangerous player on the ice for about three seconds. While you are in there, **watch the play, not the clock**, and come out facing the ice.

#### 6-on-4

```facts
Key: Pulling the goalie on a power play makes it 6-on-4, the largest advantage in hockey, and it is used far less than the arithmetic suggests
Position: As the power play, put two at the net front and send the extra body to the bumper or the backdoor, never the perimeter
Action: Move the puck quickly and accept that blocking lanes will be crowded
Countered by: The empty net — a killer who wins a puck can legally clear it 180 feet into it, and it counts
Action: As the kill, block shots from in the lane, on your feet, and never from a screening position; keep sticks in lanes and clear with your head up looking for the empty net, and clear low
Rule: Over the glass from your own zone is still a delay-of-game minor, shorthanded or not, under NHL 63.2(iii), IIHF 63.2(III) and PWHL 65.2(iii) alike; USA Hockey 610(c), Hockey Canada 10.1(ii) and CARHA 75(b) need it deliberate, but all three reach a goalie as much as a skater
Rule: The deliberate limb has no zone limit in any of the six books — flip the puck out on purpose anywhere on the ice and it is a minor (NHL and IIHF 63.2(ii), PWHL 65.2(ii), USA Hockey 610(c), Hockey Canada 10.1(ii), CARHA 75(b))
Rule: Hockey Canada 10.1(v) and CARHA 55(a) each put a minor on a goalie alone who shoots or bats the puck directly out of the playing surface with the stick, no deliberateness word; both exempt a no-glass spot, or a puck that touches glass or deflects off a player or official
Rule: On a signalled delayed penalty the non-offending team pulls its goalie for 6-on-5, and play continues until the penalised team gains control (NHL Rule 15.1)
Risk: In that window the danger is not a shot against — it is your own player shooting the puck into your own empty net
```

Pulling the goalie during a power play, late in a game, to make it 6-on-4.

- **It is the largest advantage in hockey** and is used far less than the arithmetic suggests: two extra attackers against four killers means genuine 2-on-1s everywhere and a kill that cannot pressure at all. **The kill's counter is the empty net**, and it is a real one — a killer who wins a puck can clear it 180 feet into that net legally, because a puck that enters the net is a goal regardless — and where the shorthanded icing exemption applies, a clear that misses is not icing either.
- **As the power play:** put two at the net front and send the extra body where it forces a decision — the bumper or the backdoor, never the perimeter. Move the puck quickly and accept that blocking lanes will be crowded.
- **As the kill:** shot blocking and stick lanes matter more than ever. Block from in the lane before the shot, on your feet, never from a screening position — [Shot blocking](#shot-blocking) covers it. Clear with your head up looking for the empty net, and **keep it low**. ⚠️ **Over the glass from your own zone is still a minor under the NHL, IIHF and PWHL, meant or not.** Under USA Hockey, Hockey Canada and CARHA it must be deliberate, but that reaches a goalie as much as a skater — and Hockey Canada and CARHA give the goalie their own minor with no *deliberately* in it. Keep every clear under the glass.
- **A related situation:** on a **delayed penalty in the Rule 15.1 sense** — the referee's arm is up, the infraction has been signalled, and the offending team does not yet have the puck — the non-offending team pulls its goalie for a 6-on-5. Play continues until the team to be penalised **gains control of the puck** (Rule 15.1), so the risk is not a shot against; it is your own player shooting the puck into your own empty net. See [Breakouts](breakouts.md) for how to play the puck in that window.

### Faceoffs on special teams

**In short:** Agree where the draw is going before you line up, and keep your wingers still until the puck drops.

```facts
Goal: With a man advantage, winning the draw skips the zone entry entirely; a skater short, losing it means defending a set power play from the first second
Rule: The team awarded the power play chooses which end-zone dot the draw starts at (NHL Rule 76.2) — pick the side that suits your one-timer
Rule: After an icing the other team picks the dot, and the icing team can't call a timeout or change lines bar an injury, a goalie's return or a penalty changing strength — NHL and IIHF 81.2, 81.4, 87.1; PWHL 83.2, 83.4, 89.1; Hockey Canada 6.4(b) the dot, Junior and Senior. USA Hockey imposes none
Never: Fire a loose puck the length of the ice while on the power play — under the NHL, IIHF and PWHL that hands the kill the choice of dot and freezes your tired unit; every book stops play in your end
Rule: Encroachment ejects your centre from the dot, not the encroaching player, in all six books (NHL 76.6); a second violation on the same draw is a bench minor under NHL and IIHF 76.7 and PWHL 78.7, a minor on the offender under USA Hockey 613(d), discretionary under CARHA 57(d), adult leagues only
Rule: A second violation on the same draw is a discretionary minor under Hockey Canada 6.2(e) — mandatory on a Junior draw after an icing (6.5(b)), and where the offender then contacts the Linesperson before the Linesperson could leave the dot, so let the official clear before you move
Rule: The IIHF 2025/26 book made a teammate's encroachment a warning, not an ejection; the 2026/27 book ejects the draw-taker as the NHL does (76.6) and makes helmet-to-helmet contact at the dot a faceoff violation of its own where the older book warned (76.7(III))
Rule: In Britain you lose your centre either way — the IHUK In-House Rules 2026-27 replace them "for all violations" bar post-icing draws (Section 10, Rule 76), and an Elite League draw, which those rules never name, sits on IIHF 2026/27 76.6, which ejects the centre anyway
Rule: Physical contact at the dot by a player facing off is a minor on its own, and a man short that is the 5-on-3 with no second violation needed — mandatory under USA Hockey 613(e), Hockey Canada 6.2(c) and CARHA 57(b), adult leagues only, discretionary only under NHL and IIHF 76.7 and PWHL 78.7
Rule: Home last change is forfeited after your own icing — the team in violation may not substitute before the next faceoff bar an injury, a goalie's return or a penalty changing strength (NHL 81.4, 82.1), which USA Hockey lacks and Hockey Canada 6.7(d) runs in U18AAA, Junior and optionally Senior
Rule: IIHF 81.2, 81.4 and 87.1 impose all three icing consequences in the NHL's words, so this is the UK position — bar one extra 81.4 exception, "a Player with a broken skate", which its own 82.1 omits; a substitution sent back is a warning, or a bench minor if your bench has already had one (82.2(V))
Action: Agree the play before you line up — where the win is going, or whether you are tying up for a winger to sweep, and where the clear goes
```

Faceoffs matter disproportionately when the numbers are uneven: **with a man advantage, winning the draw means you skip the zone entry entirely** — the hardest part of the power play, gone. A skater short, losing the draw means defending a set power play from the first second. Technique, alignment and the set plays themselves are in [Faceoffs](faceoffs.md); what follows is only the special-teams-specific rules layer, and it is the part almost nobody knows.

**What to actually do:** agree the play *before* you line up — on the power play, whether you are winning it back to the point, to the half-wall, or forward; on the kill, whether you are winning it clean for a clear or tying up for a winger to sweep, and where that clear is going.

**The rules that give you an edge here — check them against your own league:**

- **The team awarded the power play chooses which end-zone dot the draw takes place at** to start the power play (Rule 76.2). Pick the side that suits your one-timer, not the side the puck happened to be on.
- **Never ice the puck on the power play.** Under the NHL, IIHF and PWHL, after an icing the *attacking* team picks the dot — and when the power play ices it, that is the **kill**. Under the NHL, the IIHF and the PWHL your tired unit also **cannot change lines** (bar an injury, a goalie coming back on for the extra skater or a penalty that changes strength) **or take a timeout**, so you have handed yourself a defensive-zone draw a man up. 🇬🇧 Under the IIHF a bench that tries to change anyway is sent back with a warning, and a team already warned takes a bench minor. USA Hockey imposes neither ban, so there it costs you only the draw. (The kill itself cannot *be called* for icing, subject to the two carve-outs above.)
- **Keep your wingers off the hash marks until the drop.** In all six books encroachment gets **your centre** thrown out of the circle, not the winger — your best draw-taker, on the most important faceoff of the shift. (The NHL, IIHF and PWHL spare them on the first violation after an icing; so does Hockey Canada in Junior.) **A second violation by the same team on the same draw** is an automatic penalty in four books — a bench minor under the NHL, the IIHF and the PWHL, a minor on the offender under USA Hockey — and the referee's call under Hockey Canada and CARHA. ⚠️ **On a kill, any of those is a 5-on-3.** Hockey Canada makes it mandatory on a Junior draw after an icing, and where the offender then bumps the linesperson before they can leave the dot — **so let the official clear before you move.** [Faceoffs](faceoffs.md) sets the split out in full.

  **If you were taught the IIHF's old answer, unlearn it:** the 2025/26 book gave a warning for encroachment and left your centre on the dot; the 2026/27 book ejects them as the NHL does. **In Britain you lose your centre either way.** Below the Elite League, the IHUK In-House Rules 2026-27 replace the centre *"for all violations"* except on draws after an icing; an Elite League draw sits on the IIHF 2026/27 book, which ejects them too and spares only the first violation after an icing. A second violation is still a bench minor, post-icing draws included. ⚠️ **Read an old IIHF warning as a counted violation, not a free one.** (Only some IIHF rules, listed in the Sources, have been read in the 2026/27 edition; the rest of this document's IIHF citations rest on the 2025/26 book.)

  **Contact at the dot is a quicker route to a 5-on-3.** A centre who makes physical contact with their opponent, by body or stick, before the draw is completed takes a minor on their own — no second violation needed. ⚠️ **It is mandatory under USA Hockey, Hockey Canada and CARHA**, and the referee's call only under the NHL, the IIHF and the PWHL, though those three also have your centre replaced for it (the IIHF since 2026/27) — so plan the draw as though contact is an automatic minor. Since 2026/27 the IIHF also counts helmet-to-helmet contact between the centres as a faceoff violation, against both if the linesperson cannot tell who started it. **Win the draw with your stick, not by leaning on theirs.**

  Related, and the same in both books: do not crowd the scrum after a penalty is called; players coming into the attacking zone beyond the outer edge of the end-zone circle during a gathering move the draw out to the neutral zone (Rule 76.2), throwing away your offensive-zone start for nothing.
- **The home team has last change — but not after its own icing.** Under the NHL, the IIHF and the PWHL the team that iced it cannot change, home or away (bar an injury, a goalie coming back on for the extra skater or a penalty that changes strength), and the other team can — so when the *opponent* ices it you get the free matchup. Under USA Hockey an icing costs you only the draw, taken in your own end on the side the puck was shot from. CARHA writes no bar either, but tells referees to be strict with *"a SERIES OF ICINGS"*. Hockey Canada runs the no-change bar only in U18AAA and Junior, and in Senior at the Member's option — and even there allows the timeout, without a change. See [Faceoffs](faceoffs.md).

### The goalie's role on both

```facts
Action: On the kill, tell your goalie what you are conceding — a kill that never says this makes its goalie guess
Action: On the kill, agree the retrieval before it starts; they can stop a rim behind the net and leave it stationary for you
Rule: NHL and IIHF goalies may play the puck behind the goal line only inside the trapezoid (NHL 27.8, IIHF 27.7), bar skate contact with the crease; KHL reportedly too, unverified. USA Hockey, Hockey Canada and CARHA write none; PWHL goalies play as if it binds: 1.8 drops it, 65.2(viii) keeps the minor
Rule: No trapezoid is no licence for your goalie either — USA Hockey 614(c) still makes freezing the puck behind the goal line from outside the crease a minor, and Hockey Canada Interpretation 3 to Rule 10.1(a), clause v, gives a minor with "No warning will be issued" for freezing it outside the crease
Rule: Out of the crease under Hockey Canada, have your goalie play it — clause ii of Interpretation 3 to 10.1(a) permits a cover after a cut-the-angle save, but clause v and Rule 10.2's goalie paragraph bar a freeze outside the crease, and the book never says which governs
Rule: The goalkeeper's restricted area is IIHF Rule 27.7. Nothing in the Elite League's Casebook switches it off, and England Ice Hockey's Rules & Regulations 22.3 call it "not currently enforced", scope and standing unsettled — so in Britain play as though it binds: keep your goalie inside it
Rule: England Ice Hockey 22.3, whose standing and reach are unsettled, touches only the Restricted Area — IIHF Rule 63.2(I) is a separate minor for a goalie who freezes the puck to cause a stoppage anywhere "outside of their goal crease area"; play as though the trapezoid binds your goalie
Rule: IIHF 63.2(I)'s freeze minor has a carve-out: 63.2(VI)'s Note makes it legal for your goalie to come out, cut down the angle and cover the puck straight off the save; separately, 63.2(VII) makes a freeze in the crease a minor unless the goalie is "actually being checked by an opponent"
Read: Play it as though it binds — your goalie stays inside the trapezoid, so a rim that dies in the corner is yours to fetch, not theirs
Action: On the power play, use them as a reset on a regroup and as your safety on a failed entry
```

Your goalie is a full participant in both special teams, not a bystander. **The goalie's own game — depth, tracking, save selection under a screen — is in [Goaltender](../positions/goaltender.md).** Three things you have to do about it, and a fourth — where the line is at *their* net — is the section below:

- **On the kill, tell them what you are conceding.** If the plan is to concede point shots, they will set their depth for a shot through traffic instead of holding for a seam pass. A kill that never says this makes its goalie guess.
- **On the kill, agree the retrieval before it starts.** In the NHL and IIHF your goalie may play the puck behind the goal line only inside the trapezoid, but can still stop a rim behind the net and leave it for you. **USA Hockey, Hockey Canada and CARHA write no trapezoid**, so there your goalie is a third puck-handler. On a PWHL kill, play as if the trapezoid binds — the PWHL switches it off but still writes the minor. ⚠️ **Your goalie may move a retrieved puck on, not sit on it** — freezing it out there is a minor under USA Hockey, Hockey Canada and CARHA, with no warning under Hockey Canada. (Coming out in front of the net to make a save and covering it at once is different — allowed in the NHL, IIHF and PWHL and, in USA Hockey's Casebook, when attackers are close enough to pressure them; under Hockey Canada unsettled, so have them play it.) **🇬🇧 In Britain, play as though the trapezoid binds:** the IIHF and the Elite League carry it, and even where England Ice Hockey calls it *"not currently enforced"* (its scope and standing are unsettled), freezing the puck outside the crease is still a minor. A rim that dies in the corner is yours to fetch. See [Breakouts](breakouts.md).
- **On the power play, use them as a reset.** On a 5-on-4 regroup, passing back to the goalie is a legitimate way to buy a couple of seconds and change the angle of attack. They are also your safety on a failed entry, which is another reason not to force one.

### Against their goalie: where the net-front line is

**In short:** Screen from outside the crease with both feet off its red line, play the puck not the goalie, and stay out of their way home.

```facts
Rule: NHL and IIHF 69.1 (PWHL 71.1) let a goal come off only where an attacker's position or contact impairs the goalie's ability to move freely in the crease, or the attacker deliberately contacts the goalie in or out of the crease
Rule: USA Hockey 625(b) is a flat position bar — no goal with an attacker in the crease unless the puck got there first, a defender put them there, or the goalie is out of the crease area; its Casebook (625, Situation 9) disallows it, with a penalty, if you stop a returning goalie making a play
Rule: The shoved-into-the-crease relief answers only for your position — USA Hockey 625(a)(8) is still a minor for physical contact, by stick or body, that interferes with the goalie's movement, so it is no licence to act once you are there
Rule: NHL and IIHF 56.1, Hockey Canada's Glossary and CARHA 66(a) Note 1 put a goalie outside "possession", so blocking a returning goalie's path can be interference without contact. Stay out of the road home
Rule: Only the NHL, IIHF and PWHL write a clause for a goalie skating home, and it is narrow — 69.4 and 71.4 protect one who has already played the puck out of the crease, and say "may". USA Hockey reaches it by Casebook; Hockey Canada none, its 8.5 needing actual physical contact. Stay clear anyway
Rule: The rebound carve-out permits incidental contact only — push the goalie into the net together with the puck after a stop and the goal is disallowed with "appropriate penalties", unless a defending player fouled you into them, when it can be permitted (NHL and IIHF 69.7, 78.5(ix); PWHL 71.7)
Rule: Hockey Canada 8.5 is not flat — a teammate's goal from outside the crease stands if the net-front player stays passive, and is disallowed if they play the puck, interfere with the play or obstruct the goalie's view or movements
Rule: A screen from outside the crease is legal, and the NHL, PWHL and IIHF tables allow a goal off a screen set outside the crease; contact you start costs it anywhere under Hockey Canada 8.5, CARHA 66(b) and USA Hockey 617(c)(4)
Never: Put a skate in the blue paint or on the red line marking it. The paint risks the goal in the NHL, IIHF, PWHL, USA Hockey, Hockey Canada and CARHA; the line is where they differ — keep both feet clear
Rule: In EIH and SIHA competition there is no video review of a goaltender-interference call and no coach's challenge, so the referee's on-ice judgement is final (In-House Rules, Rules 37, 38, 69.1)
Rule: An Elite League game adds three criteria to IIHF 69.1 — can the goalie still play their position and recover, did the defence cause the contact, and was it inside the crease, where "[c]ontact in the blue paint area calls for disallowing a goal" (EIHL Casebook, Rule 69)
Action: Give the referee nothing to interpret at the net front in any British game — EIH and SIHA have no appeal from a live judgement, and an Elite League review is limited to the technology on hand
```

**Where the net-front line actually is.** Screen from **outside** the crease, in the goalie's sightline, without touching them — it is the most valuable thing a net-front player does, and it is legal. Keep both feet off the red crease line, play the puck and not the goalie in a scramble, and get out of the road of a goalie skating back to their net. ⚠️ **Never touch the goalie to make room:** in every book, contact you start can cost the goal and a penalty, inside the crease or out.

Under the NHL, IIHF and PWHL rule, a goal can come off the board where an attacker, *"either by his positioning or by contact"*, impairs the goalie's ability to move freely in their crease or defend their goal — and deliberate contact costs the goal inside or outside the crease. Their tables allow a goal scored with an attacker screening from outside the crease. **Under Hockey Canada, CARHA and USA Hockey, contact you make with the goalie costs the goal wherever it happens** — so screen without touching them.

**The line itself.** The IIHF, USA Hockey, Hockey Canada and CARHA all count the red line as part of the crease; the NHL and the PWHL do not say, and their tables (and the IIHF's) allow a goal with an attacker planted on the line. **No book resolves that, so stand a boot-width further out**, which satisfies every reading.

**Contact outside the crease.** The NHL and IIHF allow incidental contact only while the goalie is *playing the puck* outside their crease and you have made *"a reasonable effort"* to avoid it. ⚠️ **A goalie who is not playing the puck — set at the post, tracking a point shot, skating home — is not fair game:** *"The appropriate penalty should be assessed in every case where an attacking player makes unnecessary contact with the goalkeeper."* **On a power play that penalty takes the advantage with it.** Under USA Hockey even accidental contact with a goalie is penalised, as interference.

**In a rebound scramble,** incidental contact while you both go for a loose puck is allowed by the NHL, IIHF and PWHL; USA Hockey, Hockey Canada and CARHA write no such carve-out. ⚠️ **Push the goalie into the net with the puck after a stop and the goal is disallowed, with penalties if they apply** — unless a defender fouled you into the goalie. **Play the puck, not the goalie.**

**Stay out of the road home.** ⚠️ **Do not block the path of a goalie skating back to their crease, even without touching them.** Under USA Hockey's Casebook, interfering with a goalie trying to get back costs the goal **and** an interference penalty; the NHL and IIHF can penalise an attacker who deliberately keeps a goalie who has played the puck out from returning. Whether merely standing still in that lane is an offence is not settled in any book — a reason to be out of the lane, not a licence to stand in it.

**USA Hockey's crease rule is stricter in another way.** No goal may be scored with an attacker in the crease, whoever shoots, unless the puck got there first, a defender's foul put you there, or the goalie is out of the crease area. ⚠️ **And a body that settles in the crease while your team has the puck in the zone and the goalie is touching their crease stops play for a neutral-zone faceoff** — on a power play, the whistle *is* the punishment. A stick passing momentarily through the paint will not cost you the zone, but under the Casebook it will still cost you the goal.

**Hockey Canada and CARHA bar standing in the crease too**, with one exception in Hockey Canada: a teammate's goal from outside the crease stands if the player in the crease stays passive — does not play the puck, interfere with the play, or obstruct the goalie's view or movements. **Feet out of the blue under every book; only the reason differs.** See [Rules Primer](../foundation/rules_primer.md) for the full comparison.

**In Britain, play the net front as though the live call is the only one.** Below the Elite League, the In-House Rules remove video review and the coach's challenge in EIH and SIHA competition, so the referee's on-ice judgement is final. The Elite League keeps both, limited to the technology on hand, and its Casebook lists three things the referee weighs: can the goalie still play their position and recover, did a defender cause the contact, and was it inside the crease — *"Contact in the blue paint area calls for disallowing a goal."* See [UK and England Rules](../foundation/uk_rules.md).

### What contact with their goaltender costs *you* — the penalty, not the goal

**In short:** Never start contact with a goalie to make room, and if contact is coming, be the player who visibly tried to avoid it.

```facts
Key: Everything above decides whether the goal counts; this decides whether you are still in the game — and on a power play a misconduct outlasts your advantage whatever length it runs
Rule: USA Hockey has no bare minor for charging a goalie — 607(c) is "[a] minor plus a misconduct or a major plus a game misconduct" for checking or charging one in the crease or privileged area, 607(b) mandates a major plus ejection on any reckless charge anywhere — plan on a match too (602(a))
Rule: The privileged area is not the crease — USA Hockey 607(d) Note 2 runs it from the end-zone faceoff spots perpendicular to the end boards, and Casebook 607 Situation 6 confirms it "extends to the closer end boards", so it takes in the net front and the ice behind the net
Rule: USA Hockey Rule 607(d) Note 1 decides which rule you are under — accidental or unavoidable contact stays interference, a 625(a)(8) minor; deliberate body contact or a check delivered to the goalie is charging
Rule: USA Hockey Casebook 607 Situation 4 has three rungs, and the top one is not the default — no penalty only if a defender forced you in and you "clearly made every attempt to avoid the contact"; minor plus misconduct on an honest attempt; major plus game misconduct or match if you accelerate through
Rule: NHL and IIHF goalie-charge tiers are discretionary — NHL 42.2 to 42.4 minor, major, match; IIHF 42.2 to 42.4 minor, major, major plus game misconduct — except NHL 21.1 makes the match mandatory for deliberate injury or an attempt, and 42.5 a game misconduct on a major injuring face or head
Rule: Mandatory ejection: Hockey Canada 8.5(b) for any goalie charge or injuring interference, 8.5(c) a match on intent to injure; CARHA 52(b) for a crease charge or injury, 30(a) any such major — 66(b)'s deliberate-contact major is discretionary; NHL and PWHL 42.5 if a major injures face or head
Rule: USA Hockey keys its mandatory ejection to violence, not location — 607(b)'s major plus game misconduct "shall be assessed to any player who recklessly endangers an opponent as a result of charging", with no crease or injury condition, so 607(c) is a floor; plan on a match if reckless (602(a))
Rule: IHUK's In-House Rules 2026-27 leave Rule 42 to the IIHF book, so IIHF 42.2 to 42.4 — not Hockey Canada's mandatory ejection — prices a British charge; the EIHL Casebook adds a game misconduct on a major injuring face or head. Rule 42 reads alike in 2025/26 and 2026/27
Rule: Not only a charge — hit a goalie from behind and IIHF 43.2 leaves "no option to award a minor penalty", plan on 43.3's major and automatic game misconduct, head or not. A head hit not from behind is 48: a minor, or a reckless major and game misconduct
Rule: From behind: NHL and PWHL 43.2 no minor, 43.5 ejects on a major, 48 if harsher on the head; CARHA always ejects (53(a)); Hockey Canada off the head, harsher of 7.5 and 8.5: ejection at least, major and game misconduct on a charge, a match if defenceless into boards or frame (7.5(c)); head, 7.6/7.7
Rule: USA Hockey 608(a) is a minor plus misconduct or a major plus game misconduct; 608(b) mandates the major and game misconduct if reckless or head first into the boards or goal frame, and 608(c) a match for a reckless, excessive-force hit on a defenceless player; if reckless, plan on a match (602(a))
Rule: From behind into boards or net on a defenceless player: a mandatory match in CARHA 53(b) and Hockey Canada (7.5(c) off the head, 7.6/7.7(c) on it); plan on one in USA Hockey if reckless (602(a), 608(c)); NHL and PWHL 43.4 reach one for intent to injure, mandatory if deliberate (21.1; CARHA 48(a))
Action: Screen, tip and hunt rebounds from outside the paint and off its red line; never initiate contact with a goalie to make room, however much of your advantage is ticking away
```

**What every book below has in common is the instruction.** Screen, tip and hunt rebounds from **outside** the paint, and off the red line that marks its edge. Do not put a hand, a shoulder or a hip into a goalie to make room, and do not accelerate through a net drive that is already going to end in contact — under every book below, the cheapest thing you can be is the player who visibly tried to avoid it.

The section above decides whether a goal counts. **This one decides whether you are still in the game**, and on a power play that is the more expensive question: ⚠️ **a misconduct is ten minutes, and your advantage is long gone before you come back.** (USA Hockey lets youth affiliates shorten it to six or eight minutes; that changes nothing here.)

**USA Hockey: there is no bare minor for charging a goalie.** Charging or checking a goalie in the crease **or privileged area** is at least a minor plus a misconduct, or a major plus a game misconduct. ⚠️ **A reckless charge anywhere is a mandatory major and game misconduct, and plan on a match penalty too** — USA Hockey gives one to any player who *"recklessly endangers or attempts to injure"* an opponent. A game misconduct also suspends you for your team's next game. A match penalty suspends you from all USA Hockey games and practices **until your case is dealt with**. USA Hockey recommends four to eight games for a charging match, and six to ten for one under the reckless-endangerment rule; a hearing can raise or lower that.

**The privileged area is not the crease.** It runs from the end-zone faceoff spots straight to the end boards, so ⚠️ **it takes in the whole net front, the goal line and the ice behind the net** — where a 1-3-1 net-front player and a walk-down both live. Outside it a goalie is still not fair game, and deliberate contact is charging wherever it happens. [Rink Map](../foundation/rink_map.md) defines it.

**Most net-front bumping is cheaper than that.** Under USA Hockey, accidental or unavoidable contact with the goalie is interference, a minor; deliberate body contact or a check is charging. ⚠️ **The difference is deliberateness, not force.** When it is charging, USA Hockey's Casebook sets three rungs:

- **No penalty** only where a defender forced you into the goalie **and** you *"clearly made every attempt to avoid the contact"* — ⚠️ **both, not either.**
- **A minor plus misconduct** where you were going hard to the net and made *"an honest attempt to avoid the goalkeeper"* once contact was unavoidable. ⚠️ **That is the best outcome once the referee calls a charge, and it still costs ten minutes.** Keep driving the net; drive it under control.
- **A major plus game misconduct, or a match**, where you recklessly endanger the goalie by accelerating through the contact, extending your arms or dropping your shoulder.

**The other books, in short — none of them is a licence:**

- **NHL and PWHL:** a charge on a goalie in their crease is a minor, a major, or a major and game misconduct, at the referee's call; a match for an attempt to injure, mandatory where it is deliberate; and a game misconduct is automatic on a major that injures the face or head.
- **IIHF:** a minor, a major, or a major and game misconduct for reckless endangerment, at the referee's call.
- **Hockey Canada:** ⚠️ **any charge on a goalie is a mandatory major and game misconduct, wherever the goalie stands**, and so is interference that injures them; a violent interference can draw the same; there is no ten-minute rung; and a **match** for an attempt to injure. Two or more strides before contact makes it a charge.
- **CARHA (adult leagues only):** ⚠️ **a mandatory major and game misconduct for a charge on a goalie in the crease, or for one that injures.** Any major for contact with a goalie ejects under CARHA, with at least a one-game ban (32(d)) — including the major the referee may give for deliberate contact with a goalie in or out of the crease. More than two strides makes it a charge, so a hard arrival from the slot can reach it.

**Arrive from behind and you are under the checking-from-behind rule instead, which is harsher.** ⚠️ **The NHL, PWHL and IIHF write no minor for it**, and Hockey Canada (while the hit stays off the head), CARHA and USA Hockey keep one only with a misconduct or game misconduct attached. If it reaches the head, the NHL and PWHL take the harsher of that rule and head contact; under the IIHF, plan on its major and game misconduct; and Hockey Canada makes it a match wherever the player cannot protect themselves, open ice included. Under Hockey Canada, take the harsher of checking from behind and goaltender interference, where any charge is already a major and game misconduct. Put a goalie into their own net or the boards from behind and every book but the IIHF can reach a match penalty; the full ladder is under [Killing the entry](#killing-the-entry--what-all-of-this-is-for).

> **If you play in Britain — the ladder above is the IIHF's, and nothing local softens it.** The In-House Rules leave charging to the IIHF book: a minor, a major, or a major and game misconduct. ⚠️ **In the Elite League, a charging major that injures the face or head is also a game misconduct.** A hit to the head that is not from behind is a minor, or a reckless major with its automatic game misconduct. The IIHF writes no match penalty, so its ceiling is a game misconduct — off for the rest of the game. (That the IIHF's checking-from-behind rule reaches a goalie at all is a reading of the book, not a sentence it prints, so plan on its major and game misconduct anyway.) See [UK and England Rules](../foundation/uk_rules.md).

## Common Mistakes

These are the errors that survive knowing the systems.

### Playing the shape instead of playing the play

- **Making one pass and shooting.** That is even-strength hockey with a spare man. Make the first pass to move the kill and the second to beat it.
- **Shooting into a kill that is already square.** A point shot into four set bodies is a coin-flip on possession. This document's default is to shoot when the kill is out of shape — **but that is a coaching philosophy, not a law**; shot-volume power plays are coached and do work, on tips, rebounds and loose-puck retrievals. Ask your coach which one you play.
- **Being an option that needs two touches.** Stand so you could shoot or pass immediately, or the kill ignores you and covers the other three.
- **Treating a 2-on-1 at the top of the zone as the objective.** It is a step. Cash it in near the net.
- **Camping the backdoor.** Arrive as the shot or pass is released. A player standing there all shift is marked.
- **Walking to the corner instead of to the middle.** On a walk-down, head toward the dot; below the goal line and wide you are harmless. If it is denied, reset to the point.
- **Sending two players after a clear.** Only one chases — often the net-front player or the weak-side half-wall, but that is a coaching choice. The chaser arrives *before the puck stops*; if two go, nobody holds the zone.
- **Pinching as the lone point player.** Holding the line is your job, and a missed pinch puts you two strides deeper than a missed hold. If a clear beats you cleanly, turn and skate rather than lunge.
- **Describing a 1-3-1 as having "defencemen at the points".** It has **one** point player. Two means an umbrella, an overload or a 2-3 — name it right so your linemates know where to stand.
- **Standing three players level across the blue line and calling it an umbrella.** Every pass is short and lateral and the kill barely moves. An umbrella is an **arc**: apex highest in the middle, flanks lower.
- **Playing a 5-on-3 like a better 5-on-4.** Spread out, move the puck across the middle, and shoot when the goalie is moving, not into three blockers. Patient is not passive: two minutes with no shot is a failed 5-on-3.
- **Reading "the kill can't pressure at 5-on-3" as "nobody will touch me."** Three killers still jump a bobble and close on anyone walking into the slot. Move the puck before they close, protect it on every touch, and expect to be hit in the slot.

### Killing with effort instead of with discipline

- **Covering something that was never yours.** Hold your own job even when the conceded area looks dangerous — a box concedes the middle, a diamond the outside and backdoor, a wedge+1 the perimeter, and the goal comes when a killer leaves their job to cover it.
- **One player pressuring while the other three sit.** Pressure as a unit or not at all. In a wedge+1, when the puck moves away from the +1, the nearest wedge player steps out as the new +1 and the old +1 drops in; a +1 who keeps chasing leaves you killing with three.
- **Both high forwards in a box drifting to the same side.** The box **slides** with the puck as a unit; it does not rotate or bunch. Turn your inside shoulder so you can see your point man and the middle.
- **Chasing a puck that was never winnable.** Go only for a bobbled reception, a turned back, or an unsupported puck on the boards — and on a turned back go for the puck, never through the man, because a hit from behind can end your game (see [Mistakes that get you hurt](#mistakes-that-get-you-hurt)). Chase and miss, and you have opened the seam yourself.
- **Taking a shorthanded rush that is really a 2-on-2.** Take only the rush the turnover handed you, with your partner having seen it. If you have to wonder whether it is on, it is not; lose it and you are 4-on-5 against speed.
- **Changing when you must rather than when you can.** A tired killer chases. Clear, then change.
- **Never telling your goalie what you are conceding.** Tell them before the kill starts, so they can set their depth for it instead of guessing.
- **Blocking a shot from a screening position.** Deflecting a puck your goalie never saw is worse than not blocking. If you cannot get properly into the lane, get out of it.
- **Too many men on the ice during a special-teams change.** Units change together; count before you go.
- **Arguing about the system on the bench instead of before the game.** A breakdown that looks like laziness is often two players on different assumptions. Settle it in the room.

### Mistakes that cost you the advantage, or a second penalty

- **Icing the puck while on the power play.** Never fire a loose puck the length of the ice with the extra man — the exemption only protects the kill. You get a draw in your own end, and in several books the kill picks the dot and your tired unit cannot change. **The one length-of-ice shot that is never icing, in any of the six books, is a puck sent down directly off the draw.** Set out in [Faceoffs on special teams](#faceoffs-on-special-teams).
- **Declining a length-of-the-ice clear on a five-minute kill because nobody is in the penalty box.** Keep firing it through a major — the NHL's and PWHL's empty-box carve-out bites **when the major expires**, and the IIHF has none. It does end if a penalty against them puts you level. ⚠️ **And never leave the box empty: it is a separate duty.** Under the IIHF missing it is a bench minor that can land mid-major and make it a 5-on-3; under the NHL and PWHL it is a bench minor too (68.2 and 70.2), and you play a player short to the next stoppage. Book by book in [You may ice the puck freely](#you-may-ice-the-puck-freely--where-your-rule-set-allows-it).
- **Assuming last change is automatic at home.** After your *own* icing, under the NHL, the IIHF and the PWHL, the visitors can change and you cannot; other books differ. Plan the matchup on the book you are in. See [Faceoffs](faceoffs.md).
- **Flipping the puck out of the rink to buy a tired kill a whistle.** Get it out along the ice and change instead. **A deliberate shoot-out is a minor from anywhere on the ice, in six books.**
- **Winning a shorthanded defensive-zone draw straight out of the rink.** Win it hard back to your defenceman and never aim it out. **A draw steered out on purpose is a minor under USA Hockey, Hockey Canada and CARHA**; the NHL, IIHF and PWHL waive the penalty for any puck that goes straight out off a draw, but none says whether that covers one aimed out — so do not find out.
- **A goalie ringing a clear off the glass and out, on the reading that an accident is not a penalty.** Keep it under the glass, or hand it to a defenceman. Under Hockey Canada and CARHA the goalie has a limb of their own with no *deliberately* in it — neither book says in terms that it is strict liability, so it is a reading to plan for. ⚠️ **Plan for it anyway: the cost of being wrong is a 5-on-3.** Set out in [The two penalties a kill talks itself into](#the-two-penalties-a-kill-talks-itself-into).
- **Smothering a loose puck on the kill to buy the whistle.** Knock it down rather than holding it. A skater who falls on the puck or gathers it in takes a **minor** in every book, on conditions that vary; with the puck — or, under Hockey Canada, you — in your own crease it can be a **penalty shot**, and with your goalie pulled an **awarded goal**. Set out in [Smothering the puck to force a whistle](#smothering-the-puck-to-force-a-whistle).
- **Sending your best draw-taker out and then having a winger creep over the hash marks.** Keep your wingers off the hash marks until the drop — **your centre is the one thrown out**, and a second violation is a penalty in four of six books: on a kill, a 5-on-3. ⚠️ **Do not lean on theirs either: physical contact at the dot can be penalised on the first violation — mandatorily in three books.** Set out in [Faceoffs on special teams](#faceoffs-on-special-teams).
- **Confusing the two things called a "delayed penalty."** Watch for the referee's raised arm, not the phrase — players pull a goalie for what was really a third penalty stacking on the clock.

### Mistakes at their net front

- **Playing the net front as though two minutes is the worst of it.** Make your room with position and a screen, never by initiating contact with the goalie. ⚠️ **Under USA Hockey, charging or checking a goalie anywhere in the privileged area is at least two minutes plus a misconduct, and a reckless one throws you out of the game — plan on a match. Under Hockey Canada any charge on a goalie throws you out wherever the goalie stands, as does interference that injures them; under CARHA a charge in the crease does, or one that injures. Under USA Hockey and CARHA, being thrown out also costs at least the next game.** 🇬🇧 The IIHF writes a minor for a charge, but a reckless one can be a major, or a major and game misconduct, with nobody hurt, and it writes no minor at all for a hit into a goalie's back. On a power play, a misconduct outlasts your advantage. Book by book in [What contact with their goaltender costs *you*](#what-contact-with-their-goaltender-costs-you--the-penalty-not-the-goal).
- **Leaning on the goalie while you screen.** Screen without touching them. Under Hockey Canada, CARHA and USA Hockey any contact you start costs the goal, in the crease or out of it, and under the NHL, IIHF and PWHL a screen that stops the goalie moving in the crease can cost it too.
- **Treating the red crease line as the safe side of the boundary.** **Keep both feet clear of the line, not merely out of the blue paint** — every book that settles it counts the line as part of the crease. It is the cheapest thing you will ever do for a goal. [Rink Map](../foundation/rink_map.md) owns this.
- **Standing in, or skating through, the crease under USA Hockey and expecting only a "no goal".** Go *around* the paint. That book stops play for a draw outside your attacking zone when you are in the crease, your team has the puck in the zone and the goalie is in contact with the crease — on a power play the whistle *is* the punishment. Its Casebook cuts both ways: a stick briefly through the paint will not cost you the zone, but it will still cost you the goal. Set out in [Against their goalie](#against-their-goalie-where-the-net-front-line-is).
- **Pushing the goalie into the net "with" the puck in a scramble.** Play the puck, not the goalie. The rebound carve-out covers only *incidental* contact while you both go for a loose puck; drive a goalie into the net after a stop and the goal is disallowed, and expect a penalty too — in CARHA up to an ejecting major, with at least a one-game suspension (32(d)). Only a defender fouling you into them saves the goal.
- **Taking the lane away from a goalie skating back to their crease.** **Get out of the lane.** Under USA Hockey's Casebook, blocking a goalie on their way back costs the goal and an interference penalty; the NHL and IIHF can penalise it when it is deliberate. A major ends your game under Hockey Canada, CARHA and USA Hockey, and under CARHA and USA Hockey it costs at least the next game too (32(d); 404(b)). ⚠️ **Whether merely standing in the space is enough is not settled in the books — a reason to be out of the lane, not a permission to stand in it.** Each book's route is in [Against their goalie](#against-their-goalie-where-the-net-front-line-is).

### Mistakes that get you hurt

- **Reading "back turned" or "facing the boards" as a reason to arrive harder.** **Go for the puck, not through the back** — if you cannot reach the puck without going through the player, you were never winning it. ⚠️ **No book prices a hit that stays on the back at a bare minor, most throw you out for it, and five can make it a match penalty even in open ice. Into the boards on a player who cannot protect themselves it is a match under CARHA and Hockey Canada; under USA Hockey a forceful one on a player along the boards, a head-first one or a reckless one is a mandatory ejection — and plan on a match if it is reckless.** Under Hockey Canada a hit that reaches the head is priced as head contact, and one that drives a player head first into the boards or goal frame gets the harsher price. Under the NHL and PWHL, a hit from behind that reaches the head takes the harsher of checking from behind and head contact; under the IIHF, plan on the major and game misconduct. The full ladder is in [Killing the entry](#killing-the-entry--what-all-of-this-is-for).
- **Hearing "body on the wall" or "head up" as the whole of the wall posture.** **Never turn your back to anyone coming at you, and never duck** — skates parallel to the boards, forearm and hip to the wall, head up and chin off your chest, never the point of your shoulder. Holding the line and walking down the half-wall both put you on the wall with somebody closing, and **being a man up, or in a non-checking league, changes nothing:** a wall does not need an opponent to hurt you. [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md) owns the full rule.

## Key Takeaways

1. **Create two-on-ones by passing, and cash them in near the net.** A kill can cover one option, not three in two seconds. Stand where you could shoot or pass this instant — a player who needs two touches is ignored. Preferring the pass to the shot is a coaching choice; shot-volume power plays work too.
2. **When the entry is denied, turn back and come again.** Most power plays die at the blue line. **When the kill clears it, one player chases and the other four re-form** — the unit that wins that scramble gets three or four setups instead of one.
3. **On the kill, hold your own job and concede only what your shape concedes.** A box gives up the middle, a diamond the outside and the backdoor, a wedge+1 the perimeter. The goal comes when a killer leaves their job to cover what was conceded. Keep your blade flat in the seam.
4. **Kill the penalty before it reaches your zone.** Stand up at the line as a pair and put your stick in the receiver's lane. Go for the puck, not the man: contact before it arrives is interference, and where checking is barred, or in IIHF or PWHL women's hockey, so is going into the receiver when it arrives.
5. **Know your team's shape and your job in it before the puck drops.** Every formation here is a coaching choice, not a law, and so are the pressure setting and how much you block. Most breakdowns are two players assuming different systems, so ask your coach which one you run.
6. **Win the draw with your stick and keep your wingers off the hash marks.** A creeping winger gets your **centre** thrown out; a second violation is a penalty in four books of the six, and contact at the dot can be penalised on the first — mandatorily in three. A man short, either is a 5-on-3.
7. **Block shots from the lane, before the shot, on your feet.** Never block from where you screen your own goalie. Block with your body, head out of the lane, stick flat beside you, and never turn your back or lift a leg. How much to block is your coach's call.
8. **On the kill, pressure the puck, never the back.** If the carrier is between you and the puck, you were never winning it. ⚠️ **A hit from behind ejects you in most books; under USA Hockey, any forceful one along the boards, or any head-first or reckless one. If reckless there, plan on a match and a likely multi-game suspension.**
9. **A man short, fire the long clear; a man up, never.** Release it before the penalty ends. **USA Hockey allows it only at Youth 15-Only and above, Girls 16U and above, High School and Adult**, and the NHL and PWHL withdraw it when a major expires with your box empty. **A deliberate shoot-out is a minor anywhere, in every book.**
10. **Screen with your skates clear of the crease and its red line; never touch the goalie or block their road home.** ⚠️ **A charge on the goalie ejects you: under Hockey Canada anywhere, as does interference that injures them; under CARHA in the crease or on injury; under USA Hockey if reckless (plan on a match). USA Hockey and CARHA add at least the next game.** Every other book can eject.

*The empty penalty box on a major — what a failure costs in each book, read in the primary text on 14 September 2026: **NHL** 19.2, 20.3, 81.6 and **68.2**, whose closing sentence — *"[a]ny violation of this provision shall be treated as an illegal substitution under this rule calling for a bench minor penalty"* — is the IIHF's word for word · **IIHF** 8.1, 19.2, 20.3 and **68.2**, which differs from the NHL's only in requiring the substitute *"immediately"* where the NHL says *"before the major penalty expires"* · **USA Hockey** 204(c), 206(d), 403(c), 403(d), 405(a), with its Casebook at Rule 204, Situations 10 and 11 · **Hockey Canada** 4.1(b), 4.4(a), 4.5(a) and Note 1 to 4.5(c) · **CARHA** 30(a), which requires the substitute — *"[t]he penalized team shall place a substitute player on the Penalty Bench to serve five minutes actual playing time"* — and names no moment and no sanction for failing it, and 30(b), which **does** carry a timing word, *"immediate substitutions shall be made"*, for the coincidental case. ⚠️ **That is a gap in Rule 30(a), not a silence in the book:** CARHA writes *"immediately"* elsewhere — its **31(a)** second paragraph on *"a Minor and a Misconduct penalty or a Major and a Misconduct penalty at the same time"*, and its **32(a)** on a third penalty in a game — **and the two reach 30(a) differently.** 31(a) does not: 30(a) attaches a **Game** Misconduct, which CARHA treats as a different thing from a Misconduct (32(b)). 32(a) reaches part of it, by its own Note 1 — *"[a]ny combination of minor, bench minor, or **major** penalties shall apply to the personal total"* — so a major that is the player's third penalty must be seated *immediately*. What is unsettled is the general case, a major that is the player's first or second. Seat somebody anyway. [Rules Primer](../foundation/rules_primer.md) carries the full comparison.*
