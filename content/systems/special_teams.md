## Overview

**Special teams** means any situation where the two teams do not have the same number of skaters on the ice. Most often that is a **power play**, where you have the extra skater, or a **penalty kill**, where you are a skater short — written "5-on-4", counting skaters only.

**The extra skater is worth nothing until it forces somebody to cover two players at once.** A power play where nobody moves is four against four with a spare man standing somewhere useless. Make those two-against-one moments wherever the kill allows, and cash them in near the net — a coaching judgement about danger by location rather than a rule, but the one every modern power play is built on.

**Passing is what creates them.** Four defenders can cover any single option and cannot cover three in two seconds — though that is a philosophy rather than a law, and shot-volume power plays do work. Most power plays die at the blue line rather than in the zone, so be willing to turn back and come again.

**A penalty kill is a decision about which shot you are willing to concede, then two minutes of discipline making sure it is the only one.** The power play wants you to chase, and a killer who chases and misses has opened the dangerous pass himself.

**A team a skater short may usually send the puck the length of the ice without it being called back for icing — check your own book first.** Under the IIHF book, which every level of British hockey is played under, that exemption is unconditional. The NHL withdraws it from a team shorthanded by a major penalty that has left nobody waiting on the penalty bench, and USA Hockey grants it only from Youth 15-Only, Girls 16U, High School and Adult upward. Shooting the puck directly out of play from your own end is a second penalty either way.

**Blocking a shot is a technique, not an act of courage.** Be in the shooting lane before the shot rather than sliding into it, and **never block from a position where you are blocking your own goalie's view** — half-deflecting a shot they could not see is worse than letting it through. If you cannot get into the lane properly, get out of it.

## POWER PLAY

### The one idea behind every power play

```facts
Goal: Create a 2-on-1 — the extra man only pays when the kill has two attackers to worry about and one body to do it with
Never: Stand still — a static 5-on-4 is a 4-on-4 with a spare man standing somewhere useless
Action: Manufacture 2-on-1s wherever the kill lets you; every one pulls the kill further out of shape and sets up the next
Priority: Cash them in as close to the net as you can — a net-front 2-on-1 is near-certain, one at the top of the zone is a shot from distance
Convention: Danger is a function of location — a coaching judgement, not a rule, but the one every modern power play is built on
```

Five attackers against four defenders is not "one extra shooter". It is **one extra decision the penalty kill cannot make**.

Four killers can cover four attackers. That is the whole problem. A static 5-on-4 where nobody moves is, functionally, a 4-on-4 with a spare man standing somewhere useless. The advantage only becomes a goal when the kill is forced to have **two attackers to worry about and one body to do it with** — a 2-on-1 somewhere on the ice. **A 2-on-1 anywhere is worth creating**, because every one of them pulls the kill further out of shape and sets up the next one. But they are emphatically **not worth the same**: danger is a function of location. A 2-on-1 at the net front — net-front player plus a backdoor arriver against one defenceman — is a near-certain chance. A 2-on-1 at the top of the zone usually amounts to no more than a shot from distance into traffic. *(That ranking is a coaching judgement about danger by location, not a rule — but it is the judgement every modern power play is built on.)* Manufacture them wherever the kill lets you, and cash them in as close to the net as you can.

#### Why puck movement, not shooting, creates it

```facts
Key: A kill can cover any single option; it cannot cover three options in two seconds
Action: Move the kill with passes, not with skating — two crisp passes pull a box further out of shape than thirty seconds of stickhandling
Never: Shoot into a set kill — a point shot into four square bodies is a coin-flip on possession and a re-entry against a set kill
Convention: This document's default is "shoot when the kill is out of shape" — a coaching philosophy, not a law; shot-volume power plays are coached and do work
Key: The pass that beats a kill is usually the second one — the first makes them move, the second arrives where they no longer are
Position: Wherever you stand, be an option the kill must physically account for, able to shoot or pass immediately
```

A penalty kill is a shape, and a shape has to travel to cover the puck. Every pass makes at least one killer skate. Killers skating are killers arriving late, and a killer arriving late is a 2-on-1 for the two seconds before they get there.

- **A kill can cover any single option. It cannot cover three options in two seconds.** That is the entire mechanism.
- **Passing moves the kill faster than skating does.** The puck travels far quicker than a person. Two crisp passes will pull a box further out of shape than thirty seconds of one player stickhandling on the half-wall.
- **Shooting into a set kill is how you end your power play.** A shot from the point into four bodies with the killers already square is a coin-flip on possession — they block it, they clear it, and now you have to re-enter the zone against four killers already set in their structure. **This document's default is "shoot when the kill is out of shape."** That is a coaching philosophy, not a law. The realistic alternative is a genuine **shot-volume power play**, which is coached and does work: fire everything at the net from the moment you are set, on the theory that tips, rebounds and scrambles beat a goalie who is never allowed to settle, and that a retrieval unit which wins the loose pucks turns a "wasted" shot into a fresh setup. Volume-first suits units with a heavy point shot, a fearless net front and good retrievers; patience-first suits units with a distributor and a one-timer. **Find out which one your coach wants before you decide the point shot was a bad idea.**
- **Corollary: the pass that beats a kill is usually the second one.** The first pass makes them move; the second one arrives where they no longer are. Power plays that make one pass and shoot are just slow even-strength hockey.

**What this means for you personally:** whatever spot you occupy, your job is to be an option the kill must physically account for, in a body position where you could shoot or pass *immediately*. A player who has to take two touches to become dangerous is not an option — the kill can ignore them and cover the other three.

### Power play formations

```facts
Convention: Which formation your team plays is a coaching choice, not a rule of hockey — and so is who stands where inside it
Key: Every shape below wins against some kills and loses to others, and every one requires personnel it suits
Action: Find out which formation your team runs, and which spot is yours, before your first power play shift
Never: Guess — if you are guessing, you are the reason the seam is covered
```

> **Read this first.** Which formation your team plays is a **coaching choice**, not a rule of hockey. So is who stands where inside it. Every shape below wins against some kills and loses to others, and every one of them requires personnel it suits. **Find out which formation your team runs, and which spot is yours, before your first power play shift.** If you are guessing, you are the reason the seam is covered.

#### 1-3-1 — widely described as the modern default

```facts
Position: One at the point, two on the half-walls, a bumper in the middle of the slot, one at the net front — one point player, not two
Action: The point walks the line laterally — three metres sideways changes the angle of every passing lane in the zone
Technique: Half-walls play the off-wing so the puck is on the inside hand; look inside before you look up
Action: The bumper keeps the blade on the ice for a one-touch shot and holds the kill's high players down
Position: The net front lives at the edge of the blue paint, not inside it. Screen, tip and hunt rebounds from there
Convention: The bumper is most often the centre, but that is team-dependent, not a law. Ask your coach who has it
Countered by: A diamond — this is the most-scouted shape in hockey, so kills arrive with a plan for it
Risk: With only one player back, a lost puck at the blue line is an immediate odd-man rush the other way
```

> **Three words used constantly below, defined here.** The **slot** is the area directly in front of the net between the two faceoff circles, running from the goalmouth out to the tops of the circles — the most dangerous ice on the rink. Within it, the **high slot** is the upper half, from the faceoff dots out to the tops of the circles; the **goalmouth** is the immediate net front, within a stick's length of the goal line. The **point** is the position at the blue line in the offensive zone; the **half-wall** is the boards level with the faceoff dot, well below it. Point and half-wall are not the same place and are never interchangeable.

**The shape:** **one** player at the point (the blue line), **three** across the middle of the zone — two on the **half-walls** (the boards roughly level with the faceoff dot, between the corner and the point) with a **bumper** between them in the middle of the slot — and **one** at the net front.

That is one point player, not two. First units are widely **described as** running the 1-3-1 with **four forwards and a single defenceman**, and where they do, that defenceman is the point — but treat the personnel as coaching-material consensus rather than a measured prevalence. **No published count of NHL power-play deployments exists.** The *shape* is definitional; how many forwards a coach puts in it is a choice.

![](diagram:pp-131)

**The jobs:**

- **Point (the "quarterback").** Usually the lone defenceman. You are a distributor first and a shooter second. Walk the line laterally — moving three metres sideways changes the angle of every passing lane in the zone and forces the killers' heads to turn. Your shot exists mainly as a threat: the moment the kill stops respecting it, they stop stepping out to you, and the half-walls lose their space. Shoot for tips and rebounds, not to beat the goalie clean through four bodies.
- **Half-walls (two players, usually forwards).** The primary decision-makers. From here you can reach the point, the bumper, the net front and — through the seam — the far half-wall. Ideally you play your **off-wing** (a left-hander on the right side, or vice versa) so the puck is on your inside hand: that opens your body to the middle of the ice, makes the one-timer available, and lets you shoot or pass without turning. Your first move should almost always be to look inside before you look up.
- **Bumper.** The middle of the three, sitting in the soft spot between the point and the net-front player, roughly the middle of the slot. You are the reason a box fails: a box has nobody assigned to the middle. Your job is to keep your stick free and your blade on the ice for a one-touch shot or redirect, to constantly adjust your angle so a passing lane exists from at least one half-wall, and to hold the kill's high players down so they cannot step out and pressure the point.
- **Net front.** Screen the goalie, tip point shots, hunt rebounds, and occasionally slide below the goal line to open a high-to-low pass. Live at the edge of the **blue paint** — the painted goal crease in front of the net, the goalie's protected ice — not inside it. See the goaltender-interference note under [Center](../positions/center.md).

**Who plays the bumper?** **Most often the centre**, because the position is a centre's whole skill set: soft hands in traffic, back to the play, one-touch passing. But this is genuinely **team-dependent, not a law** — plenty of real NHL units put a **winger** in the bumper. The spot is chosen for the ability to receive and release in a crowd, not for what is printed on the lineup card. Ask your coach who has it; do not assume.

**Strengths:** occupies all five dangerous areas at once; creates four passing triangles, so there is always a short outlet; the bumper attacks the one place a box cannot cover; the off-wing half-walls make the cross-seam one-timer live at all times.

**Weaknesses:** every player must be comfortable making plays under pressure — a weak link in any of the five spots kills it. With only one player back, a lost puck at the blue line is an immediate odd-man rush the other way. It is also the most-scouted shape in hockey, so kills arrive with a plan (usually a diamond) specifically for it.

**Suits:** skilled units with at least one high-end distributor on a half-wall, one genuine one-timer threat, and a centre or winger brave enough to stand in the slot.

#### Umbrella — three high

```facts
Position: Three high in an arc — one apex highest in the middle at the blue line, two flanks lower either side — and two low at the net
Key: There is no bumper in a true umbrella; the middle of the slot is deliberately vacated so the high players have clean lanes into it
Options: Classic — a forward apex with both defencemen out on the flanks; modern — the lone defenceman at the apex with two forwards down at the tops of the circles
Never: String three players level across the blue line and call it an umbrella — with no depth every pass is short and lateral
Action: The three high move the puck side to side for a shot with a lane; the two low screen, tip and collect rebounds
Countered by: A diamond or an aggressive box, which will happily let you pass it around the top all night
Risk: It is far from the net, and goalies and killers are most comfortable when the puck is far from the net
```

**The shape:** **three players high**, arranged as an **arc** rather than a flat line — that curve is what the name describes. **One apex** sits highest, in the middle at or just inside the blue line, and the **two flanks sit lower than the apex** on either side. Below the arc sit **two low players**, typically one at the net front and one at the side of the net or in a circle.

**How far the flanks drop is the one real variation.** In the **classic** version the apex is a forward in the middle of the blue line with **both defencemen out on the flanks**, still close to the line — the arc is shallow. In the **modern** version the lone defenceman takes the apex and two forwards drop right down to the **tops of the faceoff circles**, where the one-timer lives — a much deeper arc. Both are umbrellas. What neither is, is three players strung out **level** across the blue line: with no depth between them every pass is short and lateral and the kill barely has to move.

![](diagram:pp-umbrella)

**There is no bumper in a true umbrella.** The middle of the slot is deliberately vacated so the high players have clean shooting lanes into it.

**The jobs:** the three high move the puck side to side looking to get it to the middle for a shot with a lane; the two low screen, tip and collect rebounds. Everything is aimed at getting a puck through to the net with a body in front of the goalie.

**Strengths:** enormous space and time up high; hard to pressure, because pressuring one high player opens a pass to another; three shooting angles from the top; very difficult for the kill to get a shorthanded break, because you always have players above the puck.

**Weaknesses:** it is far from the net, and goalies and killers are most comfortable when the puck is far from the net. Used as a permanent shape it becomes predictable, and a diamond or an aggressive box will happily let you pass it around the top all night. It concedes the middle of the ice by design.

**Suits:** teams with heavy, accurate point shots and a genuinely fearless net front; also a good change-up shape when a kill has settled comfortably against your 1-3-1.

#### Overload — four to one side

```facts
Position: Four on one side — below the goal line, in the corner or low circle, on the half-wall and at that point — with the fifth weak side as a release valve
Action: Cycle the puck relentlessly in a small area, creating repeated 3-on-2s along the strong-side wall
Read: When a killer over-commits, attack the seam or swing it cross-ice to the lone weak-side player with the goalie moving
Read: Excellent against a passive box that will not come out to the wall
Countered by: A kill that reads the pattern — the passes are short and easy to intercept, and a strong-side interception is a shorthanded break
Risk: It produces fewer shots than other shapes; cycling without ever pulling the trigger just tires your own unit
```

**The shape:** **four players loaded onto one side of the ice** — typically a player below the goal line, one in the corner or low circle, one on the half-wall and one at the point on that side — with the fifth player weak side as a release valve and safety.

**The jobs:** cycle the puck relentlessly in a small area, creating repeated 3-on-2s along the strong-side wall until a killer over-commits, then either attack the resulting seam or swing the puck cross-ice to the lone weak-side player for a shot with the goalie moving.

![](diagram:pp-overload)

**Strengths:** outnumbers the kill locally; generates chances from deception and short passes rather than long ones; excellent against a passive box that will not come out to the wall.

**Weaknesses:** demands deceptive, patient passers — if the kill reads the pattern, the passes are short and easy to intercept, and interceptions on the strong side lead straight to shorthanded breaks. It also produces fewer shots than other shapes; a unit that cycles without ever pulling the trigger achieves nothing but a tired power play.

**Suits:** skilled, patient units, and teams whose best player is a below-the-goal-line playmaker. Note that at rec and youth level overload is often run out of a **2-3 base** (below), with both defencemen staying at the points. ⚠️ **That is an impression of how those levels play rather than a measured share** — nobody publishes a count of rec or youth power-play deployments, and this document has none, so treat it as unverified like the other prevalence claims here.

#### Spread / box — pulling the kill apart

```facts
Position: Two low just outside the posts, two high and wide at the tops of the circles or points, one in the middle of the slot
Key: The point is the spacing, not the shape. Stand far enough apart that one killer cannot cover two of you
Action: Occupy space, stay still enough to be a genuine passing target, and make everything a one-touch play
Read: Use it at 5-on-4 against a kill that has collapsed so tightly it has conceded the outside entirely; it is also the standard 5-on-3 shape
Countered by: Long passes — at 5-on-4 they are interceptable and can be blocked by a well-placed stick
Risk: It surrenders the possession game — you are not cycling, you are waiting
Key: "Box" names both this power-play spacing and the standard penalty-kill shape; say which one you mean
```

**The shape:** five spots, deliberately stretched to the corners of the danger area — **two low**, one on each side of the net just outside the posts; **two high and wide**, at the tops of the circles or the points; and **one in the middle of the slot**. The point is not the shape itself but the spacing: **stand far enough apart that one killer cannot cover two of you.**

> **Terminology warning:** "box" names both this power-play spacing *and* the standard penalty-kill shape. They are unrelated. Say which one you mean.

**The jobs:** occupy space, stay still enough to be a genuine passing target, and force the kill to make full-length skates to close anyone down. Everything is a one-touch play — with this much space the kill's only defence is anticipation, and one-touch passing beats anticipation.

**Strengths:** the maximum geometric stress you can put on a small defensive unit. Creates backdoor plays almost automatically, because the weak-side low player is simply too far away for the kill's low defender to reach.

**Weaknesses:** at 5-on-4 the passes are long, and long passes are interceptable and can be blocked by a well-placed stick. It surrenders the possession game — you are not cycling, you are waiting.

**Suits:** it is the standard **5-on-3** shape (see below), and it is worth using at 5-on-4 against a kill that has collapsed so tightly it has conceded the outside entirely.

#### 2-3 — the older structure

```facts
Position: Two defencemen at the points and three forwards low — one at the net front, one in each circle or corner
Action: The points shoot and keep pucks in; the three forwards screen, tip and hunt rebounds, with the puck going low-to-high and back
Key: It is safe — two defencemen at the line means very few shorthanded breaks and good retrieval on missed shots
Countered by: A competent box, which is built to defend the two areas this attacks
Risk: It ignores the middle of the slot entirely and generates lots of low-quality shots
Read: Choose it with no puck-moving defenceman, when protecting a lead, or before a group can hold the blue line under pressure
```

**The shape:** **two defencemen at the points** and **three forwards low** — one at the net front, one in each circle or corner. This was the default power play for decades and remains common in rec, beer-league and youth hockey. ⚠️ **Both halves of that are impressions rather than measured shares, and this document has no count for either** — the same limit that applies to the prevalence claims elsewhere here.

**The jobs:** the points shoot and keep pucks in; the three forwards work the low area, screen, tip and hunt rebounds; the puck generally goes low-to-high and back.

![](diagram:pp-2-3)

**Strengths:** it is safe. Two defencemen at the line means very few shorthanded breaks and very good puck retrieval on missed shots. It is also the easiest shape to teach and the least dependent on individual skill — everyone knows where to stand.

**Weaknesses:** it attacks the two areas a box is built to defend (the points and the goalmouth) and ignores the middle of the slot entirely. It generates lots of low-quality shots. Against a competent box it is close to harmless.

**Suits:** teams without a puck-moving defenceman, teams who are protecting a lead, and any group that has not yet learned to hold the blue line under pressure. It is a perfectly respectable place to start.

### The key power-play plays

These are the plays the shapes exist to produce. Learn the plays first; the shape is just where you stand to make them available.

#### The seam pass

```facts
Key: A seam runs through or across the middle of the defensive shape rather than around it — half-wall to half-wall, or half-wall to bumper
Goal: Any puck that crosses the middle forces every killer and the goalie to re-orient at once
Technique: Flat, hard, on the ice, to the receiver's stick, with a look or a fake sold first — Passing and Receiving covers the mechanics
Never: Force a seam that is not there — intercepted in the middle it is a shorthanded breakaway, and you have four teammates above the puck
```

A **seam** is a passing lane that runs through or across the middle of the defensive shape rather than around it — most often half-wall to half-wall, or half-wall to bumper.

- It works because a kill's shape is built to defend the *perimeter*. Any puck that crosses the middle forces every killer and the goalie to re-orient at once.
- **How to throw one — flat, hard, on the ice, to the receiver's stick, with a look or a fake sold first** — is standard passing technique under pressure, covered in [Passing and Receiving](../technique/passing_and_receiving.md). What is special-teams-specific is the judgement: **if the seam is not there, do not force it.** A seam pass intercepted in the middle is a shorthanded breakaway, and you have four teammates above the puck.

#### The one-timer from the off-wing

```facts
Technique: A one-timer is a shot taken directly off an incoming pass without stopping the puck
Position: Play your off-wing — left-hander on the right side — so the puck is on your inside hand and your body turns toward the net
Technique: Weight loaded on the back foot, body still and square before the pass leaves — Shooting covers the mechanics
Key: The threat is worth as much as the shot — a live weak-side one-timer forces one killer to stay wide
Goal: That wide killer is precisely how the bumper and the net front get free, before the puck ever comes to you
```

A **one-timer** is a shot taken directly off an incoming pass without stopping the puck. Playing your **off-wing** (left-hander on the right side, right-hander on the left) puts the puck on your inside hand and turns your whole body toward the net, which is what makes it possible. The shooting mechanics — weight loaded on the back foot, body still and square before the pass leaves — are in [Shooting](../technique/shooting.md).

The power-play point is a different one: **the threat is worth as much as the shot.** A live one-timer option on the weak side forces one killer to stay wide, and that is precisely how the bumper and the net front get free. You are being useful in the two seconds *before* the puck ever comes to you.

#### The royal road / slot-line pass

```facts
Key: The royal road runs down the middle of the ice from net to net; a pass crossing it just before a shot is the highest-value pass type measured, roughly double the next one
Goal: A goalie tracking the puck across the middle cannot stay square — they are off their angle and depth when the shot arrives
Action: Before you shoot from a half-wall, check the far side — a cross-seam pass beats a shot from the same spot almost every time
Key: Offensive Zone Play covers the measured finishing rate and its qualifications — take the number from there, with those words attached
Risk: The 20-30% range quoted in coaching material has no published primary source behind it; treat it as illustrative only
```

The **royal road** (also called the slot line) is an imaginary line running straight down the middle of the ice from net to net. A pass that **crosses** it immediately before a shot is the highest-value pass type measured, roughly double the next one. *(Published descriptions of exactly where the line runs disagree — some put it net-centre to net-centre as here, others stop it at the top of the circles. [Shooting](../technique/shooting.md) sets out the disagreement; nothing here turns on which is right.)*

- **Why it works:** a goalie who has to track the puck across the middle cannot stay square. They must break from small shuffles into a hard lateral push, and while pushing they are opening up, off their angle and off their depth. The shot arrives before they are set. See [Goaltender](../positions/goaltender.md) for the save mechanics from the other side.
- The concept was popularised by former NHL goaltender **Steve Valiquette**, whose shot-tracking work classified chances by whether the puck crossed the line. **The best-sourced measured figure is 15.50%** — the finishing rate on shots following a royal road / cross-slot pass, the highest-value pass type measured, roughly double the next one (Alan Wells' analysis of Ryan Stimson's Passing Project data, [via NHL.com](https://www.nhl.com/kraken/news/analytics-with-alison-dangerous-passes-327627898)). **Carry its qualification with it, because that qualification is part of the fact:** the Passing Project was **volunteer-tracked**, the sample is *"51,308 shots at 5v5"*, and the percentage counts **all attempts, not shots on goal** — and note where that qualification comes from: it is **not** in the NHL.com article, which gives only the 15.50% figure. The sample size and the all-attempts basis are stated by Wells himself, in *Weighted Shot Rates Based on the Passing Project* (Hockey Graphs, 6 September 2016) — so it is not comparable with a shots-on-goal conversion rate, and it is not a power-play measurement. [Offensive Zone Play](offensive_zone_play.md) covers this figure and its limits. Use that number, with those words attached. **⚠️ The 20–30% range you will see quoted in coaching material has no published primary source behind it** — it is not a power-play-specific measurement of the same thing, because nobody has published a separate power-play conversion rate for seam passes; treat it as illustrative only. Valiquette's own "76% of goals are green shots" split does at least trace back to Valiquette himself — he gave the figure directly to [NHL.com in December 2014](https://web.archive.org/web/20150924092445/http://www.nhl.com/ice/news.htm?id=744483), along with the detail that 30% of goals came on plays with a pass or carry across the royal road — but the Clear Sight Analytics tracking underneath it is proprietary and has never been replicated. [Shooting](../technique/shooting.md) sets out that source and its limits in full; [Offensive Zone Play](offensive_zone_play.md) carries the same figures. The direction of the effect is not in doubt, and it is why every modern power play is designed around cross-ice passing.
- **Practical version:** a cross-seam pass beats a shot from the same spot almost every time. Before you shoot from a half-wall, check the far side.

#### The point shot for tips and rebounds

```facts
Key: The point shot is not an attempt to score from the point — it is a delivery system for a tip or a rebound
Technique: Judge it on whether it arrives low, hard and on net with a body in front
Read: Shoot when the net-front player has established position and the goalie's view is obstructed, not before
Action: Change the angle first — walking two or three metres along the line takes the shot out of the lane the kill has set
Risk: A missed net is a free clear off the end boards and twenty seconds gone
```

**The point shot is not an attempt to score from the point.** It is a delivery system for a tip or a rebound, and should be judged on whether it arrives low, hard and on net with a body in front. Release, weight transfer and getting a shot through a blocking lane are in [Shooting](../technique/shooting.md).

The two special-teams reads: **shoot when the net-front player has established position and the goalie's view is obstructed, not before** — screening a goalie from outside the crease, without touching them and without crowding them so they cannot move, is legal — and **change the angle first**, because walking two or three metres along the line takes the shot out of the lane the kill has set. A missed net on a power play is a free clear off the end boards and twenty seconds gone.

#### The backdoor play

```facts
Position: The backdoor is the weak-side post — the far side of the net, away from the puck
Read: Whenever the kill's low defender commits to the strong side or to the net-front player, it is a 2-on-1 with nobody defending it
Action: Arrive late — time it, do not camp it; a player standing at the back post all shift is marked
Key: The pass comes low-to-high-to-backdoor or straight across from below the goal line — either way it crosses the royal road, which is why it converts
```

The **backdoor** is the weak-side post — the far side of the net, away from the puck. Whenever the kill's low defender commits to the strong side or to the net-front player, it is a 2-on-1 with nobody defending it.

**Arrive late.** A player standing at the back post all shift is marked; a player arriving as the shot or pass is released is not. Time it, do not camp it. The pass comes low-to-high-to-backdoor or straight across from below the goal line — either way it crosses the royal road, which is why it converts.

#### The walk-down from the half-wall

```facts
Action: Skate the puck down from the half-wall toward the goal line and inside rather than passing
Read: If the kill's low defender steps out to you, the net front is free; if they stay, you have a shot from the top of the circle with a moving goalie
Never: Walk to the corner — below the goal line and wide you are harmless; the threat comes from heading toward the dot
Technique: Head up, feet moving toward the net — the moment your feet stop, the kill resets
Action: If it is denied, reset to the point and start over
Risk: Losing the puck below the goal line is a wasted twenty seconds and usually a clear
```

The **walk-down** is the half-wall player skating the puck down toward the goal line and inside rather than passing. It forces a decision: if the kill's low defender steps out to you, the net front is free; if they stay, you have a shot from the top of the circle with a moving goalie.

**Walk to the middle, not to the corner** — below the goal line and wide you are harmless, and the threat comes from heading toward the dot. Head up, feet moving toward the net; the moment your feet stop the kill resets. If it is denied, **reset to the point and start over.** Losing the puck below the goal line on a power play is a wasted twenty seconds and usually a clear.

### Power play zone entries

```facts
Key: This is where most amateur power plays die — at the blue line, not in the zone
Priority: A clean, controlled entry is worth far more than a fast one; enter with possession and you have ninety seconds of pressure
Key: Zone Entries covers the entry toolkit and the tracked values — read the figures there
Action: Use the drop pass — it changes the carrier without changing the speed of the attack and makes the defending line commit to the wrong player
Action: Swing one or two players back to gather speed and receive already moving forward; the single swing is less predictable than the double
Action: Dump to a spot, not a player, and rim away from the goalie's trapezoid so a defender retrieves under pressure
Rule: The goalkeeper's restricted area is IIHF Rule 27.7, not the NHL's 27.8 — and England Ice Hockey's Rules & Regulations 22.3 say it is not currently enforced in England and Wales, so plan as though their goalie may come for a dumped puck
Rule: USA Hockey, Hockey Canada and CARHA write no trapezoid at all, so under those books their goalie may fetch a rimmed puck from any corner — though not freeze it out there, which USA Hockey 614(c) and Hockey Canada's Interpretation 3 to Rule 10.1(a), clause v, each make a minor
Read: Regroup rather than force — you can afford it and the kill cannot, but the cost is clock, so do not regroup out of habit
Risk: A puck turned over at the offensive blue line with four teammates ahead of it is the way coaches most often name for conceding a shorthanded goal
```

**This is where most amateur power plays die** — not in the zone. A kill defending its blue line is compact, has four players between you and the net, and has nothing to lose by conceding the neutral zone, while you have to bring a five-player structure through a narrow gate without a turnover. Units drill the in-zone setup for hours and the entry for minutes, then wonder why they never get set up.

**The entry toolkit itself — carry, chip, dump, delay, the drive-and-drop, and the data on what each is worth — is in [Zone Entries](zone_entries.md), and everything there applies here.** What changes with the extra man is only this:

1. **A clean, controlled entry is worth far more than a fast one.** Enter with possession and set up, and you have ninety seconds of pressure. Dump and lose the race and you have ten, then you do it all again. (The tracked data still favours the carry-in on the power play — roughly two and a half times the shots of a dump-in — but by a **narrower** margin than at even strength, because the extra man also makes dump-in retrievals easier. **Note where that particular figure comes from:** the power-play numbers are **PWHPA / Sportlogiq tracking of women's professional hockey**, not the NHL zone-entry study behind the even-strength figures, and the source does not state which season it covers. [Zone Entries](zone_entries.md) covers them and carries the full qualification. The advantage is real; it just is not larger than usual.)
2. **You do not get many attempts.** A failed entry — dump, lose the race, regroup, come again — burns a serious slice of a 120-second advantage. Two or three of those and the penalty is over. *(There is no measured figure for the cost of a failed entry; treat this as calibration, not data.)*
3. **The drop pass is the signature power-play entry**, because it changes the carrier without changing the speed of the attack and makes the defending line commit to the wrong player. **Arik Parnass's** tracking for the Special Teams Project across six NHL teams in 2015-16 found drop-pass entries produced **0.84 shot attempts per entry versus 0.70** for other entries — but took **15.5 seconds to execute versus 12.6**, which cancels most of the gain. (*His three categories run drop 15.49 s, regular 12.56 s, stretch pass 10.37 s. 12.6 is the figure that pairs with the 0.70, because he removed stretch-pass results from the non-drop bucket; comparing the drop against the stretch's 10.4 s instead is not like for like.*) His conclusion is the sensible one, and it is about your own team rather than theirs: keep **both** a polished regular scheme and a drop scheme, and **go to the drop when your primary scheme is in a slump.** *(Decade-old, small-sample study — indicative, not settled.)* Every kill now expects it, and a drop stolen at the red line is a shorthanded breakaway.
4. **The swing** — one or two players arcing back to gather speed and receive already moving forward — solves the entry's hardest problem: arriving at the line with speed and the puck at once. The single swing (one forward swings, one stays wide) is less predictable than the double.
5. **Dump to a spot, not a player.** The extra man means you will usually win a loose-puck race in the corner if you time the arrival. Rim away from the goalie's **trapezoid** — the marked area behind the net where an NHL or IIHF goalie may legally play the puck (NHL Rules 1.8 and 27.8; IIHF Rules 1.8 and 27.7), the KHL reportedly too though that one is unverified here — so a defender has to retrieve under pressure. ⚠️ **Under USA Hockey, Hockey Canada and CARHA there is no trapezoid at all**, none of the three using the word anywhere in its book, so under those books the goalie may fetch a rimmed puck from any corner — though not freeze it out there, which USA Hockey **Rule 614(c)** and Hockey Canada's **Interpretation 3 to Rule 10.1(a)**, clause v, each make a minor. How much rec, beer-league and youth hockey that adds up to is a question about leagues rather than about books, and this document has no count for it. See [Defender](../positions/defender.md). **🇬🇧 In England and Wales, treat that as unsettled.** The IIHF book carries the restricted area at Rule 27.7, but England Ice Hockey's *Rules & Regulations* 22.3 (2024-25) say it is *"not currently enforced"* and the In-House Rules are silent — so for you as the attacking team, plan as though their goalie may come and get it, and look behind the net for painted lines.
6. **Regroup rather than force.** Curl back out, reset to the point defenceman or the goalie, re-form the swing, attack with new speed and a different pattern. **You can afford this and the kill cannot** — they are tiring while you are not. The cost is clock, so do not regroup out of habit. **A puck turned over at the offensive blue line with four teammates ahead of it is the way coaches most often name for a power play conceding a shorthanded goal** — coaching consensus rather than a counted breakdown; nobody publishes shorthanded goals classified by the play that produced them, the same limit that applies to the prevalence claims elsewhere here.

### Retrievals and keeping possession

Most of a power play is not the set play. It is the twenty seconds of scrambling after the kill clears it or blocks a shot. Units that win here get three or four setups per power play; units that lose here get one.

#### When the kill clears it

```facts
Action: Decide in advance who chases — most teams send the net-front player or the weak-side half-wall and leave the point at the line
Never: Send two chasers — if two players chase, nobody is holding the zone
Goal: The chaser's job is not to win the puck but to arrive before the puck stops, so it can be moved on the move
Action: Everyone else re-forms while the chase is happening, not after — possession with no options is nothing
Read: If the clear leaves the zone entirely, regroup as a unit and re-enter properly rather than chasing in ones and twos
```

- **Decide in advance who chases.** Most teams send the net-front player or the weak-side half-wall and leave the point at the line. If two players chase, nobody is holding the zone.
- **The chaser's job is not to win the puck — it is to arrive before the puck stops.** A rolling puck retrieved on the move can be moved immediately; a dead puck against the boards is a battle you might lose.
- **Everyone else re-forms while the chase is happening**, not after. If the retrieval succeeds and the shape is not rebuilt, you have possession and no options.
- **If the clear leaves the zone entirely, do not chase it in ones and twos.** Regroup as a unit and re-enter properly — see the entries section above. A disorganised re-entry loses more time than a calm one.

#### The defenceman's job holding the line

```facts
Priority: As the lone point player, keeping the puck in the zone is your first responsibility and shooting is your second
Position: Stay inside the blue line — outside it you are not a passing option, and a puck reaching you there has cleared the zone
Technique: Take the puck off the wall with your feet already moving laterally, body on the wall, stick low and flat
Never: Lunge at a clear that has beaten you cleanly. Turn and skate instead; a missed hold with one defenceman back is a 2-on-0
Key: Holding is staying at the line to stop the puck leaving; pinching is stepping down the wall into the zone, and with one defenceman back it is almost never worth it
Convention: If your team runs two defencemen, only one holds aggressively at a time — the other backs up as the safety
Rule: Drifting into the neutral zone is not offside; offside is judged only at the instant the puck crosses the line into the zone (NHL Rule 83.1)
```

If you are the lone point player in a 1-3-1, **keeping the puck in the zone is your first responsibility and shooting is your second.** A power play that gets cleared has to re-enter against four killers already set in their structure, which is a far harder problem than holding a zone you are already in.

- **Stay inside the blue line.** Drifting out into the neutral zone is not offside — offside is only ever judged at the instant the puck crosses the line into the zone (Rule 83.1), and a puck already in the zone cannot make you offside. The real cost is simpler: with your skates outside the line you are no longer an option for a pass, and if the puck reaches you there the zone is cleared and the whole unit has to re-enter against a set kill.
- **Take the puck off the wall with your feet already moving laterally**, not standing square to the boards — that is how clears go past you.
- **Body on the wall, stick low and flat.** Most clears on the power play are along the boards at ankle height.
- **A hold you miss is a 2-on-0.** With one defenceman back, "keep it in at all costs" is wrong. If the clear beats you cleanly, turn and skate; do not lunge. See [Risk Management](../hockey-iq/risk_management.md).
- **Holding the line is not pinching, and the difference matters here.** *Holding* means staying at the offensive blue line and stopping the puck leaving. (Do not call it "stepping up" — a **step up** is moving *forward* to challenge an attacker early, in the neutral zone or at your **own** blue line. Different end of the rink, different play. See [Rink Map and Glossary](../foundation/rink_map_and_glossary.md).) *Pinching* means leaving the line and stepping **down** the wall into the zone to attack a puck below you. Holding is the point player's standing job on a power play. Pinching is an extra risk you take on top of it, and with one defenceman back it is almost never worth taking — a missed pinch puts you two strides deeper than a missed hold does.
- If your team runs two defencemen (umbrella, 2-3), **only one holds aggressively at a time** — the other backs up as the safety. Same partnership discipline as an even-strength pinch, described in [Defender](../positions/defender.md).

### 5-on-3

```facts
Key: A two-man advantage is a different game, not a better power play — it fails when a unit plays it like a 5-on-4
Read: Three killers sit in a tight triangle around the slot and concede everything outside it — nobody is coming to hunt you on the perimeter
Action: Spread out into the spread/box shape and make the triangle try to cover the whole zone
Action: Move the puck side to side across the royal road, not around the perimeter, until the goalie cannot get set
Never: Shoot from the outside into three shot-blockers. Wait for the goalie to be moving instead, but do not pass for two minutes without shooting
Goal: The goal usually comes from a one-timer at the far post or the second-wave rebound, so have someone at the net for both
Rule: A team never plays fewer than three skaters — a third penalty's clock waits and a substitute keeps them at three (NHL Rule 26.1)
Rule: A goal against a shorthanded team terminates only the minor with the least time remaining, so a 5-on-3 becomes a 5-on-4 (NHL Rule 16.2)
Rule: A goal releases only a minor or bench minor (NHL and IIHF Rule 16.2), so a major is served in full however many you score (NHL and IIHF 20.2) — and a goal scored on a penalty shot releases nothing at all (16.2 in terms, and NHL 24.6)
Rule: Under the In-House Rules a fighting major in NIHL, NIHL1 or NIHL2 in the last five minutes of regulation or in overtime also carries an automatic game misconduct (Section 6, sitting on top of the IIHF book)
```

A two-man advantage is a different game, not a better power play. It should almost always produce a goal, and when it does not it is nearly always because the unit played it like a 5-on-4.

**Why it is different:** three killers can defend a triangle. They will sit in a tight triangle around the slot — usually two low in front of the net and one high — concede everything outside it, and block shots. **Sustained pressure on the puck is not available to them**, because every killer who leaves the triangle opens a hole nobody can fill. That does not mean you will never be touched: a three-man kill will still jump a bobbled reception, close hard on anyone who walks into the slot, and throw a stick or a body at a shooter. What it means is that **nobody is coming to hunt you on the perimeter** — so you have time out there that does not exist at 5-on-4, and the only way to lose it is to hurry.

![](diagram:pp-spread-5on3)

**How to play it:**

- **Spread out** into the spread/box shape above, and make the triangle try to cover the whole zone.
- **Move the puck side to side across the royal road, not around the perimeter.** Every cross-ice pass forces the goalie into a full lateral push; three or four in a row and they will not be set. **Do not shoot from the outside into three shot-blockers** — with nobody hunting you there is no reason to rush, so wait for the goalie to be moving.
- **The goal usually comes from a one-timer at the far post or the second-wave rebound.** Have someone at the net for both. Be patient but not passive: two minutes of pretty passing with no shot is a failed 5-on-3.

**Rules that matter here:**

> **Terminology warning — "delayed penalty" means two different things in the rulebook, and both appear in this document.**
> - **Sense 1 — the signalled penalty (Rule 15.1).** The referee has seen an infraction by the team *without* the puck, raises an arm, and play continues until the offending team gains control. This is the one where you pull your goalie for an extra attacker.
> - **Sense 2 — the delayed *clock* (Rule 26).** A third penalty assessed to a team that already has two players serving. The third penalty's time does not begin until one of the first two expires. Nobody's arm is raised and nobody pulls a goalie.
>
> They are unrelated. Where this document says "delayed penalty" it says which sense it means.

- **A team never plays with fewer than three skaters.** If a third player is penalised while two are already serving, the third penalty's clock does not start until one of the first two expires (a **delayed penalty in the Rule 26 sense**), and the team may put a substitute on the ice to stay at three (NHL Rule 26.1). So a 5-on-2 does not exist.
- **A goal only releases one penalty.** When you score against a shorthanded team, the minor penalty **with the least time remaining** terminates (Rule 16.2). A 5-on-3 therefore becomes a 5-on-4, not a 5-on-5 — and if you were about to get another chance, take it immediately while the kill is reorganising.
- **This does not apply to major penalties**, which are served in full regardless of goals, and **does not apply to penalty-shot goals**. Rule 16.2 carries the second of those in terms — *"This rule does not apply when a goal is scored on a penalty shot (i.e. offending team's penalized player(s) do not get released on the scoring of a goal on a penalty shot)"*, and **Rule 24.6** writes it again from the other end, *"No penalty shall expire when a goal is scored against a team on a penalty shot"* — and the first only by its scope, since it releases *"the minor or bench minor penalty"* and nothing else; the affirmative sentence for the major is **Rule 20.2**, *"the penalized player serving the major penalty does not leave the penalty bench when the opposing team scores."* IIHF 16.2 and 26.1 are the NHL's rules in the same words on all of this, including the three-skater floor and the delayed clock, and IIHF 20.2 is the NHL's 20.2 but for writing *"penalty box"* where the NHL writes *"penalty bench"* — so a British reader can take this whole subsection as written.
- **One British addition on majors.** Under the In-House Rules, fighting in NIHL (all leagues), the SNL and BUIHA Varsity is *"penalised with a 5-minute Major Penalty"* — and in **NIHL, NIHL1 and NIHL2**, *"any fighting major assessed in the last 5 minutes of regulation time (including overtime) will also carry an automatic Game Misconduct."* That does not change the kill itself: under **IIHF Rule 20.3** a substitute goes to the box immediately and serves the five minutes, so you are killing a full major either way. What it changes is your bench for the rest of the night, at exactly the point in a game when you can least afford to lose a body. See [UK and England Rules](../foundation/uk_rules.md).

## PENALTY KILL

### The one idea behind every penalty kill

```facts
Key: You cannot cover five players with four, so stop trying
Goal: Decide which shot you are willing to give up, then spend two minutes making sure it is the only shot you give up
Key: Every kill concedes something — a box the middle of the slot, a diamond the outside and the backdoor, a wedge+1 the perimeter
Never: Cover something that was never yours — that is how the thing that was yours becomes a goal
Mindset: Discipline beats effort on a penalty kill, every time
Key: Time is on your side and nothing else is — three point shots and no break beats forty clean seconds and then a break
```

You cannot cover five players with four. **So stop trying.** A penalty kill is a decision about **which shot you are willing to give up** — and then a two-minute exercise in making sure that is the only shot you give up.

Every kill concedes something. A box concedes the middle of the slot. A diamond concedes the outside and the backdoor. A wedge+1 concedes the perimeter. The failure mode is always the same: a killer decides to cover something that was never theirs, and the thing that *was* theirs becomes a goal. **Discipline beats effort on a penalty kill, every time.**

The second idea: **time is on your side and nothing else is.** Two minutes is not long. A kill that concedes zero clean looks for 40 seconds and then breaks is worse than one that concedes three shots from the point and never breaks.

### Penalty kill formations

```facts
Convention: Which shape you run is a coaching choice — the same four players can run all three in one game
Action: Know which one is called before the faceoff
Key: These are the in-zone shapes, a separate decision from the neutral-zone pressure scheme below, not a competing one
Key: The box is the shape almost everyone is taught first and the baseline the other two are described against
Risk: No public count of how many NHL teams run each as a base shape exists. Treat any "most teams use X" claim as an impression, not a measurement
```

> Again: which shape you run is a **coaching choice**. The same four players can run all three of these in one game. **Know which one is called before the faceoff.**
>
> **On "which one is most common" — an honest answer.** These three are the shapes you defend the *zone* with, once the power play is set up. They are a separate decision from the neutral-zone pressure scheme further below (the 1-3, the 1-1-2 and so on), which is how you meet the entry; a team runs one of each in the same kill and there is no contradiction. Within the in-zone group, the **box is the shape almost everyone is taught first** and the baseline the other two are described against. Coaching material commonly describes the **wedge+1** as widely used at NHL level, and the diamond as the standard counter to a 1-3-1. **No public count of how many NHL teams run each as their base shape exists**, so treat any "most teams use X" claim — including the ones here — as an impression from coaching sources rather than a measured figure.

#### Box — the shape everyone learns first

```facts
Position: A rectangle around the slot — two forwards high, one on each point, and two defencemen low covering the corners and the goalmouth
Key: A box has two players high and no single "top" — if you are describing "the top of the box", you are describing a diamond
Action: High forwards deny the point time and space, stay in shooting lanes, and never both drift to the same side
Action: The strong-side D pressures the puck below the goal line or in the low circle; the weak-side D owns the goalmouth and the backdoor
Technique: The box slides with the puck rather than rotating — all four shift together, keeping the shape intact
Read: Very good against an overload, which it can out-wait, and against a 2-3, whose attack aims where a box is strongest
Countered by: A 1-3-1 — nobody in a box is assigned to the middle of the slot, so the bumper stands there uncovered by design
```

**The shape:** a rectangle around the slot. **Two players high** — both forwards, one on each side, each responsible for one point — and **two players low**, both defencemen, covering the two low corners and the goalmouth.

**A box has no single "top".** Two forwards, side by side, is the whole point. If you find yourself describing "the top of the box", you are describing a diamond.

![](diagram:pk-box)

**The jobs:**

- **High forwards:** deny the point players time and space, stay in shooting lanes, and never both drift to the same side. Your inside shoulder should be turned so you can see your point man and the middle at once.
- **Low defencemen:** the strong-side D pressures the puck when it comes below the goal line or to the low circle; the weak-side D owns the goalmouth and the backdoor. Same partnership logic as even-strength [Defensive Zone Coverage](defensive_zone_coverage.md).
- **Everyone:** the box **slides** with the puck rather than rotating. When the puck moves across, all four shift together, keeping the shape intact.

**Strengths:** simple, teachable, hard to break down, and excellent at protecting the goalmouth and the low slot. Very good against an **overload**, which it can simply out-wait, and against a **2-3**, whose entire attack aims at the two areas a box defends best.

**Weaknesses:** **nobody in a box is assigned to the middle of the slot.** Against a 1-3-1 the bumper stands in the hole in the middle of your rectangle and is uncovered by design. It is also passive by nature: a box that never pressures gives a skilled unit unlimited time on the perimeter to find the seam.

#### Diamond — a single apex

```facts
Position: One player high at the apex, two in the middle either side, one low — exactly one player at the top
Goal: The two middles squeeze the slot from either side so a 1-3-1's bumper can be accounted for
Action: The apex pressures the point and takes away the middle shooting lane — you are the only player up there, so do not get walked around
Action: Each middle takes a half-wall and both collapse onto the bumper when the puck goes to the middle
Position: The low player owns the goalmouth, alone
Read: Use it against a 1-3-1, or against an umbrella where the apex can pressure the middle point man
Countered by: A quick cross-seam pass to the far half-wall — the shape is narrow, and the far-side one-timer is on if the middles are slow
Risk: The single low player faces a net-front presence and a backdoor arriver at once — a 2-on-1 you are choosing to concede
```

**The shape:** one player **high** at the apex, **two in the middle** on either side, and **one low**. A diamond, not a rectangle: it has exactly one player at the top.

**Why it exists:** because a box leaves the bumper unmarked. The diamond's two middle players squeeze the middle of the slot from either side, so the 1-3-1's bumper can be accounted for. Coaches also use it against a three-high **umbrella**, where the apex can pressure the middle point man who would otherwise have a free shot.

![](diagram:pk-diamond)

**The jobs:**

- **Apex** (often the centre, but this varies by team): pressure the point player, take away the middle shooting lane, and do not get walked around. You are the only player up there — if you overcommit, the point is uncovered.
- **Two middles:** each takes a half-wall, and both collapse onto the bumper when the puck goes to the middle. You are working two jobs at once; the timing of when you leave the wall for the middle is the whole art of playing this position.
- **Low:** own the goalmouth. You are alone down there.

**Strengths:** the only common shape that starts with an answer to the bumper; puts real pressure on the point; encourages turnovers high in the zone, which is where shorthanded chances come from.

**Weaknesses:** **narrow.** A quick cross-seam pass to the far half-wall pulls it apart, and the far-side one-timer is available if the middles are slow. The single low player is alone against a net-front presence *and* a backdoor arriver — a genuine 2-on-1 that you are choosing to concede. Also more mentally demanding than a box; a confused diamond is worse than a competent box.

#### Wedge+1 (also called triangle-and-one)

```facts
Position: Three killers in a tight triangle around the net — two defencemen just off the crease edges, one forward at the mid-to-high slot; the players rotate through the shape, the shape holds its ground
Action: The wedge stays tight, protects the slot and goalmouth, and does not chase; it blocks shots from in the lane and on its feet, never from a screening position
Action: The +1 pressures whoever has the puck, by whichever route your coach teaches — make every possession uncomfortable, do not try to win the puck, never end up trailing it round the outside
Technique: When your pressure forces the puck to move, the nearest wedge player becomes the new +1 and you drop into the triangle
Key: The exchange is the hard part — everyone must know who is out and who is in, every second
Countered by: A +1 who chases without purpose; you are then killing with three players
Risk: High cognitive load makes it a poor choice for a young or ad-hoc unit
Convention: Coaching material describes it as popular at NHL level, but no league-wide count is published, so treat prevalence as unverified; published descriptions also differ on the +1's route — straight out from the goal, sweeping across the top of the zone, or roaming — so ask your coach
```

**The shape:** **three killers hold a tight triangle** around the net — two defencemen just off the crease edges, one forward at the mid-to-high slot — while the **fourth player, the "+1", pressures whoever has the puck**. **Exactly how he does that is where published descriptions part company, so ask your coach which one you are running:** some describe him activating in a straight line out from the goal towards the puck carrier; some have him sweeping laterally across the top of the zone, above the circles, to keep the power play on one side of the ice; some have him roaming more freely, challenging on the wall or at the point. What every description agrees on is the handover below — and that a +1 who simply follows the puck round the perimeter has broken the shape rather than run it. When the puck moves, he stops and the nearest wedge player steps out as the new +1; a +1 who chases is the counter to this system, not the system, and leaves you killing with three players.

![](diagram:pk-wedge-plus-1)

**The jobs:**

- **The wedge (three players):** stay tight, protect the slot and the goalmouth, and rotate as a connected unit. You do not chase. The players rotate through the shape; the shape itself holds its ground. **Block shots properly or not at all:** be in the lane before the shot rather than sliding into it, stay on your feet where you can, and never block from a screening position. [Shot blocking](#shot-blocking) below sets out what is specific to the kill, and [Defender](../positions/defender.md) and [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md) own the technique itself.
- **The +1:** pressure whoever has the puck, by whichever route your coach teaches — see above; the sources differ. Your job is to make every possession uncomfortable, not to win the puck, and never to end up trailing it round the outside.
- **The exchange:** when your pressure forces the puck to move, the nearest wedge player becomes the new +1 and you drop into the triangle. This handover is the hard part and the reason the system needs practice — everyone must know who is out and who is in, every second.

**Strengths:** it protects the highest-danger ice absolutely while still applying pressure, which is why it is **commonly used at NHL level** — [The Coaches Site](https://members.thecoachessite.com/article/explained-wedge-penalty-kill-formation) describes it as popular with most NHL clubs, though **no league-wide count of who runs what is published, so treat the prevalence claim as unverified.** It is very hard for a 1-3-1 to get a clean look at the middle. It creates turnovers and therefore shorthanded chances.

**Weaknesses:** high cognitive load — it demands constant awareness of a rotating assignment, which is why it is a poor choice for a young or ad-hoc unit. If the +1 chases without purpose, you are effectively killing with three players.

#### Aggressive versus passive

```facts
Key: The pressure setting is a separate decision from the shape itself
Read: Go passive — hold structure, concede the perimeter, block lanes — when protecting a lead, when tired, when outgunned, or late in a kill
Options: Go aggressive to force errors; the best-known version is the Czech press, where the high forward genuinely attacks the point player
Risk: Aggression creates chaos, and chaos with four players against five is not usually your friend
Convention: That aggression pushes shots outside while passivity concedes closer ones is a coaching claim, not a measured one. Treat it as unverified
Never: Pressure alone. Pressure as a unit or not at all; one player pressuring while three sit is how seams open
```

Every shape has a pressure setting, and it is a separate decision from the shape itself.

- **Passive:** hold the structure, concede the perimeter, block lanes, wait for a clear. Lowest variance. Right when you are protecting a lead, when you are tired, when the opposition power play is much better than you, or late in a kill.
- **Aggressive:** attack the puck carrier and try to force errors. The best-known version is the **Czech press**, where the high forward genuinely attacks the point player rather than just threatening to. Higher variance: it forces mistakes and creates shorthanded chances, but it also creates chaos, and chaos with four players against five is not usually your friend.
- **The honest trade-off:** the coaching consensus is that aggression pushes shots outside and to the wings while passivity concedes fewer shots overall but allows more of them from close range. **That trade-off is a coaching claim, not a measured one — no published tracking data separates shot location by penalty-kill pressure setting, so treat it as unverified.** What is not in doubt is the structural point: pressure spends a killer to buy a mistake, and if the mistake does not come you are defending five with three.
- **The one rule:** **pressure as a unit or not at all.** One player pressuring while the other three sit is how seams open.

### The PK forecheck and neutral-zone pressure schemes

```facts
Key: A forecheck is pressure applied in the opponent's end; a neutral-zone scheme is how you meet them between the blue lines
Goal: On the kill these are one conversation — deciding where to make the power play uncomfortable
Key: F1/F2 are roles, not people — defined by who is first and second to the puck, not by position
```

A **forecheck** is pressure applied in the opponent's end; a neutral-zone scheme is how you meet them between the blue lines. On the kill these are the same conversation, because the whole point is deciding **where** to make the power play uncomfortable.

**F1/F2 are roles, not people** — defined by who is first and second to the puck, not by position.

#### The four you will meet

```facts
Options: The 1-3 — one forward on the puck, the other forward and both defencemen in a line across the ice behind him
Read: Passive 1-3 gives token pressure to make them commit to a side; the same-side press has F1 angle the carrier and F2 close to force a dump or turnover
Risk: With F1 beaten or the three-man line spread, the 1-3 has a lot of ice behind it
Options: The 1-1-2 — F1 on the puck, F2 layered behind on the same side, two defencemen abreast near the line
Countered by: Quick direction changes and drop passes at the red line, which slide underneath F1 and F2 at once
Options: The retreating box (2-2) — all four backing up together; safe and easy, and vulnerable to drop passes and direction changes
Options: Tandem pressure — two forwards take turns attacking the carrier; if both are beaten at once the kill is a 2-on-2 with no support
Convention: The 1-1-2 is commonly described as a current NHL default for neutral-zone pressure, but that prevalence claim is not independently verified
```

- **The 1-3** — one forward up on the puck, the other forward and both defencemen in a line across the ice behind him. In the **passive** version F1 gives token pressure to make the power play commit to a side while the three behind back up in unison, refusing to be split; the goal is not to win the puck, it is to **make them dump it in**. In the **same-side press** version F1 angles the carrier to one side and F2 closes from that side to force a dump or a turnover — more pressure, but it needs killers who can read and accelerate. **Weakness:** with F1 beaten or the three-line spread, there is a lot of ice behind it.
- **The 1-1-2** — F1 up on the puck, F2 layered behind on the *same* side forming a wall parallel to the boards, two defencemen abreast near the line. Aggressive without over-committing; it bleeds clock and forces contested entries. Coaching material commonly describes it as a current NHL default **for neutral-zone pressure**, which is a different decision from the in-zone shape above, not a competing one — and **that prevalence claim is not independently verified.** **Weakness:** quick direction changes and drop passes at the red line slide underneath F1 and F2 at once.
- **The retreating box (2-2)** — two forwards abreast, two defencemen abreast, all four backing up together. Purely reactive, safe, easy, and vulnerable to drop passes and direction changes.
- **Tandem pressure** — two forwards take turns attacking the carrier, one peeling off as the other arrives. Exhausting to play against and exhausting to play; if both get beaten at once the kill is a 2-on-2 with no support.

#### Killing the entry — what all of this is for

```facts
Goal: The most effective place to kill a penalty is before the puck enters your zone — a power play that never gets set up never uses its extra man
Action: Take away the middle and force them to one side; angle with your body, not your stick
Action: Stand up at the blue line as a pair — both defencemen step or both retreat; one of each is a hole
Action: Attack the receiver, not just the carrier — the dangerous player is the one arriving with speed off a drop or a swing
Goal: Make them dump it; force three failed entries and the penalty is over without you ever defending your own zone
Never: Lunge or dive at the blue line — a missed poke check at your own line is a clean entry with you out of the play
Read: Pressure only if you can actually get there, the carrier is looking down or facing the boards, you are fresh, and the score allows it
```

**The most effective place to kill a penalty is before the puck enters your zone**, and every scheme above serves that one sentence. The reason is arithmetic: a power play that never gets set up never gets to use its extra man. Once they are established in a shape you are managing damage; in the neutral zone you are 4-on-5 in a space where you can dictate.

- **Take away the middle and force them to one side.** A power play made to enter along one boards has removed half its own options. Angle with your body, not your stick.
- **Stand up at the blue line as a pair.** Both defencemen step, or both retreat. One of each is a hole.
- **Attack the receiver, not just the carrier.** The dangerous player on entry is usually the one arriving with speed off a drop or a swing. Taking away the target is easier than taking away the puck.
- **Make them dump it.** A dump-in with your defencemen already turned is a win. You do not have to force turnovers to kill a penalty; you just have to keep them out. Force three failed entries and the penalty is over without you ever defending your own zone.
- **Do not lunge or dive at the blue line.** A missed poke check at your own line is a clean entry with you out of the play and your team down two.

**Pressure or retreat? Judge it in this order:** (1) **Can you actually get there** — pressure arriving a stride late is worse than none, because you have spent a player for nothing. (2) **Where is the puck** — attack a carrier looking down, receiving badly, or facing the boards; never one with head up and support both sides. (3) **What is the clock** — pressure early while you are fresh; in the last 30 seconds or when gassed, retreat and make them beat structure. (4) **What is the score** — protecting a one-goal lead late is not the time for the Czech press; see [Game Management](game_management.md). (5) **Who is on** — a unit with one elite carrier is often worth attacking; five good passers will punish you for it.

### Clearing, blocking, sticks and discipline

#### You may ice the puck freely — where your rule set allows it

```facts
Rule: A team below the opposing team's on-ice numerical strength cannot be called for icing — play continues and no violation is called (NHL Rule 81.6) — but not unconditionally: the NHL withdraws it in one major-penalty case, and USA Hockey withholds it by classification (Rule 624(b)(1))
Rule: IIHF Rule 81.6 grants the shorthanded icing exemption in the same words but carries neither of the two carve-outs, so in UK hockey it is unconditional
Rule: The NHL withdraws the icing exemption from a team shorthanded by a major that has left nobody on the penalty bench — "Icing will be called" (Rule 81.6); the IIHF book has no such paragraph
Rule: USA Hockey grants the nullification only at Youth 15-Only and above, Girls 16U and above, High School and Adult (Rule 624(b)(1)); below those levels a shorthanded team can be called for icing
Rule: Release the puck before your penalty expires and the icing does not apply, even if the penalty ends while it is travelling (NHL Rule 81.6)
Rule: Shooting the puck directly out of the playing surface from your defending zone is a delay-of-game minor even while shorthanded, under NHL Rule 63.2(iii) and IIHF Rule 63.2(III) alike — so it binds a British kill too, and the IIHF's bench clause is the wider of the two
Rule: The "no glass" carve-out is boards with none mounted above them, not the benches — over the glass behind the bench is penalised, only a puck straight into the bench is exempt (NHL 63.2(iii) and NOTE; IIHF 63.2(III) says "no protective glass" and reaches glass "surrounding" the bench, wider)
Rule: USA Hockey writes no such minor for putting the puck out of play — Rule 610(c) penalises only a deliberate shoot-out, and 631(d) makes an accidental one a last-play faceoff; Hockey Canada 10.1(ii) is deliberate-only too, and reaches a goaltender as well as a skater
Action: Where the exemption applies, fire it the length of the ice the moment you have it — no faceoff, no line-change restriction, and a chance to change
Action: Aim clears hard and flat, not over the glass — clearing the glass costs the minor, not the direction; hard off the glass and staying in is fine — into the corner away from their goalie's trapezoid
Never: Stretch "numerical strength" beyond penalties — five skaters defending a pulled goalie are not below it and can be called for icing
```

Under **NHL Rule 81.1**, icing applies only to a team "equal or superior in numerical strength (power-play)" to the opponent, and **Rule 81.6** states it directly: "If the puck was so shot by a player of a side below the numerical strength of the opposing team, play shall continue and the icing violation shall not be called." **A shorthanded team cannot be iced.** The test is whether you are *below* the opponent's strength, not who is serving the penalty — so at 4-on-4 both teams *can* be iced (equal strength), and at 6-on-4 with the power play's goalie pulled the four-skater team still cannot be.

**⚠️ Do not stretch "numerical strength" beyond penalties.** It is a natural-sounding leap to say that five skaters defending a pulled goalie are below six and so cannot be iced. **They can be, and the rulebook says so three ways:** Rule 81.1 glosses the term as *"equal or superior in numerical strength **(power-play)**"*; Rule 84.1 counts a goalkeeper *inside* numerical strength, setting overtime at *"three (3) skaters **and one (1) goalkeeper**"*, so six against six is equal; and Rule 81.3 expressly preserves an icing call where the opposing *"goalkeeper is legitimately out of the crease in an attempt to go to the players' bench to be substituted for an extra attacker"* — *"the icing should not be nullified."* Defending a 6-on-5 is handled in [Game Management](game_management.md).

**There are two carve-outs — never state the exemption without them. Neither of them is the IIHF's**, which matters if you play in Britain; the UK position is set out at the end of this section.

**Carve-out 1 — the NHL's empty penalty bench, written into Rule 81.6 itself.** A team shorthanded **by reason of a major penalty** that has *"neglected to ensure there is a player on the penalty bench to exit upon the expiry of the penalty"* **is not permitted to ice the puck** — the rule says flatly, *"Icing will be called."* They keep playing shorthanded and may substitute for the penalised player at the next stoppage. In practice this is an administrative failure on the bench, not a playing decision, but it means the exemption is not quite unconditional: on a five-minute major, make sure somebody is sitting in the box ready to come out.

**Carve-out 2 — USA Hockey restricts the exemption by classification, and for most readers this is the one that matters.** The nullification in **USA Hockey Rule 624(b)(1)** is prefixed with a classification limit: *"(For all Youth 15-Only and above, Girls 16U and above, High School and Adult classifications only) The offending team is short-handed (below the on-ice numerical strength of their opponent) when the puck is shot."* **Below those levels the nullification simply does not exist, so a shorthanded team cannot ice the puck at all** — a length-of-the-ice clear on the kill comes straight back for a defensive-zone draw. If you play or coach youth hockey under USA Hockey rules, your single biggest structural advantage on a kill is not available to you, and a kill built on clearing the length of the ice will just feed the power play repeated defensive-zone faceoffs.

**When is it judged?** *The rulebook does not state a general "moment of the shot" test, so what follows is an interpretation drawn from the two places it does put a timestamp on.* Rule 81.4 says that for the line-change ban "the determination of players on ice will be made when the puck leaves the offending player's stick." Rule 81.6 says that when a penalty is about to expire, icing is judged "at the instant the penalty expires" — and, explicitly, "should the puck be released from the stick of the player shooting the puck down the ice prior to the penalty expiring, the icing infraction shall not apply." **Both point the same way: release the puck before your penalty expires and you are still exempt, even if the penalty ends while the puck is travelling.** That is a real tactical detail — with two seconds left on the kill, fire it.

**How to use it:**

- **Where the exemption applies, fire it the length of the ice the moment you have it.** There is no penalty, no faceoff, and no line-change restriction. This is your single biggest structural advantage on a kill.
- **You do not need to look for a teammate.** Getting the puck 200 feet away buys you a few seconds of clock and, crucially, a chance to change tired players. (*How many seconds depends entirely on how fast they retrieve and regroup; there is no measured figure to put on it.*)
- **Aim to make the retrieval awkward** — into the corner away from their goalie's trapezoid, or hard around the boards — so they cannot set up a clean regroup.
- **Beware the one thing that is still a penalty:** under **Rule 63.2(iii)**, shooting or batting the puck **directly (non-deflected) out of the playing surface from your defending zone** is a minor for delay of game — even while shorthanded. There is no penalty-kill exception. Off the glass and staying in is fine; a deflection out is fine; a clean flip over the glass is a second penalty and a 5-on-3 against you. Aim your clears **hard and flat rather than lofting them over the glass** — it is clearing the glass that costs you the minor, not the direction, and a clear played hard **off** the glass and staying in the rink is exactly what you want. ⚠️ **🇬🇧 And this is not a North American footnote — the IIHF writes the same provision in words of its own, so it is the rule a British kill is actually under.** Its **Rule 63.2(III)** assesses the minor *"on any Player who shoots or bats (using their hand or stick) the puck directly (non-deflected) out of the playing surface from their defending zone, except where there is no protective glass"*, with the same determining factor and the same two Note carve-outs, unchanged between the 2025/26 and 2026/27 editions. **Three written exceptions matter on a kill — and then the two books that write no such minor at all:**
  - **"Except where there is no glass"** — the rule's own wording, and **the IIHF's reads *"except where there is no protective glass"***. Where a stretch of boards has no glass mounted above it, as at many rec and older facilities, the penalty does not apply. **This is about rink construction, not about the benches** — see the next point, because the bench is the place this most often gets read backwards.
  - **Into the players' benches.** The rule's NOTE: "When the puck is shot into the players' bench, the penalty will not apply." **The IIHF Note says the same** — *"When the puck is shot into the Players' bench, the penalty will not apply."* But shooting it **over** the glass *behind* the benches **is** penalised. ⚠️ **And the IIHF's bench clause is written wider than the NHL's, not narrower.** The NHL's reads *"when the puck is shot over the glass 'behind' the players' bench, the penalty will be assessed"*; the IIHF's reads *"when the puck is shot over the protective glass **surrounding** the Players' bench, the penalty will be assessed."* **"Surrounding" appears to reach the glass at the sides of the bench that "behind" does not obviously reach** — so a British kill has slightly less room here, not more. Neither book explains the difference. ⚠️ **And the IIHF Situation Handbook narrows the bench carve-out further than the NOTE's words suggest, so do not aim for the bench.** At **Situation 63.24** — put in the same words in the 2025/26 (v1.1) and 2026/27 Handbooks — a defender clears the puck high off the glass, it goes **over** the glass and out of play, and it *lands* in the Players' Bench: the answer is *"Yes"*, still a minor. *"The determining factor… is where the puck leaves the playing surface. If you use an imaginary plane that continues upwards to the roof from the top of the glass, should the puck break that plane as it goes out of play, then a minor penalty is to be assessed regardless as to the final resting place of the puck. When the puck is shot **directly** into the Players' Bench (where there is no glass), no penalty is to be assessed."* **So the exemption is for a puck that goes straight in through the gap, not one that clears the glass and drops in.** **Situation 63.28** closes the other door: a player who has broken their stick and **scoops the puck up with a glove**, sending it over the glass behind the players' bench, takes the minor, *"considered shooting or batting the puck with the hand resulting in the puck leaving the playing surface."* And **Situation 63.3** confirms the height point at the other bench — where the puck *"clears the glass at a point in the Penalty Bench where the glass is lower than the normal height around the rink, no penalty shall be assessed."* The Handbook's Foreword ranks it below the book — *"in case of any contradictions, the valid IIHF Rulebook is always the basis"* — so treat these as how it is officiated rather than as the rule itself.
  - **Directly off a faceoff.** The same NOTE: "When the puck goes out of the playing area directly off a face-off, no penalty shall be assessed." **The IIHF Note carries it too** — *"When the puck goes out of the playing area directly off a 'face-off', no penalty shall be assessed."* This is the one you will actually use — on a defensive-zone draw while shorthanded, a centre who wins the puck straight out of the rink has given up a faceoff, not a penalty.
  - ⚠️ **The other two books do not write this limb at all, so do not carry it into their games as a rule.** USA Hockey **610(c)** reaches only a deliberate shoot-out, with **631(d)** sending an accidental one to a last-play faceoff; **Hockey Canada 10.1(ii)** likewise reaches only a puck *"deliberately"* shot, batted or thrown out of the playing surface — though by *"a player"*, which its Glossary makes include a goaltender *"except where explicitly stated"*, and its **Rule 6.3(e)(i) Note 1** sends *"any player of either team"* to that limb. What names *"a goaltender"* alone is the direct, non-deliberate limb at **10.1(v)**, confined to one who *"shoots or bats the puck directly out of the playing surface, with their stick."* Under both books a shorthanded skater who flips one over the glass **by accident** concedes possession, not two minutes — but a deliberate one is the minor, and under Hockey Canada that reaches a goaltender clearing the puck as much as a skater. ⚠️ **Hockey Canada does attach a consequence, though, and it is not nothing:** **Rule 6.4(a)** provides that *"in Junior and Senior divisions, where a player of the defending team, excluding the goaltender, unintentionally shoots, bats, throws, kicks, or otherwise directs the puck outside the playing surface, no penalty will be assessed, but the defending team may not make a line change"* — so in those divisions an accidental one costs you the change, which on a kill is most of what you were clearing for. Its Note 1 sends a *deliberate* one back to the penalty: *"they must be penalized, as per Rule 10.1 (ii) – Delay of Game."* [Risk Management](../hockey-iq/risk_management.md) carries all four books in full.
- **And beware the second penalty a kill talks itself into, because it is the more expensive: smothering the puck to beg for a whistle.** A skater other than the goalie who **deliberately falls on or gathers the puck into their body** takes a **minor** (**NHL 63.2(v)** and **IIHF 63.2(V)** for delay of game; **USA Hockey 614(a)** and **Hockey Canada 10.2(a)(iv)** each attach a condition the NHL does not, and Hockey Canada calls the penalty handling the puck) — and **where the puck is inside your own goal crease that same act is a penalty shot instead** (NHL 67.4, NHL and IIHF 63.6; Hockey Canada 10.2(a)(v) where the *infraction* occurs there; under USA Hockey 614(b) *"a penalty shot/optional minor penalty"*, the choice belonging to the non-offending team under 406(a)), **and an awarded goal if you have pulled your own goalie** — under NHL and IIHF 67.5 and Hockey Canada 10.2(a)(vi) whenever the goalkeeper has been replaced by an extra attacker, and under USA Hockey 614(b) only where the act *"has prevented an obvious and imminent goal"*. **Smothering the puck is not a legal kill tactic anywhere on the ice, and inside your own crease all four books put a penalty shot on the table.** Set out in full in [Smothering the puck to force a whistle](#smothering-the-puck-to-force-a-whistle).

> **If you play in Britain — the exemption is unconditional, and this is settled against the book.**
>
> **IIHF Rule 81.6 gives the exemption in the same words as the NHL's:** *"If the puck was so shot by a Player of a side below the numerical strength of the opposing team, play shall continue, and the 'icing' violation shall not be called."* **The expiry test is in the same words too**, differing only where the IIHF capitalises *"Player"* as the defined term it is and puts *"icing"* in quotation marks — *"Should the puck be released from the stick of the Player shooting the puck down the ice prior to the penalty expiring, the 'icing' infraction shall not apply."* So shorthanded, you may fire it the length of the ice, and with two seconds left on the kill you still may.
>
> **What the IIHF book does not have is either carve-out.** IIHF 81.6 runs to two paragraphs and stops; the NHL's third paragraph — *"When a team is 'short-handed' by reason of a major penalty, and they have neglected to ensure there is a player on the penalty bench… Icing will be called"* — **has no IIHF counterpart anywhere in Rule 81.** The duty itself survives: **IIHF Rule 20.3** still requires that when a player takes a major and is removed or injured, *"the offending team must place a substitute in the penalty box immediately."* What does not survive is the sanction. Fail it under IIHF and you have broken 20.3; you have not lost your right to ice the puck. Carve-out 2 is a USA Hockey classification limit and reaches nothing outside USA Hockey.
>
> **So for every level of British hockey the kill's single biggest structural advantage comes with no conditions attached** — and the In-House Rules do not amend Rule 81 at all, so nothing local claws it back. See [UK and England Rules](../foundation/uk_rules.md).
>
> **Rec and youth note:** under USA Hockey the exemption exists only from **Youth 15-Only, Girls 16U, High School and Adult** upward (Rule 624(b)(1)), so across most of North American youth hockey a shorthanded team is called for icing like anyone else. Many youth and rec leagues also use **no-touch (automatic) icing**. Check your league's rules before building your kill around it.

#### Smothering the puck to force a whistle

```facts
Rule: A skater other than the goalie who deliberately falls on or gathers the puck into their body takes a minor (NHL 63.2(v), IIHF 63.2(V) and USA Hockey 614(a) for delay of game; Hockey Canada 10.2(a)(iv) for handling the puck)
Rule: Two of the four books condition it — USA Hockey 614(a) reaches the act only where it is "deeming the puck unplayable and causing a stoppage of play", Hockey Canada 10.2(a)(iv) only "while standing or laying on the ice"
Rule: Where the puck is in your own goal crease that same act is a penalty shot instead of the minor (NHL 67.4, NHL and IIHF 63.6); Hockey Canada 10.2(a)(v) awards the shot "when the infraction occurs inside the offending team's goal crease"
Rule: USA Hockey writes that escalation as "a penalty shot/optional minor penalty" (614(b)), and 406(a) gives the election to the NON-OFFENDING team — so it never buys you the cheaper one, and electing the minor does put a second man off
Rule: The puck's location decides — NHL 63.6: "If the puck is in the crease, penalty shot. If the puck is outside the crease and gathered into the body of a player (other than the goalkeeper) who is inside the crease, minor penalty"; IIHF 63.6 materially identical, writing "goal crease"
Rule: With your own goalie pulled it is an awarded goal, not a shot — NHL and IIHF 67.5, and Hockey Canada 10.2(a)(vi), which locates the infraction rather than the puck; USA Hockey 614(b) only where the act "has prevented an obvious and imminent goal"
Rule: A goalie freezing the puck in his own crease with nobody pressuring him is a minor in all four books — NHL 63.2(vii), IIHF 63.2(VII) "unless he is actually being checked", USA Hockey 610(b), 614(c), Hockey Canada by Interpretation 3 to 10.1(a) after a warning; its 10.2 covers him outside the crease
Rule: The NOTE to NHL 63.2(v) exempts a puck shot under a shot-blocker or lodged in their clothing or equipment, never "any use of the hands to make the puck unplayable"; IIHF 63.2(V) and USA Hockey 614(a) carry it. The NOTE to NHL and IIHF 63.2(vi) legalises a goalie covering a save he came out to make
Never: Smother a loose puck to buy a whistle on the kill — the best it buys is a defensive-zone draw the tired unit that wanted it must then win a man short, and the worst is a goal
Risk: The worst case is not the penalty shot — with your own net empty it is an awarded goal, and under USA Hockey the other team may take a second man off you instead of the shot
Action: Play the puck, or knock it down and play it — NHL and IIHF 67.2 let you catch it out of the air but require you to "immediately place it or knock it down to the ice"
```

**A skater who deliberately falls on the puck or gathers it into their body takes a minor for delay of game — and under the NHL, where the puck is inside your own goal crease, that same act is a penalty shot instead of the minor, and an awarded goal if your own net is empty. All four books run those three tiers, each on its own terms, and USA Hockey alone conditions the last one.** The minor is **NHL Rule 63.2(v)**, *"On a player other than the goalkeeper who deliberately falls on or gathers the puck into his body"*, and **IIHF Rule 63.2(V)** in materially identical words, writing *"their body"* where the NHL writes *"his"*. **The other two books are not written the same way — each attaches a condition the NHL does not.** **USA Hockey 614(a)** reaches the act only where it is *"deeming the puck unplayable and causing a stoppage of play"*, so a smother that leaves the puck playable is not that minor; **Hockey Canada 10.2(a)(iv)** reaches it only *"while standing or laying on the ice"*, and files it under Rule 10.2 Handling or Falling on the Puck as a minor for **handling the puck** rather than delay of game. ⚠️ **On a penalty kill you spend most of your time in your own end, a stride or two from the paint where the escalation lives**, so read the crease version as the one that applies to you and the bare minor as the exception.

**It is the puck's location that decides, not yours.** **NHL Rule 63.6** prints the test in terms: *"The significant factor when determining whether or not a penalty shot is warranted is the location of the puck at the time it was held, grabbed or gathered into the body. If the puck is in the crease, penalty shot. If the puck is outside the crease and gathered into the body of a player (other than the goalkeeper) who is inside the crease, minor penalty."* **IIHF 63.6 carries that test in materially identical words**, writing *"goal crease"* for the NHL's *"crease"* and spelling out *"a 'Penalty Shot' shall be awarded"* and *"a minor penalty shall be assessed"* where the NHL clips both. **NHL 67.4** says it from the other end — a defending player who *"falls on the puck, holds the puck, picks up the puck, or gathers the puck into his body or hands from the ice in the goal crease area"* concedes the shot. **USA Hockey applies the same test in its Casebook**: to Rule 614 Situation 1, where a defender covers a puck outside the crease and slides into the crease with it, the answer is a minor, because *"The location of the puck at the instant it is covered or held is the determining factor as to whether or not a penalty shot is to be awarded"*; Situation 4 runs it the other way and awards the shot. **⚠️ Hockey Canada states it less plainly than the other three, so do not read its clause as their equal on the page**: 10.2(a)(v) awards the shot *"when the infraction occurs inside the offending team's goal crease"*, which locates the infraction rather than the puck, and it is **Interpretation 5** that decides a body case on the puck — a player outside the crease who scoops an in-crease puck out and then falls on it draws a minor and *"no penalty shot may be awarded."*

**USA Hockey does not make the shot automatic, and that does not make the trade cheaper for you.** Rule **614(b)**: *"A penalty shot/optional minor penalty shall be assessed to the non-offending team when any player, except the goalkeeper, falls on the puck, gathers the puck into their body or uses the hands to hold the puck while in the goal crease."* **The election is the other team's**, under Rule **406(a)** — they *"may, prior to the penalty shot, elect that the minor or bench minor penalty be assessed to the offending player or team in lieu of the penalty shot"* — so on a kill they take whichever is worth more to them, and at 5-on-4 that may well be the second man rather than the shot. 614(b) also fixes when it is judged: *"the decision as to whether the puck is in the crease is made at the moment the infraction occurs."*

**⚠️ And with your own goalie pulled there is no shot to face — the goal is simply given.** That is not hypothetical on a kill: the condition 67.5 attaches to is simply that *"the goalkeeper has been replaced for an extra attacker"* — it does not ask why, or at what strength. **NHL Rule 67.5**: *"If a player, when the goalkeeper has been replaced for an extra attacker, falls on the puck, holds the puck, picks up the puck, or gathers the puck into his body or hands from the ice in the goal crease area, the play shall be stopped immediately and goal awarded to the non-offending team."* **IIHF 67.5 is materially identical**, writing *"If a skater"* and *"their body"* where the NHL writes *"If a player"* and *"his body"*. **Hockey Canada 10.2(a)(vi)** awards the goal on the same structure, *"when the infraction occurs inside the offending team's goal crease and the goaltender has been legally substituted for an extra skater"* — in the same location wording as its 10.2(a)(v), so the same qualification attaches to it. **⚠️ USA Hockey alone conditions the upgrade**: 614(b) awards the goal *"if the goalkeeper has been removed from the ice at the time of the infraction and the action under this rule has prevented an obvious and imminent goal"*, so under that book it stays a penalty shot or optional minor unless the smother saved a goal that was going in.

**What the rule protects is the shot-blocker, not the smother** — and blocking a shot is a technique with its own rules of engagement, set out in [Shot blocking](#shot-blocking) immediately below. The NOTE to **NHL 63.2(v)** reads: *"Any player who drops to his knees to block a shot should not be penalized if the puck is shot under him or becomes lodged in his clothing or equipment but any use of the hands to make the puck unplayable should be penalized promptly."* **IIHF 63.2(V) and USA Hockey 614(a) carry materially identical NOTEs**, the IIHF writing *"their knees"*, *"under them"* and *"their clothing"*, and USA Hockey the same again except that it writes *"the use of hands"* where the NHL and IIHF write *"any use of the hands"*. **A puck that ends up under you having been shot there is nothing; reaching for it is everything.** USA Hockey's Casebook works exactly that pair with the net empty — a player lying in the crease with the puck shot under them draws *"No penalty shall be assessed"* (Rule 614 Situation 2), but *"if the player makes any action to cover the puck by gathering it into their body, the penalty shot or awarded goal situation would apply."*

**And there is a free option, which is the reason none of this has to happen: NHL and IIHF Rule 67.2 let you knock the puck down.** Three of the four books grant that in an express permission; USA Hockey reaches the same place by penalising only the hand you close and keep. **NHL Rule 67.2**: *"A player shall be permitted to catch the puck out of the air but must immediately place it or knock it down to the ice."* **IIHF 67.2 is that sentence word for word**, differing only in capitalising *"Player"* as the defined term it is. **Hockey Canada's Rule 10.2 preamble** is broader still — *"A player will be permitted to stop, knock down, or push the puck with their hand"* — and **10.2(a)(iii)** spells out what is not caught: a player who closes their hand *"to catch or stop the puck and immediately drops it to the ice without gaining or attempting to gain an advantage."* **USA Hockey writes the same idea as a stoppage rather than a permission**: **618(a)** stops play *"any time a player (except the goalkeeper) closes their hand on the puck and does not immediately drop the puck to the ice"*, so under that book the immediate drop is what keeps play alive rather than what keeps you out of the box. **Its express words are one volume over**, in the separate *Rules and Casebook*: **Rule 618, Situation 1** answers that *"[t]he motion of catching, then dropping the puck, must be virtually one continuous motion"*, and that *"[p]rovided the player catches the puck and drops it to their stick in one continuous motion, play should be allowed to continue"* — a casebook interpretation rather than rule text, and the same permission in practice. ⚠️ **The permission ends the moment you hold it**, and in your own crease that moment is the penalty shot. **Knocking it down in your own defending zone costs you nothing on the hand-pass rule either** — **NHL Rule 79.2**: *"Play will not be stopped for any hand pass by players in their own defending zone"*; **USA Hockey 618(b)** lets play continue where the hand pass is *"initiated and completed in their defending zone"*; and **Hockey Canada 10.2(a)(i)** stops play only where *"a teammate in the neutral or attacking zone gains control of the puck"*. Fuller treatment of the hand on the puck is in [Puck Handling](../technique/puck_handling.md) and [Risk Management](../hockey-iq/risk_management.md).

**So the kill tactic is the opposite of the instinct.** A tired unit wants the whistle, and the whistle it buys is a defensive-zone draw it then has to win a man short — bought inside your own paint, it is a penalty shot instead. **Under three of the four books that is the whole price.** **NHL and IIHF 63.6** add that on that infringement a penalty shot *"shall be ordered against the offending team, but no other penalty shall be given"*, and **Hockey Canada's Rule 4.11 preamble** makes the shot *"a non-time penalty, which is awarded to the non-offending team, in lieu of a Minor Penalty"* — so under the NHL, IIHF and Hockey Canada books you face the shot and you do not go two men short as well. **⚠️ Under USA Hockey that price is not capped.** Rule **406(a)** protects your strength only if the shot is the option taken — *"If the penalty shot is selected, the offending team shall not be reduced in on-ice strength as a result of the infraction"* — and the selection belongs to the **non-offending team**, so where they elect the minor instead you serve it, and a kill already a man short is at 5-on-3. Get a stick on the puck and clear it. **And leaving it for your goalie is not free either:** **NHL 63.2(vii)** makes a goalie who, in his own crease, *"deliberately falls on or gathers the puck into his body… in such a manner as to cause a stoppage of play"* a minor as well, *"unless he is actually being checked by an opponent"* — and **NHL 63.2(vi)** reaches him again if he *"races out of his crease in an attempt to beat the attacking player to the puck and instead of playing the puck jumps on the puck causing a stoppage of play."* **IIHF 63.2(VII) and 63.2(VI) are materially identical**, writing *"they"* and *"their goal crease"* for the NHL's *"he"* and *"his crease"*. ⚠️ **Do not carry that rule one act further than it goes, though, because both books attach a NOTE to it legalising the thing it looks like it forbids.** The **NOTE to NHL 63.2(vi)** reads: *"If a goalkeeper comes out of his crease to 'cut down the angle' on a shot and after making the save covers the puck, this shall be legal."* **IIHF 63.2(VI) carries the same NOTE**, writing *"their goal crease"* for the NHL's *"his crease"*. **Hockey Canada writes the permission as a clause of its own** — clause ii of the same Interpretation 3 to Rule 10.1(a): *"The goaltender may come out of their crease to cut down the angle and catch the shot or, after stopping the shot, cover the puck."* **What is penalised is racing an attacker to a loose puck and jumping on it. Covering a shot you have come out and stopped is not that**, and a goalie in a crowd who has been told otherwise will let a savable rebound sit. ⚠️ **Hockey Canada's own clauses ii and v point in opposite directions on the same facts** — v says a goaltender who has left the crease *"will not be allowed to freeze the puck"*, with no warning — **and the book does not say which governs.** Clause iv, in between them, is the one that names the actual offence: coming out to beat an attacking player to the puck and *"simply jump[ing] on the puck"*. ⚠️ **And the other two books reach the same freeze by their own routes, so do not read your own crease as a sanctuary under either of them.** **USA Hockey Rule 610(b)** is the closest of the four to this exact situation — an uncontested puck a goalie had time to play — and it issues no warning first: *"A minor penalty shall be assessed to a goalkeeper who has an opportunity to play the puck prior to being pressured by an attacking player, but instead intentionally causes a stoppage of play"*, its Note adding that *"any action that makes the puck unplayable without an immediate scoring opportunity must be penalized."* **Rule 614(c)** reaches a goalie who falls on or gathers the puck on four listed conditions, one of which is that they *"fail to play the puck with their stick when provided the opportunity to do so prior to being pressured by an attacking player."* **Hockey Canada's route is an interpretation rather than a lettered clause, and it is the one book that gives a warning first**: clause iii of **Interpretation 3 to Rule 10.1(a)** provides that *"A Minor penalty will be assessed to a goaltender who, after one warning, freezes the puck in the crease when not under pressure from attacking players"* — clause i having allowed the freeze *"when under pressure from attacking players"*, and clause v removing even the warning once the goalie has left the crease. **Hockey Canada's Rule 10.2 is that book's outside-crease clause**, a minor for handling the puck against a goaltender who does so *"while outside their crease, thus delaying the game"*, and it is not the whole of what Hockey Canada says about a freeze. **So the freeze is his to make under pressure, in his own crease, not yours to ask for when nobody is near him.**

#### Shot blocking

```facts
Key: Blocking shots is a technique, not an act of courage
Position: Be in the lane before the shot, not sliding into it — sliding gets you a puck off the ankle and a shooter who has already changed his angle
Never: Block from a screening position — half-deflecting a shot your goalie could not see is worse than letting it through cleanly
Action: If you cannot get properly into the lane, get out of it
Convention: How much your team blocks at all is a coaching choice, not a law — some kills want defenders collapsing and blocking aggressively, others want the lane taken away on your feet with the goalie seeing everything; find out which yours plays and ask your goalie
Key: Block from close and on your feet, head out of the lane and hands tucked — Body Contact and Battles and Defender own the rest of the technique; this section adds only what is specific to the kill
```

Blocking shots is a **technique**, not an act of courage. **The technique — how close to get, how to present the biggest surface, why to stay on your feet — is in [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md) and [Defender](../positions/defender.md), and this document does not set it out in full.** The one part of it that travels with everything below, because all of it puts you in a shooting lane, is where your head and hands go: **block from close and on your feet, head out of the lane and hands tucked.**

Two things are specific to the kill:

- **You are blocking on purpose, not by accident**, which means you should be **in the lane before the shot**, not sliding into it. Sliding gets you a puck off the ankle and a shooter who has already changed his angle.
- **Never block from a screening position.** Half-deflecting a shot your goalie could not see is worse than letting it through cleanly. If you cannot get properly into the lane, get out of it.

**How much your team blocks at all is a coaching choice, not a law.** Some kills want defenders collapsing and blocking aggressively in front of the goalie; others want the lane taken away on your feet, with the goalie left seeing everything. Neither is right in the abstract, and a kill that blocks less is not a kill that is being soft. Find out which yours plays, ask your coach which they want, and ask your goalie what they want — [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md) owns the choice and the full list of when *not* to block.

#### Stick lanes

```facts
Position: Blade flat on the ice, out in front of your body, in the passing lane — not on the puck carrier
Key: Sticks in lanes deter far more passes than they intercept
Priority: Take away the seam first, and point the stick at the most dangerous option rather than at the puck
Action: Concede the perimeter pass — the puck is being handled by someone your teammate already has
Technique: Stay in a low stance with your knees bent; you cannot make a quick lateral adjustment from standing
```

The most valuable and least visible thing you do on a kill.

- **Blade flat on the ice, out in front of your body, in the passing lane** — not on the puck carrier. Sticks in lanes deter far more passes than they intercept.
- **Take away the seam first**, and point the stick at the most dangerous option rather than at the puck. The puck is being handled by someone your teammate already has; the perimeter pass is the one you are happy to concede.
- **Stay in a low stance with your knees bent.** You cannot make a quick lateral adjustment from standing.

#### The discipline not to chase

```facts
Mindset: The power play wants you to chase — perimeter possession is not a threat, taking the bait is
Read: Chase only when the puck can actually be won — a bobbled reception, a player with their back turned, a puck on the boards with no support
Risk: If you chase and miss, you have created the seam yourself
Action: Talk constantly — call switches, call the bumper, call the backdoor
Action: Clear, then change, in that order; change when you can, not when you must, because a tired killer chases
```

**The power play wants you to chase.** Every pass around the perimeter is an invitation, and perimeter possession is not a threat — taking the bait is. **If you chase and miss, you have created the seam yourself**: four players cannot cover a five-man shape with one of them out of position.

Chase only when the puck can **actually be won** — a bobbled reception, a player with their back turned, a puck on the boards with no support. Talk constantly while you do it; most kill breakdowns are two players making different assumptions rather than two players being lazy, so call switches, call the bumper, call the backdoor ([On-Ice Communication](../foundation/on_ice_communication.md)). And **change when you can, not when you must** — a tired killer chases. Clear, then change, in that order.

### Shorthanded offence

```facts
Read: Take it when the turnover handed you a genuine 2-on-1 or breakaway, not one you have to manufacture with speed
Read: Take it when the turnover happened above the puck, so their players are behind you, and you can get there before their point player recovers
Never: Take it on a 2-on-2 or worse, when your partner has not seen it, when protecting a lead late, or when you are the last player back
Priority: The default is always get the puck out, then change — the rush is the exception you take when it is free
Key: If it is a footrace you will lose it is not a rush; if you have to think about whether it is on, it is not on
```

A shorthanded rush is one of the highest-value plays in hockey and one of the easiest ways to lose a game. The difference is entirely the read.

**Take it** when you have a genuine odd-man rush — a 2-on-1 or a breakaway — that the turnover *handed* you rather than one you have to manufacture with speed; when the turnover happened **above** the puck, so their players are behind you (a power play with four players below the puck is defenceless); and when you can get there before their point player recovers. If it is a footrace you will lose, it is not a rush.

**Do not take it** when it is a 2-on-2 or worse — lose the puck and you are 4-on-5 again with two players out of the play and them entering with speed; when your partner has not seen it, so the "rush" is you alone turning it over at their line and skating 180 feet back; when you are protecting a lead late (clear it and change — see [Game Management](game_management.md)); or when you are the last player back. That last one is obvious and it still happens every week.

**The default is always: get the puck out, then change.** The rush is the exception you take when it is free. If you have to think about whether it is on, it is not on. Fuller treatment of the trade-off in [Risk Management](../hockey-iq/risk_management.md).

## BOTH

### 4-on-4 and 3-on-3

#### 4-on-4

```facts
Key: Each player has a quarter more ice, and the extra room is mostly in the middle, so skill and skating matter more and structure matters less
Risk: With only two forwards back you have one fewer support layer, and a 2-on-1 arrives faster and with more room
Never: Activate as a defenceman more than you would at even strength — being caught is an immediate odd-man rush the other way
Action: Change on possession in the offensive zone or after a clear; a change with the puck live hands them a rush against three
Action: Support the puck with short options and quick movement — do not spread out, exactly as at 5-on-5
```

Coincidental penalties (below) put both teams at four skaters **under NHL rules**; under the IIHF book they bring immediate substitution and the teams stay five a side, so a British 4-on-4 comes from a minor against a major instead. Two fewer bodies on the same sheet changes the game more than the numbers suggest: each player has **a quarter more ice**, and the extra room is mostly in the middle, so skill and skating matter more and structure matters less.

- **You have one fewer support layer defensively.** With only two forwards back, the backchecking centre's middle-lane job (see [Center](../positions/center.md)) becomes everyone's problem, and a 2-on-1 arrives faster and with more room.
- **Defencemen should activate less, not more.** The open ice tempts you into the rush; the punishment for being caught is an immediate odd-man rush the other way.
- **Change with extreme care** — a change with the puck live at 4-on-4 hands them a rush against three. Change on possession in the offensive zone, or after a clear.
- **Support the puck, do not spread out.** The instinct in open ice is to stretch; the right play is short support options and quick movement, exactly as at 5-on-5.

#### 3-on-3 overtime

```facts
Rule: NHL regular-season overtime is five minutes of 3-on-3 sudden death then a shootout; playoff overtime is 20-minute periods at 5-on-5 (Rules 84.1, 84.4, 84.5)
Priority: Possession is the whole game — you cannot be scored on while you have the puck
Action: Carry the puck back out to the neutral zone and reset rather than forcing a low-percentage attack; the regroup is legal and standard practice
Read: Attack only with a numerical advantage — a 2-on-1 is nearly a goal, a 1-on-1 is nothing
Action: Isolate a defender in space and go at them, but do not chase the puck as a group
Never: Change on the fly with the puck loose. Change by holding possession instead, often passing back to the goalie, who retains and replays it rather than freezing it
Rule: A goalie who deliberately freezes the puck in his own crease to cause a stoppage takes a minor in all four books — NHL 63.2(vii) and IIHF 63.2(VII) "unless he is actually being checked by an opponent", USA Hockey 610(b) with no warning, Hockey Canada Interpretation 3 to 10.1(a) after a warning
Rule: A penalty in overtime makes it 4-on-3, not 3-on-2 — the non-offending team adds a skater (NHL Rule 84.3, matched by IIHF 84.3)
Rule: A goalie pulled for an extra attacker in regular-season overtime must wait for a stoppage to return and cannot change on the fly (NHL Rule 84.2); IIHF 84.2 sets no such restriction
Rule: IIHF overtime is 3-on-3 throughout — five minutes in preliminary games and ten in playoff and bronze medal games, with no 5-on-5 playoff format (IIHF Rules 84.1, 84.5)
Rule: Under the In-House Rules overtime is played only in NIHL, and in SNL where its regulations provide; NIHL National is five minutes of 3-on-3 then three shooters a side (Rule 84)
```

NHL regular-season overtime is **five minutes of 3-on-3, sudden death**, followed by a shootout if nobody scores (Rule 84.1, 84.4). **Playoff overtime is full 20-minute periods at 5-on-5** — the 3-on-3 format does not apply (Rule 84.5).

**How the game changes:** there is no forecheck worth the name, no cycle, and nowhere to hide. Everything is possession and odd-man rushes.

**Strategy that actually works:**

- **Possession is the whole game.** You cannot be scored on while you have the puck. The format has shifted from run-and-gun to a deliberate, possession-first approach precisely because of this.
- **The regroup is a weapon.** Carrying the puck back out of the offensive zone into the neutral zone to reset — rather than forcing a low-percentage attack — is standard practice at NHL level. (It is unpopular with fans, and the League's general managers **have** discussed limiting it. Reporting the November 2023 GM meetings for NHL.com, **Dan Rosen** wrote that Colin Campbell, the NHL's senior executive vice president of hockey operations, *said* there is a growing concern about the regroup in 3-on-3 overtime, when teams cross the blue line with possession but pull it back out into the neutral zone to reset and retain possession — and that Campbell said the talking points included potentially eliminating the ability to pull the puck out of the attacking zone once you cross the blue line. **Those are Rosen's words summarising Campbell, not Campbell's own**; the article carries no direct quotation from him on the regroup. But **discussion is not adoption**: the NHL Official Rules 2025-2026 contain no restriction on regrouping, in overtime or otherwise, so as of writing the regroup remains entirely legal. Flagged as a live issue, not a rule.)
- **Attack only with a numerical advantage.** A 2-on-1 in 3-on-3 is nearly a goal. A 1-on-1 is nothing. Wait for the extra man rather than driving into a set defender.
- **Never change on the fly with the puck loose.** A bad change at 3-on-3 is a breakaway. Teams change by holding possession — often passing back to the goalie, who **retains and replays** it: settles it, keeps it live, and moves it back out once the change is made. **Not freezes it.** A goalie who covers the puck in his crease to cause a stoppage is a minor under **NHL Rule 63.2(vii)** and **IIHF Rule 63.2(VII)** in materially identical words — the exception in both is a goalie who is *"actually being checked by an opponent"* — and a stoppage hands the draw and the reset to the other team anyway. **The other two books reach the same act by their own routes**, so this is not an NHL peculiarity to be ignored in a rec-league overtime: **USA Hockey Rule 610(b)** assesses a minor to a goalkeeper *"who has an opportunity to play the puck prior to being pressured by an attacking player, but instead intentionally causes a stoppage of play"*, with no warning first, and **Hockey Canada's Interpretation 3 to Rule 10.1(a)**, clause iii, assesses one *"to a goaltender who, after one warning, freezes the puck in the crease when not under pressure from attacking players."* ⚠️ **And in the NHL and IIHF books the general clause above is wider than the specific one.** **Rule 67.3** opens that *"any action taken by the goalkeeper which causes an unnecessary stoppage must be penalized without warning"*, and 67.3(ii) reaches a goalie who *"[d]eliberately holds the puck in any manner which, in the opinion of the Referee, causes an unnecessary stoppage of play"* — with no crease limit at all. Where the NHL's 67.3(i) excepts a goalie *"actually being checked"*, the **IIHF's 67.3(I) excepts one** *"actually being 'checked / pressured'"* — wider again, so a British goalie has slightly more room than the NHL text alone suggests. **The safe instruction is the same under every book: pass it back to him to be replayed, not to be caught.**
- **Isolate and attack.** With one defender to beat, the point is to get a defender alone in space and go at them. Support arrives too slowly to help them.
- **Do not chase the puck as a group.** Three players chasing is nobody home.

**Penalties in overtime** (Rule 84.3), which are worth knowing because they decide games:

- A penalty in 3-on-3 overtime makes it **4-on-3**, not 3-on-2 — the non-offending team adds a skater. The three-skater floor from the 5-on-3 section above is what forces this.
- If **both** teams take a minor at the same stoppage with nothing else on the clock, play **continues 3-on-3**.
- A two-man advantage in overtime is played **5-on-3**: the offending team stays at three and the other team goes to five.
- Carry-over: 5-on-3 at the end of regulation starts overtime 5-on-3; 4-on-4 at the end of regulation starts overtime **3-on-3**.
- **Pulled goalies in regular-season overtime cannot change on the fly.** Rule 84.2: once removed for an extra attacker, the goalie "must wait for the next stoppage of play before returning to his position. He cannot change 'on the fly.' If he does, a bench minor penalty shall be assessed for having an ineligible player." **This one is NHL-only.** IIHF Rule 84.2 is a single sentence — *"A team shall be allowed to pull its goalkeeper in favor of an additional skater in the overtime period"* — and attaches no return restriction and no bench minor.

> **If you play in Britain — check whether you get overtime at all, and then which one.**
>
> **The five-on-the-clock rules above are yours.** IIHF Rule 84.3 is materially identical to NHL 84.3: a penalty makes it **4-on-3**, coincidental minors with nothing else on the clock continue **3-on-3**, a two-man advantage is played **5-on-3**, the carry-over ladder comes to the same thing, and both books write in the three-skater floor — the IIHF's *"at no time will a team have less than three (3) skaters on the ice"*, the NHL's *"at no time will a team have less than three players on the ice."*
>
> **The format is not.** IIHF overtime is 3-on-3 all the way up: **five minutes** in a round-robin or preliminary-round game (84.1) and **ten minutes** in a playoff, relegation, classification or bronze medal game (84.5), each *"with each team at a numerical strength of three (3) skaters and one (1) goalkeeper"*. **There is no IIHF equivalent of the NHL's 20-minute 5-on-5 playoff overtime**, so the caveat in the Rule 84.5 line above is a North American one.
>
> **And the IHUK In-House Rules amend IIHF Rule 84 — overtime — on top of that.** Overtime *"is only played in NIHL fixtures and in SNL fixtures where specifically provided for in competition regulations"* — in most English and Scottish hockey a tied game simply ends tied, and none of this section applies. Where it is played, NIHL National league and group-stage playoff games use **five minutes of 3-on-3**, then a game-winning-shots competition with **three shooters per team**; the NIHL National playoff semi-finals use **20 minutes of 5-on-5** and the Final successive 20-minute 5-on-5 sudden-death periods with no shootout. Two details that catch people out in the rest period: teams **do not change ends** for overtime, and **penalised players must stay in the box** while goalkeepers may go to the bench. The full amendment list is in [UK and England Rules](../foundation/uk_rules.md); your league's own competition regulations are above both.

### Coincidental penalties, expiring penalties and 6-on-4

#### Coincidental penalties

```facts
Rule: One minor to each team at the same stoppage, with nothing else on the clocks, is served without substitution and both teams play 4-on-4 (NHL Rule 19.1)
Rule: Under IIHF Rule 19.1 coincidental minors bring immediate substitution and the teams stay five a side — Britain reaches 4-on-4 by a minor against a major at the same stoppage (Appendix IV, Table 15 in the 2025/26 book and Table 13 in the 2026/27 one), just not by this one
Rule: But not late — a minor against a major at the same stoppage is 5-on-4 under IIHF 19.3 in the last five minutes of regulation and at any time in overtime, the differential served as a major. NHL 19.4 is the same rule
Rule: If at least one team was already shorthanded, substitution is immediate and the on-ice strength does not change (NHL Rule 19.1)
Rule: Coincidental minors make neither team shorthanded, so a goal scored during 4-on-4 releases nobody from the box (NHL Rule 16.2)
Rule: Where several penalties are assessed at once, equal majors cancel first, then equal minors, and any remaining differential is served normally (NHL Rule 19.5)
Convention: Rec and minor-hockey leagues frequently differ — several substitute immediately so both teams stay at five; check your league
```

Under **NHL Rule 19.1**, when one minor is assessed to a player from each team at the same stoppage, **with nothing else on the penalty clocks**, the penalties are served **without substitution** and both teams play **4-on-4** for two minutes. 🇬🇧 **The *coincidental-minors* 4-on-4 is NHL-only.** IIHF Rule 19.1 requires *"immediate substitution"* for an equal number of minors, and the IIHF **Situation Handbook** — the name the IIHF gives its worked interpretations — confirms it at **Situation 84.10**, on two coincidental roughing minors late in the third: *"Since the penalties are coincidental, the penalties are not served on the clock and the teams are playing 5 – 5."* Britain still reaches 4-on-4 by other routes at the same stoppage — a minor to one team against a major to the other does it (IIHF Appendix IV — Table 15 in the 2025/26 book, renumbered Table 13 in the 2026/27 edition, examples 18-20 and 32 word for word in both) — so the tactical material below still applies. It is only this trigger that differs.

⚠️ **The minor-against-major route to 4-on-4 closes in the last five minutes of regulation and at any point in overtime.** **IIHF Rule 19.3** is headed *"Last Five Minutes and Overtime"*, and it takes exactly that pair — a minor or double minor to one team, a major to the other, at the same stoppage — and directs that the three-minute (or one-minute) differential *"shall be served immediately as a major penalty."* The Appendix IV table's own example 19, the same number in both editions, answers the pair twice: normally the teams play 4-on-4, but late *"Team B will play short-handed for three minutes and it will be served in the same manner as a major penalty."* So the strength is **5-on-4 for three minutes**, not 4-on-4, and the team that took the major must place *"the penalized Player or replacement Player in the penalty box immediately."* **NHL Rule 19.4 is the same rule in materially the same words, so this is not a British departure.** Two details in the NHL's do differ: it reaches a **match** penalty as well as a major, and for a major it wants the replacement in the box *"prior to expiration of the penalty"* rather than immediately.

- **If at least one team was already shorthanded**, the rule works differently: immediate substitution is made for the matching penalties, so the on-ice strength does not change, and those players wait for the **first stoppage after their time expires** to come out.
- **Coincidental minors do not make either team "shorthanded"** (Rule 16.2). This matters: a goal scored during 4-on-4 does **not** release anybody from the box.
- Where multiple penalties are assessed at once, referees cancel out equal numbers of majors first and then equal numbers of minors, and any remaining time differential is served normally (Rule 19.5).
- **Rec and minor-hockey leagues frequently differ here** — several play coincidental minors with immediate substitution so both teams stay at five. Check your league.

#### Penalties expiring

```facts
Rule: A goal against a shorthanded team ends the minor with the least time remaining — minors and bench minors only, not majors, and not on a penalty shot (NHL Rule 16.2)
Action: On the power play, know the clock — the last ten seconds are your best chance, because the kill's concentration dips
Read: On the kill, the player coming out of the box is a fresh attacker arriving from the side at full speed with nobody assigned to him
Action: Whoever is nearest calls "man coming out!" and picks him up
Action: If you are the player leaving the box, sprint — you are the most dangerous player on the ice for about three seconds
Technique: While you are in the box, watch the play rather than the clock, and come out facing the ice
```

The seconds around a penalty ending are among the most dangerous in hockey, in both directions.

- **A goal against a shorthanded team ends the minor with the least time remaining** (Rule 16.2). Minors and bench minors only — **not majors**, and **not when the goal comes on a penalty shot**.
- **On the power play, know the clock.** The last ten seconds are your best chance: the kill is watching the clock too, and their concentration dips.
- **On the kill, the player coming out of the box is a fresh attacker** arriving from the side of the rink at full speed with nobody assigned to him. Whoever is nearest must call it — "man coming out!" is one of the most useful things anyone says on a rink — and pick him up. If *you* are the player leaving the box, sprint; you are the most dangerous player on the ice for about three seconds. While you are in there, **watch the play, not the clock**, and come out facing the ice.

#### 6-on-4

```facts
Key: Pulling the goalie on a power play makes it 6-on-4, the largest advantage in hockey, and it is used far less than the arithmetic suggests
Position: As the power play, put two at the net front and send the extra body to the bumper or the backdoor, never the perimeter
Action: Move the puck quickly and accept that blocking lanes will be crowded
Countered by: The empty net — a killer who wins a puck can legally clear it 180 feet into it, and it counts
Action: As the kill, block shots from in the lane and on your feet, keep sticks in lanes, and clear with your head up looking for the empty net
Rule: Over the glass from your own zone is still a delay-of-game minor, shorthanded or not, under NHL Rule 63.2(iii) and IIHF Rule 63.2(III) alike; USA Hockey 610(c) and Hockey Canada 10.1(ii) reach only a deliberate one
Rule: On a signalled delayed penalty the non-offending team pulls its goalie for 6-on-5, and play continues until the penalised team gains control (NHL Rule 15.1)
Risk: In that window the danger is not a shot against — it is your own player shooting the puck into your own empty net
```

Pulling the goalie during a power play, late in a game, to make it 6-on-4.

- **It is the largest advantage in hockey** and is used far less than the arithmetic suggests: two extra attackers against four killers means genuine 2-on-1s everywhere and a kill that cannot pressure at all. **The kill's counter is the empty net**, and it is a real one — a killer who wins a puck can clear it 180 feet into that net legally, because a puck that enters the net is a goal regardless — and where the shorthanded icing exemption applies, a clear that misses is not icing either.
- **As the power play:** put two at the net front and send the extra body where it forces a decision — the bumper or the backdoor, never the perimeter. Move the puck quickly and accept that blocking lanes will be crowded.
- **As the kill:** shot blocking and stick lanes matter more than ever. Block from in the lane before the shot and on your feet, never from a screening position; [Shot blocking](#shot-blocking) above covers it. Clear with your head up looking for the empty net, and remember that over the glass from your own zone is still a penalty under the NHL and the IIHF alike (63.2(iii)/(III)) — under USA Hockey and Hockey Canada only if you meant it.
- **A related situation:** on a **delayed penalty in the Rule 15.1 sense** — the referee's arm is up, the infraction has been signalled, and the offending team does not yet have the puck — the non-offending team pulls its goalie for a 6-on-5. Play continues until the team to be penalised **gains control of the puck** (Rule 15.1), so the risk is not a shot against; it is your own player shooting the puck into your own empty net. See [Breakouts](breakouts.md) for how to play the puck in that window.

### Faceoffs on special teams

```facts
Goal: With a man advantage, winning the draw skips the zone entry entirely; a skater short, losing it means defending a set power play from the first second
Rule: The team awarded the power play chooses which end-zone dot the draw starts at (NHL Rule 76.2) — pick the side that suits your one-timer
Rule: After an icing the attacking team picks the dot (NHL Rule 81.2), and the team that iced it can neither change lines (81.4) nor take a timeout (87.1) — NHL and IIHF only
Never: Fire a loose puck the length of the ice while on the power play — you hand the kill the dot and freeze your own tired unit
Rule: Encroachment ejects your centre from the dot, not the encroaching player (NHL Rule 76.6); a second violation by the same team on the same draw is a bench minor (76.7)
Rule: The IIHF 2025/26 book made a teammate's encroachment a warning, not an ejection, but the 2026/27 book ejects the draw-taker as the NHL does (IIHF 76.6, both editions)
Rule: In Britain that no longer turns on the edition — the IHUK In-House Rules 2026-27 have the linesperson "immediately replace the centre for all violations" bar those on post-icing draws, so plan on losing your centre (Section 10, Rule 76)
Rule: IIHF Rule 76.7 makes a second violation by the same team on the same draw a bench minor in both editions, so the 5-on-3 risk on the kill is identical throughout
Rule: Home last change is forfeited after your own icing — the team in violation may not substitute before the ensuing faceoff (NHL Rules 81.4, 82.1), which USA Hockey does not replicate
Rule: IIHF 81.2, 81.4 and 87.1 impose all three icing consequences in the NHL's words, so this is the UK position — bar one extra 81.4 exception, "a Player with a broken skate", which its own 82.1 omits; a substitution sent back is a warning, or a bench minor if your bench has already had one (82.2(V))
Action: Agree the play before you line up — where the win is going, or whether you are tying up for a winger to sweep, and where the clear goes
```

Faceoffs matter disproportionately when the numbers are uneven: **with a man advantage, winning the draw means you skip the zone entry entirely** — the hardest part of the power play, gone. A skater short, losing the draw means defending a set power play from the first second. Technique, alignment and the set plays themselves are in [Faceoffs](faceoffs.md); what follows is only the special-teams-specific rules layer, and it is the part almost nobody knows.

**The four rules that give you an edge here — verify these against your own league:**

- **The team awarded the power play chooses which end-zone dot the draw takes place at** to start the power play (Rule 76.2). Pick the side that suits your one-timer, not the side the puck happened to be on.
- **Following an icing, the *attacking* team chooses the dot** (Rule 81.2) — and on special teams this cuts the opposite way to the way most players assume. **The kill cannot *be called for* icing** (Rule 81.6 — subject to the two carve-outs above, the NHL's empty penalty bench on a major and USA Hockey's classification limit), so where the exemption applies this essentially never comes up against them. (Read that the right way round: the kill may shoot the puck the length of the ice as much as it likes; what it cannot do is be whistled for it.) It comes up when the **power play** ices it, which is entirely possible and happens on a rushed clear after a failed entry. The draw then goes back to the power-play team's own end; the attacking team at that end is the **penalty kill**, so the *kill* picks the dot, and — **under the NHL and the IIHF, though not under USA Hockey; see the paragraph below** — the power play **cannot change lines** (Rule 81.4) **and cannot take a timeout**, that second one being **Rule 87.1**: *"No time-out shall be granted to the defensive team following an icing…"* (the sentence runs on to two further cases — a goalkeeper causing a stoppage from a shoot-in beyond the centre red line, and a defender accidentally dislodging the net) — and you have handed a tired unit a defensive-zone draw while a man up. On a power play, never fire a loose puck the length of the ice.

  **All three land identically in the IIHF book, and that is now checked rather than assumed.** IIHF **81.2**: *"Following an 'icing', the attacking team will have the choice of which end zone dot the 'face-off' will take place."* IIHF **81.4**: *"A team that is in violation of this rule shall not be permitted to make any Player substitutions prior to the ensuing 'face-off'."* (Its list of exceptions to that runs to four where the NHL's runs to three: the IIHF also lets you replace *"a Player with a broken skate"*. ⚠️ **Do not count on that fourth one, because the same book prints a shorter list for the same stoppage.** IIHF **82.1** bars substitutions for a team *"in violation of ➔ Rule 63.8 – Delaying the Game or ➔ Rule 81 – Icing"* — naming icing expressly — and then lists only the NHL's three. The book does not say which of its two rules governs, and the 2026/27 edition prints both lists unchanged. **And a refused attempt is not free.** Under IIHF **82.2(V)** the Referee will *"send the Players who have attempted to change back to their Players' bench"* and warn the team through its Coach that *"any subsequent violations during the rest of the game (including overtime), shall result in a bench minor penalty"* for delaying the game — **one warning per team per game, shared with that rule's other two acts**, so a bench that has already been warned takes the bench minor immediately. On a power play, that is a delay-of-game penalty against the team with the man advantage.) IIHF **87.1**: *"No 'time-out' shall be granted to the defensive team following an icing…"* — the same sentence as the NHL's, with the same two companion cases. **So icing your own power play costs a British reader exactly what it costs an NHL one.** **USA Hockey imposes neither the line-change ban nor the timeout ban**, so the self-inflicted wound is smaller there.
- **Encroachment gets your centre ejected from the dot, not the encroaching player** (Rule 76.6). A winger whose skate crosses into the circle, or who creeps into the area between the hash marks before the drop, costs you your best draw-taker on the most important faceoff of the shift. **A second violation by the same team on the same draw is a bench minor** (Rule 76.7) — on the kill, that is a 5-on-3 against you.

  **⚠️ This is one where the IIHF used to differ and no longer does — and if you were taught the old answer, unlearn it.** In the **IIHF Official Rule Book 2025/26**, where NHL 76.6 said *"the offending team's player taking the face-off shall be ejected from the face-off circle"* and, on the establish-position-first violation, that the Linesperson *"shall order the center of the offending team replaced"*, **IIHF 76.6 made both a warning**: the offending team *"shall receive a warning for a 'face-off' violation"*, and on the encroachment version *"the Linesperson shall assess a warning for a 'face-off' violation to the offending team."* Your centre stayed on the dot.

  **The IIHF Official Rule Book 2026/27, published June 2026, replaced both sentences with the NHL's.** Its 76.6 now reads *"the offending team's player taking the face-off shall be ejected from the face-off circle. This shall be considered a face-off violation,"* and on the establish-position-first violation *"the Linesperson shall order the center of the offending team replaced."*

  **⚠️ And a British reader does not have to establish which IIHF edition their league is administering before planning for it, because IHUK has written the removal into its own rules.** The **IHUK In-House Rules 2026-27**, published 25 July 2026, add a *Section 10 – Game Flow* amendment to Rule 76 that the 2025-26 edition did not contain: *"With the exception of face-offs following icings, the linesperson shall immediately replace the centre for all violations."* **So on a British penalty kill, a winger creeping into the circle costs you your best draw-taker on a defensive-zone draw a man short — the worst possible moment for it.** Note the one exception, because on the kill it is the draw you will meet most: **after an icing the centre is not replaced**, which is the one shift where your specialist survives a violation. **What never changed is the second violation** — IIHF 76.7 is materially identical in both editions, *"When two (2) 'face-off' violations have been committed by the same team during the same 'face-off', this team shall be penalized with a bench minor penalty"*, the NHL's rule counts from two violations as well, and the In-House Rules do not amend it — so a second violation is still a bench minor and still a 5-on-3 against you, post-icing draws included. *(Only IIHF Rules 76.3, 76.4, 76.6 and 76.7 have been read in the 2026/27 edition; the In-House Rules apply in England, Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland and say nothing about the Elite League; nothing here says whether the rest of this document's IIHF layer is current under either.)* ([Faceoffs](faceoffs.md) covers the full comparison.)

  Related, and the same in both books: do not crowd the scrum after a penalty is called; players coming into the attacking zone beyond the outer edge of the end-zone circle during a gathering move the draw out to the neutral zone (Rule 76.2), throwing away your offensive-zone start for nothing.
- **The home team has last change — but not after its own icing.** Rule 82.1 gives the visiting team first change, then: *"The home team may then make any desired substitution, **except in cases following an icing, which does not result in the delay of the game**."* Later in the same rule the exception is identified: *"A team that is in violation of Rule 63.8 – Delaying the Game or Rule 81 – Icing shall not be permitted to make any player substitutions prior to the ensuing face-off."*

  Rule 82.1's two sentences are the **same bar stated twice**, and the second statement is the one that says whose. The NHL's Rule 81.4 repeats it in the same words, with the same three exceptions (a goalkeeper who had come off for an extra attacker, an injured player, a penalty affecting on-ice strength) — none of which would make sense if both benches were frozen. The team that iced it cannot change; the other team can.

  What that means on special teams: **under the NHL and the IIHF, if your unit ices the puck you cannot change, home or away** — that is the expensive one. **Under USA Hockey it costs you nothing but the draw**, and under Hockey Canada only if you are in U18AAA, Junior or a Senior division whose Member has taken the option (Rule 6.7(d)). But if the *opponent* ices it, you get the free matchup wherever you are playing, and at home you still get it last. See [Faceoffs](faceoffs.md), which covers the same situation from the draw's point of view.

**What to actually do:** agree the play *before* you line up — on the power play, whether you are winning it back to the point, to the half-wall, or forward; on the kill, whether you are winning it clean for a clear or tying up for a winger to sweep, and where that clear is going.

### The goalie's role on both

```facts
Action: On the kill, tell your goalie what you are conceding — a kill that never says this makes its goalie guess
Action: On the kill, agree the retrieval before it starts; they can stop a rim behind the net and leave it stationary for you
Rule: Under NHL and IIHF rules your goalie may only play the puck behind the goal line inside the trapezoid (NHL 27.8, IIHF 27.7) — the sole exception is skate contact with the crease; the KHL reportedly too but unverified, and USA Hockey and Hockey Canada write no trapezoid at all
Rule: No trapezoid is no licence for your goalie either — USA Hockey Rule 614(c) still makes freezing the puck behind the goal line a minor, and Hockey Canada Interpretation 3 to Rule 10.1(a), clause v, gives a minor with "No warning will be issued" for freezing it outside the crease
Rule: The goalkeeper's restricted area is IIHF Rule 27.7, not the NHL's 27.8; England Ice Hockey's Rules & Regulations 22.3 say it is "not currently enforced" in England and Wales. Either way, keep your goalie inside it
Read: Play it as though it binds — your goalie stays inside the trapezoid, so a rim that dies in the corner is yours to fetch, not theirs
Action: On the power play, use them as a reset on a regroup and as your safety on a failed entry
```

Your goaltender is a full participant in both special teams, not a bystander. **The goalie's own game — depth, tracking, save selection under a screen — is in [Goaltender](../positions/goaltender.md).** Three things you have to do about it, and a fourth — where the line is at *their* net — is the section below:

- **On the kill, tell them what you are conceding.** If the plan is to concede point shots, they will set their depth for a shot through traffic instead of holding for a seam pass. A kill that never says this makes its goalie guess.
- **On the kill, agree the retrieval before it starts.** Under NHL and IIHF rules your goalie may only play the puck behind the goal line inside the trapezoid (NHL Rules 27.8 and 63.2(viii); IIHF Rules 27.7 and 63.2(VIII)) — the KHL reportedly too, though that one is unverified here — but they *can* stop a rim behind the net and leave it stationary for you. **USA Hockey, Hockey Canada and CARHA write no trapezoid at all** — none of the three uses the words *trapezoid*, *restricted area* or *designated area* anywhere in its book — and under those books your goalie is a genuine third puck-handler behind the net and your clears get far easier. How much rec, beer-league and youth hockey that adds up to is a question about leagues rather than about books, and this document has no count for it. **They still may not sit on the puck out there, though.** USA Hockey **Rule 614(c)** is a delay-of-game minor for a goalkeeper who falls on or gathers the puck causing a stoppage where *"(1) The puck is behind the goal line and their body is entirely outside of the goal crease"*, and Hockey Canada's **Interpretation 3 to Rule 10.1(a)**, clause v, reads *"When a goaltender leaves their crease, they will not be allowed to freeze the puck. If they do, they will be assessed a Minor penalty. No warning will be issued."* On a kill that is the difference between a goalie who can move a rim to your defenceman and one who can end the shift — they can do the first and not the second. See [Breakouts](breakouts.md). **🇬🇧 In England and Wales, treat that as unsettled.** The IIHF book carries the restricted area at Rule 27.7, but England Ice Hockey's *Rules & Regulations* 22.3 (2024-25) say it is *"not currently enforced"* and the 2025-26 In-House Rules do not mention it — though their Introduction says *"unless otherwise stated, all rules will be enforced in line with the IIHF Rule Book"*, which points the other way. Play it as though it binds — your goalie stays inside the trapezoid, so a rim that dies in the corner is yours to fetch.
- **On the power play, use them as a reset.** On a 5-on-4 regroup, passing back to the goalie is a legitimate way to buy a couple of seconds and change the angle of attack. They are also your safety on a failed entry, which is another reason not to force one.

### Against their goalie: where the net-front line is

```facts
Rule: A goal is disallowed where an attacker, by positioning or by contact, impairs the goalie's ability to move freely in the crease or defend the goal (NHL Rule 69.1, matched by IIHF 69.1)
Rule: USA Hockey 625(b) runs no such test — a flat position bar voiding the goal with an attacking player in the crease whoever scored, unless the puck got there first, a defender's interference put them there, or the goalie is out of the crease area — and that last one is only half a rule
Rule: The goalie-out limb of USA Hockey 625(b) is only half a rule — its Casebook, at Rule 625, Situation 9, disallows the goal and adds an interference penalty where the goalie is returning to the crease and the attacker prevents them making a play
Rule: The shoved-into-the-crease relief answers only for your position — USA Hockey 625(a)(8) is still a minor for physical contact, by stick or body, that interferes with the goalkeeper's movement, so it is no licence to act once you are there
Rule: Three books protect a goalkeeper on the way back — NHL and IIHF 69.4's returning-goalkeeper clause says only "may be penalized" for "deliberate actions", USA Hockey's Casebook is mandatory; Hockey Canada writes no such clause, but its 8.5 penalises unnecessary contact anywhere on the ice
Rule: The rebound carve-out permits incidental contact only — push the goalkeeper into the net together with the puck after a stop and the goal is disallowed with "appropriate penalties", unless a defending player fouled you into them, when it can be permitted (NHL and IIHF 69.7, 78.5(ix))
Rule: Hockey Canada 8.5 is not flat — a teammate's goal from outside the crease stands if the net-front player stays passive, and is disallowed if they play the puck, interfere with the play or obstruct the goalie's view or movements
Key: Screening from outside the crease without contact is legal in all four books — but under the NHL and IIHF, outside the paint is no safe harbour if you crowd the goalie out of their crease
Never: Put a skate in the blue paint. Keep your feet outside the crease instead — a foot in there risks the goal under all four books, and only the reason differs
Rule: In EIH and SIHA competition there is no video review of a goaltender-interference call and no coach's challenge, so the referee's on-ice judgement is final (In-House Rules, Rules 37, 38, 69.1)
Action: In England and Scotland, give the referee nothing to interpret at the net front — there is nothing to appeal a live judgement to
```

**Where the net-front line actually is.** Screening from **outside** the crease, in the sightline, without contact, is legal and is the single most valuable thing a net-front player does. But the qualifier attaches to the *impairment*, not to where your skates are: Rule 69.1 disallows the goal where an attacker *"either by his positioning or by contact, impairs the goalkeeper's ability to move freely within his crease or defend his goal"* — **positioning alone can do it**. Standing outside the paint is not a safe harbour if you have crowded them out of their own crease. Contact inside the crease that impairs a save is out; incidental contact outside it is fine if you made a reasonable effort to avoid it (Rule 69.4), and a rebound scramble is expressly carved out (NHL and IIHF Rule 69.7 — neither USA Hockey nor Hockey Canada writes a rebound carve-out).

⚠️ **Read the whole of Rule 69.7 before you lean on that carve-out, because the paragraph after it is the one that takes the goal off the board.** The permission is narrow and it is worth having the words: *"In a rebound situation, or where a goalkeeper and attacking player(s) are simultaneously attempting to play a loose puck, whether inside or outside the crease, incidental contact with the goalkeeper will be permitted, and any goal that is scored as a result thereof will be allowed."* **Incidental, and both of you going for the same loose puck.** It is not a licence to drive the goaltender backwards, and the next paragraph says so: *"In the event that a goalkeeper has been pushed into the net together with the puck by an attacking player after making a stop, the goal will be disallowed. If applicable, appropriate penalties will be assessed."* On a power play that is the worst trade on offer — the goal wiped off, and if a penalty comes with it the advantage goes too. Both books also print the play on their disallowed-goal lists, at **NHL 78.5(ix)** and **IIHF 78.5(IX)**: *"When a goalkeeper has been pushed into the net together with the puck after making a save."* ⚠️ **It does not run automatically against you, though, and the relief is in the same paragraph:** *"If, however, in the opinion of the Referee, the attacking player was pushed or otherwise fouled by a defending player causing the goalkeeper to be pushed into the net together with the puck, the goal can be permitted."* **So a defenceman who cross-checks you into his own goalie can hand you the goal that your own shove would have cost you.** A third paragraph closes the other shape of the same play — where the puck is under a player in or around the crease, deliberately or otherwise, *"a goal cannot be scored by pushing this player together with the puck into the goal."* Those are the NHL's words throughout; **the IIHF writes all four limbs at the same number and in the same order**, in *"goal crease"* and *"Player"*.

**In Britain, the standard is the same but the safety net is gone.** IIHF 69.1 disallows a goal on the same two grounds in materially identical terms — *"either by their positioning or by a 'relevant contact', impairs the goalkeeper's ability to move freely within their goal crease or defend their goal"*, where NHL 69.1 writes *"either by his positioning or by contact"* — and the IIHF text then adds that the call *"may be subject to a Video Review… or to a Coach's Challenge."* **The In-House Rules delete both:** *"On-ice video review is not available in EIH or SIHA competition"* (Rule 69.1, and Rule 37 generally), and *"a Coach's Challenge is not permitted"* (Rule 38). So the referee's live judgement of a net-front scramble is the final one, with nothing to appeal it to. If you play net front in England or Scotland, the practical lesson is to give the referee nothing to interpret. See [UK and England Rules](../foundation/uk_rules.md).

⚠️ **Under USA Hockey and Hockey Canada there is no 69.1 to know, because neither book runs its premise — and the two of them do not agree with each other either.** **USA Hockey Rule 625(b) is a flat position bar:** *"No goal may be scored with an attacking player in the goal crease unless the puck has preceded the player(s) into the goal crease or the goalkeeper is out of the goal crease area."* It does not matter who shot it. **But read the two exceptions in that sentence before you treat it as absolute** — a puck that reached the paint ahead of you, or a goaltender who has left the crease area, and the bar does not bite. ⚠️ **Take that second one only with its own second half, because it reverses the moment the goaltender starts back.** The Casebook puts the case directly, at Rule 625, Situation 9: with the goalkeeper *"completely out of their crease"* an attacking player may precede the puck into it and the goal stands — *"However, if the goalkeeper is attempting to return to the goal crease and there is any interference by the attacking player who prevents the goalkeeper from making a play, then the goal must be disallowed and an interference penalty shall be assessed."* A goaltender skating back is not an empty crease, and on the power play blocking that path is the worst trade available to you: no goal, and a minor that takes the man advantage away with it. 🇬🇧 **The NHL and the IIHF reach the same play at their own Rule 69.4** — a player who prevents a goalkeeper returning to the crease *"due to the deliberate actions of an attacking player"* *"may be penalized"* for goalkeeper interference — a narrower and softer test than USA Hockey's, but not an absence, and the IIHF writes it identically across its 2025/26 and 2026/27 books. ⚠️ **And there is a third way the goal stands, which matters most to a net-front forward on the power play, because it is the one someone else does to you.** The next paragraph of USA Hockey Rule 625(b): *"if the attacking player has been physically interfered with by the actions of a defending player that causes them to be in the goal crease, play shall not be stopped and any legal goal scored shall be allowed."* That relief is **USA Hockey's, and broader than the NHL's** — no reasonable-effort proviso of the kind 69.1 attaches — but it answers only for your **position**; 625(a)(8) is still a minor for physical contact that interferes with the goalkeeper's movement, so being shoved in is not licence to do anything once you are there. ⚠️ **And 625(b) does one more thing, which on a power play is the one that will actually cost you, because it is a whistle rather than a disallowed goal.** The clause everyone quotes is the rule's *second* paragraph; its **first** reads: *"A face-off shall be conducted at the nearest neutral zone face-off spot any time an attacking player stands, holds their stick, or skates through the goal crease provided the puck is in the attacking zone, the attacking team has possession of the puck and the goalkeeper is in contact with the crease."* **All three conditions have to hold** — puck in the attacking zone, your team in possession, the goaltender touching their crease — so it is not every incursion. But it is precisely the situation a set power play is in, and the price is the whole of it: possession gone, the zone gone, and a neutral-zone draw to win before you can start again. **The IIHF writes a discretionary version of the same stoppage** at Rule 69.1 — where an attacking player *"is in the goal crease and does not leave immediately, the Referee has the possibility to stop the game"*, with the face-off at the same spot — **and the NHL writes no such stoppage at all.** **Hockey Canada writes no returning-goalkeeper clause at all — and that is not a licence either**, because its Rule 8.5 says a goaltender *"is not 'fair game' just because they are outside their goal crease"* and that a penalty *"will be called where an opposing player makes unnecessary contact with the goaltender anywhere on the ice"*, which catches most of the same act by a different route. **Hockey Canada Rule 8.5 is not flat.** It opens the same way — *"a player of the attacking team may not stand in the goal crease. If the puck should enter the net while such conditions prevail, the goal will NOT BE ALLOWED"* — and then carves out precisely the goal a power play is trying to score: *"if an attacking player is in the goal crease but does not interfere with the Goaltender and another attacking player (who is outside the goal crease) scores, the goal WILL BE ALLOWED provided that the player who was in the goal crease does not attempt to play the puck, interfere with the play or obstruct the Goaltender's view or movements."* **So on that goal — a body in the paint, a shot from outside it — the two books give opposite answers.** Hockey Canada's own test is in Interpretation 3 to Rule 8.5(a): standing there is *"NOT committing a foul"*, but *"if while standing in the crease, the attacking player attempts to play the puck, interfere with the play, or impede the goaltender's vision or movements, then no goal may be scored."* **The divergence changes shape outside the paint:** nothing in either book reaches a screen set from *outside* the crease — USA Hockey writes no goaltender-vision provision at all, and Hockey Canada's two references to the goaltender's view both reach only a player standing in the crease — so 69.1's positioning-alone ground has no counterpart in either, and that screen is simply legal there. **Feet out of the blue under all four books; only the reason differs.** See [Rules Primer](../foundation/rules_primer.md) for the full comparison.

## Common Mistakes

These are errors the body above does not cover — the ones that survive knowing the systems.

- **Icing the puck while on the power play.** Nothing in the numerical-strength exemption protects the team with the extra man. You hand the kill the choice of dot, you cannot change (Rule 81.4), you cannot take a timeout (Rule 87.1), and a tired unit now defends a draw in its own end. **The IIHF book imposes all three in the same words** — 81.2, 81.4 and 87.1, each checked against the rulebook — so this holds in British and European league hockey; USA Hockey imposes only the defensive-zone draw. On the power play, a rushed length-of-ice clear is a self-inflicted wound.
- **Assuming last change is automatic at home.** It is not, after your *own* icing. If you are the home team and you ice it, the visitors change and you do not (Rules 81.4 and 82.1) — Rule 82.1's exception reads *"except in cases following an icing, which does not result in the delay of the game"*, and the rule's later paragraph identifies who it catches: the team in violation of Rule 81. After a *visitor's* icing your last change is intact. Plan the matchup accordingly, and remember that your own icing forfeits it. See [Faceoffs](faceoffs.md).
- **Winning a shorthanded defensive-zone draw straight out of the rink and bracing for a penalty.** Rule 63.2(iii)'s NOTE exempts a puck that leaves the playing area **directly off a faceoff**, and **the IIHF's 63.2(III) Note carries the same words**, so this holds in British and European league hockey too. It is a faceoff, not a minor. Knowing this stops a centre steering a draw he should be firing.
- **Smothering a loose puck on the kill to buy the whistle.** A skater other than the goalie who falls on the puck or gathers it into their body takes a **minor** (NHL 63.2(v) and IIHF 63.2(V); USA Hockey 614(a) only where the act is *"deeming the puck unplayable and causing a stoppage of play"*, Hockey Canada 10.2(a)(iv) only *"while standing or laying on the ice"*) — and where the **puck** is inside your own crease when you do it, a **penalty shot** (NHL 67.4, NHL and IIHF 63.6; Hockey Canada 10.2(a)(v) where the *infraction* occurs there; a penalty shot **or** optional minor under USA Hockey 614(b), the other side choosing under 406(a)), or an **awarded goal** if you have pulled your own goalie — NHL and IIHF 67.5 and Hockey Canada 10.2(a)(vi) on the bare fact of the substitution, USA Hockey 614(b) only where the act *"has prevented an obvious and imminent goal"*. The instinct that a tired kill wants a stoppage is the one that hands the power play a penalty shot — and under USA Hockey, where the choice is theirs, electing the minor puts a second man off you instead. **The free option is to knock it down:** NHL and IIHF 67.2 permit you to catch a puck out of the air provided you *"immediately place it or knock it down to the ice"*, and Hockey Canada's Rule 10.2 preamble permits a player *"to stop, knock down, or push the puck with their hand."*
- **Sending your best draw-taker out and then having a winger creep over the hash marks.** Under the NHL's Rule 76.6 the encroaching player is not the one ejected — your centre is. **⚠️ That is now the British and European answer too.** The IIHF's **2025/26** book made the first one only a warning to your team; its **2026/27** book ejects the draw-taker in the NHL's words — and in Britain the IHUK In-House Rules 2026-27 settle it whichever edition is underneath, having the linesperson *"immediately replace the centre for all violations"* on every draw but one following an icing. **Expect to lose your centre for it.** What is the same everywhere, and always was, is the second one: a second violation on the same draw is a bench minor and, on the kill, a 5-on-3 against (NHL and IIHF 76.7) — or, under USA Hockey Rule 613(d), a minor on the individual who committed it, which is the same 5-on-3. [Faceoffs](faceoffs.md) covers the full split.
- **Confusing the two things called a "delayed penalty."** Players pull a goalie because they heard "delayed penalty" when what was actually announced was a third penalty stacking on the clock. Listen for the referee's raised arm, not the phrase.
- **Reading "the kill can't be pressured at 5-on-3" as "nobody will touch me."** Three killers will still jump a bobble and close on anyone walking into the slot. What you have is *time on the perimeter*, not immunity.
- **Treating a 2-on-1 at the top of the zone as the objective.** Creating it is a step, not the finish. Cash it in near the net, where the shot is actually worth something.
- **Blocking a shot from a screening position** and deflecting a puck your goalie never saw. That is worse than not blocking at all — you have changed the direction of a shot they could not track in the first place. If you cannot get properly into the lane, get out of it.
- **Describing a 1-3-1 as having "defencemen at the points".** It has **one** point player. Two means you are running an umbrella, an overload or a 2-3 — call it what it is, so the person next to you knows where to stand.
- **Standing three players level across the blue line and calling it an umbrella.** With no depth between them, every pass is short and lateral and the kill barely moves. An umbrella is an **arc**: apex highest in the middle, flanks lower — barely lower in the classic version with both defencemen out wide, all the way down to the tops of the circles in the modern one.
- **Pushing the goaltender into the net "with" the puck in a net-front scramble.** Rule 69.7's carve-out covers *incidental* contact where you and the goalie are simultaneously going for the same loose puck. It does not cover driving them backwards: push a goalkeeper into the net together with the puck after they have made a stop and the goal is disallowed under **NHL and IIHF Rule 69.7**, listed again at **78.5(ix)**, with *"appropriate penalties"* on top if the referee judges they apply. The one thing that saves the goal is a **defending** player having fouled you into them — which is not something you can arrange.
- **Standing in, or skating through, the crease under USA Hockey and expecting only a "no goal".** Rule 625(b)'s first paragraph stops play for a face-off at the nearest neutral zone spot — not in your end, out of the zone — the moment an attacking player stands in, holds their stick in, or skates through the crease, **provided the puck is in the attacking zone, your team has possession and the goalkeeper is in contact with the crease**. There is no penalty in it, but on a power play the whistle is the punishment.
- **Too many men on the ice during a special-teams change.** Units change together; count before you go. It is the only penalty in hockey that is purely an administrative failure.
- **Arguing about the system on the bench instead of before the game.** A breakdown that looks like laziness is often two players operating on different assumptions, and the fix is a thirty-second conversation in the room.

## Key Takeaways

1. **A man advantage is a 2-on-1 waiting to be created, and passing is what creates it** — a kill can cover any single option, not three in two seconds. That is a philosophy rather than a law: shot-volume power plays work too.
2. **Create the 2-on-1 anywhere, but cash it in near the net**, because danger is a function of location. That is a coaching judgement, not a rule.
3. **Most power plays die at the blue line, not in the zone.** Drill your entries — drop, swing, carry, controlled dump, regroup — and be willing to reset rather than force one.
4. **A penalty kill is a decision about which shot you are willing to concede**, then two minutes of discipline making sure it is the only one. A box concedes the middle, a diamond the outside and backdoor, a wedge+1 the perimeter.
5. **A box has two players high and no single top; a diamond has one apex.** Use the diamond against a 1-3-1, because a box leaves the bumper unmarked.
6. **The best place to kill a penalty is before the puck enters your zone.** Three failed entries and the penalty is over.
7. **Blocking a shot is a technique, not an act of courage — and on a kill it is the thing you do most.** Be **in the shooting lane before the shot** rather than sliding into it: sliding gets you a puck off the ankle and a shooter who has already changed the angle. And **never block from a position where you are blocking your own goalie's view** — half-deflecting a shot they could not see is worse than letting it through cleanly. If you cannot get properly into the lane, get out of it. Block from close and on your feet, head out of the lane and hands tucked. **How much your team blocks at all is a coaching choice, not a law** — find out which yours plays and ask your goalie what they want. The rest of the technique is owned by [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md) and [Defender](../positions/defender.md).
8. **A shorthanded team may usually ice the puck — check your own book first.** The IIHF's exemption is unconditional. The NHL withdraws it from a team shorthanded by a major with nobody on the penalty bench (Rule 81.6), and USA Hockey grants it only at Youth 15-Only, Girls 16U, High School and Adult (Rule 624(b)(1)) — below those levels there is no exemption at all.
9. **Never ice the puck while on the power play**, which hands the kill the choice of dot and — under the NHL and the IIHF, though not under USA Hockey — bars your own change and your timeout as well. And two acts are still penalties while shorthanded: shooting the puck directly out of play from your defending zone is a delay-of-game minor, though not directly off a faceoff — **NHL Rule 63.2(iii) and IIHF Rule 63.2(III) alike, and the IIHF's is the version a British player is under, its bench clause the wider of the two**, while USA Hockey 610(c) and Hockey Canada 10.1(ii) reach only a *deliberate* shoot-out; and **falling on or gathering the puck into your body is a minor too** (NHL 63.2(v)) — **a penalty shot where the puck is in your own crease** (NHL 67.4, NHL and IIHF 63.6; a penalty shot or optional minor under USA Hockey 614(b)), and an awarded goal if your own goalie is pulled — that last step conditioned, under USA Hockey alone, on the act having prevented an obvious and imminent goal.
10. **Know your team's shape, and your job inside it, before the puck drops.** These are coaching choices, and a breakdown is often two players assuming different systems rather than anyone playing badly.
