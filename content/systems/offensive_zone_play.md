## Key focus

**Take your shot from a dangerous place, with the goalie out of position.** Most teams can get the puck into the zone; far fewer turn it into a shot from in front of the net, after a pass across the middle, or through a screen.

**Look across the middle of the ice before you look up the wall.** A pass across the middle just before the shot makes the goalie push across and re-set. That gap closes fast; the pass up the wall will still be there.

**Put a body at the net front every time a shot is coming.** Screen from outside the crease — feet out of the blue paint and off its red line — blade on the ice. Never initiate contact with the goalie; if a shove carries you into them, give way.

**Crash the net for the rebound, and decide to go before the shot.** The goalie is often down and the puck loose. Arrive with your stick on the ice, play the puck and not the goalie, and shoot it straight back.

**Somebody is always above the puck.** The high forward is the safety at the top of the circle, so a rebound straight out is yours and a counter-attack goes through you. If a defenceman steps down, a forward fills the line.

**Keep the triangle and keep moving.** One forward below the puck, one level, one above, so the carrier always has two passing options — and one forward always in, or attacking, the area in front of the net.

**End every cycle in something.** A shot from in front of the net, a pass across the middle, a screened point shot or a drive to the net — otherwise the cycle did nothing.

**From the point, shoot to get it through, not to beat the goalie.** Walk the line to move the shot-blocker, keep it low, at a tipper's stick or the far pad, and hold it if nobody is in front.

**Defencemen: keep the puck in, and pinch only when you will arrive as the puck does and a forward can cover the line.** Stop a rim at the line before anything else. A half-hearted pinch is how 3-on-2s against you start.

**Learn your team's system before you judge a teammate out of position.** Who takes the net front, whether defencemen join the attack and how forwards rotate are coaching choices; the shape above is the common default, not a law.

## Overview

```facts
Position: You already have the puck in the offensive zone — getting there is Zone Entries, winning it back is Forechecking Systems
Goal: You are not trying to take a shot — you are trying to take a shot from a dangerous place, with the goalie out of position
Action: Put a body at the net front for every shot, outside the crease, and crash the net for the rebound — decide before the shot, and play the puck, never the goalie
Priority: Somebody is always above the puck — the high forward is the safety, and a forward fills the line when a defenceman steps down
Risk: Almost every amateur team can get the puck into the offensive zone; far fewer turn possession there into a dangerous shot
Convention: The attack triangle with both defencemen on the line is the default this document is written from, not a law — an overload loading one side, a five-player rotation, and a 1-3-1 spread with a bumper in the slot, common on the power play, are real alternatives, so ask which your team plays
```

Once you have the puck at the other team's end — [carried or dumped in](zone_entries.md), or [won back on the forecheck](forechecking_systems.md) — the problem changes shape. Almost every amateur team can get the puck down there; far fewer turn it into a dangerous shot.

**Look across the middle first, because that pass moves the goalie.** A pass that crosses the centre line of the ice just before the shot forces the goalie to push across and re-set, and in volunteer-tracked NHL 5-on-5 data it is the most dangerous pass type, at roughly double the next.

**A screen takes the goalie's eyes, and from outside the crease, without contact, it is normally legal.** Do it on every shot. Inside the blue paint, or on its red line, your skate can cost the goal, and under some books the zone as well. Your blade on the ice turns the screen into a tip.

**The shot is the start of the play, not the end of it.** In the NHL's own play-by-play data, a team that has just put a shot on goal scores again within three seconds about five times as often as in a random three-second window, because the goalie has committed and the puck is loose. So decide before the release where you are going: the net-front player is already planted, and a forward from the wall arrives as the puck does. Play the puck, never the goalie.

**Shape keeps the possession alive and the counter-attack cheap.** With one forward below the puck, one level with it and one above it, the carrier always has two options, and the high forward at the top of the circle is both the shooting option and the safety. One forward is always in or attacking the scoring area; a triangle that stays on the perimeter is three players passing around a defence happy to let them. If a defenceman steps down, a forward fills in.

**Moving the puck around the outside is a set-up, not an outcome.** End it in a shot from the middle, a pass through the middle, a screened shot from the blue line, or a drive at the net. A point shot is a good play with a screen and two teammates hunting the rebound, and a giveaway without them. At the point, walk the line until the shot-blocker has to move, and shoot low — at a tipper's stick or the far pad.

**Defencemen keep the puck in, and pinch on a read rather than a hope.** Step down when you will arrive as the puck does, the winger is flat-footed, and a forward can cover your spot; otherwise hold, because a late pinch leaves the puck behind you and your forwards below it. On a rim, stop the puck at the line first; if you cannot control it, put it back deep. How often to pinch is your coach's call — ask.

**The shape is a coaching choice.** This document assumes three forwards rotating in a triangle with both defencemen on the blue line. Real teams also play an **overload**, stacking four players on the puck side and giving up the weak-side seam; a **five-player rotation**, where defencemen take forward spots as routine; and a **1-3-1 spread**, common on the power play, with a **bumper** in the middle who is not the net-front player. Nobody has published a count of what teams actually run, so treat any "most teams play X" claim, this one included, as an impression. Find out which yours plays before deciding somebody is out of position.

## 1. The Goal: Shot Quality, Not Shot Quantity

### The scoring area — "home plate"

![](diagram:oz-home-plate)

```facts
Position: Home plate runs from the tops of the two faceoff circles, in along the tops, down to the dots, then angles in to the goalposts
Key: Shots inside home plate are counted as scoring chances; those from the inner part, close to the net and between the dots, as high-danger chances
Position: The slot is straight out in front of the net between the circles; the high slot runs from the dots up to the tops of the circles
Position: The goalmouth is the immediate net front; the point is the area just inside the blue line; the half-wall is the boards level with the faceoff dot
Key: The faceoff circles and the goal crease are your on-ice ruler for everything below
```

Analysts describe the dangerous part of the offensive zone as a **home-plate shaped area** (as in the baseball plate). It runs from the tops of the two faceoff circles, in along the tops of the circles, down to the faceoff dots, and then angles in to the two goalposts. Shots inside it are counted as **scoring chances**; shots from the inner part of it, close to the net and between the dots, are counted as **high-danger chances**.

Some geography, in plain terms:

- **The slot** is the area straight out in front of the net, between the two faceoff circles.
- **The high slot** is the upper part of it, roughly from the level of the faceoff dots up to the tops of the circles.
- **The goalmouth** is the immediate net front — the few feet either side of the blue paint.
- **The point** is the area just inside the blue line, where defencemen usually stand — an area, not the line itself.
- **The half-wall** is the boards area level with the faceoff dot, between the corner and the point.

For reference, the end-zone faceoff circles have a **15-foot radius** and the goal crease reaches **six feet out from the goal line** at its deepest (NHL Rules 1.9 and 1.7). Those two markings are your on-ice ruler for everything below. **The red line drawn around the crease is treated as crease in most books**, which is why the net-front instruction here is off the line, not merely out of the paint — see *Screening*.

### Why location matters so much

**In short:** At even strength, get the puck into the slot before shooting: a clean look there beats an unscreened point shot several times over.

The numbers are not close:

- Distance from the net is **"the single most important feature by a wide margin"** in expected-goals models — models that assign every shot a probability of becoming a goal based on where and how it was taken ([Hockey Graphs](https://hockey-graphs.com/2019/08/15/expected-goals-model-with-pre-shot-movement-part-4-variable-importance/)).
- **At even strength**, **goals per unblocked shot** from **outside** the dangerous area have been **under 5%** across a decade of NHL data (Micah Blake McCurdy / HockeyViz, 2007–2017, on [McCurdy's own page](https://hockeyviz.com/txt/edgar), retrieved 28 August 2026, and restated [by The Coaches Site](https://members.thecoachessite.com/article/the-problem-with-point-shots-according-to-analytics)). **That is not shooting percentage**: McCurdy counts misses as well as saves and goals, so the two numbers are not comparable. **On the power play the picture changes** — his power-play map puts the 5% line out near the blue line, so this figure is no reason to pass up a point shot there. The source is a contour plot with no table and no sample size, so exactly where that line falls is approximate; that it reaches the blue line is not.
- Point shots convert at roughly **3% with traffic in front and about 1% or less without**. **That figure is Greg Revak's**, first printed with no source at all in his newsletter on 20 September 2020 ([Hockey's Arsenal — Point Shots Suck](https://hockeysarsenal.substack.com/p/point-shots-suck)). Republishing it in 2024 he credited it jointly to the HockeyViz shot-location map and to *"other data such as Clear Sight Analytics"* ([The Coaches Site](https://members.thecoachessite.com/article/the-problem-with-point-shots-according-to-analytics), 11 April 2024). Neither half can be checked: the map has no traffic variable, and Clear Sight is named only as an example, with no dataset. **So take the direction and nothing else: point shots score rarely, and less often still with nobody in front.** [Shooting](../technique/shooting.md) gives a 1–3% location range for point shots; that is not independent support for the traffic split.
- Shots from the middle beat shots from sharp angles, and danger climbs steeply as you get closer. NHL.com's summary of Sportlogiq data says *"even the tiniest bit further in can make a 5% improvement in your chances to score"* ([NHL.com](https://www.nhl.com/kraken/news/seattle-kraken-analytics-with-alison-high-danger-chances-328378484), Alison Lukan, 27 November 2021). Its chart shows where goals came from in ten-foot bands, so read the 5% as the direction and steepness of the effect, not five points on your own shooting percentage.

So at even strength a clean look from the slot is worth several times an unscreened point shot, and "just get pucks to the net" is incomplete advice. Volume from the perimeter is not a plan; it is what a team does when it has no plan.

### The two levers you actually have

```facts
Key: There are only two ways to make a shot dangerous — be able to name which one you are pulling at any moment
Action: Move the puck to a dangerous place — carry it, pass it or drive it into the home-plate area before you shoot
Action: Move the goalie — take away their eyes with a screen, make them move laterally with a pass across the middle, or make them commit with a rebound
Priority: The best chances do both at once — a pass across the slot to a player already in the home-plate area
```

There are only two ways to make a shot dangerous, and you should be able to name which one you are pulling at any moment:

1. **Move the puck to a dangerous place.** Get it into the home-plate area before you shoot — by carrying it there, passing it there, or driving it there off a rebound.
2. **Move the goalie so a shot from anywhere becomes dangerous.** Take away their eyes with a screen, make them move laterally with a pass across the middle, or make them commit with a rebound.

The best chances do both at once: a pass across the slot to a player already in the home-plate area.

## 2. The Royal Road — the Highest-Value Pass Type in the Passing Project's 5-on-5 Tracking

### The Royal Road: What it is

**In short:** Look to pass across the middle of the ice right before a shot; in tracked five-on-five play, those shots score far more often.

```facts
Key: The royal road is an imaginary line down the centre of the ice, lengthwise, from the middle of one net to the middle of the other
Key: A royal road pass — also called a slot-line, cross-seam or cross-slot pass — crosses that line in the offensive zone immediately before a shot
Key: Valiquette's split: green shots follow movement across the road, deflections, one-timers, screens and broken plays; red shots are clean looks with none of that
Risk: Valiquette's percentages are attributed but uncheckable — treat them as illustrative, not as data; the effect itself has been replicated on public data
Risk: Published descriptions of where the line runs disagree — the net-to-net version used here is kept for being the more conservative instruction, not the better supported one
```

The **royal road** is an imaginary line running down the centre of the ice, lengthwise, from the middle of one net to the middle of the other. The term was popularised by former NHL goalie **Steve Valiquette**, who separated shots into "green" (high-probability — preceded by movement across the road, plus deflections, one-timers, screens and broken plays) and "red" (low-probability — clean looks with no such movement).

![](diagram:oz-royal-road)

**Valiquette's percentages are attributed but cannot be checked — treat them as illustrative, not as data.** Green shots are reported as **76% of NHL goals**. NHL.com's Kevin Woodley reported the figure from Valiquette in December 2014, *The Hockey News* repeated it in 2015, and Chris Boyle, writing for the OMHA, gives the sample as 100 games of the 2014/15 season.

What is missing is the data. The shot-by-shot tracking, the classification rules and the counts have never been published. Woodley's own article says *"There is no database, at least not one available to the public, tracking every shot in the NHL with that kind of detail"*. The 24% for red shots is only what is left over, and Boyle's seven green categories add up to 74%, not 76%.

**The effect itself, unlike the number, has been replicated on public data.** Alan Wells' analysis of Ryan Stimson's volunteer-tracked Passing Project — *"51,308 shots at 5v5"*, counting all attempts rather than shots on goal — puts shooting percentage after a royal road pass at **15.50%**, far above every other pass type. That is a 5-on-5 figure: it leaves out the power play, and 5-on-5 is narrower than even strength. Alex Novet's public expected-goals model, built on Corey Sznajder's tracking of about 72,000 shots, found the effect is not just shot location in disguise: *"there is a big jump in Shapley value when the pass is royal road."*

**Published descriptions of where the line runs do not agree.** This document uses net centre to net centre. The two sources closest to Valiquette's figures, Blue Seat Blogs and NHL.com, both describe a narrower line that stops at the tops of the circles, so the net-to-net version is kept because it gives the more conservative coaching instruction, not because it is better supported. The exact geometry has never been published. [Shooting](../technique/shooting.md) sets this out in full.

A **royal road pass** — also called a **slot-line pass**, **cross-seam pass** or **cross-slot pass** — is a pass that crosses that centre line in the offensive zone immediately before a shot. Note the *cross-*: a **seam** on its own is any gap between two defenders' coverage areas and the lane through it, which need not cross the centre of the ice at all.

### Why it beats everything else

```facts
Key: A goalie's save is mostly positioning, not reflexes — a set goalie is already covering most of the net before the shot leaves
Action: Make them move sideways — crossing the road forces a lateral push across the crease to re-square to the new puck position
Read: A goalie forced to move laterally must also re-establish depth and angle, re-find the puck visually, and reset their stick and pads — which is where the five-hole opens
Goal: Get the shot away before they finish that sequence — then it is a desperation save, not a positioning save
```

Because a goalie's save is mostly *positioning*, not reflexes. A set goalie has already squared up to the puck, cut down the angle, and sealed the ice. They are, in effect, already covering most of the net before the shot leaves.

Now make them move sideways. Crossing the royal road forces four jobs on the goalie at once:

- The goalie has to **push laterally across the crease** to re-square to the new puck position.
- They have to **re-establish depth and angle** on the new line, which takes time they may not have.
- They have to **re-find the puck visually** — their sightline was set to one side of the ice and the puck is now on the other.
- And they have to **reset their stick and pads**, which is where technical errors creep in and the five-hole opens.

If the shot comes before they finish that sequence, they are not making a positioning save — they are making a desperation save.

### The evidence

- Shots following a **royal road / cross-slot pass** finished at **15.50%** — the highest-value pass type measured, roughly double the next one (Alan Wells' analysis of **Ryan Stimson's** Passing Project data, [via NHL.com](https://www.nhl.com/kraken/news/analytics-with-alison-dangerous-passes-327627898)). The data are **volunteer-tracked, 51,308 shots at 5-on-5, counting all attempts rather than shots on goal**.
- Royal road passes are the **most important pre-shot movement variable** in expected-goals modelling — shots are much more likely to score when preceded by a pass moving across the ice in front of the goalie ([Hockey Graphs](https://hockey-graphs.com/2019/08/15/expected-goals-model-with-pre-shot-movement-part-4-variable-importance/)).

**A note on the "33% versus 3%" you will hear quoted.** It comes from Chris Boyle's OMHA report of Valiquette's study: a shot *"from above the face-off circles and no lateral movement"* scores about 3%, and crossing the Royal Road through the slot raises it to 33%. But the 3% is a distant shot, not the same shot without the crossing, so distance does much of the work — and the study is unpublished. The **20–30%** range in coaching material has no published source at all. **Quote the 15.50% instead**, and only as a 5-on-5 figure for one pass type. Nobody has published a power-play conversion rate for seam passes, so a higher number attached to the power play is unsourced.

### How to actually create one

```facts
Action: Look across before you look along — your first scan off the wall is the far side of the slot, the backdoor and the weak-side dot
Never: Scan up the wall first — by the time you come back, the seam has opened and closed
Technique: Pass to where the shooter is going, not where they are — a pass into the feet costs the extra touch the goalie needed
Options: Carry it across yourself — the half-wall into the high slot, or curling out from behind the net into the slot, both cross the line
Options: Use the player behind the net — a pass out from below the goal line crosses the road almost by definition
Read: Expect the seam to be defended, and expect it to open only briefly
Never: Drive off the half-wall or curl out from behind the net with your head down — you cannot brace for a hit you have not seen, and coming out past the post you can be driven into the goal frame
```

Four habits produce a royal road pass:

- **Look across before you look along.** When you receive the puck on the wall, your first scan is the far side of the slot — the **backdoor** (the far post, behind the defence) and the weak-side dot. Only when that is dead do you look at the safe option up the wall. Scanning in the opposite order is what costs you the seam: it opens briefly and closes permanently, and the wall option does not.
- **Pass to where the shooter is going, not where they are.** A seam pass into a player's feet takes an extra touch, and the extra touch is exactly the time the goalie needed.
- **Carry it across yourself.** A puck carrier who cuts through the middle drags the goalie laterally the same way a pass does. Driving from the half-wall into the high slot, or curling out from behind the net into the slot, both cross the line.
- **Use the player behind the net.** A pass out from below the goal line to the slot crosses the royal road almost by definition, and the goalie has to turn their head to track it. See section 8.

![](diagram:oz-cross-the-road-yourself)

**The corresponding defensive truth:** taking away the middle of the ice is the whole point of most defensive-zone systems. Expect the seam to be defended, and expect it to open only briefly. See [Defensive Zone Coverage](defensive_zone_coverage.md).

## 3. Offensive Zone Structure — The Standard Setup

> **This section describes a coaching choice, not a law of hockey.** The structure below — three forwards rotating in an **attack triangle**, both defencemen holding the blue line, the strong-side defenceman free to pinch — is the common default this document assumes. Realistic alternatives are named at the end of the section. **Find out which one your team plays.**

### F1, F2, F3 are roles, not people

**In short:** In the common attack triangle, keep one forward below the puck, one level with it and one above, whoever arrived first, second or third.

```facts
Key: F1, F2 and F3 are roles defined by order of arrival — first, second and third to the puck — not by whether you are a centre or a winger
Key: Where arrival order and depth in the zone do not coincide, arrival order is what decides the label
Convention: "Hunt, help, high" is the forecheck shorthand — F1 hunts the puck, F2 helps F1, F3 stays above the puck, which is the sense of "high" here: high relative to the puck, not held at the blue line
Position: One player below the puck, one level with it and one above — the outlet that lets the puck go back to safety and can attack the net from behind, the close support, and the shooting option
Position: The level player is close support, usually a short pass away, in the inside lane rather than flat on the boards — that is the player who receives the bump
Key: The player above the puck is the shooting option on a low-to-high and your first line of defence the moment you lose it
Convention: Rotation rules are a coaching choice and change who may drop below the puck — ask which rotation your team runs before you decide someone is out of position
Goal: Give the puck carrier at least two options at all times; players without the puck keep moving to provide them
```

**F1, F2 and F3 are roles defined by order of arrival — who gets to the puck first, second and third — not by whether you are a centre or a winger.** They rotate constantly, and they are reassigned every time the sequence of arrival changes. If the left winger is first in on the puck below the goal line, they are F1; if the centre is the last of the three to arrive and ends up at the top of the circle, they are F3. A common shorthand for the forecheck version is **"hunt, help, high"** — F1 hunts the puck, F2 helps F1, F3 stays high.

In an established possession, order of arrival and depth in the zone usually coincide — the first forward in is the deepest — which is why the roles map so neatly onto the below / level / above shape described next. When they do not coincide, arrival order is what decides the label.

In an established offensive-zone possession, the three forwards should always be arranged so there is:

- **One player below the puck** — deeper toward the goal line than the puck carrier. This is the outlet that lets the puck go backwards to safety, and the player who can attack the net from behind. Without them, every loss of the puck is a clean escape for the defence.
- **One player level with the puck** — close support, usually a short pass away, in the inside lane rather than flat on the boards. This is the player who receives the bump and keeps the possession alive.
- **One player above the puck** — higher in the zone, toward the top of the circles or the point. This player is the shooting option on a low-to-high pass *and* the first line of defence if you lose it.

That "below / level / above" arrangement is the **attack triangle**. The triangle gives you **width and depth at once**, which means the puck carrier always has at least two passing options at different angles. Coaching material states the requirement plainly: the puck carrier should have **at least two options** at all times, and players without the puck should keep moving to provide them ([Creating Offense with a 5-Man Unit](https://cdn1.sportngin.com/attachments/document/dfc7-2294372/Creating_Offense_5_man_unit_Oct_20.pdf)).

### Rotation: the triangle moves, the shape does not

```facts
Key: The three forwards hold no fixed spots — as the puck moves, everyone rotates so the shape is preserved
Read: Puck from the half-wall down to the corner → the corner player is now F1, the old carrier rotates up, the high player holds
Read: Puck low-to-high to the point → the low forwards attack the net and the slot; the high forward screens or gets to the top of the circle
Read: Puck behind the net → the net-front player becomes level support at the far post; the far-side forward slides into the slot
Never: Let the triangle sit entirely on the perimeter — at least one forward is always in or attacking the home-plate area
```

The three forwards do not hold fixed spots. As the puck moves, everyone rotates so the *shape* is preserved:

- Puck goes from the half-wall down to the corner → the corner player is now F1 (below/level), the old carrier rotates up, the high player holds.
- Puck goes low-to-high to the point → the low forwards attack the net and the slot; the high forward screens or gets to the top of the circle.
- Puck goes behind the net → the player who was net-front becomes the level support at the far post; the far-side forward slides into the slot.

![](diagram:oz-five-man-shape)

![](diagram:oz-rotation-one-pass-later)

The one non-negotiable: **at least one forward is always in or attacking the home-plate area.** A triangle that is entirely on the perimeter is just three players passing the puck around the outside of a defence that is perfectly happy about it.

### The defencemen's three depths

```facts
Position: Just inside the blue line — the point — is the default: the low-to-high outlet, the shooting threat that stops the defence collapsing, and the safety net
Technique: Stay a stride inside the line, not on it — if the puck completely crosses out, the zone is gone and everyone has to re-enter
Rule: Losing the zone is not offside — a delayed offside arises when the puck is played back IN with attackers still inside (NHL and IIHF 83.3, PWHL 85.3); the puck merely leaving nullifies one (USA Hockey 630(d)(1))
Rule: ⚠️ **Check your book before counting on tagging up** — USA Hockey 630(d) grants the delayed offside only at Youth 15-Only and Girls 16U and above, High School and Adult; below those 630(c) stops play at once. The NHL, IIHF, PWHL and Hockey Canada 6.11(b) set no such floor
Position: Down to the top of the circle is the activated position — shoot from a better angle, walk the line (skate sideways along the blue line), or receive in the high slot, and still recover
Risk: Below the circles is always a commitment — below the hash marks you are effectively a fourth forward
Never: Go that deep before someone has taken your job at the blue line — before you go, not after
```

An offensive-zone defenceman is always at one of three depths:

![](diagram:defender-three-depths)

1. **Just inside the blue line (the point).** The default. You are the outlet for low-to-high, the shooting threat that stops the defence collapsing, and the safety net against a counter-attack. Stay a stride *inside* the line, not on it: if the puck crosses out, the zone is gone and everyone has to re-enter. That breaks no rule; it simply costs the zone. **Tagging up depends on your book.** If the puck is played back in while your teammates are still inside, it is a delayed offside, and they clear it by tagging up. ⚠️ **USA Hockey grants that only at Youth 15-Only and above, Girls 16U and above, High School and Adult (Rule 630(d)); below those levels play stops at once.** The NHL, the IIHF and Hockey Canada set no such floor, and in Britain the In-House Rules leave the IIHF's rule alone — [Rink Map](../foundation/rink_map.md) sets that out. See [Rules Primer](../foundation/rules_primer.md).
2. **Down to the top of the circle.** The activated position. You step down to shoot from a better angle, to **walk the line** — skate sideways along the blue line to change the shooting angle — or to receive a pass in the high slot. You are still recoverable: you can turn and get back to the line before most counter-attacks develop.
3. **Below the circles — too deep.** Not always wrong, but always a commitment. If you go below the hash marks (the short lines at the sides of the faceoff circles), you are effectively a fourth forward and someone must take your job at the blue line *before* you go, not after.

### Keeping the puck in at the line

```facts
Action: Stop a rim first, then play it — your blade, a skate turned along the boards, or your body in its path, skates parallel to the wall, head up and chin off your chest
Action: Stop a clear up the middle first — square up in front of it, head up and out of its line, then play it
Read: Watch the defender about to move the puck, not the puck already on the wall, and be a stride inside the line before it comes
Options: A hard, high or bouncing puck you cannot settle — chip it back down the wall to the corner rather than fight it at the line
Never: Chase a puck that is already past you at the line — turn and skate back, because the forward covering for you is already on the way
Convention: How hard the point fights for the line is a coaching choice — hold anything you can reach is the common default, and some teams give up a hard rim rather than risk it; ask
```

The defending team's way out is usually a rim along the boards or a clear up the middle, and the point is where it either stays in or leaves. What to do, in order:

- **Read it before it arrives.** Watch the defender who is about to move the puck, not the puck already travelling up the wall, and be a stride inside the line before it gets there.
- **Stop it first, then play it.** On a rim, put your stick blade, a skate turned along the boards, or your body against the wall in its path; if it is your body, skates parallel to the wall, head up and chin off your chest. On a clear up the middle, square up and get your body in front of it — head up and out of its line, padded surfaces to it, stick out of the lane, as [shot blocking](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md#shot-blocking-technique) sets out. A puck settled at the line is a new possession; a puck you swiped at and missed can be an odd-man rush the other way.
- **If you cannot control it, put it back deep.** A hard, high or bouncing puck is not the moment to settle it and walk the line — chip it back down the wall to the corner for your forwards and keep the shift alive.
- **If it is already past you, get back.** Turn and skate rather than chase a lost cause at the line; the forward covering for you should already be on the way.

How hard your defencemen fight for the line is a coaching choice. The common default is to hold anything you can reach and get back on anything you cannot; some teams tell defencemen to give up a hard rim rather than risk it, and some have the weak-side forward slide up to the far point whenever the puck is rimmed. Ask which yours plays.

### Who covers for whom

```facts
Convention: If one defenceman leaves the blue line, the other stays — two on the line at all times is this guide's default setup, not a law
Options: Teams running a five-player rotation, or green-lighting both defencemen, cover with whoever is highest; others leave one on the line, the weak-side defenceman sliding to the middle
Action: If a defenceman goes below the line and gets the puck, they play as a forward until the play ends — F3 fills in at the blue line
Action: If a defenceman goes down and does not get the puck, recover defensive position first, then look to attack again
Never: Chase after a failed activation — that is how odd-man rushes against you start
Action: Say it out loud — "I'm going", "I've got you", "hold"; silent rotations are the ones that leave the blue line empty
```

These are the rules of the standard setup, with both defencemen on the line, not laws of hockey:

- **If one defenceman leaves the blue line, the other stays.** You want two players on the line at all times. This is the standard setup here and the assumption to play on if nobody has told you otherwise; it is widely taught, though nobody has published a count. But teams running a **five-player rotation**, or giving both defencemen a standing green light, deliberately break it and cover the line with whichever player is highest. See the pinch note at the end of section 10 and [Forechecking Systems](forechecking_systems.md).
- **If a defenceman goes below the line and gets the puck**, they play as a forward until the play ends — and **F3 must fill in at the blue line for them**.
- **If a defenceman goes down and does *not* get the puck**, their first job is to recover defensive position, then look to attack again. Chasing after a failed activation is how odd-man rushes against you start.
- **Say it out loud.** "I'm going," "I've got you," "hold." Silent rotations are the ones that leave the blue line empty. See [On-Ice Communication](../foundation/on_ice_communication.md).

### Realistic alternatives

```facts
Options: Overload / strong-side load — four players on the puck side, one weak-side outlet; wins board battles, gives up the weak-side seam
Options: Five-player rotation — defencemen rotate into forward spots and forwards cover the line as routine rather than emergency
Options: 1-3-1 spread — one at the point, two half-walls, one bumper in the middle, one net front; common on the power play
Risk: The five-player rotation requires everyone to be comfortable at the blue line
Never: Confuse the bumper with the net-front player — two different jobs at two different depths
```

- **Overload / strong-side load.** Four players are loaded onto the puck side, with only one weak-side outlet. Wins board battles and grinds teams down; gives up the weak-side seam and the quick change of point.
- **Five-player rotation ("position-less" or high cycle).** Defencemen rotate into forward spots and forwards cover the line as a matter of routine rather than emergency. This is how modern NHL teams with skating defencemen play. It requires everyone to be comfortable at the blue line.
- **1-3-1 spread.** One at the point, two on the half-walls, one **bumper** in the middle of the ice between them, one net front. Common on the power play (see [Special Teams](special_teams.md)) and occasionally used at even strength. **The bumper is not the net-front player** — they are two different jobs at two different depths.

## 4. Cycling

### Cycling: What it is

```facts
Key: A cycle rotates the forwards around the low offensive zone — corner, half-wall, behind the net — protecting the puck from the defence
Action: Move the puck short distances, a few feet at a time, and rotate through the positions
Goal: Make the defence keep re-solving the same problem
```

A **cycle** is a possession tactic in which the forwards rotate around the low offensive zone — corner, half-wall, behind the net — passing the puck short distances to each other while protecting it from the defence. Think of a wheel: the puck moves a few feet at a time, the players rotate through the positions, and the defence has to keep re-solving the same problem.

### Why it works

```facts
Goal: Force defenders to move and decide repeatedly — every rotation asks "collapse low or hold the slot?", and over 30 or 40 seconds someone answers wrong
Key: A cycle is a low-turnover way to hold the puck — a board pass that fails stays near the wall, where one picked off through the middle goes the other way with your forwards below it
Key: Ranking the middle pass as the more expensive one to lose is coaching consensus rather than a counted comparison — nothing published classifies odd-man rushes by the play that created them
Goal: Tire the defenders — they skate without the puck and cannot change, and tired coverage shrinks toward the puck, which opens the slot
Goal: Buy time for your defencemen and late forwards to join and get set
```

- **It forces defenders to move and decide, repeatedly.** Each rotation asks the defence "do you collapse low or hold the slot?" Over 30 or 40 seconds of this, someone answers wrong.
- **It is a low-turnover way to hold the puck.** Passes along the boards, away from the middle of the ice, are the cheapest ones to miss: the puck stays near the wall with bodies around it. One picked off through the middle is the one that goes the other way with your forwards below it. **Ranking the middle pass as the more expensive one to lose is coaching consensus rather than a counted comparison** — nothing published classifies odd-man rushes by the play that created them, and [Defending the Rush](defending_the_rush.md) sets out how they are made.
- **It tires the defenders.** They are skating without the puck, which is far more exhausting than skating with it, and they cannot change lines. A tired defender's coverage shrinks toward the puck, which opens the slot.
- **It buys time for your defencemen and late forwards to join.** A cycle that lasts eight seconds is often just a cycle that lasted long enough for your point men to get set.

### The mechanics

**In short:** Read the checker: rim it on if they are still in front of you, reverse it if they have skated past.

```facts
Technique: Low-to-low — pass backwards and down to a teammate arriving behind you, then rotate up into the space they just left
Read: If the checker is still in front of you, rim it — onward around the boards, past the player sealing you to the wall
Read: If the checker has skated past you, reverse it — back against the flow, into the space they have just vacated
Risk: Rimming when you should reverse, or reversing when you should rim, is a leading cycle turnover — nobody has counted cycle turnovers by cause, so the ranking is coaching craft; read the checker
Technique: Bump pass — fire the puck hard at a spot on the wall, not at your teammate, and call it; a bump nobody expects is a turnover
Technique: Pivot / cutback — cut back up the wall you came down; a defender committed toward the corner cannot turn as fast as you. Turn before they can reach you, never to face the wall with them behind you
Options: Scissor / switch — carrier and support skate at each other and cross, support passing on the outside; the carrier may drop it or keep it
Never: Turn your back to anyone coming at you, or duck — keep your head up and your chin off your chest
```

**Low-to-low (the true cycle).** The puck carrier on the half-wall or in the corner passes the puck **backwards and down** to a teammate arriving behind them, then rotates up into the space their teammate just left. Two variants matter and they are not the same play:

- **Rim** — the puck goes *onward* around the boards, following the curve of the rink, past the checker who is sealing you against the wall. Beats pressure that has you pinned.
- **Reverse** — the puck goes *back against the flow*, into the space a checker has just skated out of. Beats pressure that has over-committed and gone past you.

Picking the wrong one is a leading cycle turnover — **nobody has counted cycle turnovers by cause, so take that as coaching craft rather than a measurement**. The rule of thumb: **if the checker is still in front of you, rim it; if they have skated past you, reverse it.**

![](diagram:oz-cycle-rim)

![](diagram:oz-cycle-reverse)

**The bump pass off the wall.** Rather than passing to a teammate directly, you fire the puck hard into the boards so it caroms to a spot. It is unblockable by a stick in the passing lane and it does not care about a defender standing between you. Aim at a *spot on the wall*, not at your teammate, and call it — a bump nobody expects is a turnover.

**The pivot / cutback.** You skate down the boards, run out of space, and cut back to skate back up the wall where you came from. This is a high-percentage individual cycle move, and widely taught as the pick of them — **a coaching judgement, not a measured one; no source here ranks cycle moves** — because a defender who has committed their momentum toward the corner cannot change direction as fast as you can. It buys you two or three seconds and it usually opens the low-to-high pass. Make the turn while the defender is still too far away to hit you. Once they can reach you it is too late to turn: go side-on to the wall, forearm and hip against it, and never turn to face the wall with them behind you.

**Low-to-high.** The puck goes up to the point or the top of the circle. Covered in full in section 5.

**The scissor / switch.** The puck carrier and a supporting teammate skate towards each other and cross, with the support player passing on the outside. The carrier can drop the puck or keep it. Defenders who are switching assignments during a scissor are defenders who are briefly covering nobody.

**The cycle lives on the wall, so how you take contact there matters more than winning the puck.** ⚠️ **Never turn your back to anyone coming at you, and never duck.** Get your **skates parallel to the wall** and take it on your **forearm and hip**, head up and chin off your chest. **Never the point of your shoulder** — USA Hockey's *Introduction To Body Contact*: *"Never hit the boards or glass with the tip of your shoulder – it can cause injury"* — and no part of the shoulder if you can help it, which is USA Hockey's own softer advice. If you are already facing the wall and cannot turn, that is the last resort, not the aim: get your stick and gloves up and spread out on the glass. [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md), §6, sets out the full rule and the evidence behind it.

### When to cycle and when to attack the net

```facts
Read: Cycle when the defence is set, structured and between you and the net, and no dangerous shot exists
Read: Cycle when your defencemen are not yet established at the line, or you are protecting a lead and burning clock with possession
Read: Attack immediately when a defender turns their back to the puck, or a defenceman pinches and the ice behind them opens
Read: Attack immediately when the slot is unoccupied by a defender, or the goalie is moving or unset
Read: Attack immediately when your net-front player has beaten their check to the inside
```

**Cycle when:**
- The defence is set, structured and between you and the net;
- No dangerous shot exists and forcing one means a low-percentage attempt or a turnover;
- Your defencemen are not yet established at the line;
- You are protecting a lead or trying to burn clock with possession (see [Game Management](game_management.md)).

**Stop cycling and attack immediately when:**
- A defender turns their back to the puck;
- A defenceman pinches or over-commits and the ice behind them opens;
- The slot is unoccupied by a defender;
- A goalie is moving or unset;
- Your net-front player has beaten their check to the inside.

### The failure: cycling for its own sake

```facts
Key: A cycle is a set-up, not an outcome
Never: Cycle for its own sake — three or four rotations that never threaten the net burn thirty seconds at zero risk to the defence
Goal: End the cycle in one of four things — a shot from home plate, a royal road pass, a low-to-high with a screen, or a net drive
Risk: Nobody in front of the net means even a good shot has no tip, no screen and no rebound support
Read: If the puck never goes above the goal line and never goes to the middle, you are passing around the outside of a box with no reason to move
```

This is the defining amateur error in the offensive zone. A team gets the puck low, cycles it around three or four times, looks organised and busy, and never once threatens the net. The defence is entirely content — you have used up thirty seconds of your own shift, in their zone, at zero risk to them.

**A cycle is a set-up, not an outcome.** If a cycle does not end in one of: a shot from the home-plate area, a royal road pass, a low-to-high with a screen, or a net drive, it did not accomplish anything. Two clear symptoms:

- **Nobody is in front of the net.** Cycling with an empty goalmouth means even a good shot has no tip, no screen and no rebound support.
- **The puck never goes above the goal line and never goes to the middle.** You are passing along the outside of a box that has no reason to move.

## 5. Low-to-High

### The play

```facts
Action: Pass from a low position — corner, half-wall, behind the net — up to a defenceman at the point or the top of the circle, who shoots
Read: Low-to-high is the standard way to generate a shot when the defence has collapsed low and taken away everything in tight
Action: The passer feeds the point then supports toward the strong-side hash marks — bail-out outlet and rebound position
Technique: The defenceman walks the puck toward the middle before shooting — it changes the angle, moves the goalie laterally and opens a lane
Position: One forward to the net front to screen and tip; one supporting in the high slot to form a triangle with the point
Read: The weak-side defenceman reads for a rebound squirting to their side and is ready to jump on it
Options: D-to-D across the top first — the goalie has to slide and the shot comes from the other side
```

**Low-to-high** means passing the puck from a low position (corner, half-wall, behind the net) up to a defenceman at the point or the top of the circle, who shoots. It is the standard way to generate a shot when the defence has collapsed low and taken away everything in tight.

The sequence, with everyone's job named:

1. **The passer** feeds the point and then **supports** — skating toward the strong-side hash marks, both to be an outlet if the defenceman needs a bail-out and to be in position for a rebound.
2. **The defenceman** receives it and **walks the puck toward the middle** before shooting. Walking the line changes the angle, forces the goalie to track laterally, and often opens a lane through the shot-blockers.
3. **One forward gets to the net front** to screen and tip.
4. **One forward supports in the high slot**, giving the defenceman a second option and forming a triangle with the point — meaning the puck can be moved again quickly instead of being fired into a shin pad.
5. **The weak-side defenceman** reads for a rebound squirting to their side and is ready to jump on it.

![](diagram:oz-low-to-high)

A common addition is **D-to-D across the top** before the shot: the strong-side defenceman moves it to their partner, the goalie has to slide across, and the shot comes from the other side. On the net-centre-to-net-centre geometry this document uses, that is a royal road crossing at the top of the zone — less valuable than one in the slot, but far better than a static point shot. *(Under the narrower, zone-limited description of the road noted in Section 2, a lateral pass out at the blue line would not cross it at all. The coaching point survives either way: the goalie still has to slide, and a shot arriving before they are set is worth more than one arriving after.)*

### Why it beats a cycle that never shoots

```facts
Goal: Release the puck — a screened point shot produces a rebound, a tip, a scramble, a whistle in your favour and a line change for you
Key: Even at a low conversion rate with traffic, the shot generates chaos in the home-plate area — and that is where goals live
Never: Cycle without ever releasing — it produces none of that
```

Because a shot from the point with a screen and a net-front presence produces things a cycle does not: a **rebound**, a **tip**, a **scramble**, a **whistle in your favour**, a **line change for you** and none for them. Even at a with-traffic conversion rate of roughly 3% — Greg Revak's figure, and uncheckable — the shot generates chaos in the home-plate area, and chaos in the home-plate area is where goals live. A cycle that never releases produces none of that.

### The honest caveat

```facts
Key: Low-to-high is a means, not an end — at even strength a point shot is among the lowest-percentage shots on the ice, and blocked more often than any other
Key: The point-shot caution is an even-strength one — on the power play the same map puts roughly the whole zone at or above that under-5% figure, so it is no argument against a point shot there
Risk: They also generate fewer rebounds than shots from lower in the zone
Priority: Low-to-high is only worth doing when the net front is occupied — a screen, a tip threat and two players hunting the rebound
Never: Fire it from the point with nobody in front — the identical shot is a giveaway with extra steps
```

Low-to-high is a *means*, not an end. At even strength a point shot is among the **lowest-percentage shots on the ice**, and it is **blocked more often** than a shot from anywhere else. But section 1's under-5% figure is an even-strength one: on the power play the same map puts the whole zone at or above it — approximate in where the line falls, not in its direction. And analysis by Alex Novet found point shots **generate fewer rebounds** than shots from lower in the zone ([via The Coaches Site](https://members.thecoachessite.com/article/the-problem-with-point-shots-according-to-analytics)).

There is a cautionary tale. The Seattle Kraken under Dave Hakstol built their 2021-22 offensive-zone scheme around low-to-high, generated the third-most point shots per sixty minutes in the league, and produced the **lowest expected value per shot of any team** ([Sound of Hockey](https://soundofhockey.com/2022/03/30/kraken-coach-dave-hakstols-scheme-and-philosophies-offensive-zone-plays-part-7/)). Neither number is Sound of Hockey's own — it credits Corey Sznajder's tracking and TopDownHockey/JFreshHockey, both private — so treat this as a reported anecdote about one team in one season, not a checkable statistic.

The lesson is not "don't go low-to-high." It is: **low-to-high is only worth doing when the net front is occupied.** A point shot with a screen, a tip threat and two players hunting the rebound is a good play. The identical shot with nobody in front is a giveaway with extra steps.

## 6. Net-Front Play

The net front is the least glamorous piece of productive real estate in hockey, and the place where players most often do the job badly while believing they are doing it.

### Screening

**In short:** Get between the goalie's eyes and the puck, chest square and still at the release, both skates clear of the crease and its red line.

```facts
Position: Inside the faceoff dots, six to eight feet out from the goal line — and both feet off the red crease line, which the arc reaches at six, so live at the far end of that range
Technique: Stand between the goalie's eyes and the puck, not between the goalie and the net. Slide with the point man as they walk the line
Technique: Chest up and square to the shooter, hips low, feet wide, knees bent — the posture of a squat, not of a bow
Action: Be still at the moment of release — constant shuffling gives the goalie flashes of the puck through the gaps
Never: Turn to follow the shot toward the net — it opens the goalie's view. Keep your chest to the shooter and your eyes on the puck, ready for the tip or the rebound
Never: Plant at the net front without knowing where the near post is — you get moved sideways by a defender you cannot see. Head up and chin off your chest instead
Never: Brace against the goalie the way you brace against a defender — if a shove is carrying you into the goalie, give way rather than hold your spot
Never: Duck away from the shot. Meet a post or the boards with an arm or a leg instead, never your head first — USA Hockey's wording, and a flexed neck is what turns that collision into a spinal injury
Rule: A no-contact screen from outside the crease is normally legal — but under NHL and IIHF 69.1, PWHL 71.1, positioning that stops the goalie moving freely in their crease can cost the goal; their tables allow a screen outside it that impairs their defending (5C, 5E)
Rule: USA Hockey writes no goalie-vision rule, Hockey Canada's two references to the goalie's view reach only a player in the crease, and NHL and IIHF 69.3, PWHL 71.3, key theirs to a "significant position" in the crease. Inside the paint every book can cost you
Rule: Rule 69.4 reaches your position too (NHL, IIHF; PWHL 71.4), for the penalty rather than the goal — an attacker whose "deliberate actions" prevent a goalie who has played the puck outside their crease from returning to it "may be penalized" for goalkeeper interference — no contact needed
```

A **screen** is standing in the goalie's line of sight so they cannot see the puck. **From outside the crease, without contact, it is normally legal**, and referees allow it constantly. Two limits. Under the NHL, IIHF and PWHL, positioning that stops the goalie moving freely in their crease can cost the goal; their tables allow a screen outside the crease even where it impairs the goalie's ability to defend (5C, 5E). And once a goalie has played the puck outside the crease, deliberately keeping them from getting back to it can be penalised, contact or not. USA Hockey, Hockey Canada and CARHA write nothing about a screen from outside the paint. ⚠️ **So the line, not the sightline, is what you respect: keep your skates clear of the paint and of the red line around it.** Where exactly the line runs differs between the books; [Rink Map](../foundation/rink_map.md) sets that out.

The mechanics of doing it well:

- **Stand between the goalie's eyes and the puck, not between the goalie and the net.** These are different lines, and they change as the puck moves. If the point man walks the line, you must slide with them to stay on the line. A screen that was correct three seconds ago is now just a player standing near the net.
- **Tall chest, low hips — the two halves do different jobs.** Keep your **chest up and square to the shooter** so you present the biggest possible obstruction to the goalie's sightline; crouching, turning sideways or ducking away from the shot all shrink the surface you are blocking and let the goalie find the puck around you. ⚠️ **Ducking has a second cost, and it outlives the screen:** it puts your chin on your chest, which is the posture the head-first spinal-injury mechanism turns on. USA Hockey's *Heads Up Hockey* makes *"Heads Up — Don't Duck!"* its Rule One — a safety principle about collisions, not shots, so it still holds on the shift where the screen came to nothing. At the same time keep your **hips low and your feet wide, knees bent** — that is what stops a defenceman moving you. Standing tall *and* narrow, with straight legs, is why players get shoved out of the paint; folding at the waist is why goalies see the shot. You want the posture of a squat, not of a bow.
- **Be still at the moment of release.** Plant and obstruct. Constant shuffling gives the goalie flashes of the puck through the gaps.
- **Track the puck, not the shot.** Your chest is square to the shooter, so the puck is in front of you: keep your eyes on it at their stick, ready to tip it, and do not turn to follow it toward the net. Turning your body to follow the flight of the shot is how you both un-screen the goalie and miss the rebound.
- **Position: inside the faceoff dots, roughly six to eight feet out from the goal line — with both feet off the red crease line.** ⚠️ **The crease arc is six feet at its deepest**, straight out from the middle of the goal line, so the *bottom* of that range is the line itself: live at the far end of it. Deep enough to obstruct the goalie's view and to reach rebounds, far enough out to stay clear of the crease. The crease is **8 feet wide at the goal line and 6 feet deep**, because the arc that closes it is struck with a **six-foot radius from the centre of the goal line** (Rule 1.7) — the six feet is the *depth*, not the half-width. So "three feet out from the goal line" is *inside* the paint, not clear of it.
- **Know where the near post is before you plant, and take contact with your head up.** You are standing still with your eyes on the puck, so a defender walking you sideways moves you by a body you **feel rather than see**, along a path with a steel post at one edge and the goalie at the other. Keep your **head up and chin off your chest, and never duck**: USA Hockey's *Heads Up Hockey* says *"almost all on-ice cervical spine injuries have been due to the head being slightly flexed (head down) while making head-on contact with the boards or goal post"*, and that it *"can occur at walking speed"*. Its *Introduction To Body Contact* adds: *"Hit the boards or goal posts with an arm, a leg, or anything but your head first."* A box-out moves you along the same path, so this holds whether or not your league allows checking. ⚠️ **And never take the goalie braced.** Wide feet and bent knees are how you refuse to be moved by a *defender*; if a shove is carrying you into the goalie, give way and make the effort to avoid them visible. [Body Contact and Battles → Net-Front Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md#9-net-front-battles) sets it out in full and outranks this section.

![](diagram:oz-net-front-screen)

### Tips and deflections

**In short:** Keep your blade on the ice, change the puck's angle slightly without following through, and tip it low.

```facts
Technique: Blade on the ice, always — the overwhelming majority of tippable pucks come in low
Key: You are redirecting, not shooting — ten to thirty degrees beats a set goalie, and a big swing usually misses the puck entirely
Technique: Angle the blade, absorb slightly, do not follow through — hard hands send it into the glass
Action: Present a target — hold the blade out in the lane where you want the puck, and shout for it
Rule: A puck hitting an attacker's stick above the crossbar is no goal, and where it hits the stick is what counts (NHL and IIHF 80.3, PWHL 82.3, NHL 78.5(vi), Hockey Canada 6.9(b)) — but only the goal off that contact dies: a teammate who gains it and shoots scores (IIHF Handbook 2025/26, 80.9(c))
Rule: USA Hockey is not that rule — 621(d) sets the ceiling at the shoulder and writes no crossbar test, and its Casebook 621 Situation 7 refuses the contact point: "When any part of the stick is carried above the shoulders, the entire stick is considered to be high"
Rule: A tip contacted above the crossbar but below your own shoulders is no goal under NHL and IIHF 80.3 or PWHL 82.3 — IIHF Handbook 80.9 answers "No" twice — while USA Hockey 621(d) and CARHA 62(d) cap at the shoulder and do not reach it; Hockey Canada 6.9(b) pulls both ways, so tip low
Rule: NHL and IIHF 80.1, PWHL 82.1 and Hockey Canada 6.9(a) stop play only once the offending team gains possession and control of the high-batted puck, so a loose one is played on until both teams abandon it, which stops play under those four and under USA Hockey too (72.3; PWHL 74.3)
Rule: USA Hockey 621(d) prints the opposite default — play stops "immediately" unless the puck "is batted to an opponent who gains possession" — but its Casebook 621 Situation 15 applies the same possession test, so follow the tip up unless it goes straight in; CARHA 62(d) has no such test
Rule: A high stick on the puck is one rule; one on a person is another — NHL and IIHF 60, PWHL 61, USA Hockey 621(a) to 621(c), Hockey Canada 9.5, or 7.6 Head Contact via 6.9(c) in minor and female hockey, none capped at a minor
Rule: In CARHA leagues a high stick to a person above the shoulders is a major whether or not it injures (62(b)); if it injures, add a game misconduct; if intentional, a game misconduct and at least a one-game ban (32(d)); a deliberate injury, or an attempt at one, is a match (62(c))
Rule: A raised stick can be a penalty with no contact at all — USA Hockey Casebook 621 Situation 2 answers "No" to whether contact must occur and says high sticking "must be called" where a player raises the stick above the shoulders to intimidate or is careless; 621(a) is the minor, not 621(d)
Rule: A puck deflected in off your skate or body is a good goal with no distinct kicking motion (NHL Rules 78.4 and 78.5(ii)) and no deliberate hand or body bat (78.5(i))
```

A **tip** or **deflection** is changing the direction of an incoming shot with your stick blade. It is devastating because it destroys the goalie's read: they have tracked and set for a puck travelling one way, and it is now travelling another way from six feet out.

- **Blade on the ice, always.** The overwhelming majority of tippable pucks come in low. A stick held off the ice at the net front is a stick doing nothing.
- **You are redirecting, not shooting.** A small change of angle — ten to thirty degrees — is enough to beat a set goalie. A big swing usually misses the puck entirely.
- **Angle the blade, absorb slightly, do not follow through.** Soft hands change direction; hard hands send it into the glass.
- **Present a target.** Shooters at the point aim at the tipper's stick. Hold the blade out in the lane where you want the puck, and shout for it.

**Two rules to know before you tip.** The first is not the same in every book.

- **A puck that goes in off your stick above the height of the crossbar is not a goal** under the NHL (Rules 80.3 and 78.5(vi)). What counts is where the puck meets the stick, not where your hands are: *"The determining factor is where the puck makes contact with the stick."* Tip low.

  **The IIHF prints 80.3 in the same words**, so a British player is under the crossbar test too, and **the PWHL (82.3) and Hockey Canada (6.9(b)) use the same contact-point test**: the goal counts where the puck met a part of the stick below the crossbar, even if another part was above it. The IIHF Situation Handbook (80.6) and Hockey Canada (Interpretation 4 to 6.9(a)(i)) both allow a goal off a blade held high with the puck striking the shaft low.

  **Between the crossbar and your shoulders there is no high-sticking violation**, because the high-stick ceiling is the shoulders. The goal dies only along the chain 80.3 names — in directly, or deflected off any player or official. The IIHF Situation Handbook (80.9) answers "No" to that band deflected in off a defender or a teammate, but "Yes" where a teammate gains the puck and **shoots**. **So a puck you tip between the crossbar and your shoulders is still live: break the chain and the next shot counts.**

  **USA Hockey runs a different test.** **Rule 621(d)** sets the ceiling at the **shoulder**, not the crossbar, and the word "crossbar" appears nowhere in it. Nor does it use the contact point: its *Casebook* (Rule 621, Situation 7) rules out a goal where the blade was above the shoulders and the puck struck the stick low — *"When any part of the stick is carried above the shoulders, the entire stick is considered to be high."* **So a blade held above your shoulders that the puck meets low is a goal under the NHL, the IIHF, Hockey Canada, the PWHL and CARHA, and no goal under USA Hockey alone.** The commoner case runs the other way: a puck you meet above the crossbar but below your shoulders is no goal under the NHL, IIHF and PWHL, while USA Hockey and CARHA 62(d), whose only ceiling is the shoulder, do not reach it. **Hockey Canada prints both tests and settles neither**, and this document does not resolve it. Find out which book your league runs, and tip low whichever it is.

  **The whistle decides whether there is a rebound to hunt.** Under the NHL, the IIHF, the PWHL and Hockey Canada, a puck batted above the shoulders stops play only once the offending team gains possession and control of it, so a loose one is played on. USA Hockey 621(d) prints the opposite default — an immediate whistle unless an opponent gains possession — but its *Casebook* (Situation 15) lets play go on until the offending team gains it. **CARHA 62(d)** stops play at once, with no possession test. ⚠️ **A high tip that goes straight in is no goal and an immediate whistle**, so there is no rebound to chase.

  **Where the faceoff goes afterwards differs most of all.** Declining to play a high-sticked puck wins your team territory under USA Hockey, whose *Casebook* sends the draw to the offending team's defending zone (Rule 621, Situations 8, 10 and 12). It wins nothing under the NHL, IIHF and PWHL, which give the offending team the least territorial advantage (80.2), or under Hockey Canada, which stops play once both teams refuse the puck. CARHA 62(d) sends every high-stick stoppage to the offending team's defensive zone. [Shooting](../technique/shooting.md) sets out each ruling.

  **What it costs when the blade reaches a person.** Everything above decides whether the goal counts. A stick that catches an opponent above the shoulders is a different rule — NHL and IIHF Rule 60, PWHL 61, USA Hockey 621(a)–(c), Hockey Canada 9.5, and in Hockey Canada minor and female hockey the Head Contact rule (7.6), through 6.9(c) — ⚠️ **and none of them is capped at a minor.** **USA Hockey needs no contact at all**: its *Casebook* (Rule 621, Situation 2) says high sticking *"must be called"* where a player raises the stick above the shoulders to intimidate or is careless. **CARHA 62(b)** makes contact above the shoulders a major *"whether or not injury results"*, plus a game misconduct if it was intentional or injures; its minor, 62(a), is for carrying the stick above an opponent's waist to check or intimidate. **Injure someone with a high stick deliberately, or try to, and it is a match penalty** under CARHA 62(c), NHL 60.4, PWHL 61.4, USA Hockey 602(a) and Hockey Canada's head-contact rule (7.6(c) or 7.7(c)). You tip in a crowd, so keep the blade down after the tip as well as before it.

- **A puck deflected into the net off your skate or body is a good goal** — provided there is **no "distinct kicking motion"** (NHL Rules 78.4 and 78.5(ii)), and provided you did not deliberately bat it in with your hand or body (Rule 78.5(i)). So you may absolutely turn your skate to redirect a puck; you may not kick it.

### Hunting rebounds

**In short:** Decide to go before the shot, read where the rebound will squirt, and shoot it back at once.

```facts
Read: Anticipate where it goes — a pad tends to squirt back toward the corner it came from, a blocker to the shooter's off side, a chest to drop in the paint
Risk: Treat those as tendencies, not rules — a goalie angling their surfaces deliberately will break all three on purpose
Action: Shoot it back immediately — do not settle it, do not look for a pass; the window is one or two seconds while the goalie is down
Options: Go to the far post when you get tied up — nobody is covering it and it opens the weak-side chance
Key: The defending team recovers roughly 60% of rebounds — Greg Revak's figure, read off a chart by Alex Novet — so you win that race by deciding to go before the shot, not by reacting after it
Convention: Time the arrival by which job you have — planted at the net front before the release if that is your assignment, arriving with the release if you are driving in from the wall or your circle; the split is coaching craft, not a measured finding
Read: Revak's 60% summarises the whole chart rather than one zone — Novet plots offensive recovery flat at 38–42% wherever the shot came from; no league, season or sample size is stated anywhere
```

- **Anticipate where it goes, don't react to where it went.** The rough tendencies: a puck hitting a pad tends to squirt back toward the corner it came from, a puck hitting the blocker toward the shooter's off side, a puck hitting the chest to drop in the paint. **Treat these as tendencies, not rules** — they are coaching craft, not measured rebound distributions, and a goalie who is angling their surfaces deliberately (which is what [Goaltender](../positions/goaltender.md) teaches them to do) will break all three on purpose. Know where the shot is coming from, pre-load your feet that way, and be ready to be wrong.
- **Shoot it back immediately.** Do not settle it, do not look for a pass. The window is one or two seconds, while the goalie is down and out of position.
- **Go to the far post when you get tied up.** If your check has sealed you off from the near side, the far post is a rebound location nobody is covering and it opens the weak-side chance.
- **Time your arrival by which of the two jobs you have.** If the net front is *your assignment*, you are planted there before the release — a screen you are still walking into is not a screen. If you are **driving in from the wall or your own circle**, arrive as the puck is released rather than well before it: go early and the defender has the time to establish position on you and turn you away from the puck. That split is coaching craft rather than a measured finding.
- **Understand the odds.** The defending team recovers roughly **60% of rebounds**, which is why the net-front job is a *job*: you win that race by deciding to go before the shot, and reacting afterwards loses it. The 60% is **Greg Revak's** arithmetic ([Hockey's Arsenal](https://hockeysarsenal.substack.com/p/point-shots-suck), 20 September 2020, restated [at The Coaches Site](https://members.thecoachessite.com/article/the-problem-with-point-shots-according-to-analytics)) on a chart by **Alex Novet**, which shows the attacking team recovering 38–42% of rebounds wherever the shot came from. No league, season or sample size is given for it, so treat it as a direction rather than a number you can check.

### Goaltender interference — the actual standard

**In short:** Stay out of the paint, keep off the goalie, and give way the moment they set their position.

```facts
Rule: NHL and IIHF 69.1, PWHL 71.1 — an attacker's position, inside or outside the crease, does not by itself decide the goal; goals "should be disallowed only if" you impair the goalie's ability to move freely or defend it, by positioning or contact — a ceiling, not a direction to disallow
Rule: NHL and IIHF 69.1's crease sentence names the crease, and 69.4 takes the goal outside it for contact — and 69.1's first limb lets a position that stops the goalie moving freely in the crease cost the goal; the tables allow a screen that impairs defending (5C, 5E)
Rule: NHL Rule 69.1 — a goal is disallowed where you initiate intentional or deliberate contact with the goalie, inside or outside the crease; and under IIHF 69.1 an attacker who does not leave the crease at once can cost the team a neutral-zone faceoff, at the Referee's discretion
Rule: NHL Rule 69.4 answers two questions from different clauses — on the goal, contact outside the crease that is more than incidental disallows it, and incidental contact does not
Rule: NHL 69.4: a goalie out of the crease is not "fair game" — unnecessary contact is penalised "in every case"; incidental contact is allowed only as they play the puck, if you made a reasonable effort. USA Hockey, Hockey Canada and CARHA write none; USA Hockey files accidental contact as interference
Rule: Inside the crease (NHL 69.3), contact you initiate — incidental or otherwise — while the goalie is in the crease voids the goal, subject to the rebound and loose-puck exception at 69.7
Rule: The no-contact ground in NHL and IIHF 69.3, PWHL 71.3, needs a significant position in the crease — a substantial part of your body there for more than an instantaneous period — that both obstructs the goalie's vision and impairs their ability to defend the goal
Rule: The IIHF Situation Handbook does not apply 69.3's test — asked about a player "in the crease screening the goalkeeper", its Situation 69.1 answers "the goal shall be disallowed". The Handbook ranks below the rulebook, but do not play the crease to that edge
Rule: NHL and IIHF 69.3, PWHL 71.3 — after any contact by a goalie establishing position in the crease, give ground at once or a goal is disallowed. The rule adds a penalty "whether or not a goal is scored", but their own tables assess none where the goal is scored
Rule: No book caps arriving into a goalie at a minor. NHL and PWHL 42.1 write "[a] minor, major or a major and a game misconduct" for charging one in the crease, 42.5 and EIHL Casebook 42 making a game misconduct mandatory on a major hurting face or head; IIHF 42.1's "A penalty" is tiered at 42.2–42.4
Rule: Keep your skates off the painted crease line — USA Hockey's Note to 625(b) counts "(including crease lines)" as crease, and Hockey Canada's and CARHA's glossaries agree; the NHL's, IIHF's and PWHL's Situation 5E allows a goal on the line, while IIHF Rule 1.7 says the line belongs to the crease
Never: Plant your feet in the blue paint. Screen from outside it instead, where a screen without contact is normally legal and worth doing constantly
```

**What to do holds under every book: screen from outside the crease, never initiate contact with the goalie, and give ground when they establish their position.** Here is what **NHL Rule 69** actually says.

**The governing premise (69.1):** an attacking player's *position*, whether inside or outside the crease, **should not by itself** determine whether a goal is allowed. The rule then names two things and says a goal *"should be disallowed **only if**"* one of them is present:

1. an attacking player, **by positioning or by contact, impairs the goalie's ability to move freely within the crease or defend the goal**; or
2. an attacking player **initiates intentional or deliberate contact** with the goalie, inside or outside the crease.

"Contact" means any contact between goalie and attacker, **by stick or by any part of the body**.

**Read that "only if" as a ceiling, not a trigger.** It limits when a goal may come off; it does not order one off whenever a goalie is screened. The sentence in 69.1 that disallows a goal for where you stood is keyed to the crease — an attacker who *"enters the goal crease"* and impairs the goalie — and 69.4, the outside-the-crease rule, takes the goal for contact rather than position. The IIHF prints all of this at the same numbers, and the PWHL at 71. **But 69.1's first limb still lets positioning that stops the goalie moving freely in their crease cost the goal; their tables allow a screen outside the crease even where it impairs the goalie's ability to defend** (NHL Table 14 and the IIHF's matching table, Situations 5C and 5E), on the referee's judgement. The same tables disallow the goal for the same conduct inside the crease (5B and 5D).

**An empty crease is not a free crease either.** ⚠️ **Never stand in a goalie's road back to the net:** the returning-goalie clause in NHL and IIHF 69.4 and PWHL 71.4 says an attacker who deliberately prevents a goalie who has played the puck outside the crease from getting back to it *"may be penalized"* — no contact needed.

**And none of it buys you the crease line.** ⚠️ **Screen with your skates clear of the paint and clear of the line.** The NHL's and IIHF's tables, and the PWHL's matching row, allow a goal for an attacker planted *"on the crease line"* (Situation 5E). But the IIHF's own Rule 1.7 says the line belongs to the crease, and **USA Hockey (Note to 625(b)), Hockey Canada and CARHA (in their glossaries) all count the line as crease**, so under those three a skate on the red line is a skate in the crease. [Rink Map](../foundation/rink_map.md) sets that out.

**Outside the crease (69.4), the goal and the penalty are separate questions.** On the goal, contact that is more than incidental takes it off, and incidental contact does not. On the penalty, a goalie out of the crease is **not "fair game"**: the penalty *"should be assessed in every case where an attacking player makes unnecessary contact"*, and incidental contact is excused only while the goalie is *"in the act of playing the puck"* and you made a reasonable effort to avoid it. Rule 42.1 says the same in the NHL, IIHF and PWHL books. ⚠️ **So bumping a goalie who has come out to challenge, or who is set outside the paint with the puck elsewhere, is a penalty.** Neither book says how the two fit where contact was incidental but the goalie was not playing the puck. USA Hockey sends accidental contact to its interference rule (607(d) Note 1), and Hockey Canada and CARHA penalise unnecessary contact with a goalie out of the crease without excusing the incidental kind. [Rules Primer](../foundation/rules_primer.md) sets out each book.

**Inside the crease (69.3):** this is much stricter. If you **initiate contact with a goalie who is in the crease — incidental or otherwise — and a goal is scored, the goal is disallowed.** Further, if you **establish a significant position within the crease so as to obstruct the goalie's vision and impair their ability to defend the goal**, the goal is disallowed even with no contact at all. **Both halves of that second ground are load-bearing, and the rule defines the first half rather than leaving it to the eye — drop either and it sounds a good deal stricter than it is.** The operative wording is *"establishes a significant position within the goal crease, so as to obstruct the goalkeeper's vision **and** impair his ability to defend his goal"*. And a player does that *"when, in the Referee's judgment, his body, or a substantial portion thereof, is within the goal crease **for more than an instantaneous period of time.**"* So a body that crosses the paint and is gone has not met it, and neither has a body that is in there without impairing the goalie's ability to defend the goal.

**Do not read that narrowing as room to work in, because the IIHF's own Situation Handbook does not apply it.** Asked what the referee decides where a *"Team A player is in the crease screening the goalkeeper, and Team A scores"*, the Handbook answers flatly: *"The goal shall be disallowed, and no additional penalty assessed"*. It cites *"Rule 69.1 and Rule 69.3"* and asks nothing about duration, the goalie's vision or their ability to defend (Situation 69.1, in the same words in the 2025/26 and 2026/27 editions). **The Handbook ranks below the rulebook and says so itself** — its Foreword: *"in case of any contradictions, the valid IIHF Rulebook is always the basis"* — so 69.3's conjunctive test is still the rule, and this is guidance reading it loosely rather than a rule contradicting it. ⚠️ **But the official in front of you may be working from that answer, and you will not get to argue the limbs from the ice.** Screen from outside the paint, and off the red line around it.

**When the goalie makes contact to establish position, give ground at once.** 69.3 also disallows a goal where a goalie establishing position in the crease contacts you there and that impairs them — even if you got out immediately, as the IIHF Situation Handbook confirms (69.6). ⚠️ **And it puts the duty on you by name:** an attacker who *"does not immediately vacate his current position in the goal crease (i.e. give ground to the goalkeeper)"* loses any goal scored. The PWHL prints the same paragraph, with "her", at 71.3.

**Whether you also go to the box is unclear, and it does not matter.** The rule adds a penalty *"whether or not a goal is scored"* (a minor in the NHL and PWHL). But the NHL's, IIHF's and PWHL's own tables say no penalty where the goal comes off (Situation 6D) and a penalty where no goal is scored (6B). No book reconciles the two, and this document does not either. Either way, holding your spot pays nothing. ⚠️ **Give ground the moment a goalie leans into you, whatever your book.** USA Hockey and Hockey Canada write no give-ground rule because under them, and CARHA, you should not be in the crease at all. Hockey Canada 8.5 and CARHA 66(b) bar an attacker from standing there unless the puck is in it, and USA Hockey 625(b) lets no goal stand with you in there unless the puck got there first or the goalie is out of the crease area.

**The practical line: screen from outside the blue paint and off its red line, without contact, and do it constantly. Screening from inside the paint is not legal.** Coaching material that says you may stand in the crease while "playing the puck" overstates it. ⚠️ **If a goalie leans into you to establish position, give ground at once.**

### Rebounds, loose pucks and being pushed into the goalie

**In short:** In a scramble, play the puck rather than the goalie, and make a visible effort to avoid contact even when shoved.

```facts
Rule: NHL and IIHF 69.7, PWHL 71.7 — on a rebound or a loose puck you both play, incidental contact in or out of the crease is permitted, goal allowed, not shoving the goalie in with the puck. USA Hockey, Hockey Canada and CARHA grant none; in the EIHL, blue-paint contact "calls for disallowing a goal"
Rule: NHL and IIHF Rule 69.7, PWHL 71.7, disallow the goal where a goalie is pushed into the net together with the puck by an attacking player after making a stop, and appropriate penalties may follow — the rule needs all three of those conditions, not any push at all
Rule: CARHA 66(b) disallows the goal where "the goalkeeper has been pushed into the net with the puck after making a stop", and penalties follow if applicable — stricter than the NHL, IIHF and PWHL: no "by an attacking player" limb, two conditions not three (CARHA-affiliated adult leagues only)
Rule: NHL and IIHF 69.7, PWHL 71.7 — where the referee judges that a defending player pushed or fouled the attacker into causing the goalie to be pushed into the net together with the puck, the goal "can be permitted", not must, and 69.7 sets no condition on it — the reasonable-effort test is 69.1's
Rule: NHL and IIHF Rule 69.7, PWHL 71.7 — where the puck is under a player in or around the crease, deliberately or otherwise, no goal can be scored by pushing that player and the puck into the net, and appropriate penalties follow if applicable
Rule: A defending player other than the goalie who deliberately covers the puck in the crease gives up a penalty shot (NHL and IIHF 63.6, cross-referenced at 67.4 and 69.7; PWHL 65.6) — a goalie covering it in there is simply a whistle
Rule: NHL and IIHF Rule 69.1, PWHL 71.1 — a defender who pushes, shoves or fouls you into the goalie is not deemed contact initiated by you, provided you made a reasonable effort to avoid it; but pushing a defender into their own goalie is, and a goal scored off it is disallowed
Rule: NHL and IIHF 69.2, PWHL 71.2 — intentional or deliberate contact with a goalie, in or out of the crease, is a minor or major, goal or no goal; under 42.1 and 69.4 (PWHL 71.4) a penalty "should be assessed in every case" of unnecessary contact; charging one in the crease can bring a game misconduct
Rule: USA Hockey 607(d) Note 1 files accidental or unavoidable goalie contact under interference, a 625(a)(8) minor; deliberate contact is charging — 607(a) at least a minor plus misconduct; a reckless one a mandatory major plus game misconduct (607(b)) — plan on a match penalty (602(a))
Rule: USA Hockey Casebook 607 Sit. 4, goalie charged in crease or privileged area: pushed in by a defender AND "clearly made every attempt" to avoid it, no penalty; honest try, minor and misconduct; driving through, arms out or shoulder down, major and game misconduct or match: plan on the match (602(a))
Rule: Some CARHA net-front majors are mandatory; each ejects and bans at least a game — 52(b) for charging a goalie in the crease or any charge that injures; 54(c) the same for a cross-check; 66(e) for injury by interference (30(a), 32(d)); over two steps or strides is a charge (CARHA adult leagues only)
Rule: CARHA Rule 30(a) — every major but an accidental high stick that injures nobody ejects: a player assessed one "shall be ruled off the ice for the remainder of the game", and 32(d) adds at least a one-game suspension save under "the Accidental High Stick Rule" (CARHA-affiliated adult leagues only)
Never: Drive the goalie backwards in a scramble — play the puck. Shoving them into the net with the puck loses the goal in every book that writes it, and deliberate contact draws a penalty, goal or not
Action: Play the puck, not the goalie, when you both arrive at a loose one — only the NHL, IIHF and PWHL forgive even incidental contact in that race, and not in the EIHL's blue paint
```

**Three more provisions worth knowing**, and the first is the one net-front players lean on hardest:

- **Rebounds and loose pucks (69.7):** in a rebound, or where you and the goalie are both going for a loose puck, **incidental contact is permitted inside or outside the crease and the goal is allowed** — incidental contact, in a race you are both making for the same puck. ⚠️ **That carve-out is NHL, IIHF and PWHL only** (the PWHL numbers it 71.7). USA Hockey, Hockey Canada and CARHA write no such permission, and Hockey Canada's Interpretation 1 to 8.5(a) disallows the goal for any contact you initiate, *"regardless of whether the contact occurs inside or outside of the goal crease."* **Nor can you count on it in the Elite League**, although it plays the IIHF book: the EIHL Casebook adds that *"Contact in the blue paint area calls for disallowing a goal"*.

  **69.7 has three more limbs, and they run the other way.** *"In the event that a goalkeeper has been pushed into the net together with the puck by an attacking player after making a stop, the goal will be disallowed"*, with penalties if applicable. All three conditions count — pushed **by an attacker**, **with the puck**, **after a stop** — so it is the rule against scoring by driving a goalie and the puck over the line. **CARHA 66(b) writes it stricter**, with no *"by an attacking player"* limb and no relief for an attacker a defender shoved in: in a CARHA game a defenceman who shoves you into their own goalie can cost your team the goal (CARHA-affiliated adult leagues only). ⚠️ **Play the puck, not the goalie.**

  **The relief sits in the same paragraph — never carry the limit without it.** Where the referee judges that a defender pushed or fouled you into causing it, *"the goal can be permitted"* — can, not must. 69.7 sets no condition on that relief. The reasonable-effort test is 69.1's, and there it decides whether a defender's shove makes the contact yours: NHL Table 14 group 3, and the IIHF's matching table, allow the goal where you made a reasonable effort to avoid contact (3A) and disallow it where you did not (3C). Neither book says whether that test feeds 69.7, so make the effort regardless.

  **A third limb covers a puck underneath somebody.** Where the puck is under a player in or around the crease, *"(deliberately or otherwise)"*, *"a goal cannot be scored by pushing this player together with the puck into the goal"*. Digging at a covered puck is fair; shovelling the pile into the net is not a goal. A defender **other than the goalie** who deliberately covers it in the crease gives you a penalty shot (Rule 63.6, through 67.4); a goalie covering it there is simply a whistle.

  **The IIHF prints all four limbs at the same number**, and both books list the pushed-goalie play again, more loosely, at 78.5(ix): take the conditions from 69.7.
- **Pushed into the goalie (69.1):** if a **defender** pushes, shoves or fouls you into the goalie, that is **not treated as contact initiated by you** — provided you made a reasonable effort to avoid it. Conversely, if **you** push a defender into their own goalie, that **counts as contact initiated by you** and the goal is disallowed.
- **Penalty (69.2):** deliberate contact with a goalie, inside or outside the crease, is a minor or a major **whether or not a goal is scored**, and under 42.1 and 69.4 (PWHL 71.4) a penalty *"should be assessed in every case"* of unnecessary contact. ⚠️ **Arriving into a goalie is charging, and no book caps it at a minor.** NHL 42.1, and PWHL 42.1 with "her": *"A minor, major or a major and a game misconduct shall be imposed on a player who charges a goalkeeper while the goalkeeper is within his goal crease"*. Each book's 42.4 allows a match penalty for a deliberate injury or attempt, its 21.1 makes one mandatory for a deliberate attempt to injure, and its 42.5 makes the game misconduct mandatory when a major is called for an injury to the face or head — as the EIHL Casebook does at its Rule 42. The IIHF's 42.1 says only *"A penalty"*, tiered at 42.2–42.4 up to a major plus a game misconduct at the referee's discretion, and it has no match penalty.
- **Under USA Hockey, what you did decides the rule: accidental contact is an interference minor; deliberate contact is charging, never two minutes alone.** Accidental or unavoidable contact with a goalie is interference, a 625(a)(8) minor (607(d) Note 1); *"Any deliberate body contact or check that is delivered to the goalkeeper shall be penalized as charging."* ⚠️ **Charging is never a bare minor there:** 607(a) and 607(c) are a minor plus a misconduct or a major plus a game misconduct, **607(b) makes a reckless charge a mandatory major plus game misconduct** — *"shall be assessed"* — and 607(e) allows a match penalty. **For a reckless charge, plan on a match penalty**: Rule 602(a) says one *"shall be assessed"* for reckless endangerment. **The privileged area is not the blue paint**: it runs between the end-zone faceoff spots out to the end boards (607(d) Note 2; *Casebook* Rule 607, Situation 6), which is exactly where you work.

  **The Casebook (Rule 607, Situation 4) sets three rungs.** Forced into the goalie by a defender **and** having *"clearly made every attempt to avoid the contact"*: no penalty. Going hard to the net and making *"an honest attempt to avoid the goalkeeper once it is realized contact is unavoidable"*: a minor plus misconduct. Accelerating through, arms out or shoulder dropped: a major plus game misconduct, or a match penalty. **So an honest effort alone is not a full defence — the no-penalty rung needs the defender's foul as well.**

### The same play under the other books

**In short:** Whatever book you play under, keep both skates out of the paint and off its line, and arrive under control.

```facts
Rule: USA Hockey 625(b) flatly bars the goal with an attacker in the crease whoever scored, unless the puck got there first, the goalie is out of the crease area, or a defender put them there — none of which licenses interference: a minor under 625(a)(8), and the goal off (Casebook Situation 9)
Rule: USA Hockey 625(b): their team's puck in the attacking zone, goalie touching the crease — an attacker standing, holding a stick or skating through it is a neutral-zone faceoff; Casebook Situation 10: a momentary stick or skate costs no whistle unless it interferes with the goalie, but voids a goal
Rule: Hockey Canada 8.5 is not flat — where a teammate outside the crease scores, the goal stands "provided that the player who was in the goal crease does not attempt to play the puck, interfere with the play or obstruct the Goaltender's view or movements"
Rule: Hockey Canada has an impairment ground too, and it names vision — Interpretation 3 to 8.5(a) disallows the goal where a player standing in the crease impedes "the goaltender's vision or movements"; it answers to NHL 69.3, not to 69.1
Rule: Hockey Canada 8.5 puts the onus in the rule — attackers "who do not make an effort to avoid the goaltender must be penalized" — but keeps the questions apart: a referee may disallow a goal "without assessing an attacking player a penalty"
Rule: Hockey Canada's Interpretation 2 to 8.5(a) makes the penalty on an attacker fouled into the goalie mandatory — "must be penalized" — where you make no effort to avoid the contact; USA Hockey asks the same effort in its Casebook, at Rule 607 Situation 4, not in its rules
Rule: Interpretation 2 also directs the referee to penalise the defender who fouled you; Hockey Canada's Interpretation 1 to 8.5(a) is no relief from that minor — it excepts the goal, and only for an attacker whom the foul left unable to avoid the contact
Rule: On a screen from outside the crease the books split — the IIHF prints the NHL's Rule 69, so a British reader is under it; USA Hockey writes no goalie-vision provision in its rules or its Casebook, and Hockey Canada's two references reach only a player in the crease
Rule: Hockey Canada is not capped at a minor, nor is USA Hockey (607(a)–(c), (e)) — 8.5(b) reaches a major plus game misconduct on degree of violence with nobody hurt, and mandatorily for charging the goalie and for any such foul that injures; 8.5(c) a match penalty, and 8.5(d) bars a misconduct
Rule: CARHA is not crease-only — 66(b) makes deliberate goalie contact a minor "whether in or out of the goal crease" or a discretionary major; 66(e)'s mandatory major for injury by interference names no location; either major ejects and bans you at least a game (30(a), 32(d); CARHA adult leagues only)
Rule: CARHA 66(b) (CARHA leagues) bars standing in the crease unless "the puck is in the goal crease area", like Hockey Canada 8.5, but voids a goal whoever scores; neither has the NHL's incidental-contact allowance. CARHA's push-in relief saves position only — contact impeding the goalie is still a minor
Rule: Fouled into the goalie, still try to avoid them — under CARHA 66(b) an attacker whose team has the puck, fouled by a defender, who "fails to attempt to avoid" contact takes a minor and loses any goal; tripped or caused to fall without the puck, the same failure is a minor wherever the goalie is
Never: Plant your feet in the blue paint, or on the crease line that every book settling the question counts as crease — the paint risks the goal under every book, and the zone under USA Hockey 625(b)
```

**The play does not change from book to book — feet out of the paint, no contact with the goalie, give ground when they establish position. What changes is how much getting it wrong costs you.**

**USA Hockey is stricter, and it changes how you play the net front.** Its Rule 625(b) does not use the NHL's "position alone does not decide it" premise: *"No goal may be scored with an attacking player in the goal crease unless the puck has preceded the player(s) into the goal crease or the goalkeeper is out of the goal crease area."* No contact or impairment is needed. The same clause calls a neutral-zone faceoff whenever an attacker stands, holds a stick or skates through the crease while the puck is in the attacking zone, your team has it, and the goalie is touching the crease — so a foot in the paint can cost the zone as well as the goal. The crease there includes its lines and the space above them, up to the top of the goal frame.

The *Casebook* (Rule 625, Situation 10) softens the whistle but not the goal: a stick or skate momentarily in the crease will not stop play unless you interfere with the goalie, ⚠️ **but a goal scored while it is there, with the goalie in the crease, comes off.** The printed rule's one relief: if a defender's interference put you in the crease, play continues and a legal goal counts.

**An empty crease is open only while the goalie is genuinely out of the play.** The *Casebook* (Situation 9) allows the goal where the goalie is completely out of the crease and you get there before the puck, ⚠️ **but if the goalie is trying to get back and you interfere, the goal comes off and you take an interference penalty.**

**The IIHF's Rule 69 is the NHL's, with one addition**: where an attacker is in the crease and *"does not leave immediately"*, the referee may stop play for a neutral-zone faceoff — so, as under USA Hockey, loitering in the paint can cost the zone before anyone shoots. It also says *"relevant contact"*, and weighs the degree and nature of the contact over the goalie's exact location.

**Hockey Canada (Rule 8.5) bars standing in the crease unless the puck is in it — but carves the case back out.** If you are in the crease without interfering and a teammate outside it scores, the goal stands, *"provided that the player who was in the goal crease does not attempt to play the puck, interfere with the play or obstruct the Goaltender's view or movements."* **USA Hockey has no such carve-out**, so the same play — a body in the paint, a teammate scoring from outside — is a goal under Hockey Canada and no goal under USA Hockey. Hockey Canada's impairment test (Interpretation 3 to 8.5(a)) names vision but reaches only a player standing in the crease, and it keeps goal and penalty apart: a referee may disallow the goal without penalising you.

**Make the effort to avoid the goalie, whatever put you there** — ⚠️ **being fouled into them is not on its own a defence in any of these books.** They differ in what the effort buys:

- **NHL and PWHL** (Table 14, Situation 3B): with no reasonable effort and no goal scored, *"A minor and/or major penalty"*; with a goal, the goal comes off and the penalty is the referee's call. **The IIHF is firmer**: its no-effort rows say the referee *"must assess the appropriate penalty"*.
- **Hockey Canada** (Interpretation 2 to 8.5(a)): a fouled attacker who makes no effort *"must be penalized"*, and the defender who fouled you is penalised too. The goal survives only if the foul left you **unable** to avoid the contact (Interpretation 1).
- **CARHA 66(b)**: failing to try to avoid the goalie after a defender's interference is a minor and loses any goal; tripped without the puck, the same failure is a minor wherever the goalie is.
- **USA Hockey**: its push-in relief (617(c)(4), 625(b)) saves your **position** only; contact that interferes with the goalie is at least a 625(a)(8) minor, and deliberate contact is charging, never a bare minor (607), and the effort test lives in its *Casebook*, set out above.

**Outside the paint the books split.** Only NHL and IIHF 69.1 (PWHL 71.1) can take a goal off for a screen outside the crease, and only one that stops the goalie moving freely in their crease. USA Hockey writes nothing about screening or a goalie's vision, and Hockey Canada's two references to the goalie's view reach only a player in the crease. **Inside the paint, USA Hockey, Hockey Canada and CARHA are all stricter than the NHL. Whichever you are under, screen from outside the blue, and off the red line around it.**

**CARHA (Rule 66(b)) bars the crease flatly**: no standing in it unless the puck is there, and a goal scored meanwhile does not count — Hockey Canada's bar without its carve-out, close to USA Hockey's. Any contact that impedes the goalie is a minor, with no incidental-contact allowance. Its relief for a defender's interference putting you in the crease sits beside ⚠️ **a separate minor, and a lost goal, for failing to avoid the goalie**, and the book does not say which governs. Some rec leagues add a stricter house rule still; this document has no count of how many. CARHA governs CARHA-affiliated leagues only, so read your own league's rule.

**What it costs.** ⚠️ **Arriving into the goalie can end your game in every book — arrive under control and stop short of them.**

- **Hockey Canada 8.5**: 8.5(a) is the minor. **8.5(b)** is a major plus a game misconduct on the violence of the impact, and ⚠️ **mandatory for charging the goalie, and for any interference with the goalie that injures** — *"will be assessed"*. Charging there means a jump, two or more strides, excessive distance, a violent and unnecessary check, or a blind-side hit. **8.5(c)** is a match penalty for attempting to injure or deliberately injuring a goalie. 8.5(e) makes the game misconduct automatic on any 8.5(b) major, and 8.5(d) rules out a misconduct, so there is no ten-minute step.
- **CARHA**: 66(b)'s minor is the floor, and its major for deliberate contact with a goalie *"whether in or out of the goal crease"* is the referee's call. ⚠️ **Three rungs are mandatory:** **52(b)**, a major and game misconduct for charging a goalie **in the crease, or for any charge that injures an opponent**; **54(c)**, the same for cross-checking a goalie in the crease or any cross-check that injures; and **66(e)**, a major for injuring anyone by interference, anywhere. Under **30(a)** any major puts you off for the rest of the game, except an accidental high stick that injures nobody, and **32(d)** adds at least a one-game suspension, so each costs this game and at least your next. More than two strides is a charge (Note to 52(a)), which you can reach from the slot. CARHA-affiliated adult leagues only.
- USA Hockey's ladder is set out above, at 607; the NHL's, the PWHL's and the IIHF's at 69.2 and 42.

## 7. Working the Half-Wall

The **half-wall** is the boards area level with the faceoff dot, between the corner and the point. It is the winger's home base in the offensive zone (see [Winger](../positions/winger.md)) and the most common place from which offensive-zone possessions are organised.

It is a good place to hold the puck because you have the boards protecting one side, you can see the whole zone, and you are equidistant from four different plays.

### The four options from the half-wall

```facts
Action: Know all four before the puck arrives, and know which one is on
Options: Low — to the corner or behind the net; the safe continuation that feeds the cycle and resets the defence's problem
Options: High — to the point; the low-to-high shot generator, best when the defence has collapsed and left the point unpressured
Options: Middle — the seam pass into the slot or the backdoor; highest value, highest risk, shortest window
Options: Yourself — drive the net or cut to the middle, best when your check is flat-footed or has turned their hips
Never: Arrive on the half-wall with only one option in mind — you will play it regardless of whether the slot was open
```

Know all four before the puck arrives, and know which is on:

1. **Low — to the corner or behind the net.** The safe continuation. Feeds the cycle, keeps possession, resets the defence's problem.
2. **High — to the point.** The low-to-high shot generator. Best when the defence has collapsed low and left the point unpressured.
3. **Middle — the seam pass into the slot or the backdoor.** The royal road pass. Highest value, highest risk, shortest window.
4. **Yourself — drive the net or cut to the middle.** Attack the inside off the wall. Best when your check is flat-footed, has turned their hips, or has over-committed to taking away a pass.

![](diagram:oz-half-wall-options)

The failure is having only one of these in mind. A player who arrives on the half-wall intending to pass to the point will do that regardless of whether the slot was open.

### Buying time

```facts
Technique: Cut back — skate a few feet toward the corner and cut back up the wall before the defender can reach you, never to face the wall with them behind you; their momentum has to reverse, yours does not
Technique: Delay and curl — before the checker can reach you, turn away from pressure, protect the puck and curl toward the middle into open ice, coming out facing the ice
Technique: Receive with your body between checker and puck, then spin off once they lean on you — turning into pressure immediately is how you get pinned
Never: Turn your back to anyone coming at you near the wall, or duck — keep your head up and your chin off your chest. This outranks everything else here
Options: Bump it to the level-support player and take an immediate return pass — it resets the defender's read entirely
Action: Feet moving, always — a stationary player on the wall gets pinned; one with momentum can go three directions
```

You often need two more seconds than you have. Ways to manufacture them:

- **Cut back.** Skate a few feet toward the corner and cut back up the wall. The defender's momentum has to be reversed; yours does not. Turn while they are still too far away to hit you; once they can reach you it is too late to turn, so go side-on to the wall, forearm and hip against it, and never turn to face the wall with them behind you.
- **Delay and curl.** Before the checker can reach you, turn away from the pressure, protect the puck, and curl toward the middle — into open ice, not against the wall, where turning puts your back to anyone coming at you; the safety qualifier in the next bullet governs this one too. Coming out of the curl you will be facing the ice with your options refreshed.
- **Don't turn into pressure first.** When you receive the puck under pressure, take it with your body between checker and puck, then spin off once they are leaning on you, never while they are still coming at you. Turning into pressure immediately is how you get pinned. **Safety qualifier that outranks all of this:** the orientation to aim for at the wall is skates parallel to the boards, forearm and hip to the wall, head up and chin off your chest. Never turn your back to anyone coming at you near the wall, never duck, never the point of your shoulder, and not the whole shoulder if you can help it. Chest-square to the wall is the fallback, not the target. See [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md).
- **Bump it and get it back.** A short pass to the level-support player and an immediate return pass re-sets the defender's read entirely, and often you get the puck back in more space than you gave it away in.
- **Feet moving, always.** A stationary player on the wall gets pinned. A player with momentum can go three directions.

## 8. Behind the Net as an Attacking Position

Behind the net is not where the puck goes to die. It is one of the best attacking positions on the ice, and most amateur teams treat it as a place you end up rather than a place you go on purpose.

### Why it is so good

**In short:** Treat behind the net as a place to attack from: you can see the whole zone and the goalie has to keep turning.

```facts
Key: From behind the goal line you look up at the whole zone with all five defenders and the goalie in front of you
Key: Nobody can come at you from directly behind, because the net and the end boards are there — that is geometry, not immunity
Risk: You can still be driven into the goal frame behind the net. Keep your head up and know who is arriving
Rule: Checking from behind is a major "anywhere on the playing surface" (NHL 43.3), with no minor written for it (NHL 43.2) and a mandatory game misconduct (NHL 43.5)
Rule: USA Hockey makes a check from behind a major plus game misconduct where it is reckless or head first into the boards or goal frame (608(b)), or forceful on a player standing along the boards, back to the middle of the ice (Casebook 608, Situation 1); plan on a match penalty if reckless (602(a))
Rule: Driven into the goal — Hockey Canada 7.5(c)'s frame off the head or head first (7.6(c)/7.7(c) on the head), CARHA 53(b)'s net — beats the major: if defenceless, both mandate a Match (CARHA adult leagues only); NHL 43.4 allows one; NHL and PWHL 21.1 and CARHA 48(a) require one if meant to injure
Rule: Hockey Canada does not confine its Match penalty for checking from behind to the boards or goal frame — 7.5(c)’s second paragraph assesses one for deliberately attempting to injure or deliberately injuring an opponent by checking them from behind (7.6(c) or 7.7(c) if it reaches the head)
Rule: CARHA 53(a) reaches a check from behind anywhere on the ice and allows a Match penalty there too (CARHA-affiliated adult leagues only)
Rule: USA Hockey 608(c) needs neither boards nor net — its match penalty is for checking from behind “with excessive force while the opponent is in a vulnerable or defenseless position”, and Rule 608’s Note includes open ice
Read: Every time the puck crosses behind the net the goalie must pick a post and rotate — every rotation is a moment they are not square
Key: Passes from below the goal line finished at 6.73%, second only to royal road passes — volunteer-tracked Passing Project data, 51,308 shots at 5v5, all attempts
```

- **You can see everything and nobody can see you the same way.** From behind the goal line you are looking *up* at the whole zone with all five defenders and the goalie in front of you. You have the best view on the ice and the defence has to defend with their backs to their own net.
- **Nobody can come at you from directly behind** — there is nothing there but the net and the end boards, so a defender can only come from one of two sides and you can see them coming. ⚠️ **That is geometry, not immunity: keep your head up and know who is arriving, because you can still be driven into the goal frame back here.**
- **The goalie has to turn their head.** Every time the puck crosses behind the net, the goalie must decide which post to seal and rotate to track you. Every rotation is a moment when they are not square to a shot.
- **Passes out to the slot are exceptionally dangerous.** Passes originating below the goal line finished at **6.73%** — the second-most dangerous pass type measured, behind only royal road passes (Alan Wells' analysis of **Ryan Stimson's** Passing Project data, [via NHL.com](https://www.nhl.com/kraken/news/analytics-with-alison-dangerous-passes-327627898); **volunteer-tracked, 51,308 shots at 5v5, counting all attempts rather than shots on goal**).

**What it costs to hit someone from behind back here.** ⚠️ **Checking from behind is never just two minutes, and driven into the goal frame it is the hit several books make a mandatory match penalty.**

- **NHL and PWHL**: a major *"anywhere on the playing surface"* (43.3), with no minor at all (43.2) and a mandatory game misconduct (43.5). 43.4 allows a match penalty for an attempt to injure, and 21.1 requires one for a deliberate attempt to injure. On the head, the harsher of Rule 43 and Rule 48 applies.
- **IIHF**: 43.1 reaches a hit from behind *"into the boards, the goal frame, or on open ice in any manner"*. There is no minor (43.2), so plan on a major and an **automatic** game misconduct (43.3), with supplementary discipline beyond that (43.4), and at a club in England or Wales, outside the Elite League, IHUK adds at least a one-game ban. It has no match penalty. On the head, plan on the same major and automatic game misconduct (43.3, 48.3), never on a minor.
- **USA Hockey**: never a bare minor — 608(a) is a minor plus a misconduct or a major plus a game misconduct — and 608(b) **requires** a major plus game misconduct where the check *"recklessly endangers an opponent, or causes them to go head first into the boards or goal frame"*. Its *Casebook* (608, Situation 1) says the major plus game misconduct, or a match penalty, *"must be called"* in three cases: any forceful check on a player standing along the boards with their back to the middle of the ice; any player thrust head first into the boards or goal frame; and any reckless check from behind. The first needs no recklessness. 608(c) **requires** a match penalty for a reckless check from behind with excessive force on a vulnerable player, open ice included. For any reckless endangerment, plan on a match (602(a)).
- **Hockey Canada 7.5(c)**: a match penalty *"will be assessed"* for a hit from behind *"into the boards or goal frame, in such a way that the player is unable to protect or defend themselves"*, and another for deliberately injuring, or trying to, by checking from behind anywhere. Short of that, a hit from behind is at least a minor plus a game misconduct (7.5(a), (e)). A hit that reaches the head is head contact (7.6(c); 7.7(c) in Junior and Senior), also a match penalty where they could not protect themselves or you meant to injure them. A head-first drive into the boards or goal frame that reaches the head pays the harsher of 7.5 and 7.6.
- **CARHA 53(b)**: the same mandatory match penalty into the boards or *"goal net"*, and its Note tells referees *"not to substitute other penalties"*. 53(a) reaches a hit from behind anywhere on the ice, where a match *"could"* also be given, and 48(a) makes one mandatory for a deliberate injury or an attempt. CARHA-affiliated adult leagues only.

### What to do from there

**In short:** From behind the net, look first for the pass out to the slot; if nothing is on, reverse or rim it and keep possession.

```facts
Action: Pass out to the slot — the primary play, not the last resort; look for the high-slot forward or the backdoor player
Options: The wrap-around — carry round the post and stuff it far side before the goalie seals; best as a threat, because sealing hard opens the slot pass
Options: The walkout — come out into the slot yourself on your forehand, crossing the royal road with the puck
Options: The bank off the goalie — from a tight angle behind the goal line, off the back of their pads into the net; legal, and occasionally free
Options: Reverse or rim to restart the cycle if nothing is on — possession retained is a perfectly good outcome
Rule: The goalkeeper's restricted area restricts only the goalie (geometry NHL Rule 1.8, penalty 27.8 / 63.2(viii)) — skaters may go anywhere behind the net at any time
Read: USA Hockey and Hockey Canada write no trapezoid at all, so under those books their goalie may retrieve a dumped puck anywhere behind the net — it changes the calculus of dumping it in
Rule: No trapezoid is not no restriction on their goalie — USA Hockey 614(c)(1) and CARHA 58(b) make it a minor to freeze the puck behind the goal line, body out of the crease, and Hockey Canada's Interpretation 3 to 10.1(a), clause v, gives a minor with "No warning" for freezing it outside the crease
Rule: Both books write a clause for your dump-in exactly — Hockey Canada's clause iv for a goalie who comes out "to beat an attacking player to the puck" and jumps on it, no warning, and USA Hockey's Casebook Situation 6, which answers the identical facts a minor
Read: Under USA Hockey when pressured, a goalie who came out to cut the angle may still cover after a save out front, never behind the goal line (under Hockey Canada, unsettled)
Rule: In any British competition, plan as though their goalie may come out for a dump-in — England Ice Hockey's R&R 22.3 calls the restricted area "not currently enforced", its scope and standing unsettled; the Elite League's Casebook leaves IIHF 27.7 standing
```

- **Pass out to the slot.** This is the primary play, not the last resort. Look for the high-slot forward or the backdoor player. Because the pass comes from below the goal line, it crosses the royal road and arrives at a player already inside the home-plate area — both levers at once: it moves the goalie and it puts the puck in a dangerous place.
- **The wrap-around.** Carry the puck around the post and try to stuff it in on the far side before the goalie can push across and seal. It works when the goalie has committed to the near post, and it is at its best as a *threat*: a goalie who fears the wrap seals hard to the post, which opens the pass to the slot.
- **The walkout.** Come out from behind the net into the slot yourself, on your forehand, with the puck. You cross the royal road carrying the puck and you arrive in the most dangerous area on the ice.
- **The bank off the goalie.** Legal, and occasionally free. From a tight angle behind the goal line you can bank the puck off the back of the goalie's pads into the net.
- **Reverse or rim to restart the cycle.** If nothing is on, feed it back out to the corner you came from or rim it to the far corner and rotate. Possession retained is a perfectly good outcome.

![](diagram:oz-behind-the-net)

**The trapezoid does not apply to you.** The goalkeeper's restricted area behind the net (NHL Rule 1.8; the penalty is 27.8 / 63.2(viii)) restricts only the **goalie**. Skaters may go anywhere behind the net. It exists in NHL and IIHF play, but **not under USA Hockey, Hockey Canada or CARHA**, and the PWHL's book says it does not enforce one (Rules 1.8 and 27.8) while still printing the goalie's minor (65.2(viii)) — so against a PWHL goalie, plan for her to come out. Under those books their goalie may fetch a dumped puck anywhere behind the net, which changes the case for dumping it in.

**No trapezoid does not mean no limit on their goalie.** USA Hockey 614(c) and CARHA 58(b) make it a minor for a goalie to freeze the puck behind the goal line with their body entirely outside the crease, and Hockey Canada (Interpretation 3 to Rule 10.1(a), clause v) gives a minor, with *"No warning"*, for freezing it outside the crease. Hockey Canada and USA Hockey both write a clause for your dump-in exactly: Hockey Canada's clause iv, for a goalie who comes out *"to beat an attacking player to the puck"* and jumps on it, and USA Hockey's *Casebook* (614(c), Situation 6), which answers the same play with a minor. Those are retrieval clauses, not save clauses. A goalie who came out to cut the angle may cover straight off a save out front, under USA Hockey only when pressured, though never behind the goal line, and Hockey Canada's wording leaves even that a reading; [Goaltender](../positions/goaltender.md) sets out both books. So dump it in expecting them to move it, and chase as though they cannot smother it. If the goalie comes out for it, pull up rather than finish on them.

**In Britain, plan as though their goalie may come out, whatever competition you are in.** The IIHF book carries the restricted area (27.7) and the Elite League's Casebook leaves it standing, but England Ice Hockey's *Rules & Regulations* 22.3 call it *"not currently enforced"*, its scope and standing unsettled: nothing read here says whether it reaches an Elite League fixture, or Scotland and Northern Ireland, and the In-House Rules do not mention it. Ask your league, and look whether the lines are painted — though IIHF 27.7 turns on where the puck is, not on the markings.

## 9. Attacking Off the Rush vs Attacking Off a Set Possession

These are two different games and most amateur teams have only one of them.

### Off the rush

```facts
Read: You enter with speed and attack before the defence is set — the chance comes from numbers and momentum
Key: Chances appear in the first three or four seconds and then vanish; speed and the moment of entry are the resource
Options: The late-arriving trailer into the high slot, the drive to the net that occupies a defender, and the delay — curling back to let a second wave arrive
Key: Shots off the rush are more dangerous than equivalent shots from a set possession, because the goalie is also moving and re-establishing depth
```

You enter the zone with speed and attack before the defence is set. The chance comes from **numbers and momentum** — a 2-on-1, a 3-on-2, a trailer arriving late into space.

- The defining resource is **speed and the moment of entry**. Chances appear in the first three or four seconds and then vanish.
- The high-value plays are the **late-arriving trailer** into the high slot, the **drive to the net** that occupies a defender, and the **delay** — curling back up toward the blue line to let a second wave arrive.
- Shots off the rush are more dangerous than equivalent shots from a set possession, because the goalie is also moving and re-establishing depth.
- Full treatment of getting into the zone in the first place is in [Zone Entries](zone_entries.md).

### Off a set possession

```facts
Read: The defence is established, everyone is back and nothing is open — the chance comes from movement and patience
Key: The defining resource is time on the puck and the willingness to hold it
Options: Cycle, low-to-high with a screen, seam pass, net-front chaos, behind-the-net feed
Read: Coverage lapses arrive after a long shift of defending — a defence that has been chasing for forty seconds is about to make a mistake
```

The defence is established, everyone is back, and nothing is open. The chance comes from **movement and patience** — cycling, rotating, forcing the defence to move until coverage breaks.

- The defining resource is **time on the puck and the willingness to hold it**.
- The high-value plays are the ones in sections 4 to 8: cycle, low-to-high with a screen, seam pass, net-front chaos, behind-the-net feed.
- Coverage lapses tend to arrive after a long shift of defending — a defence that has been chasing for forty seconds is a defence about to make a mistake.

### Why having only one is a problem

```facts
Risk: A rush-only team has nothing once the rush is denied at the blue line — they enter, lose it, and go back the other way
Risk: A cycle-only team never threatens quickly, so the defence gets set every time and the cycle only ever produces perimeter shots
Action: Have a plan for the second wave — a chip past the defenceman, a soft area dump, or a controlled retreat and re-attack
Action: When a cycle produces a neutral-zone turnover, somebody must be anticipating and going the other way with speed
```

- **A rush-only team** dies against any structured defensive team, because when the rush is denied at the blue line they have nothing. They enter, lose the puck, and go back the other way. Their offensive-zone time is measured in seconds.
- **A cycle-only team** is easy to defend because it never threatens quickly. The defence gets set every single time, and the cycle only ever produces perimeter shots.

**How to have both:** the practical test is what your team does on the *second* wave. When the rush is stopped at the blue line, do you have a plan to establish possession — a chip past the defenceman, a soft area dump, a controlled retreat and re-attack? And when a cycle produces a turnover in the neutral zone, is anyone anticipating and going the other way with speed? See [Risk Management](../hockey-iq/risk_management.md) and [Scanning and Anticipation](../hockey-iq/scanning_and_anticipation.md).

## 10. Regaining Possession After a Shot

**Treat the seconds immediately after a shot as the most dangerous moment in a possession — and this one is measured.** The NHL's public play-by-play feed timestamps every shot and every goal, so the hazard can be read straight off it. Across games 2024020001–2024020400 of the 2024-25 regular season (`api-web.nhle.com/v1/gamecenter/{id}/play-by-play`), a team that put a shot on goal scored again within the next **three seconds 1.37%** of the time, against roughly **0.26%** for a three-second window picked at random — about **five times** the base rate. Put the other way round: **11.4% of all goals came within three seconds of a preceding shot on goal by the same team**, and 19.7% within ten. These are figures derived from the raw feed rather than published statistics, but the direction is not in doubt. The goalie has committed, is often down, and is out of position. The puck is loose in the most dangerous area on the ice. Every defender has just been pulled toward the shot.

This is also the moment most amateur teams stop playing — the shot goes in, everyone watches, and the defence walks out with it.

### F3's job

```facts
Convention: With three forwards rotating and both defencemen holding the blue line — the default shape this section describes — F3 is the high forward; a coaching choice, not a law
Position: Be the safety — at or near the top of the circle, so a rebound straight out is yours and a counter-attack has to go through you
Action: Cover the vacated point if a defenceman activates — F3 takes the blue line in this guide's default setup, which wants two on the line
Options: Under a five-player rotation whichever player is highest covers; a third variant slides the weak-side defenceman across to the middle instead
Read: If the rebound squirts to the high slot, jump — you are the closest player with forward momentum
Position: The other two forwards go to the net — one to the front for the screen and tip, one hunting the strong-side rebound
```

In the standard setup, **F3 is the high forward** and their responsibilities on a shot are, in order:

1. **Be the safety.** Sit at or near the top of the circle so a rebound that comes straight out is yours, and so a counter-attack has to go through you.
2. **Cover the vacated point if a defenceman activates.** If the strong-side defenceman pinches or dives to the middle, **F3 takes the blue line** — in the standard setup described earlier, which wants two players on the line at all times. This is a coaching choice, and a common alternative is the **five-player rotation**, where nobody has a fixed line job and whichever player is highest at that moment covers, defenceman or forward. A third variant simply accepts one player on the line while the D is down and asks the *weak-side* defenceman to slide across to the middle instead. Find out which your team plays — the shape described in section 3 is a default, not a law.
3. **Read the rebound and jump.** If the puck squirts to the high slot, you are the closest player with forward momentum. That is a high-danger chance handed to you.

The other two forwards go **to the net**: one to the front for the screen and tip, one hunting the strong-side rebound.

### The defenceman's pinch

**In short:** Pinch only when you will arrive as the puck does and a forward can rotate up to cover your line.

```facts
Read: "If you can't arrive on contact, don't go" — and clearly first on an uncontested race. Pinch when the winger is flat-footed or facing the glass or on a loose puck or tired line
Never: Pinch when your forwards are already below you, when the opponent has turned up ice with speed, when protecting a narrow lead late, or when your partner has not seen you go
Technique: Stick in the lane before your feet commit; angle the winger to the boards, not the middle; seal the wall with position, pin the puck, not them; check only where your league allows, never their back
Priority: Take the puck, or — where your league permits body checking — take the player; achieve neither and you have created an odd-man rush against your own team. A winger facing the glass is not that player
Rule: A winger facing the glass is defenceless: a hit in the back is a major and game misconduct, no minor (NHL, IIHF, PWHL 43). USA Hockey: minor and misconduct at least; forceful, an ejection (Casebook 608 Situation 1); hit into a puck battle, a minor — reckless, plan on a match (640(e),(g),(h), 602(a))
Rule: At a club in England or Wales, outside the Elite League, a game misconduct for checking from behind or a head check, or a boarding major, is at least a one-game ban (IHUK DOPS C.1)
Rule: CARHA has no checking: charging (52(a)) is a minor or major, a major on injury; intentional body contact, or not averting it (49(a)) likewise, a major if intentional contact injures; a major ejects (30(a)); a minor into the boards doubles (49(b)), two of three that eject (32(a)) (adult leagues only)
Rule: Body checking is banned at USA Hockey 12U and below, in all girls'/women's and non-check adult classifications (604(a)), and at Hockey Canada U13 and below, in female hockey and any Member-approved division (7.3) — and both are floors: a local governing body may bar it in any classification
Rule: IHUK juniors check from U14, not U10 or U12; ask other leagues; Women's NIHL is non-checking. Outside the WNIHL, women's IIHF 101.1 allows a body check only with clear intent to play or win the puck; any step or glide into her is at least a minor; never use the boards to eliminate, push or pin her
Rule: Checking a player without the puck is an NHL 56.2(iii) minor, not capped there — IIHF 56.5 and Hockey Canada 8.3(b) reach a major and game misconduct with nobody hurt; USA Hockey: a minor (640(b)); if reckless, that pair (640(g)) and plan on a match (602(a)); adult men: Note 2 roughing at least
Rule: Where you may check at all, finish only a check already being delivered — NHL 56.1 "immediately" after loss of possession, Hockey Canada 8.3 an arm and stick length at release, IIHF 56.1 a reasonable window; USA Hockey 640(b) bars any avoidable one, and its Casebook wants four conditions at once
Rule: The PWHL limits the check (52.1): hold ground you established, lean in a race for the puck alone, angle her off moving her way — never step or glide into her, leave your lane, meet her head-on, or extend an arm, shoulder or hip after angling her. Each is an illegal hit; a major ejects (52.5)
Convention: How aggressively defencemen pinch is a coaching choice — some green-light both, some only the strong side, some forbid going below the circles; ask
```

A **pinch** is stepping down from the offensive blue line to keep the puck in the zone or to win a puck on the wall. **Do not confuse it with a step-up**, which is challenging an attacker in the neutral zone or at your own line — different play, different zone. ⚠️ **And whatever the criteria say, arrive while the winger still has the puck** — a check you decide on after it has gone is a late hit, and it is not capped at a minor in any book.

The decision criteria, which are consistent across coaching sources:

**Pinch when:**
- You can **arrive on contact** — you and the puck get there at the same time. "If you can't arrive on contact, don't go." That is the test when an opposing player is arriving too. Getting there level lets you play the puck or, where your book allows contact at all, seal the wall — or, where your league permits body checking, check a winger who can see you coming. Arriving a beat late is the failure: a check already being delivered as the puck goes may be finished, but one you decide on afterwards is a late hit (NHL 56.1 and 56.2, below). On an **uncontested race for a loose puck or a rim**, the bar is higher — you need to arrive **clearly first**, since there is nobody to seal and second place just means your back is to the play. [Forechecking Systems](forechecking_systems.md) applies the same two-part test.
- The opposing winger is **flat-footed or facing the glass**, not already turned up ice with speed — a reason to go for the puck, never licence to hit a player facing the glass.
- A forward is **able to rotate high** and cover your line.
- It is a **loose puck, a bobble or a tired line** — the best windows.

**Do not pinch when:**
- Your forwards are already below you and nobody can cover the line;
- The opponent has already turned up ice with speed and their head up;
- You are protecting a narrow lead late (see [Game Management](game_management.md));
- Your partner has not seen you go.

![](diagram:pinch-centre-high)

**Technique:** stick in the passing lane *before* your feet commit. Angle the winger into the boards rather than toward the middle, and close with short explosive steps, getting your feet under you for the last two or three strides rather than accelerating into the arrival. Where your book allows contact, seal the wall with your position — never by extending a hip or pinning them; check only where your league permits body checking, and never into a back turned to you. Where it does not, and in women's hockey, the limits are set out below.

The governing principle is **take the puck, or — where your league permits body checking — take the player**; achieve neither and you have created an odd-man rush against your own team — and a winger facing the glass is never that player: pull up and play the puck.

**If the pinch fails:** retreat toward the **slot**, not along the boards. Skating back down the wall follows the puck and abandons the middle; cutting to the slot re-establishes your gap and delays the attack until support arrives.

**How aggressively defencemen pinch is a coaching choice.** Some teams give both defencemen a green light in the offensive zone; some allow the strong-side defenceman only; some forbid going below the top of the circles. **Ask.** A defenceman pinching in a system that does not expect it is, in coaching experience, among the commonest sources of odd-man rushes against amateur teams — coaching emphasis, not a count.

**What it costs.** Every rule below is avoided the same way: ⚠️ **arrive while the winger still has the puck, take the puck or seal the wall, and never hit a player who cannot see you coming.** [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md) §5 and §6 own this and outrank anything here.

**First, find out whether you may hit at all.** Body checking is banned under USA Hockey 604(a) at 12U and below, in every girls'/women's classification and in every non-check adult classification, and under Hockey Canada (7.3) at U13 and below, throughout female hockey and in any Member-approved division. ⚠️ **Those lists are floors, not ceilings**: USA Hockey lets *"[a] local governing body"* ban checking in any classification, and 604(b) bans it whenever a Competitive Contact team plays a Body Checking team. Most adult rec leagues do not allow it either. Where checking is not permitted, the pinch is angling, body position and playing the puck.

- **CARHA has no checking division at all.** Its 49(a) makes intentional body contact, or failing to avert it, a minor or a major at the referee's discretion, and a major **shall** follow where intentional contact injures. ⚠️ **A major in that book ejects you** (30(a)), with at least a one-game suspension (32(d)). Into the boards, the minor becomes a double minor (49(b)), which counts as two of the three penalties that eject you (32(a)), and its Casebook allows a match penalty for a serious one (Situations 1 and 2). CARHA-affiliated adult leagues only.
- **In Britain** neither rule book sets a checking age. IHUK's Rules of Competition make junior U10 and U12 non-checking and U14, U16, U19 and NIHL Divisions 1 and 2 checking; in any other league, ask — see [UK and England Rules](../foundation/uk_rules.md). ⚠️ **Britain's Women's NIHL is non-checking at U16 and senior: do not hit at all.**
- **Elsewhere in IIHF women's hockey (Rule 101.1)** a body check is legal only with a clear intention of playing or winning the puck — a condition, not a licence. Get to the ice between her and the puck first and hold it; you may lean into her in a race for the puck as long as the puck is all you are after. ⚠️ **Never step or glide into her — that is at least a minor — and never use the boards to push her, pin her or eliminate her from the play.** An illegal hit is a minor, or a major plus an automatic game misconduct.
- **In the PWHL (52.1)** you may hold ground you have established, lean into her in a race for the puck, and angle her off moving her way. ⚠️ **Never step or glide into her, leave your lane, meet her head-on, or extend an arm, shoulder or hip after angling her** — each is an illegal hit, and a major for it ejects you (52.5). Seal with position alone.
- **In a USA Hockey non-checking classification you keep the body position.** Its Glossary counts players competing for the puck along the boards or in front of the goal as competitive contact, which the Note to 604(c) says *"shall not be penalized"* — but *"deliberate physical contact with an opponent, with no effort to legally play the puck, shall be penalized."*

**A winger facing the glass is defenceless, not a target.** That posture is in the criteria because they cannot escape with the puck, not because you may hit them. NHL Rule 41.1 puts the onus on you: *"The onus is on the player applying the check to ensure his opponent is not in a defenseless position and if so, he must avoid or minimize contact."* ⚠️ **If you can see their numbers, pull up and play the puck.** A hit in their back is checking from behind:

- **Pull up and take the puck: no book prices this at two minutes, and most eject you.** Along the boards, USA Hockey's Casebook makes a forceful one a major plus a game misconduct, or a match. Hockey Canada ejects at every tier (7.5(a), (e)). Into the boards so that they cannot protect themselves, it is a **mandatory match penalty** under Hockey Canada 7.5(c) and, in CARHA-affiliated adult leagues, CARHA 53(b). [Section 8](#why-it-is-so-good) sets out each book's ladder.
- **If the hit reaches the head, Hockey Canada prices it as head contact**, which overrides every rule but fighting. Under 7.6 (minor and female hockey) it is a minor if accidental, a double minor if intentional, and a third in one game is an ejection. The referee may give a major plus a game misconduct on violence alone, and **must** give it if they are hurt or it was a cross-check to the head. It is a match penalty if they could not protect themselves or you meant to injure them. Under 7.7 (Junior and Senior) it is a minor plus a ten-minute misconduct at moderate violence. It is a major plus a game misconduct at high violence, for a cross-check to the head, or where their head hits the glass, ice or boards and they are injured. A match penalty applies on the same two grounds as 7.6. **A head-first drive into the boards or goal frame that also reaches the head pays the harsher of 7.5 and 7.6.**

**Never check into a battle your teammate is already in.** USA Hockey 640(e) makes it a minor to body check *"an opponent who is physically engaged for possession of the puck with one or more other players"*, and its Note says such players *"are not eligible to be body checked."* It names no location, so a net-front battle counts as much as a wall battle, and unlike the late-hit rule it has no adult-men exception. ⚠️ **It is not capped at a minor:** a reckless one is a major plus a game misconduct (640(g)), a match penalty may also be given (640(h)), and for reckless endangerment plan on the match — Rule 602(a) says one *"shall be assessed"*. Arrive beside your teammate, seal the wall rather than their opponent's body, keep your arms, stick and skates out of it, and if you end up behind them, play the puck.

**Never finish a check on a player who no longer has the puck, unless you were already delivering it as they released it — and then ease off.** A late hit is not capped at a minor in any book. NHL 56.2(iii) makes a check on an opponent *"who is not in possession of the puck"* a minor. ⚠️ **56.4 allows a major on the violence, 56.5 adds a game misconduct when a major injures, and 21.1 makes a match penalty mandatory if you meant to injure** — the PWHL (57.5, 21.1) runs the same way. **The IIHF and Hockey Canada do not wait for an injury**: IIHF 56.5 and Hockey Canada 8.3(b) reach a major plus a game misconduct with nobody hurt, and Hockey Canada makes it mandatory where a late hit injures. What keeps a check legal is **immediacy**. NHL 56.1 permits one *"rendered immediately following his loss of possession"*. Hockey Canada 8.3 describes immediate as within an arm and stick length of the carrier at the instant the puck is released. IIHF 56.1 asks for an *"objectively reasonable window of opportunity"* and the immediate vicinity of the carrier, with no measured distance.

**USA Hockey's window is the narrowest:** ⚠️ **every *avoidable* check on a player who has released a shot or pass is a minor (640(b))** — they *"are not eligible to be body checked"* — and its Declaration of Player Safety calls *"finishing the check"* an *"unacceptable action"*. That minor excepts adult men, but 640 Note 2 reaches them as roughing. A reckless one is a major plus a game misconduct (640(g)) with a match possible (640(h)); whether those reach adult men the book does not say, and this document does not know — but 602(a)'s match penalty for reckless endangerment is tied to no named foul. Its *Casebook* allows a finish only if all four hold: you first tried to win the puck; you were within *"an arm's length plus stick length"* when it went; you had the check already *"in the process of being delivered"*; and you minimised the force (Standard of Play, Situation 10). *"It is not acceptable to skate one or two more strides after the puck is gone and then decide to deliver the check."* **You cannot judge a beat at speed, so arrive while they still have it — or play the puck.**

### Sealing and arriving: where the rules bite

**In short:** Keep your arms and skates out of the seal, and arrive with your feet under you rather than building speed into contact.

```facts
Rule: Seal the wall with body position — never hold with an arm, never with a skate; USA Hockey 622 Note makes an arm wrapped around an opponent, pinning them against the boards so they cannot play the puck or skate, a 622(a) holding minor, and a skate "push off" is 627(a), a major plus a game misconduct
Rule: Charging is speed built into a hit, not a stride count — jumping into a check is a charge in all six books, and so is travelling an excessive distance under IIHF 42.1, Hockey Canada 7.4 and USA Hockey 607
Rule: Stride counts: two or more under Hockey Canada 7.4, more than two under USA Hockey 607 and CARHA 52 (adult leagues), "multiple" under IIHF 42.1; NHL and PWHL 42.1 judge distance travelled. Fewer is no licence: Hockey Canada 7.4 also names a violent and unnecessary check, or a blind-side one
Rule: USA Hockey writes no bare charging minor — reckless is a major and game misconduct (607(b)), and plan on a match (602(a)); Hockey Canada makes an injuring charge the same (7.4(b)), a defenceless target a match (7.4(c))
Rule: Once the puck is past you, do not cut across into the winger — NHL and IIHF 56.1 and PWHL 57.1 make moving laterally, without establishing body position, into a non-puck carrier interference; USA Hockey 625(a)(4) a minor
Rule: A USA Hockey game misconduct is not only this game — 404(b) also suspends the player for their team's next game, and for at least two where it follows a second major in the same game (403(b))
```

**Seal with body position alone, and arrive with your feet under you rather than accelerating in.** ⚠️ **How you seal and how you arrive are rules, not style.**

**What you seal with.** Body position is allowed; a hold is not. USA Hockey's Rule 622 Note names *"wrapping one or both arms around the opponent along the boards in a manner that pins them against the boards and prevents them from playing the puck or skating"* as holding, a minor under 622(a) at every level. Under CARHA the same hold can be a major (63(a)), and a major there ejects you and costs at least the next game (30(a), 32(d)). ⚠️ **Keep your skates out of the seal too.** USA Hockey Rule 627 calls using *"their skate(s) in a non-kicking motion to make contact with the opponent"* a push-off (Note 2), and 627(a) makes it a major plus a game misconduct, at every level. Any USA Hockey game misconduct — this one, a reckless charge, a major for a hit from behind — also suspends you for the team's next game (404(b)), and for at least two if it follows a second major in the same game (403(b)). USA Hockey has published no ruling applying 627(a) to a seal on the wall; this is a reading of Note 2, so treat it as a reason to keep your feet out, not a gap to work in. [Forechecking Systems](forechecking_systems.md) sets out what this does to the "hip and skates" cue.

**How you arrive.** Building speed into contact is charging, and the books count it differently: two or more strides under Hockey Canada 7.4, more than two under USA Hockey 607 and CARHA 52 (where the count sets the charging tier, since that book has no checking), *"multiple strides"* under IIHF 42.1, and distance travelled, with no count, under NHL and PWHL 42.1. **No stride count is a safe harbour**: all six books name jumping into a check, the IIHF, Hockey Canada and USA Hockey name travelling an excessive distance, and Hockey Canada adds a violent and unnecessary check, or a blind-side one.

**What it costs.** ⚠️ **Under USA Hockey no charge is ever two minutes and nothing else**: 607(a) is a minor plus a misconduct or a major plus a game misconduct, **607(b) makes a reckless charge a mandatory major plus game misconduct**, 607(e) allows a match, and for a reckless charge plan on the match and a likely multi-game suspension (602(a)). **A charge that injures is a mandatory major plus game misconduct under Hockey Canada 7.4(b) and CARHA 52(a)**, and under NHL and PWHL 42.5, and the EIHL Casebook's Rule 42, the game misconduct is mandatory once a major for one injures the face or head. **Under Hockey Canada 7.4(c) a charge on a player who cannot defend themselves — the winger facing the glass — is a match penalty, injury or not.** [Forechecking Systems](forechecking_systems.md) sets out the same counts.

**Once the puck is past you, do not cut across into the winger.** A pinch comes down across their path, and once they have chipped it past you they are a player without the puck. NHL 56.1 (IIHF 56.1, PWHL 57.1) allows a block only from in front, moving the same way: *"Moving laterally and without establishing body position, then making contact with the non-puck carrier is not permitted and will be penalized as interference."* USA Hockey 625(a)(4) makes changing your lane or speed to play the body of a player without the puck a minor, and the same hit can be called under rules that eject. In Hockey Canada's U13-and-below, female and Member-approved divisions, leaving your lane to make contact is body checking under Rule 7.3 — a minor, a major plus a game misconduct on violence and mandatorily where it injures (7.3(b)), and a match penalty for trying to injure (7.3(c)). Above those divisions it is interference with a player without the puck (8.3), priced the same way at 8.3(b) and (c). ⚠️ **Turn and skate to the puck, and never cut across into the winger or hit them.**

## 11. When to Shoot and When Not To

### Shoot when

```facts
Read: Inside the home-plate area with any kind of look — almost any shot from there beats almost any pass from there
Read: The goalie is unset, moving or screened
Read: You have a lane — a genuine one, not the memory of one
Read: There is someone at the net front — a tip threat and a rebound hunter make it a different play
Read: A rebound is available and the goalie is down — shoot immediately, do not settle it
```

- You are **inside the home-plate area** with any kind of look. Almost any shot from there beats almost any pass from there.
- The goalie is **unset, moving, or screened**.
- You have **a lane** — a genuine one, not the memory of one.
- There is **someone at the net front**. A shot with a tip threat and a rebound hunter is a different play from the same shot with nobody there.
- **A rebound is available and the goalie is down.** Shoot immediately. Do not settle it.

### Do not shoot when

```facts
Never: Fire into a shin pad from the point with the lane blocked — a blocked point shot usually goes the other way with your team spread out
Never: Shoot when a better shot is one pass away — specifically, a pass across the royal road
Never: Shoot with nobody at the net front — you are giving the puck to the goalie and starting their breakout
Never: Shoot from a sharp angle with no traffic — goalies seal the post, and there is nothing to hit and no rebound to chase
Never: Treat the cross-slot finishing rate and the point-shot conversion rate as a before-and-after pair — different studies, measuring different things
Priority: Act on the direction — a shot after the goalie has had to move across is worth materially more, so the half-second is usually worth it
```

- The lane is blocked and you are firing into a shin pad from the point. A blocked point shot is worse than no shot: it usually goes the other way, and your team is spread out.
- A **better shot is one pass away** — specifically, a pass across the royal road. Do not put the 15.50% and the 3% side by side: the first is a 5-on-5 finishing rate after a cross-slot pass, and the second is either Boyle's distant unscreened shot from Valiquette's unpublished tracking or Revak's point-shot-with-traffic rate; neither measures the same shot with and without the crossing. Act on the direction: **a shot taken after the goalie has had to move across is worth materially more**, so the half-second is usually worth it.
- **Nobody is at the net front.** You are giving the puck to the goalie and starting their breakout.
- You are on a **sharp angle with no traffic**. Goalies seal the post; there is nothing to hit and no rebound to chase.

### What to shoot *for*

```facts
Mindset: From the perimeter you are not trying to score — you are trying to manufacture a second chance
Technique: Shoot for a rebound — low, to the far pad, or off the pads; chest and glove get swallowed and the whistle goes
Technique: Shoot for a tip — low, hard, along the ice, through the screen, at the tipper's stick, and say who you are shooting at
Options: Shoot to be missed — a hard shot off the end boards to a spot for a teammate is a legitimate pass; say it first
Never: Pick corners against a set, sighted goalie — the lowest-percentage thing you can do with a puck. Aim for a corner instead only when they cannot see it or cannot get there
```

From the perimeter, **you are not trying to score. You are trying to manufacture a second chance.**

- **Shoot for a rebound.** Pucks aimed low, to the far pad, or off the pads generally produce controllable rebounds. Pucks aimed at the goalie's chest and glove do not — they get swallowed and the whistle goes.
- **Shoot for a tip.** Low, hard, along the ice, through the screen, at the tipper's stick. Say who you are shooting at. The tipper's job is to present a blade; yours is to hit it.
- **Shoot to be missed.** A shot deliberately aimed to miss wide can be a play — a hard shot off the end boards to a specific spot for a teammate is a legitimate pass. Say it first.
- **Aim for a corner only when the goalie cannot see it or cannot get there.** Picking corners against a set, sighted goalie is the lowest-percentage thing you can do with a puck.

### Getting pucks through from the point

```facts
Key: Your job is not to shoot harder, it is to shoot through — point shots are blocked more than shots from anywhere else on the ice
Technique: Walk the line first — skate sideways along the blue line toward the middle until the shot-blocker has to move their feet, then release from the new angle
Technique: Change the release point — a quick snap from a different spot beats a blocker who has already dropped
Technique: Take something off it — a slower, lower shot that gets through to be tipped beats a 95 mph slapshot into a shin pad; in CARHA a slapshot is a minor; if it injures, ejection and at least a one-game ban
Options: Fake the shot — a blocker on the ice opens the lane for the real one, or the seam pass; a faked slap shot can be a CARHA minor, so sell it with your shoulders
Options: Take the D-to-D first — it drags both the blocker and the goalie, and you shoot into a lane just vacated
```

Point shots are blocked more than shots from anywhere else on the ice. If you are a defenceman, your job is not to shoot harder — it is to shoot *through*:

- **Walk the line first.** Skating sideways along the blue line toward the middle changes the angle and moves the shot-blocker's feet. A defenceman who receives and fires from the same spot is shooting at a stationary wall. How far is feel rather than a measurement — go until the blocker has to move, which is usually a step or two.
- **Change the release point.** A quick snap shot from a different spot than the one they set up for beats a blocker who has already dropped.
- **Take something off it.** A deliberately slower, lower shot that gets through and gets tipped beats a 95 mph slapshot into a shin pad, every time. **In a CARHA league, shoot with a short backswing:** that book's Rule 79 makes any backswing past fifteen inches a slap shot — a minor, and a major if it injures, which ejects you and sits you out at least the next game (Rules 30(a) and 32(d)).
- **Fake the shot.** A shot fake that puts a blocker on the ice opens the lane for the real one, and often opens the seam pass instead. In a CARHA league a faked slap shot can be a minor under Rule 79(b), so sell the fake with your shoulders and eyes rather than the backswing.
- **Take the D-to-D first.** Moving it across the top drags both the blocker and the goalie, and you shoot into a lane that has just been vacated.

More detail on mechanics is in [Shooting](../technique/shooting.md).

## 12. Offensive-Zone Faceoffs

An offensive-zone faceoff is as close as the game gets to a moment where you can arrange all five players exactly where you want them, with the puck about to arrive at a known place — **a coaching judgement, not a measured claim; no source here catalogues every dead-puck moment in the game.** Most amateur teams waste it entirely.

Full treatment of the draw itself — technique, stick placement, the violations that get your centre ejected — is in [Faceoffs](faceoffs.md). What matters here is what happens in the second *after* the draw.

### Set plays off a win

**In short:** Make sure all five players know the set play before the whistle, so the shooter and the net-front player are already moving.

```facts
Key: The point of a set play is that four players start moving on the drop, before anyone knows who won it
Options: Win back to the point and screen immediately — inside winger straight to the net front, outside winger to the strong-side rebound spot
Options: Win back to a winger set for a one-timer — a shooter before the drop, not a defender; in CARHA a backswing past fifteen inches is a slap-shot minor; if it injures, ejection and at least a one-game ban
Options: Win it forward — attack directly; high reward, but it puts your centre below the puck and fails badly if the draw is lost
Options: Lift or press their centre's stick with yours — the stick, never the hands — then play the puck for a winger to sweep in on; never hook their stick, hold it down or grab it. Best on a dominant centre
Options: Delay for the screen — the defenceman skates laterally for a beat first; a shot half a second late with a screen beats one on time without
Risk: A winger creeping into the circle before the drop is encroachment, and it gets your centre thrown out of the draw
Never: Reach the whistle without everyone knowing the play — the common failures are a net-front player arriving after the shot and a shot into the first shin pad
```

The point of a set play is that four players start moving on the drop, before anyone knows who won it. Common families:

- **Win back to the point, screen immediately.** The centre draws the puck back to the strong-side defenceman; the inside winger goes straight to the net front to screen; the outside winger takes the strong-side rebound position. Simplest, most reliable, and it converts because everyone was already moving.
- **Win back to the winger for a one-timer.** The centre draws it back to a winger set up in the shooting position (usually a left shot on the right circle, or vice versa) who shoots first-time. Requires the winger to be aligned as a shooter before the drop, not as a defender. Keep the backswing short — in a CARHA league, a backswing past fifteen inches makes it a slap shot: a minor penalty, and a major that ends your game, and at least the next, if it injures an opponent.
- **Win it forward / "quick strike."** The centre draws the puck forward or to the inside and attacks directly. High reward if the opposing centre is slow to react, but it puts your centre below the puck and it fails badly if the draw is lost.
- **Lift their stick and let a winger take it.** The centre does not try to win the puck cleanly but lifts or presses the opposing centre's stick with their own — the stick, never the opposing centre's hands — then plays the puck for a winger to sweep in on — never hooking it, holding it down or grabbing it with a hand. Useful when the opposing centre is dominant on draws. Watch the **encroachment** rule — a winger creeping into the circle before the drop gets your *centre* thrown out of the draw.
- **Delay for the screen.** The defenceman receives the win but skates laterally for a beat before shooting, giving the net-front player time to establish. A shot half a second late with a screen beats a shot on time without one.

Whatever your team runs, **everyone must know it before the whistle**, and the two most common failures are a net-front player who arrives after the shot and a defenceman who fires it into the first shin pad.

### Structure off a loss

```facts
Mindset: Assume you will lose roughly half the draws — structuring for the loss is the other half of the plan, not defeatism
Action: One forward pressures immediately, usually the winger nearest the puck, to deny the clean breakout pass and force a rim or a chip
Action: The other two forwards turn and skate rather than watch — a forward who is stationary when the puck is lost is beaten
Read: The defencemen hold the line and read — is this a rim to be intercepted, or a clean exit to be retreated against?
Convention: The strong-side defenceman is the one most likely to pinch on a rim; the weak-side one covers the middle
Key: Having a defined assignment for the loss is what lets you be aggressive on the win
```

Assume you will lose roughly half the draws. Structure for the loss is not defeatism, it is the other half of the plan.

- **One forward pressures immediately** — usually the winger nearest the puck — to deny the clean breakout pass and force a rim or a chip.
- **The other two forwards get defensive momentum going**, turning and skating rather than watching. A forward who is stationary when the puck is lost is beaten.
- **The defencemen hold the line and read**: is this a rim to be intercepted, or a clean exit to be retreated against? The strong-side defenceman is the one most likely to pinch on a rim; the weak-side one covers the middle.
- **Nobody is a spectator.** Four players still processing the fact that they lost the draw hand the other team a clean breakout.

**Having a defined assignment for the loss is what lets you be aggressive on the win.** Teams play tentatively on offensive-zone draws precisely because they have no plan for losing them.

## Common Mistakes

- **Treating "get pucks to the net" as a strategy.** **Before you release, either the puck is in the home-plate area or the goalie has moved — a screen, a pass across the middle, a rebound. If neither, move it first.** At even strength a perimeter shot nobody screens or tips rarely beats a set goalie. On the power play the point shot is worth more, so this is an even-strength caution.
- **Cycling with nobody in front of the net.** **Fill the goalmouth before the cycle starts, and end it in a shot from the home-plate area, a royal road pass, a screened low-to-high, or a net drive.** It is a very common reason a busy possession produces nothing — a coaching observation rather than a count, because nobody has published a tally of offensive-zone failures by cause.
- **Firing the point shot into the first shin pad.** **Walk the line until the shot-blocker has to move, take something off it, or go D-to-D first — and hold it if nobody is in front.** A blocked point shot usually goes the other way with your team spread out.
- **Scanning up the wall before scanning across the middle.** **Look across first, every time — of the two options, only the seam expires.**
- **Passing to where a teammate is instead of where they are going.** **Put it where their stick will be, so they can shoot without settling it.** In the slot, the extra touch is the difference between a goal and a save.
- **Standing still on the half-wall.** **Keep your feet moving — cutbacks and curls buy the two extra seconds you need.** A stationary player gets pinned and stripped. When contact arrives, keep your head up and your chin off your chest — never turn your back to anyone coming at you, never duck.
- **Turning to watch the shot from the net front.** **Keep your chest to the shooter and your eyes on the puck.** Turning to follow it un-screens the goalie and takes you out of the rebound.
- **Planting at the net front without knowing where the post is.** **Know where the post is before the shot comes, keep your head up with your chin off your chest, and never duck.** You get walked sideways by a body you feel rather than see, with the post on one side and the goalie on the other. A flexed neck is what turns a collision with a post into a spinal injury, and USA Hockey names the goal post in that mechanism. **A box-out puts you on the same path, so this is not a checking-league point.** **And if a shove is carrying you into the goalie, give way rather than hold your spot.**
- **Standing in the blue paint to screen.** **Take the goalie's eyes, not their room: screen from outside the crease, feet off the red line too, and never initiate the contact.** Outside the crease a no-contact screen is normally legal, though under the NHL, IIHF and PWHL positioning that stops the goalie moving freely in their crease can cost the goal; their tables allow a screen outside the crease even where it impairs the goalie's ability to defend (5C, 5E). ⚠️ **Inside it, your position alone can take the goal off.** Once a goalie has played the puck outside the crease, get out of their road back — under the NHL, the IIHF and the PWHL, blocking it can draw a penalty, contact or not. [Goaltender interference — the actual standard](#goaltender-interference--the-actual-standard) sets out each book's test.
- **Arriving into the goalie.** **Never initiate contact; if it is coming anyway, visibly avoid it — no accelerating through it, no arms out, no dropped shoulder.** ⚠️ **No book caps it at two minutes and it can eject you in all six — automatically for a charge under Hockey Canada, under CARHA in the crease or for any charge that injures them, and under USA Hockey for a reckless one — and under CARHA and USA Hockey it costs at least the next game too.** Being shoved in is not a complete defence, so make the effort early. [Rebounds, loose pucks and being pushed into the goalie](#rebounds-loose-pucks-and-being-pushed-into-the-goalie) and the section after it set out each book's ladder.
- **Holding your position when the goalie leans into you.** **Give ground the moment they make that contact, then re-set the screen outside the crease, both skates off the red line.** Under the NHL, the IIHF and the PWHL, holding your spot costs the goal if one is scored and a minor if not.
- **Treating the rebound carve-out as a licence to move the goalie.** **In a scramble, play the puck, not the goalie, and never finish a shove that carries them into the net.** ⚠️ **The most any book forgives is incidental contact in a race for a loose puck you are both making, and several forgive none of it** — push the goalie in with the puck and expect the goal to come off. [Rebounds, loose pucks and being pushed into the goalie](#rebounds-loose-pucks-and-being-pushed-into-the-goalie) sets out each book.
- **Tipping with a high stick, assuming the ceiling is the crossbar wherever you play.** **Keep the blade on the ice, where most tippable pucks are anyway.** The ceiling is the crossbar in some books and your shoulder in others, so find out. ⚠️ **And the moment the blade reaches a person it is a high-sticking penalty, which no book caps at a minor.** [Tips and deflections](#tips-and-deflections) sets out each book's ceiling.
- **Two defencemen leaving the blue line at once, when nobody called for it.** **Somebody stays above the puck: if your partner has gone, you hold the line, or a forward fills in.** [Defending the Rush](defending_the_rush.md) calls this the subtlest way a team manufactures an odd-man rush against itself, because nothing looks wrong until the puck turns over. **Both going is not always the error:** a five-player rotation, a standing green light, a full-ice press, a delayed penalty or a 6-on-5 sends both on purpose. Unless one is on, look for your partner before you go.
- **Pinching without arriving on contact.** **Go only when you will arrive as the puck does and a forward can cover your line; otherwise hold.** Take the puck, or — where your league permits body checking — the player. Half-hearted pinches create 3-on-2s the other way. ⚠️ **Arrive while they still have the puck:** a check after it has gone is a late hit, which no book caps at a minor, and under USA Hockey any avoidable check on a player who has released the puck is a penalty. [The defenceman's pinch](#the-defencemans-pinch) sets out each book's window.
- **Pinching in as a check onto a battle your teammate is already in.** **Arrive beside your teammate, seal the wall with your position and pin the puck rather than the player, keep your arms, stick and skates out of it, and if you end up behind them, play the puck.** ⚠️ **Under USA Hockey a player engaged in a puck battle may not be body checked at all, at any level, anywhere on the ice — a reckless one ejects you, and so does a skate used to push off them, which costs you the next game too.**
- **Accelerating into the arrival.** **Get the speed on the way in and your feet under you for the last two or three strides.** ⚠️ **Building speed into the contact is charging, and no stride count is a safe harbour** — every book penalises jumping into a check. A charge that injures ends your game under Hockey Canada and CARHA and can in the rest. Under USA Hockey no charge is two minutes and nothing else, and an ejection there or under CARHA costs at least the next game too. [Sealing and arriving](#sealing-and-arriving-where-the-rules-bite) sets out each book's count and tier.
- **Pinching to hit a winger who is facing the glass.** **Take the puck, not the body** — they cannot see you or protect themselves, and the onus is on you to avoid the contact. ⚠️ **A hit on their back is checking from behind: never two minutes and nothing else in any book, and in most books it ends your game — plan on it.** Under USA Hockey's Casebook a forceful one on a player along the boards must be a major plus a game misconduct, or a match.

  ⚠️ **A hit from behind that leaves them unable to protect themselves is a mandatory match penalty** under Hockey Canada (head, boards or net) and CARHA (boards or net). ⚠️ **Under USA Hockey, for any reckless hit, plan on a match and a likely multi-game suspension** (602(a)). The NHL and PWHL must give a match for a deliberate attempt to injure (21.1). [Section 8](#why-it-is-so-good) sets out each book's ladder.
- **Cutting across the winger's path once they have chipped the puck past you.** **Once the puck is past you, turn and skate to the puck — never cut across into the winger or hit them.** ⚠️ **Cutting across into them can be called interference wherever you play, and where checking is barred an illegal check as well — and the same hit can cost more than two minutes.**
- **Pinching to hit without knowing whether your league allows checking.** ⚠️ **Find out before you go; where checking is not permitted, the pinch is angling, body position and playing the puck.** Body checking is banned at USA Hockey 12U and below, Hockey Canada U13 and below, throughout girls' and women's hockey in both, in every non-check adult classification, and wherever a local governing body bars it. CARHA's adult book never permits it, and a major for body contact there ejects you and costs at least the next game. In Britain the rule books set no checking age: IHUK's competitions make junior U10 and U12 non-checking, and the Women's NIHL is non-checking at U16 and senior — in any other British league, ask. Under IIHF women's rules and the PWHL you may hold your ground and lean into her in a race for the puck, but never step or glide into her — under the IIHF never pin her on the boards, and under the PWHL never extend an arm, shoulder or hip after angling her.
- **Retreating along the boards after a failed pinch.** **Cut to the slot instead.** Following the puck down the wall abandons the middle of the ice.
- **Quitting after the shot.** **Decide before the release: go to the net with your stick on the ice and shoot the rebound straight back — or, as the high forward, get above the puck as the safety.** The three seconds after a shot are the most dangerous of the possession, and most teams spend them watching.
- **Having exactly one way to attack.** **Have a second-wave plan — a chip past the defenceman, a soft dump, or a retreat and re-attack — and somebody ready for the other way when a cycle turns over.** Rush-only teams are shut down by any structured defence; cycle-only teams never threaten before the defence is set.
- **Not knowing your team's rules for defenceman activation.** **Ask before your first shift** — whether your defencemen pinch, and how deep, varies enormously between coaches.

## Key Takeaways

1. **Shoot from a dangerous place, or with the goalie out of position.** That means the home-plate area, through a screen, or at a goalie who is moving. From the perimeter, shoot low for a second chance — at a tipper's stick or the far pad — and hold it when nobody is in front.
2. **On the half-wall, look across the middle before you look up the wall, and know your four options before the puck arrives** — low, high, across, or to the net yourself. A pass across the middle just before the shot makes the goalie push across and re-set. That seam closes; the wall pass waits.
3. **A cycle is a set-up, not an outcome.** Fill the goalmouth before it starts, and end it in a shot from the home-plate area, a pass across the middle, a screened point shot or a net drive. On the wall, if the checker is still in front of you, rim it; if they have skated past you, reverse it.
4. **At the net front, screen with your skates clear of the crease and its red line, and without contact.** Head up, chin off your chest, know where the post is, and give ground at once if the goalie leans in. ⚠️ **Never arrive into the goalie — it can eject you in every book, and in some it must.**
5. **When you win the puck below the goal line, take it behind the net and pass out to the slot — that is the primary play, not a last resort.** Nobody can come at you from directly behind, but you can still be driven into the goal frame, so keep your head up.
6. **Decide before the release to crash the net for the rebound.** Stick on the ice, shoot it straight back, and slide to the far post if you are tied up; the high forward stays above the puck. **Play the puck, never the goalie — and if you are shoved, still try to avoid them.**
7. **Keep the shape, and have both attacks.** One forward below the puck, one level, one above, both defencemen on the line — a common default, not a law, so find out what your team plays. Rush offence lives on speed in the first seconds, set offence on patience, and a team with only one gets shut down.
8. **Pinching, take the puck, seal the wall with position and pin the puck rather than the player, or angle the winger off; never check into a teammate's battle.** Arrive while they have the puck, without accelerating in, keep arms and skates out, and hit only if your league allows checking. ⚠️ **Never hit a winger facing the glass: checking from behind is never just two minutes, and in most books ejects you — plan on it.**
9. **Tip with the blade on the ice — that is where most tippable pucks are, and a low tip is not a high stick in any book.** Above that, the ceiling is the crossbar under the NHL, the IIHF and the PWHL, your shoulder under USA Hockey and CARHA, and Hockey Canada prints both. Find out which you are under.
10. **Know the faceoff play, and the plan for losing the draw, before the whistle.** On a loss, one forward pressures at once, the other two turn and skate, and the defencemen hold the line and read. An assignment for the loss is what lets you be aggressive on the win.
