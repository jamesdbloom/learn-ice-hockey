## Overview

```facts
Position: You already have the puck in the offensive zone — getting there is Zone Entries, winning it back is Forechecking Systems
Goal: You are not trying to take a shot — you are trying to take a shot from a dangerous place, with the goalie out of position
Key: The cycle, the low-to-high and the net-front battle are machinery for producing one shot from a dangerous place, with the goalie out of position
Risk: Almost every amateur team can get the puck into the offensive zone; far fewer turn possession there into a dangerous shot
```

Once you already have the puck at the other team's end of the ice, **you are not trying to take a shot. You are trying to take a shot from a dangerous place, with the goalie out of position.** Almost every amateur team can get the puck down there; far fewer turn that into a shot from anywhere dangerous, and everything else is machinery for producing that one moment.

**Look across the middle of the ice before you look up the wall.** A pass that crosses the line running down the centre of the rink from goal to goal, immediately before the shot, is the highest-value pass type measured — roughly double the next one in volunteer-tracked NHL data on 5-on-5 shot attempts, which ranks pass types against each other and not against everything else you could do with a puck — because the goalie cannot re-square, find the puck again and reset their stick in the time available. The scan order is what decides whether you ever see it: by the time you have looked up the wall and come back, that gap has opened and closed.

**Moving the puck around the outside is a set-up, not an outcome.** If it does not end in a shot from the middle, a pass through the middle, a shot from the blue line with the goalie's view blocked, or a drive at the net, it did nothing. And that shot from the blue line is a good play with somebody blocking the goalie's view and two teammates hunting the rebound, and a giveaway without them — you win the race for a rebound by deciding to be there before the shot, not by reacting after it.

**Standing in the goalie's sightline is normally legal, and worth doing constantly — from outside the painted crease, and without contact.** Inside the crease it is not — though the rule is narrower than it sounds. What voids the goal in there with no contact at all is a **significant** position: a substantial part of your body in the paint for more than an instantaneous period, obstructing the goalie's vision **and** impairing their ability to defend. Keep your feet out of the paint whichever book you play under — a foot in there risks the goal under all four books, and under USA Hockey rules it costs you the zone as well, and under the IIHF's it can — 69.1 lets the referee stop play where you are in the crease and *"do not leave immediately"*.

**Almost everything else here is a coaching choice** — who takes the front of the net, whether the defencemen join the attack, how the forwards rotate. Find out what yours plays before deciding somebody else is out of position.

## 1. The Goal: Shot Quality, Not Shot Quantity

### The scoring area — "home plate"

```facts
Position: Home plate runs from the tops of the two faceoff circles, in along the tops, down to the dots, then angles in to the goalposts
Key: Shots inside home plate are counted as scoring chances; those from the inner part, close to the net and between the dots, as high-danger chances
Position: The slot is straight out in front of the net between the circles; the high slot runs from the dots up to the tops of the circles
Position: The goalmouth is the immediate net front; the point is the area just inside the blue line; the half-wall is the boards level with the faceoff dot
Key: The faceoff circles and the goal crease are your on-ice ruler for everything below
```

Analysts describe the dangerous part of the offensive zone as a **home-plate shaped area** (as in the baseball plate). It runs from the tops of the two faceoff circles, in along the tops of the circles, down to the faceoff dots, and then angles in to the two goalposts. Shots inside it are counted as **scoring chances**; shots from the inner part of it, close to the net and between the dots, are counted as **high-danger chances**.

![](diagram:oz-home-plate)

Some geography, in plain terms:

- **The slot** is the area straight out in front of the net, between the two faceoff circles.
- **The high slot** is the upper part of it, roughly from the level of the faceoff dots up to the tops of the circles.
- **The goalmouth** is the immediate net front — the few feet either side of the blue paint.
- **The point** is the area just inside the blue line, where defencemen usually stand — an area, not the line itself.
- **The half-wall** is the boards area level with the faceoff dot, between the corner and the point.

For reference, the end-zone faceoff circles have a **15-foot radius** (NHL Rule 1.9) and the goal crease — the blue paint — extends **six feet out from the goal line** at its deepest, with straight side lines one foot outside each post (Rule 1.7). Those two markings are your on-ice ruler for everything below.

### Why location matters so much

The numbers are not close:

- Distance from the net is **"the single most important feature by a wide margin"** in expected-goals models — models that assign every shot a probability of becoming a goal based on where and how it was taken ([Hockey Graphs](https://hockey-graphs.com/2019/08/15/expected-goals-model-with-pre-shot-movement-part-4-variable-importance/)).
- **At even strength**, **goals per unblocked shot** from **outside** the dangerous area have been **under 5%** across a decade of NHL data (Micah Blake McCurdy / HockeyViz, 2007–2017 — the map is published on [McCurdy's own page](https://hockeyviz.com/txt/edgar), retrieved 28 August 2026, and restated [by The Coaches Site](https://members.thecoachessite.com/article/the-problem-with-point-shots-according-to-analytics)). **Read that metric literally — it is not shooting percentage.** McCurdy defines his terms on the page the map appears on: *"'shot' means 'unblocked shot', that is, a shot that is recorded by the NHL as either a goal, a save, or a miss (this latter category includes shots that hit the post or crossbar)."* Misses are therefore in the denominator, where shooting percentage counts only shots on goal, so the two numbers are not comparable. **And the strength state matters more here than the metric does.** McCurdy publishes **three** maps, one per strength state, and on the **power-play** one the 5% contour runs out to roughly the blue line, enclosing effectively the whole offensive zone including both points. **On the power play, this figure is not a reason to pass up a point shot.** **What sits behind all of it:** a rendered contour plot titled *"Goals per Unblocked Shot, 2007-2017"*, on a 2017 model-description page — no table, no stated sample size, no data release. The power-play reading above is a contour traced off a 484 × 589 image, so where exactly the line falls is approximate. That it reaches the blue line is not.
- Point shots specifically convert at roughly **3% with traffic in front and about 1% without**, attributed to **Clear Sight Analytics** — the proprietary tracking behind Steve Valiquette's royal-road percentages. **⚠️ Attributed but uncheckable** — that tracking has never been published and the figure has never been independently replicated, so treat it as illustrative. **[Shooting](../technique/shooting.md) covers shot-location value**, and gives a point-shot conversion of roughly 1–3%; take the number from there rather than from here. **Note it is not independent corroboration** — both figures are Greg Revak's readings off the same HockeyViz map.
- Shots taken from the middle of the ice are more dangerous than shots from sharp angles, and danger climbs steeply as you close the distance: the NHL.com summary of Sportlogiq data charts *"the percentage of goals that have been scored from the past three seasons by distance in 10-foot increments from the net"*, and says of it that *"even the tiniest bit further in can make a 5% improvement in your chances to score"* ([NHL.com](https://www.nhl.com/kraken/news/seattle-kraken-analytics-with-alison-high-danger-chances-328378484), Alison Lukan, 27 November 2021). **Be careful with that sentence: the underlying chart is a distribution of where goals came from, so the 5% is a step between ten-foot bands of that distribution, not five percentage points added to your own shooting percentage.** The article's own phrasing blurs the two; the figure tells you the direction and steepness of the effect, not its per-shot size.

So a clean look from the slot is worth several times an unscreened shot from the blue line — not a bit more, several times. This is why "just get pucks to the net" is incomplete advice. Volume from the perimeter is not a plan; it is what a team does when it has no plan.

### The two levers you actually have

```facts
Key: There are only two ways to make a shot dangerous — be able to name which one you are pulling at any moment
Action: Move the puck to a dangerous place — carry it, pass it or drive it into the home-plate area before you shoot
Action: Move the goalie — take away their eyes with a screen, make them move laterally with a pass across the middle, or make them commit with a rebound
Priority: The best chances do both at once — a pass across the slot to a player already in the home-plate area
```

There are only two ways to make a shot dangerous, and you should be able to name which one you are pulling at any moment:

1. **Move the puck to a dangerous place.** Get it into the home-plate area before you shoot — by carrying it there, passing it there, or driving it there off a rebound.
2. **Move the goalie so a shot from anywhere becomes dangerous.** Take away their eyes with a screen, make them move laterally with a pass across the middle, or make them commit with a rebound.

The best chances do both at once: a pass across the slot to a player already in the home-plate area. That is the subject of the next section, and a pass across the slot is the highest-value pass type measured.

## 2. The Royal Road — The Highest-Value Pass Type Measured

### The Royal Road: What it is

```facts
Key: The royal road is an imaginary line down the centre of the ice, lengthwise, from the middle of one net to the middle of the other
Key: A royal road pass — also called a slot-line, cross-seam or cross-slot pass — crosses that line in the offensive zone immediately before a shot
Key: Valiquette's split: green shots follow movement across the road, deflections, one-timers, screens and broken plays; red shots are clean looks with none of that
Risk: Valiquette's percentages are attributed but uncheckable — treat them as illustrative, not as data; the effect itself has been replicated on public data
Risk: Published descriptions of where the line runs disagree — the net-to-net version used here is kept for being the more conservative instruction, not the better supported one
```

The **royal road** is an imaginary line running down the centre of the ice, lengthwise, from the middle of one net to the middle of the other. The term was popularised by former NHL goaltender **Steve Valiquette**, who separated shots into "green" (high-probability — preceded by movement across the road, plus deflections, one-timers, screens and broken plays) and "red" (low-probability — clean looks with no such movement).

> **⚠️ Attributed but uncheckable — treat as illustrative, not as data.** Green shots are reported as accounting for roughly **76% of NHL goals**, with red shots the other 24%. The figure does have a named source, contemporaneous with the data: NHL.com's Kevin Woodley reported in December 2014 that *"Valiquette said 76 percent of goals in the NHL this season have been scored off Green Shots, including 30 percent on plays that involved either a pass or possession moving across the Royal Road,"* and Ken Campbell repeated it in *The Hockey News* in February 2015. Chris Boyle, writing for the OMHA, gives the sample — Valiquette *"reviewed 100 games during the 2014/15 NHL season"* — and breaks the 76% into seven categories.
>
> **What is missing is not the attribution but the data.** The shot-by-shot tracking, the classification rules and the counts have never been published; Clear Sight Analytics' public glossary carries none of it. Woodley's own 2014 article concedes the point: *"There is no database, at least not one available to the public, tracking every shot in the NHL with that kind of detail."* Two further cautions. The **24% is a residual**, not a measured quantity — nobody reports it directly. And the retellings do not reconcile: Boyle's seven green categories sum to 74%, not 76%, and he puts direct red-shot goals at 18% rather than 24%. The direction of the finding is coaching consensus and survives independent testing on public data (see below); the specific number cannot be checked. [Shooting](../technique/shooting.md) carries the same figure with the same label.
>
> **The effect itself, unlike the number, has been replicated on public data.** Alan Wells' analysis of Ryan Stimson's Passing Project — volunteer-tracked and publicly released, *"51,308 shots at 5v5"* counting all attempts rather than shots on goal — puts shooting percentage on attempts preceded by a royal road pass at **15.50%**, far above every other pass type tracked. **That sample is 5-on-5, which is not the same as even strength** — 4-on-4 and 3-on-3 are even strength as well — and it excludes special teams entirely, so every Passing Project figure in this document is a 5-on-5 figure and nothing wider. More importantly, Alex Novet's public expected-goals model built on Corey Sznajder's tracking (~72,000 shots) tested whether the royal road effect is just shot location in disguise, and found it is not: *"there is a big jump in Shapley value when the pass is royal road."* So the mechanism is real and independently supported. It is Valiquette's particular percentages that are not checkable.

> **⚠️ Published descriptions of where the line actually runs do not agree.** This document uses the **net-centre-to-net-centre** version above, because it is the more common description and gives the more conservative coaching instruction. But **both** of the sources closest to Valiquette's own figures describe something narrower and zone-limited: Blue Seat Blogs has it running only from the centre bar of the net to the top of the circles, and NHL.com describes *"a line that halves the offensive zone in two below the tops of the faceoff circles"*. On that reading the road stops at the top of the circles rather than spanning the rink — and it is **two-to-one against the popular version**, so the net-to-net geometry used here is kept for being the more conservative coaching instruction, not for being better supported. The framework is proprietary and the exact geometry has never been published, so the disagreement cannot be resolved from public sources. [Shooting](../technique/shooting.md) sets this out in full.

A **royal road pass** — also called a **slot-line pass**, **cross-seam pass** or **cross-slot pass** — is a pass that crosses that centre line in the offensive zone immediately before a shot. Note the *cross-*: a **seam** on its own is any gap between two defenders' coverage areas and the lane through it, which need not cross the centre of the ice at all.

![](diagram:oz-royal-road)

### Why it beats everything else

```facts
Key: A goaltender's save is mostly positioning, not reflexes — a set goalie is already covering most of the net before the shot leaves
Action: Make them move sideways — crossing the road forces a lateral push across the crease to re-square to the new puck position
Read: A goalie forced to move laterally must also re-establish depth and angle, re-find the puck visually, and reset their stick and pads — which is where the five-hole opens
Goal: Get the shot away before they finish that sequence — then it is a desperation save, not a positioning save
```

Because a goaltender's save is mostly *positioning*, not reflexes. A set goalie has already squared up to the puck, cut down the angle, and sealed the ice. They are, in effect, already covering most of the net before the shot leaves.

Now make them move sideways. Crossing the royal road forces four jobs on the goalie at once:

- The goalie has to **push laterally across the crease** to re-square to the new puck position.
- They have to **re-establish depth and angle** on the new line, which takes time they may not have.
- They have to **re-find the puck visually** — their sightline was set to one side of the ice and the puck is now on the other.
- And they have to **reset their stick and pads**, which is where technical errors creep in and the five-hole opens.

If the shot comes before they finish that sequence, they are not making a positioning save — they are making a desperation save. That is a completely different, and much worse, proposition for them.

### The evidence

- Shots following a **royal road / cross-slot pass** finished at **15.50%** — the highest-value pass type measured, roughly double the next one (Alan Wells' analysis of **Ryan Stimson's** Passing Project data, [via NHL.com](https://www.nhl.com/kraken/news/analytics-with-alison-dangerous-passes-327627898); **volunteer-tracked, 51,308 shots at 5v5, counting all attempts rather than shots on goal** — see the sourcing note above, and carry that qualification wherever you quote the figure).
- Royal road passes are the **most important pre-shot movement variable** in expected-goals modelling — shots are much more likely to score when preceded by a pass moving across the ice in front of the goalie ([Hockey Graphs](https://hockey-graphs.com/2019/08/15/expected-goals-model-with-pre-shot-movement-part-4-variable-importance/)).

> **A note on numbers you will hear quoted.** The "33% versus 3%" figure does have a traceable origin, and it is not what most people who quote it think. It comes from Chris Boyle, writing for the OMHA, reporting Valiquette's study: *"Valiquette's study identifies a shot from above the face-off circles and no lateral movement will results in around a 3% chance to score. If the attacking forward is able to cross the Royal Road through the slot, his chances increase to 33%."* Read that carefully. Both numbers are shooting percentages, and the 3% baseline is **a distant shot from above the circles**, not the same shot taken without the crossing. It is not a clean crossed-versus-not-crossed comparison — shot distance is doing a great deal of the work, and quoting it as "cross the road and you triple your chances" overstates what was measured. Beyond that the underlying study is unpublished, and the **20–30%** range you will see in coaching material — which [Special Teams](special_teams.md) does not quote but flags for exactly this reason — has no published primary source behind it at all, so treat both as illustrative rather than exact. **The 15.50% figure above is the best-sourced number here and is the one to quote.** It is a **5-on-5** measurement of one pass type in one tracking project — and 5-on-5 is narrower than even strength, because 4-on-4 and 3-on-3 are even strength too. It therefore says nothing at all about the power play, which is excluded from the sample. Nobody has published a power-play-only conversion rate for seam passes, so if you see a higher number attached to power-play seam passes, it is not this figure adjusted for the man advantage — it is an unsourced number.

### How to actually create one

```facts
Action: Look across before you look along — your first scan off the wall is the far side of the slot, the backdoor and the weak-side dot
Never: Scan up the wall first — by the time you come back, the seam has opened and closed
Technique: Pass to where the shooter is going, not where they are — a pass into the feet costs the extra touch the goalie needed
Options: Carry it across yourself — the half-wall into the high slot, or curling out from behind the net into the slot, both cross the line
Options: Use the player behind the net — a pass out from below the goal line crosses the road almost by definition
Read: Expect the seam to be defended, and expect it to open only briefly
```

Knowing the concept is worthless without the habits that produce it. Four practical ones:

- **Look across before you look along.** When you receive the puck on the wall, your first scan is the far side of the slot — the **backdoor** (the far post, behind the defence) and the weak-side dot. Only when that is dead do you look at the safe option up the wall. Scanning in the opposite order is what costs you the seam: it opens briefly and closes permanently, and the wall option does not.
- **Pass to where the shooter is going, not where they are.** A seam pass into a player's feet takes an extra touch, and the extra touch is exactly the time the goalie needed.
- **Carry it across yourself.** A puck carrier who cuts through the middle drags the goalie laterally the same way a pass does. Driving from the half-wall into the high slot, or curling out from behind the net into the slot, both cross the line.
- **Use the player behind the net.** A pass out from below the goal line to the slot crosses the royal road almost by definition, and the goalie has to turn their head to track it. See section 8.

**The corresponding defensive truth:** taking away the middle of the ice is the whole point of most defensive-zone systems. Expect the seam to be defended, and expect it to open only briefly. See [Defensive Zone Coverage](defensive_zone_coverage.md).

## 3. Offensive Zone Structure — The Five-Man Shape

> **This section describes a coaching choice, not a law of hockey.** The structure below — three forwards rotating in an **attack triangle**, both defencemen holding the blue line, the strong-side defenceman free to pinch — is the **house default** assumed here. **Nobody has published a count of what teams actually run in the offensive zone**, as far as this document has been able to establish, so treat any "most teams play X" claim — this shape included — as an impression rather than a measurement. Realistic alternatives are named at the end of the section. **Find out which one your team plays.**

### F1, F2, F3 are roles, not people

```facts
Key: F1, F2 and F3 are roles defined by order of arrival — first, second and third to the puck — not by whether you are a centre or a winger
Key: Where arrival order and depth in the zone do not coincide, arrival order is what decides the label
Convention: "Hunt, help, high" is the forecheck shorthand — F1 hunts the puck, F2 helps F1, F3 stays high
Position: One player below the puck, one level with it and one above — the outlet that lets the puck go back to safety and can attack the net from behind, the close support, and the shooting option
Position: The level player is close support, usually a short pass away, in the inside lane rather than flat on the boards — that is the player who receives the bump
Key: The player above the puck is the shooting option on a low-to-high and your first line of defence the moment you lose it
Convention: Rotation rules are a coaching choice and change who may drop below the puck — ask which rotation your team runs before you decide someone is out of position
Goal: Give the puck carrier at least two options at all times; players without the puck keep moving to provide them
```

**F1, F2 and F3 are roles defined by order of arrival — who gets to the puck first, second and third — not by whether you are a centre or a winger.** They rotate constantly, and they are reassigned every time the sequence of arrival changes. If the left winger is first in on the puck below the goal line, they are F1; if the centre is the last of the three to arrive and ends up at the top of the circle, they are F3. A common shorthand for the forecheck version is **"hunt, help, high"** — F1 hunts the puck, F2 helps F1, F3 stays high.

In an established possession, order of arrival and depth in the zone usually coincide — the first forward in is the deepest — which is why the roles map so neatly onto the below / level / above shape described next. When they do not coincide, arrival order is what decides the label.

In an established offensive-zone possession, the three forwards should always be arranged so there is:

- **One player below the puck** — deeper toward the goal line than the puck carrier. This is the outlet that lets the puck go backwards to safety, and the player who can attack the net from behind. Without them, every loss of the puck is a clean escape for the defence.
- **One player level with the puck** — close support, usually a short pass away, in the inside lane rather than flat on the boards. This is the player who receives the bump and keeps the possession alive.
- **One player above the puck** — higher in the zone, toward the top of the circles or the point. This player is the shooting option on a low-to-high pass *and* the first line of defence if you lose it.

That "below / level / above" arrangement is what forms the **attack triangle**, and it is the actual content of the advice "keep the triangle." The triangle gives you **width and depth at once**, which means the puck carrier always has at least two passing options at different angles. Coaching material states the requirement plainly: the puck carrier should have **at least two options** at all times, and players without the puck should keep moving to provide them ([Creating Offense with a 5-Man Unit](https://cdn1.sportngin.com/attachments/document/dfc7-2294372/Creating_Offense_5_man_unit_Oct_20.pdf)).

### Rotation: the triangle moves, the shape does not

```facts
Key: The three forwards hold no fixed spots — as the puck moves, everyone rotates so the shape is preserved
Read: Puck from the half-wall down to the corner → the corner player is now F1, the old carrier rotates up, the high player holds
Read: Puck low-to-high to the point → the low forwards attack the net and the slot; the high forward screens or gets to the top of the circle
Read: Puck behind the net → the net-front player becomes level support at the far post; the far-side forward slides into the slot
Never: Let the triangle sit entirely on the perimeter — at least one forward is always in or attacking the home-plate area
```

The three forwards do not hold fixed spots. As the puck moves, everyone rotates so the *shape* is preserved:

- Puck goes from the half-wall down to the corner → the corner player is now F1 (below/level), the old carrier rotates up, the high player holds.
- Puck goes low-to-high to the point → the low forwards attack the net and the slot; the high forward screens or gets to the top of the circle.
- Puck goes behind the net → the player who was net-front becomes the level support at the far post; the far-side forward slides into the slot.

![](diagram:oz-five-man-shape)

![](diagram:oz-rotation-one-pass-later)

The one non-negotiable: **at least one forward is always in or attacking the home-plate area.** A triangle that is entirely on the perimeter is just three players passing the puck around the outside of a defence that is perfectly happy about it.

### The defencemen's three depths

```facts
Position: Just inside the blue line — the point — is the default: the low-to-high outlet, the shooting threat that stops the defence collapsing, and the safety net
Technique: Stay a stride inside the line, not on it — if the puck completely crosses out, the zone is gone and everyone has to re-enter
Rule: Losing the zone is not off-side — a delayed off-side arises when the puck is played back IN with attackers still inside (NHL and IIHF 83.3); the puck merely leaving nullifies one (USA Hockey 630(d)(1))
Rule: ⚠️ Check your book before counting on tagging up — USA Hockey 630(d) grants the delayed off-side only at Youth 15-Only and Girls 16U and above, High School and Adult; below those 630(c) stops play at once. The NHL, IIHF and Hockey Canada 6.11(b) set no such floor
Position: Down to the top of the circle is the activated position — shoot from a better angle, walk the line, or receive in the high slot, and still recover
Risk: Below the circles is always a commitment — below the hash marks you are effectively a fourth forward
Never: Go that deep before someone has taken your job at the blue line — before you go, not after
```

An offensive-zone defenceman is always at one of three depths, and the difference between them is the difference between a good defenceman and a liability:

1. **Just inside the blue line (the point).** The default. You are the outlet for low-to-high, the shooting threat that stops the defence collapsing entirely, and the safety net against a counter-attack. Stay a stride *inside* the line, not on it — if the puck completely crosses out, the zone is cleared and everyone has to re-enter. To be precise, a puck leaving is not "offside" and is not a delayed off-side either — it breaks no rule at all. Offside under **NHL Rule 83.1** is about attackers' skates preceding the puck *into* the zone, and what a puck leaving costs you is simply the zone: everyone must now re-enter behind it. The **delayed off-side** is the situation that arises *next*, when the puck is played back **into** the zone while your teammates are still inside it — **NHL and IIHF Rule 83.3** describe *"an attacking player (or players) has preceded the puck across the attacking blue line"*, and the linesperson's arm goes up rather than the whistle going. Your teammates clear it by **tagging up**. ⚠️ **Before you count on that recovery, find out which book you are under.** **USA Hockey Rule 630(d)** grants the delayed off-side and the tag-up only *"(For all Youth 15-Only levels and above, all Girls 16U levels and above, High School and Adult classifications only)"*; below those classifications **Rule 630(c)** simply stops play, so the whistle is immediate and there is nothing to recover. **The NHL, the IIHF and Hockey Canada set no such floor** — Hockey Canada's Rule 6.11(b) carries no classification clause at all, and under those three books the recovery is yours at every level. **🇬🇧 Britain runs the IIHF book, and the IHUK In-House Rules carry no amendment to Rule 83**, so the tag-up is yours at every age band there too — [Rink Map and Glossary](../foundation/rink_map_and_glossary.md) sets out that derivation and the limits on it. See [Rules Primer](../foundation/rules_primer.md).
2. **Down to the top of the circle.** The activated position. You step down to shoot from a better angle, to walk the line toward the middle, or to receive a pass in the high slot. You are still recoverable: you can turn and get back to the line before most counter-attacks develop.
3. **Below the circles — too deep.** Not always wrong, but always a commitment. If you go below the hash marks (the short lines at the sides of the faceoff circles), you are effectively a fourth forward and someone must take your job at the blue line *before* you go, not after.

### Who covers for whom

```facts
Convention: If one defenceman leaves the blue line, the other stays — two on the line at all times is the house default, not a law
Options: Teams running a five-man rotation, or green-lighting both defencemen, cover the line with whichever player is highest instead
Action: If a defenceman goes below the line and gets the puck, they play as a forward until the play ends — F3 fills in at the blue line
Action: If a defenceman goes down and does not get the puck, recover defensive position first, then look to attack again
Never: Chase after a failed activation — that is how odd-man rushes against you start
Action: Say it out loud — "I'm going", "I've got you", "hold"; silent rotations are the ones that leave the blue line empty
```

These are the house default's rules, not laws of hockey, and they are short and worth memorising:

- **If one defenceman leaves the blue line, the other stays.** You want two players on the line at all times. This is the house default here and the assumption to play on if nobody has told you otherwise; it is widely taught, though nobody has published a count. But teams running a **five-man rotation**, or giving both defencemen a standing green light, deliberately break it and cover the line with whichever player is highest. See the pinch note at the end of section 10 and [Forechecking Systems](forechecking_systems.md).
- **If a defenceman goes below the line and gets the puck**, they play as a forward until the play ends — and **F3 must fill in at the blue line for them**.
- **If a defenceman goes down and does *not* get the puck**, their first job is to recover defensive position, then look to attack again. Chasing after a failed activation is how odd-man rushes against you start.
- **Say it out loud.** "I'm going," "I've got you," "hold." Silent rotations are the ones that leave the blue line empty. See [On-Ice Communication](../foundation/on_ice_communication.md).

### Realistic alternatives

```facts
Options: Overload / strong-side load — four players on the puck side, one weak-side outlet; wins board battles, gives up the weak-side seam
Options: Five-man rotation — defencemen rotate into forward spots and forwards cover the line as routine rather than emergency
Options: 1-3-1 spread — one at the point, two half-walls, one bumper in the middle, one net front; common on the power play
Risk: The five-man rotation requires everyone to be comfortable at the blue line
Never: Confuse the bumper with the net-front player — two different jobs at two different depths
```

- **Overload / strong-side load.** Four players are loaded onto the puck side, with only one weak-side outlet. Wins board battles and grinds teams down; gives up the weak-side seam and the quick change of point.
- **Five-man rotation ("position-less" or high cycle).** Defencemen rotate into forward spots and forwards cover the line as a matter of routine rather than emergency. This is how modern NHL teams with skating defencemen play. It requires everyone to be comfortable at the blue line.
- **1-3-1 spread.** One at the point, two on the half-walls, one **bumper** in the middle of the ice between them, one net front. Common on the power play (see [Special Teams](special_teams.md)) and occasionally used at even strength. **The bumper is not the net-front player** — they are two different jobs at two different depths.

## 4. Cycling

### Cycling: What it is

```facts
Key: A cycle rotates the forwards around the low offensive zone — corner, half-wall, behind the net — protecting the puck from the defence
Action: Move the puck short distances, a few feet at a time, and rotate through the positions
Goal: Make the defence keep re-solving the same problem
```

A **cycle** is a possession tactic in which the forwards rotate around the low offensive zone — corner, half-wall, behind the net — passing the puck short distances to each other while protecting it from the defence. Think of a wheel: the puck moves a few feet at a time, the players rotate through the positions, and the defence has to keep re-solving the same problem.

### Why it works

```facts
Goal: Force defenders to move and decide repeatedly — every rotation asks "collapse low or hold the slot?", and over 30 or 40 seconds someone answers wrong
Key: A cycle is a low-turnover way to hold the puck — a board pass that fails stays near the wall, where one picked off through the middle goes the other way with your forwards below it
Key: Ranking the middle pass as the more expensive one to lose is coaching consensus rather than a counted comparison — nothing published classifies odd-man rushes by the play that created them
Goal: Tire the defenders — they skate without the puck and cannot change, and tired coverage shrinks toward the puck, which opens the slot
Goal: Buy time for your defencemen and late forwards to join and get set
```

- **It forces defenders to move and decide, repeatedly.** Each rotation asks the defence "do you collapse low or hold the slot?" Over 30 or 40 seconds of this, someone answers wrong.
- **It is a low-turnover way to hold the puck.** Passes along the boards, away from the middle of the ice, are the cheapest ones to miss: the puck stays near the wall with bodies around it. One picked off through the middle is the one that goes the other way with your forwards below it. **Ranking the middle pass as the more expensive one to lose is coaching consensus rather than a counted comparison** — nothing published classifies odd-man rushes by the play that created them, and [Defending the Rush](defending_the_rush.md) owns how they are made.
- **It tires the defenders.** They are skating without the puck, which is far more exhausting than skating with it, and they cannot change lines. A tired defender's coverage shrinks toward the puck, which opens the slot.
- **It buys time for your defencemen and late forwards to join.** A cycle that lasts eight seconds is often just a cycle that lasted long enough for your point men to get set.

### The mechanics

```facts
Technique: Low-to-low — pass backwards and down to a teammate arriving behind you, then rotate up into the space they just left
Read: If the checker is still in front of you, rim it — onward around the boards, past the player sealing you to the wall
Read: If the checker has skated past you, reverse it — back against the flow, into the space they have just vacated
Risk: Picking the wrong one of those two is a leading cycle turnover — nobody has counted cycle turnovers by cause, so treat the ranking as coaching craft and the read itself as the point
Technique: Bump pass — fire the puck hard at a spot on the wall, not at your teammate, and call it; a bump nobody expects is a turnover
Technique: Pivot / cutback — cut back up the wall you came down; a defender committed toward the corner cannot turn as fast as you
Options: Scissor / switch — carrier and support skate at each other and cross, support passing on the outside; the carrier may drop it or keep it
```

**Low-to-low (the true cycle).** The puck carrier on the half-wall or in the corner passes the puck **backwards and down** to a teammate arriving behind them, then rotates up into the space their teammate just left. Two variants matter and they are not the same play:

- **Rim** — the puck goes *onward* around the boards, following the curve of the rink, past the checker who is sealing you against the wall. Beats pressure that has you pinned.
- **Reverse** — the puck goes *back against the flow*, into the space a checker has just skated out of. Beats pressure that has over-committed and gone past you.

Picking the wrong one is a leading cycle turnover — **nobody has counted cycle turnovers by cause, so take that as coaching craft rather than a measurement**; what is not in doubt is that the two reads point in opposite directions and one of them is wrong every time. The rule of thumb: **if the checker is still in front of you, rim it; if they have skated past you, reverse it.**

**The bump pass off the wall.** Rather than passing to a teammate directly, you fire the puck hard into the boards so it caroms to a spot. It is unblockable by a stick in the passing lane and it does not care about a defender standing between you. Aim at a *spot on the wall*, not at your teammate, and call it — a bump nobody expects is a turnover.

**The pivot / cutback.** You skate down the boards, run out of space, and cut back to skate back up the wall where you came from. This is a high-percentage individual cycle move, and widely taught as the pick of them — **a coaching judgement, not a measured one; no source here ranks cycle moves** — because a defender who has committed their momentum toward the corner cannot change direction as fast as you can. It buys you two or three seconds and it usually opens the low-to-high pass.

**Low-to-high.** The puck goes up to the point or the top of the circle. Covered in full in section 5.

**The scissor / switch.** The puck carrier and a supporting teammate skate towards each other and cross, with the support player passing on the outside. The carrier can drop the puck or keep it. Defenders who are switching assignments during a scissor are defenders who are briefly covering nobody.

### When to cycle and when to attack the net

```facts
Read: Cycle when the defence is set, structured and between you and the net, and no dangerous shot exists
Read: Cycle when your defencemen are not yet established at the line, or you are protecting a lead and burning clock with possession
Read: Attack immediately when a defender turns their back to the puck, or a defenceman pinches and the ice behind them opens
Read: Attack immediately when the slot is unoccupied by a defender, or the goalie is moving or unset
Read: Attack immediately when your net-front player has beaten their check to the inside
```

**Cycle when:**
- The defence is set, structured and between you and the net;
- No dangerous shot exists and forcing one means a low-percentage attempt or a turnover;
- Your defencemen are not yet established at the line;
- You are protecting a lead or trying to burn clock with possession (see [Game Management](game_management.md)).

**Stop cycling and attack immediately when:**
- A defender turns their back to the puck;
- A defenceman pinches or over-commits and the ice behind them opens;
- The slot is unoccupied by a defender;
- A goalie is moving or unset;
- Your net-front player has beaten their check to the inside.

### The failure: cycling for its own sake

```facts
Key: A cycle is a set-up, not an outcome
Never: Cycle for its own sake — three or four rotations that never threaten the net burn thirty seconds at zero risk to the defence
Goal: End the cycle in one of four things — a shot from home plate, a royal road pass, a low-to-high with a screen, or a net drive
Risk: Nobody in front of the net means even a good shot has no tip, no screen and no rebound support
Read: If the puck never goes above the goal line and never goes to the middle, you are passing around the outside of a box with no reason to move
```

This is the defining amateur error in the offensive zone. A team gets the puck low, cycles it around three or four times, looks organised and busy, and never once threatens the net. The defence is entirely content — you have used up thirty seconds of your own shift, in their zone, at zero risk to them.

**A cycle is a set-up, not an outcome.** If a cycle does not end in one of: a shot from the home-plate area, a royal road pass, a low-to-high with a screen, or a net drive, it did not accomplish anything. Two clear symptoms:

- **Nobody is in front of the net.** Cycling with an empty goalmouth means even a good shot has no tip, no screen and no rebound support.
- **The puck never goes above the goal line and never goes to the middle.** You are passing along the outside of a box that has no reason to move.

## 5. Low-to-High

### The play

```facts
Action: Pass from a low position — corner, half-wall, behind the net — up to a defenceman at the point or the top of the circle, who shoots
Read: Low-to-high is the standard way to generate a shot when the defence has collapsed low and taken away everything in tight
Action: The passer feeds the point then supports toward the strong-side hash marks — bail-out outlet and rebound position
Technique: The defenceman walks the puck toward the middle before shooting — it changes the angle, moves the goalie laterally and opens a lane
Position: One forward to the net front to screen and tip; one supporting in the high slot to form a triangle with the point
Read: The weak-side defenceman reads for a rebound squirting to their side and is ready to jump on it
Options: D-to-D across the top first — the goalie has to slide and the shot comes from the other side
```

**Low-to-high** means passing the puck from a low position (corner, half-wall, behind the net) up to a defenceman at the point or the top of the circle, who shoots. It is the standard way to generate a shot when the defence has collapsed low and taken away everything in tight.

The sequence, with everyone's job named:

1. **The passer** feeds the point and then **supports** — skating toward the strong-side hash marks, both to be an outlet if the defenceman needs a bail-out and to be in position for a rebound.
2. **The defenceman** receives it and **walks the puck toward the middle** before shooting. This matters enormously: walking the line changes the angle, forces the goalie to track laterally, and often opens a lane through the shot-blockers.
3. **One forward gets to the net front** to screen and tip.
4. **One forward supports in the high slot**, giving the defenceman a second option and forming a triangle with the point — meaning the puck can be moved again quickly instead of being fired into a shin pad.
5. **The weak-side defenceman** reads for a rebound squirting to their side and is ready to jump on it.

![](diagram:oz-low-to-high)

A common addition is **D-to-D across the top** before the shot: the strong-side defenceman moves it to their partner, the goalie has to slide across, and the shot comes from the other side. On the net-centre-to-net-centre geometry this document uses, that is a royal road crossing at the top of the zone — less valuable than one in the slot, but far better than a static point shot. *(Under the narrower, zone-limited description of the road noted in Section 2, a lateral pass out at the blue line would not cross it at all. The coaching point survives either way: the goalie still has to slide, and a shot arriving before they are set is worth more than one arriving after.)*

### Why it beats a cycle that never shoots

```facts
Goal: Release the puck — a screened point shot produces a rebound, a tip, a scramble, a whistle in your favour and a line change for you
Key: Even at a low conversion rate with traffic, the shot generates chaos in the home-plate area — and that is where goals live
Never: Cycle without ever releasing — it produces none of that
```

Because a shot from the point with a screen and a net-front presence produces things a cycle does not: a **rebound**, a **tip**, a **scramble**, a **whistle in your favour**, a **line change for you** and none for them. Even at a 3% conversion rate with traffic, the shot generates chaos in the home-plate area, and chaos in the home-plate area is where goals live. A cycle that never releases produces none of that.

### The honest caveat

```facts
Key: Low-to-high is a means, not an end — at even strength a point shot is among the lowest-percentage shots on the ice, and blocked more often than any other
Key: The point-shot caution is an even-strength one — on the power play the same map puts roughly the whole zone at or above that under-5% figure, so it is no argument against a point shot there
Risk: They also generate fewer rebounds than shots from lower in the zone
Priority: Low-to-high is only worth doing when the net front is occupied — a screen, a tip threat and two players hunting the rebound
Never: Fire it from the point with nobody in front — the identical shot is a giveaway with extra steps
```

Low-to-high is a *means*, not an end. Remember the numbers from section 1, **and their scope**: at even strength a point shot is among the **lowest-percentage shots on the ice**, and it is **blocked more often** than a shot from anywhere else — but section 1's under-5% figure is an even-strength one, and on the power play the same map puts the whole zone at or above it — approximate in where the line falls, not in its direction — and analysis by Alex Novet found they **generate fewer rebounds** than shots from lower in the zone ([via The Coaches Site](https://members.thecoachessite.com/article/the-problem-with-point-shots-according-to-analytics)).

There is a real-world cautionary tale here. The Seattle Kraken under Dave Hakstol built an offensive-zone scheme heavily around low-to-high and generated the third-most point shots per sixty minutes in the league — while producing shots with the **lowest expected value per shot of any team** ([Sound of Hockey](https://soundofhockey.com/2022/03/30/kraken-coach-dave-hakstols-scheme-and-philosophies-offensive-zone-plays-part-7/), 2021-22 season). **Both quoted phrases were checked against that page verbatim on 28 July 2026 — but neither number is Sound of Hockey's own.** The article credits the point-shot rate to **Corey Sznajder's** manual tracking and the expected-value figure to **TopDownHockey/JFreshHockey**, which it labels "paid access". Neither underlying dataset is public, so this is a secondary source restating two private ones; treat it as a reported anecdote about one team in one season, not as a checkable statistic.

The lesson is not "don't go low-to-high." It is: **low-to-high is only worth doing when the net front is occupied.** A point shot with a screen, a tip threat and two players hunting the rebound is a good play. The identical shot with nobody in front is a giveaway with extra steps.

## 6. Net-Front Play

The net front is the least glamorous piece of productive real estate in hockey, and section 1 has the numbers behind the second half of that: shots from the home-plate area convert several times better than shots from outside it. It is also the place where players most often do the job badly while believing they are doing it.

### Screening

```facts
Position: At the edge of the blue paint, inside the faceoff dots — roughly six to eight feet out, living just beyond the arc
Technique: Stand between the goalie's eyes and the puck, not between the goalie and the net. Slide with the point man as they walk the line
Technique: Chest up and square to the shooter, hips low, feet wide, knees bent — the posture of a squat, not of a bow
Action: Be still at the moment of release — constant shuffling gives the goalie flashes of the puck through the gaps
Never: Watch the shot. Look at the puck through your legs or over your shoulder instead, then get your eyes back to the ice in front of you
Rule: A screen from outside the crease without contact is normally legal — the sentence in NHL and IIHF Rule 69.1 that actually disallows a goal for positioning is keyed to an attacker who "enters the goal crease", and 69.4, the outside-the-crease rule, has no positioning limb at all
Rule: All four books leave the no-contact screen set from outside the crease alone — NHL and IIHF 69.3 key it to a "significant position" within the crease, and USA Hockey and Hockey Canada write no screening or goaltender-vision provision at all. Inside the paint all four cost you
```

A **screen** is standing in the goaltender's line of sight so they cannot see the puck. **A screen from outside the crease, without contact, is normally legal**, and referees allow it constantly. The qualification — see the rules section below — is the **crease**, not the sightline: the sentence in Rule 69.1 that actually disallows a goal reaches an attacker who *"enters the goal crease"*, 69.3's no-contact screen ground asks for a *"significant position within the goal crease"*, and 69.4, which is the rule for outside the crease, has no positioning limb at all. **For a screen set from outside the paint, the other two books have nothing either** — USA Hockey writes no goaltender-vision provision at all, in its playing rules or its Casebook, and Hockey Canada's only two references to the goaltender's view both reach a player standing *in* the crease. ⚠️ **So the line, not the sightline, is what you have to respect — and where exactly the line runs is itself unsettled between the books.** [Rink Map and Glossary](../foundation/rink_map_and_glossary.md) sets that out. Keep your skates clear of both.

The mechanics of doing it well:

- **Stand between the goalie's eyes and the puck, not between the goalie and the net.** These are different lines, and they change as the puck moves. If the point man walks the line, you must slide with them to stay on the line. A screen that was correct three seconds ago is now just a player standing near the net.
- **Tall chest, low hips — the two halves do different jobs.** Keep your **chest up and square to the shooter** so you present the biggest possible obstruction to the goalie's sightline; crouching, turning sideways or ducking away from the shot all shrink the surface you are blocking and let the goalie find the puck around you. At the same time keep your **hips low and your feet wide, knees bent** — that is what stops a defenceman moving you. Standing tall *and* narrow, with straight legs, is why players get shoved out of the paint; folding at the waist is why goalies see the shot. You want the posture of a squat, not of a bow.
- **Be still at the moment of release.** Plant and obstruct. Constant shuffling gives the goalie flashes of the puck through the gaps.
- **Do not watch the shot.** Look at the puck through your legs or over your shoulder, then get your eyes back to the ice in front of you. Turning your body to follow the flight of the shot is how you both un-screen the goalie and miss the rebound.
- **Position: at the edge of the blue paint, inside the faceoff dots — roughly six to eight feet out from the goal line.** Deep enough to obstruct the goalie's view and to reach rebounds, far enough out to stay clear of the crease. Note the geometry, because it is easy to get wrong: the crease is **8 feet wide at the goal line and 6 feet deep**, because the arc that closes it is struck with a **six-foot radius from the centre of the goal line** (Rule 1.7) — the six feet is the *depth*, not the half-width. So "three feet out from the goal line" is *inside* the paint, not clear of it. Live just beyond the arc.

![](diagram:oz-net-front-screen)

### Tips and deflections

```facts
Technique: Blade on the ice, always — the overwhelming majority of tippable pucks come in low
Key: You are redirecting, not shooting — ten to thirty degrees beats a set goalie, and a big swing usually misses the puck entirely
Technique: Angle the blade, absorb slightly, do not follow through — hard hands send it into the glass
Action: Present a target — hold the blade out in the lane where you want the puck, and shout for it
Rule: A puck contacting an attacking player's stick above the crossbar is not a goal, and where it hits the stick is what counts (NHL and IIHF 80.3, NHL 78.5(vi), Hockey Canada 6.9(b)) — but only the goal off that contact dies: a teammate who gains it and shoots scores (IIHF Handbook 2025/26, 80.9(c))
Rule: USA Hockey is not that rule — 621(d) sets the ceiling at the shoulder and writes no crossbar test, and its Casebook 621 Situation 7 refuses the contact point: "When any part of the stick is carried above the shoulders, the entire stick is considered to be high"
Rule: So a tip between your own shoulders and the crossbar is a goal under the NHL, the IIHF and Hockey Canada and no goal under USA Hockey — tip low in all four, which is where the pucks are anyway
Rule: The whistle diverges less than the ceiling does — NHL and IIHF Rule 80.1 and Hockey Canada 6.9(a) stop play only once the offending team gains possession and control of the high-batted puck, so a loose one is played on until both teams abandon it, which stops play in all four (NHL and IIHF 72.3)
Rule: USA Hockey 621(d) prints the opposite default — play stops "immediately" unless the puck "is batted to an opponent who gains possession" — but its Casebook 621 Situation 15 applies the same possession test the other books do, so follow the tip up unless it goes straight in
Rule: A high stick on the puck is one rule; one on a person is another — NHL and IIHF 60, USA Hockey 621(a) to 621(c), Hockey Canada 9.5, or 7.6 Head Contact via 6.9(c) in minor and female hockey, none capped at a minor; in CARHA leagues 62(b) is a major, plus a game misconduct if intentional or injuring
Rule: A puck deflected in off your skate or body is a good goal with no distinct kicking motion (NHL Rules 78.4 and 78.5(ii)) and no deliberate hand or body bat (78.5(i))
```

A **tip** or **deflection** is changing the direction of an incoming shot with your stick blade. It is devastating because it destroys the goalie's read: they have tracked and set for a puck travelling one way, and it is now travelling another way from six feet out.

- **Blade on the ice, always.** The overwhelming majority of tippable pucks come in low. A stick held off the ice at the net front is a stick doing nothing.
- **You are redirecting, not shooting.** A small change of angle — ten to thirty degrees — is enough to beat a set goalie. A big swing usually misses the puck entirely.
- **Angle the blade, absorb slightly, do not follow through.** Soft hands change direction; hard hands send it into the glass.
- **Present a target.** Shooters at the point aim at the tipper's stick. Hold the blade out in the lane where you want the puck, and shout for it.

**Two rules to know before you tip**, and both cost teams goals every season. **The first of them is not the same rule in every book, and the difference is a whole hand's width of stick height:**

- **A puck that enters the net after contacting an attacking player's stick above the height of the crossbar is not a goal** (NHL Rules 80.3 and 78.5(vi)). Rule 80.3 is the fuller statement: *"When an attacking player causes the puck to enter the opponent's goal by contacting the puck above the height of the crossbar, either directly or deflected off any player or official, the goal shall not be allowed. The determining factor is where the puck makes contact with the stick. If the puck makes contact with the stick at or below the level of the crossbar and enters the goal, this goal shall be allowed."* It is where the puck contacts the stick that matters, not where your hands are. Tip low.

  **🇬🇧 The IIHF prints 80.3 in the same words**, down to *"the determining factor is where the puck makes contact with the stick"*, unchanged between the 2025/26 (v1.1) and 2026/27 editions — so a British player is under the crossbar test too. **Hockey Canada 6.9(b) is the most explicit of the three** about what that test forgives: the deciding factor *"is the height of the stick relative to the height of the crossbar"*, and the goal counts where the puck contacts a part of the stick below the crossbar *"(despite some other portion of the stick being above the crossbar)"*. ⚠️ **Both sides of the split are answered again at the interpretation layer, on the same fact pattern, and they still disagree.** The **IIHF Situation Handbook, Situation 80.6**, puts an attacker carrying the blade *"clearly above the crossbar"* with the puck striking the shaft near the waist and going in, and answers ***"No. This is a good goal."*** **Hockey Canada's Interpretation 4 to Rule 6.9(a)(i)** puts the same play — stick above the shoulder, puck striking the butt-end below it — and answers ***"GOAL."*** That is the exact fact pattern USA Hockey's Casebook answers *"No"* to, below. ⚠️ **And the crossbar ceiling only kills the goal along the chain Rule 80.3 names** — *"either directly or deflected off any Player or official"* — **while between the crossbar and your own shoulders there is no high-sticking violation at all, because Rule 80.1’s ceiling is the shoulders.** The IIHF Situation Handbook works that band through at **Situation 80.9**: contact above the crossbar but below the shoulders, deflected off a defender **(a)** or off a teammate **(b)**, is *"No"* both times; but where the puck *"ends up on the stick"* of a teammate who then **shoots** and scores, **(c)** answers ***"Yes"*** — *"the goal was scored as a result of the teammate’s shot, not the batting of the puck out of the air by the teammate. Since the stick was below the height of the player’s shoulders when the player contacted the puck, it was not an illegal high-stick."* *(That answer says "teammate" where it means the player who batted the puck; the slip is the book’s and it is quoted as printed.)* **So a puck you tipped between the crossbar and your shoulders is still live and no whistle is coming: break the chain and the next shot counts.** Above your shoulders it is the other rule, and there — under the NHL and the IIHF — play stops as soon as your own team gains possession and control; USA Hockey prints a different default, set out below.

  ⚠️ **USA Hockey is the fourth book, and there this is not the same rule twice over.** **Rule 621(d)** sets the ceiling at the **shoulder**, not the crossbar: *"Batting the puck above the normal height of the shoulder with the stick is prohibited and no goal can be scored as a result of an attacking player playing the puck above the shoulder with the stick and directly entering the goal."* **The word "crossbar" appears nowhere in that book's high-stick rule.** ⚠️ **And it does not run the contact-point test at all.** Its *Casebook*, at **Rule 621, Situation 7**, puts a player holding the blade above the shoulders while the puck strikes the butt-end *below* shoulder height and goes in, and answers ***"No"***: *"When any part of the stick is carried above the shoulders, the entire stick is considered to be high."* **So a tip taken between your own shoulders and the crossbar is a goal under the NHL, the IIHF and Hockey Canada, and no goal under USA Hockey** — where the ceiling is your shoulder and any part of the stick above it makes the whole stick high. How much North American rec and youth hockey runs on USA Hockey's book is a question about leagues rather than about books, and this document has no count for it, so find out which book your league runs. Tip low in all four: that is where the pucks are anyway.

  ⚠️ **The whistle looks as though it differs on the same lines, and it decides whether there is a rebound to hunt — but read to the end of USA Hockey's Casebook before you believe that it does.** Under **NHL and IIHF Rule 80.1** and **Hockey Canada Rule 6.9(a)**, batting the puck above shoulder height stops play only once **the offending team** gains possession and control of it — so a high-batted puck that runs loose, or reaches an opponent, is played on. **USA Hockey 621(d) prints the opposite default:** play *"shall be immediately stopped"* **unless** the puck *"is batted to an opponent who gains possession"*, or a defender bats it into their own goal, where the goal stands. ⚠️ **Do not stop skating on that sentence.** Its own *Casebook* puts the whistle back close to where the other three books have it. **Rule 621, Situation 15** asks whether a high-sticked puck that deflects off a teammate and reaches an opponent with a clear chance to advance it may be played on, and answers ***"Yes"***: *"For play to be stopped for the high stick violation, an offending team player must be the first to gain possession of the puck. A deflection off of a player from either team would not cause the play to be completed."* ⚠️ **The same answer prints the exception that saves 621(d)'s *"immediately"*, and it is the tip itself:** *"The exception to this rule is when the puck played with the high stick goes directly in the goal of the non-offending team, which calls for an immediate stoppage of play and a face-off in the defending zone of the offending team."* **So a high tip that goes in leaves no rebound to chase — no goal, and the whistle at once.** **Situation 6** does the same from the other side, holding the whistle until a defender's own goalkeeper — a teammate — gains possession, although neither printed exception applies. So a tip above your shoulder does **not** end the play on contact, and there is something to follow up. ⚠️ **On the puck nobody claims, all four books stop play — what differs is where the face-off goes, and USA Hockey is the outlier.** Its Casebook completes the violation when the puck goes dead (**Situation 8**: *"complete as soon as the puck became unplayable"*), when nobody on the opposing team establishes possession (**Situation 10**: *"completed because the puck was not in possession of the opposing team"*), and when an opponent who could play it declines to (**Situation 12**) — and in each of those it sends the face-off to *"an end face-off spot in the Defending Zone of the offending team."* **The other three refuse that reward.** **NHL Rule 72.3 and IIHF Rule 72.3 carry the same sentence** — the IIHF's differs only in its own house style, capitalising *Player*, writing *their stick* for *his stick*, and setting *"normal height of the shoulders"* in quotation marks — and both stop play where a high-sticker's teammate *"elects not to play the puck to avoid the stoppage of play, and the opposing team also abstains from playing the puck"*. Both put the face-off *"at the face-off spot in the zone to nearest to where the play was stopped"*, not down the ice, and the **IIHF Situation Handbook** gives the reason for that at **Situation 72.5**: *"the team that batted or high-sticked the puck cannot play the puck, only the opposing team can, and should they abstain, the whistle is blown, and the face-off stays right there."* **Hockey Canada's Interpretation 2 to Rule 6.9(a)(i)** says why: play stops *"immediately"*, the face-off is *"at the location nearest to where the stoppage of play occurred"*, and *"both teams have committed an infraction of the rules — one team contacted the puck with a high stick; the other, by not pursuing possession and control, has failed to keep the puck in motion."* **So declining to play a high-sticked puck wins your team territory under USA Hockey and wins it nothing under the other three.** ⚠️ **Situation 10's simultaneous touch by opposing players is the fact pattern no other book on disk prints** — the IIHF Situation Handbook’s fifteen Rule 80 rulings and its seven Rule 72 rulings do not reach it, and neither the NHL, Hockey Canada nor CARHA prints it. *(Those two blocks are not the whole of the IIHF’s interpretation layer on high-sticking the puck — its Rule 85 block carries more, on the puck leaving the playing surface rather than on a contested touch — so read that negative as covering the blocks named.)* **But the question it turns on is answered the same way in three of them**: a touch or a deflection by an opponent is not the possession and control that nullifies the violation. IIHF Handbook **80.2** — *"Deflections off opposing players or an official does not constitute control of the puck and therefore the play must be stopped"*; IIHF Handbook **80.15** — *"The only way this is nullified is if an opponent gains possession and control of the puck"*; **Hockey Canada's Interpretation 1 to Rule 6.9(a)(i)** — *"A deflection results in possession but not control of the puck."* Situation 10's own reasoning is that same test, *"the puck was not in possession of the opposing team"*. ⚠️ **The adjacent case splits them outright, though, and it is the one you will actually meet: an opponent who high-sticks the same puck himself.** The IIHF Handbook’s **Situation 80.3** has a defender knocking the puck while it is still airborne and holds that his high stick *"would not be considered control of the puck"*, so play stops on the **original** violation; **Situation 80.15** puts the principle generally — the puck deflecting off *"anything (stick, body, teammate, opponent, official)"* does not nullify it. USA Hockey’s Casebook, **Rule 621, Situation 11**, answers the reverse: *"once the Team B player strikes the puck with their high stick, possession of the puck is established and the violation against Team A is nullified."* **So a mere deflection nullifies nothing in either book, but an opponent’s own high stick nullifies yours under USA Hockey and not under the IIHF** — which decides whether you are still playing. **So on whether play stops it is confirmed. What has no counterpart outside USA Hockey is where the face-off then goes.** ⚠️ **And Situations 4 and 5 decide a different question from this one.** Both ask *"Does the goal count?"* Both answer *"No"*, holding that *"[t]he puck must come into possession, however briefly, of the defending player for it to be considered 'batted to an opponent'"* — that settles the goal, not the timing of the whistle, and Situation 15 forecloses reading it across.

  ⚠️ **Where the face-off then goes is where the books part company hardest, and two identical plays get opposite answers.** **Your high stick, their goalkeeper freezes it:** the **IIHF Situation Handbook, Situation 80.12**, keeps the draw *"at one of the end zone face-off circles in Team B's end"* — right where it happened, in the attacking zone — while **USA Hockey's Casebook, Rule 621, Situation 9**, sends the identical play *"[a]t an end face-off spot in the offending player's Defending Zone."* **The same play, and opposite ends of the rink.** USA Hockey gives its reason in the same situation: the goalkeeper *"has not elected to 'play' the puck in a manner that would allow play to continue, but has instead determined to stop play and, in effect, allowed the high stick violation to be enforced."* **And the high tip that goes straight in:** the Casebook's Situation 15 gives *"an immediate stoppage of play and a face-off in the defending zone of the offending team"*, where **NHL and IIHF Rule 76.2** both send an illegally scored goal *"by directing, batting, kicking or high-sticking the puck into the goal"* to *"the neutral zone at the nearest face-off spot"* — confirmed at IIHF Handbook **Situation 80.13**. ⚠️ **And "USA Hockey is the outlier" is true inside those four and not beyond them.** A fifth book on disk is stricter than any of them: **CARHA Rule 62(d)** prints no possession test and no exception at all — *"Contacting the puck with the stick above the normal height of the player's shoulders is prohibited. When it occurs, play shall be stopped immediately and a face-off shall take place in the offending team's defensive zone at the nearest face-off spot. No penalty is assessed to the player."* Its Rule 62 prints no crossbar test anywhere. **CARHA governs CARHA-affiliated adult leagues only**, so this is the paragraph that decides it if that is your book and irrelevant if it is not. ⚠️ **Two more of these, both on the same split.** **The puck that goes dead:** USA Hockey’s Casebook completes the violation and sends the draw to the offending team’s defending zone (**Situation 8**), where the IIHF answers the same facts directly — **Situation 85.31**, a high-sticked puck out of play in the attacking zone, gives *"one of the face-off spots in the neutral zone"*, because *"[t]he attacking team should not gain a benefit from the illegal act of high-sticking the puck."* **And the delayed off-side, where they swap sides:** IIHF Handbook **Situation 80.10** — delayed off-side signalled, Team B high-sticks the puck and touches it — keeps the draw *"in Team B's defending zone at one of the end zone face-off spots"*, while USA Hockey’s Casebook **Situation 13**, on the same delayed-off-side facts, sends it to *"the nearest Neutral Zone face-off spot"* because *"the original infraction was offside."* **Opposite ends of the rink again, and this time it is the IIHF that keeps it deep.** Both Handbook rulings read the same in the 2025/26 v1.1 and 2026/27 editions.

  ⚠️ **Everything above is about the puck. The moment the blade reaches a person it is a different rule and a different price.** Rules 80 and 621(d) and 6.9 and 62(d) decide whether the goal counts and when the whistle goes; contact with an opponent above the shoulders is **NHL and IIHF Rule 60**, **USA Hockey 621(a), 621(b) and 621(c)** and **Hockey Canada 9.5** — and in Hockey Canada minor and female divisions **Rule 6.9(c)** routes a high stick at the puck, or an attempt at one, that catches an opponent to **Rule 7.6 Head Contact**, whose preamble supersedes every rule in the book but fighting. **None of them is capped at a minor**, and **CARHA Rule 62(b) has no minor rung above the shoulders at all** — its minor, 62(a), is a different offence rather than a lower rung, reaching a player who checks or intimidates an opponent while carrying the stick above **the waist height of their *opponent***, with no contact required — **the book measures that rung against the other player's body, not your own**, and prints it twice, in 62(a) and again in its Rule 62 Situation 1 (*"above waist height of opponent"*, ruled a minor): *"A player who accidentally or intentionally contacts an opponent above the normal height of the opponent's shoulders with a high stick shall be assessed a Major penalty, whether or not injury results."* **And 62(b)'s Note adds a game misconduct** where the contact was intentional, or where an accidental one injures, so only an accidental high stick that hurts nobody leaves you a major alone — CARHA-affiliated adult leagues only. [Shooting](../technique/shooting.md) sets out each book's full ladder. **You tip in a crowd, so the blade comes down after the tip as well as before it.**

- **A puck deflected into the net off your skate or body is a good goal** — provided there is **no "distinct kicking motion"** (NHL Rules 78.4 and 78.5(ii)), and provided you did not deliberately bat it in with your hand or body (Rule 78.5(i)). So you may absolutely turn your skate to redirect a puck; you may not kick it.

### Hunting rebounds

```facts
Read: Anticipate where it goes — a pad tends to squirt back toward the corner it came from, a blocker to the shooter's off side, a chest to drop in the paint
Risk: Treat those as tendencies, not rules — a goalie angling their surfaces deliberately will break all three on purpose
Action: Shoot it back immediately — do not settle it, do not look up; the window is one or two seconds while the goalie is down
Options: Go to the far post when you get tied up — nobody is covering it and it opens the weak-side chance
Key: The defending team recovers roughly 60% of rebounds — Greg Revak's figure, read off a chart by Alex Novet — so you win that race by being there before the shot
Read: Revak's 60% summarises the whole chart rather than one zone — Novet plots offensive recovery flat at 38–42% wherever the shot came from; no league, season or sample size is stated anywhere
```

- **Anticipate where it goes, don't react to where it went.** The rough tendencies: a puck hitting a pad tends to squirt back toward the corner it came from, a puck hitting the blocker toward the shooter's off side, a puck hitting the chest to drop in the paint. **Treat these as tendencies, not rules** — they are coaching craft, not measured rebound distributions, and a goalie who is angling their surfaces deliberately (which is what [Goaltender](../positions/goaltender.md) teaches them to do) will break all three on purpose. Know where the shot is coming from, pre-load your feet that way, and be ready to be wrong.
- **Shoot it back immediately.** Do not settle it, do not look up. The window is one or two seconds, while the goalie is down and out of position.
- **Go to the far post when you get tied up.** If your check has sealed you off from the near side, the far post is a rebound location nobody is covering and it opens the weak-side chance.
- **Understand the odds.** The defending team recovers roughly **60% of rebounds**. That is exactly why the net-front job is a *job* and not a bonus — you have to be there, deliberately, before the shot, or you lose that race by default. **Note whose figure this is**, because its two halves have different authors. The chart behind it is **Alex Novet's**, and it never prints 60%: it plots the share of rebounds recovered by the *attacking* team, zone by zone, at 38–42%. The 60% is **Greg Revak's** — what is left of 100 once Novet's number is taken off, with the chart's eight zones rolled into one — published in his [Hockey's Arsenal newsletter](https://hockeysarsenal.substack.com/p/point-shots-suck) on 20 September 2020 and restated under his byline [at The Coaches Site](https://members.thecoachessite.com/article/the-problem-with-point-shots-according-to-analytics). **Rolling the zones together is fair here** — recovery is flat wherever the shot came from, and Revak says so in terms: *"Rebounds recovered by the offense around about 40%, despite where the shot that created the rebound started from."* **What the figure lacks is scope:** no league, no season and no sample size is stated for it — not on the chart, and not in either article, whose other dates belong to other studies. So unlike the neighbouring figures in this document, it is a direction rather than a number you can check.

### Goaltender interference — the actual standard

```facts
Rule: NHL and IIHF Rule 69.1 — an attacker's position, inside or outside the crease, does not by itself decide the goal; goals "should be disallowed only if" you impair the goalie's ability to move freely or defend it, by positioning or contact — a ceiling, not a direction to disallow
Rule: The sentence in NHL and IIHF 69.1 that actually disallows a goal is keyed to the crease — an attacker who "enters the goal crease" and impairs the goalkeeper's ability to defend it; 69.4, the outside-the-crease rule, has no positioning limb at all
Rule: NHL Rule 69.1 — a goal is disallowed where you initiate intentional or deliberate contact with the goalie, inside or outside the crease
Rule: NHL Rule 69.4 answers two questions from different clauses — on the goal, contact outside the crease that is more than incidental disallows it, and incidental contact does not
Rule: NHL 69.4's penalty permission is conditional — a goalie outside the crease is not "fair game" and unnecessary contact is penalised "in every case"; incidental contact is permitted only "when the goalkeeper is in the act of playing the puck outside his goal crease" and you tried to avoid it
Rule: Inside the crease (NHL 69.3), contact you initiate — incidental or otherwise — while the goalie is in the crease voids the goal, subject to the rebound and loose-puck exception at 69.7
Rule: NHL and IIHF 69.3's no-contact ground needs a significant position in the crease — a substantial part of your body there for more than an instantaneous period — that both obstructs the goalie's vision and impairs their ability to defend the goal
Rule: The IIHF Situation Handbook does not apply 69.3's test — asked about a player "in the crease screening the goalkeeper", its Situation 69.1 answers "the goal shall be disallowed". The Handbook ranks below the rulebook, but do not play the crease to that edge
Rule: NHL and IIHF 69.3 — after any contact by a goalkeeper establishing position in the crease, give ground at once or a goal is disallowed. The rule adds a penalty "whether or not a goal is scored", but both books' own tables assess none where the goal is scored
Rule: Keep your skates off the painted crease line — USA Hockey's Note to 625(b) counts "(including crease lines)" as crease and Hockey Canada's glossary agrees; the NHL's and IIHF's own Situation 5E allows a goal on the line, while IIHF Rule 1.7 says the line belongs to the crease
Never: Plant your feet in the blue paint. Screen from outside it instead, where a screen without contact is normally legal and worth doing constantly
```

This is the most misunderstood rule in the offensive zone, so here is what **NHL Rule 69** actually says.

**The governing premise (69.1):** an attacking player's *position*, whether inside or outside the crease, **should not by itself** determine whether a goal is allowed. The rule then names two things and says a goal *"should be disallowed **only if**"* one of them is present:

1. an attacking player, **by positioning or by contact, impairs the goalkeeper's ability to move freely within the crease or defend the goal**; or
2. an attacking player **initiates intentional or deliberate contact** with the goalkeeper, inside or outside the crease.

"Contact" means any contact between goalie and attacker, **by stick or by any part of the body**.

⚠️ **Read that "only if" the way it is written, because it is the sentence most often read backwards.** It sets a **ceiling** on when a goal may be taken off the board — one of those two things has to be true — rather than directing that a goal be taken off whenever positioning impairs the goalie. **The sentence in 69.1 that actually disallows a goal is keyed to the crease:** *"If an attacking player **enters the goal crease** and, by his actions, impairs the goalkeeper's ability to defend his goal, and a goal is scored, the goal will be disallowed."* **69.3's no-contact screen ground is keyed to the crease too** — a *"significant position within the goal crease"* — and **69.4, the outside-the-crease rule, has no positioning limb at all**, only contact. 🇬🇧 **The IIHF prints all three the same way**, at the same numbers. ⚠️ **That is a reading of the rule's structure rather than something either book states in terms**, and both add that the rule *"will be enforced exclusively in accordance with the on-ice judgement of the Referee(s)"* — so treat it as what the text supports, not as a guarantee about the call you will get. What it does mean is that both books' own reference tables are **applying** Rule 69 rather than contradicting it — **and those tables answer the screening case in terms.** **NHL Reference Table 14** and **IIHF 2025/26 Appendix IV Table 16**, at **Situations 5C and 5E**, put an attacker *"outside the crease"* who *"impairs the goalkeeper's ability to defend his goal"*, and one who *"plants himself on the crease line or outside the goal crease, as to obstruct the goalkeeper's vision and impair his ability to defend his goal, and a goal is scored"* — and both answer *"Goal is allowed."* (The IIHF renumbers that table to **Table 14** in its 2026/27 edition; the rows are identical.) ⚠️ **Do not carry that permission one step further in than it goes** — the rows either side of them, **5B and 5D**, are the same conduct inside the crease, and both answer *"Goal is disallowed."*

⚠️ **And none of it buys you the crease line.** Situation 5E allows the goal for an attacker planted *"on the crease line"*, while the IIHF's **Rule 1.7** has it that *"[t]he marked line belongs to the Goal Crease"* — the same play, answered in opposite directions inside one book, and the NHL's settles it neither way. ⚠️ **The other two books do settle it, and they settle it against you:** USA Hockey's Note to **Rule 625(b)** defines the goal crease area as the space outlined by the semi-circular crease lines ***"(including crease lines)"***, and Hockey Canada's **glossary** says *"The lines of the crease are considered part of the crease."* **So under those two books a skate on the red line is a skate in the crease, and 625(b)'s flat position bar is already running.** [Rink Map and Glossary](../foundation/rink_map_and_glossary.md) sets that collision out. **Screen with your skates clear of the paint and clear of the line.**

**Outside the crease (69.1, 69.4):** **Rule 69.4 asks two questions and answers them from different clauses, so keep them apart.** **On the goal**, contact that is more than incidental disallows it, and incidental contact does not. **On the penalty, the permission is conditional, and the condition is the half that gets dropped.** A goalie outside the crease is explicitly **not "fair game"**, and the appropriate penalty *"should be assessed in every case where an attacking player makes unnecessary contact with the goalkeeper."* Incidental contact is permitted only *"when the goalkeeper is in the act of playing the puck outside his goal crease"*, and then only if you made a reasonable effort to avoid it. ⚠️ **So a goaltender who has come out to challenge, or who is set outside the paint with the puck somewhere else, is not inside that permission at all** — bump him there and the penalty is the one the rule says should be assessed in every case. **How those two paragraphs interact where the contact was genuinely incidental but the goalkeeper was not playing the puck is stated in neither book, and is not resolved here.** See [Rules Primer](../foundation/rules_primer.md), which sets out all four books.

**Inside the crease (69.3):** this is much stricter. If you **initiate contact with a goalie who is in the crease — incidental or otherwise — and a goal is scored, the goal is disallowed.** Further, if you **establish a significant position within the crease so as to obstruct the goalie's vision and impair their ability to defend the goal**, the goal is disallowed even with no contact at all. ⚠️ **Both halves of that second ground are load-bearing, and the rule defines the first half rather than leaving it to the eye — drop either and it sounds a good deal stricter than it is.** The operative wording is *"establishes a significant position within the goal crease, so as to obstruct the goalkeeper's vision **and** impair his ability to defend his goal"* — the obstruction **and** the impairment, not the obstruction on its own. And a player does that *"when, in the Referee's judgment, his body, or a substantial portion thereof, is within the goal crease **for more than an instantaneous period of time.**"* So a body that crosses the paint and is gone has not met it, and neither has a body that is in there without impairing the goalie's ability to defend the goal.

⚠️ **Do not read that narrowing as room to work in, because the IIHF's own Situation Handbook does not apply it.** Asked what the referee decides where a *"Team A player is in the crease screening the goalkeeper, and Team A scores"*, the Handbook answers flatly — *"The goal shall be disallowed, and no additional penalty assessed"* — and cites *"Rule 69.1 and Rule 69.3"* while asking nothing about duration, nothing about the goalie's vision and nothing about their ability to defend (Situation 69.1, in the same words in the 2025/26 and 2026/27 editions). **The Handbook ranks below the rulebook and says so itself** — its Foreword: *"in case of any contradictions, the valid IIHF Rulebook is always the basis"* — so 69.3's conjunctive test is still the rule, and this is guidance reading it loosely rather than a rule contradicting it. **But the official in front of you may be working from that answer, and you will not get to argue the limbs from the ice.** Take the narrowing as an account of what the rule requires, not as space to play in: screen from outside the paint.

⚠️ **69.3 has two further paragraphs, and they run the other way — they are about contact the *goalkeeper* initiates, and the second attaches a penalty to you as well as voiding the goal.** If a goalkeeper, *"in the act of establishing his position within his goal crease"*, initiates contact with you while you are in the crease, and that *"results in an impairment of the goalkeeper's ability to defend his goal"*, a goal scored on the play is disallowed — even though the goalie made the contact, and even if you got out immediately. The IIHF's Situation Handbook answers exactly that play: the goal is disallowed *"even though the attacking player vacates the established position immediately, the contact impairs the goalkeeper's ability to defend its goal"* (Situation 69.6). **The paragraph after it puts an obligation on you by name:** *"If, after any contact by a goalkeeper who is attempting to establish position in his goal crease, the attacking player does not immediately vacate his current position in the goal crease (i.e. give ground to the goalkeeper), and a goal is scored, the goal will be disallowed. In all such cases, whether or not a goal is scored, the attacking player will receive a minor penalty for goalkeeper interference."* **🇬🇧 The IIHF prints that paragraph in the same terms**, with *"the appropriate penalty for 'Goalkeeper interference'"* where the NHL writes a minor — so internationally it is not stated as capped at two minutes.

⚠️ **On that penalty sentence, the rule text and the book's own reference table do not agree — and what turns on the disagreement is whether you go to the box.** 69.3 sends you to the table in terms, and **NHL Table 14** puts the identical facts — a goalkeeper initiating contact to establish position and the attacker who *"refuses to give ground at the time a goal is scored"* — at **Situation 6 D**, in the group headed *"6. CROWDING THE GOALKEEPER"*, and answers: *"Goal is disallowed. A minor penalty is not assessed (loss of goal only)."* ⚠️ **Carry the group number with the row letter, because the table is divided into numbered groups and each is lettered from A again.** There is a row **D** in the group headed *"5. SCREENING SITUATIONS"* as well — an attacking player who *"plants himself within the goal crease"* — and it is a different fact pattern with an answer that says nothing about a penalty either way. The IIHF's Appendix IV table gives the same answer in the same words at **Situation 6 D**, and its Situation Handbook is blunter still: *"No goal – no penalty. The attacking player must give ground to the goalkeeper to play its position within the goal crease"* (Situation 69.2). **Where the minor does land is the case with no goal in it.** Table 14's **Situation 6 B** — you do not vacate, and *"no goal is scored on the play"* — reads *"Minor penalty. This player runs the risk of 'bad things' happening by being in the crease"*, and the table qualifies even that: *"A possible penalty depends on the Referee's judgment as to the degree of contact and degree of resistance with the attacking player and whether the goalkeeper was truly trying to establish a position."* **How the flat sentence in 69.3 and Situation 6 D's "not assessed" are meant to be reconciled is stated in neither book, and is not resolved here.** What the two readings agree on is that standing your ground pays nothing: on one you lose the goal, on the other you take two minutes. **Give ground the moment a goalie leans into you.** ⚠️ **This is an NHL and IIHF provision only** — no give-ground obligation, and nothing about a goalkeeper establishing position, appears in either USA Hockey volume or anywhere in Hockey Canada's Rule 8.5 and its four Interpretations.

> **This is the practical line to remember: screening from outside the blue paint, without contact, is normally legal and you should do it constantly. Screening from inside the blue paint is not.** Every clause of Rule 69 that voids a goal for where you stood is keyed to the goal crease, and the outside-the-crease rule reaches contact only — ⚠️ **but the crease *line* is the unresolved edge, so do not screen from on it.** Some coaching resources say you may stand in the crease as long as you are "playing the puck" — that overstates it, and Rule 69.3 does void a goal for a **significant** position inside the crease — a substantial part of your body in there for more than an instantaneous period — that obstructs the goalie's vision **and** impairs their ability to defend. Keep your feet out of the paint — and if a goalkeeper leans into you in there to establish their own position, give ground at once, because 69.3 disallows a goal scored while you hold yours.

### Rebounds, loose pucks and being pushed into the goalie

```facts
Rule: NHL and IIHF Rule 69.7 — on a rebound, or in a loose-puck race you and the goalie are both making, incidental contact is permitted in or out of the crease and the goal is allowed — but not so far as pushing the goalie into the net with the puck; USA Hockey and Hockey Canada write no such carve-out
Rule: NHL and IIHF Rule 69.7 disallow the goal where a goalkeeper is pushed into the net together with the puck by an attacking player after making a stop, and appropriate penalties may follow — the rule needs all three of those conditions, not any push at all
Rule: NHL and IIHF Rule 69.7 — where the referee judges that a defending player pushed or fouled the attacker into causing the goalkeeper to be pushed into the net together with the puck, the goal "can be permitted", not must, and 69.7 sets no condition on it — the reasonable-effort test is 69.1's
Rule: NHL and IIHF Rule 69.7 — where the puck is under a player in or around the crease, deliberately or otherwise, no goal can be scored by pushing that player and the puck into the net, and appropriate penalties follow if applicable
Rule: A defending player other than the goalkeeper who deliberately covers the puck in the crease gives up a penalty shot (NHL and IIHF Rule 63.6, cross-referenced at 67.4 and 69.7) — a goaltender covering it in there is simply a whistle
Rule: NHL Rule 69.1 — a defender who pushes, shoves or fouls you into the goalie is not deemed contact initiated by you, provided you made a reasonable effort to avoid it; but pushing a defender into their own goalie is, and a goal scored off it is disallowed
Rule: NHL Rule 69.2 — intentional or deliberate contact with a goalie draws a penalty whether or not a goal is scored
Rule: USA Hockey 607(d) Note 1 splits contact with a goaltender by what you did — accidental or unavoidable is interference, a 625(a)(8) minor; any deliberate body contact or check is charging, never a bare minor: 607(c) is "a minor plus a misconduct or a major plus a game misconduct", 607(e) a match
Rule: USA Hockey Casebook 607 Situation 4, a three-rung charging ladder: no penalty only where a defender pushed you in AND you "clearly made every attempt to avoid the contact"; minor plus misconduct for an honest attempt alone; major plus game misconduct or match if you accelerate through
Never: Drive the goaltender backwards in a scramble. Rule 69.7 permits incidental contact only, and shoving them into the net with the puck loses the goal you just scored
Action: Play the puck, not the goaltender, when you both arrive at a loose one — what the rule forgives is incidental contact in a race you are both making for the same puck
```

**Three more provisions worth knowing**, and the first is the one net-front players lean on hardest:

- **Rebounds and loose pucks (69.7):** in a rebound situation, or where you and the goalie are simultaneously going for a loose puck, **incidental contact is permitted inside or outside the crease and the goal is allowed.** The words are worth having: *"In a rebound situation, or where a goalkeeper and attacking player(s) are simultaneously attempting to play a loose puck, whether inside or outside the crease, incidental contact with the goalkeeper will be permitted, and any goal that is scored as a result thereof will be allowed."* This is the exception that lets net-front players do their job in a scramble — **incidental contact, in a race you are both making for the same loose puck.** ⚠️ **That carve-out is NHL and IIHF only** — neither USA Hockey nor Hockey Canada writes one, and Hockey Canada's Interpretation 1 to 8.5(a) disallows the goal for any contact you initiate, *"regardless of whether the contact occurs inside or outside of the goal crease."*

  ⚠️ **Rule 69.7 runs to four limbs and only the first of them is a permission. Do not stop reading at the end of it.** The paragraph immediately after takes the goal off the board: *"In the event that a goalkeeper has been pushed into the net together with the puck by an attacking player after making a stop, the goal will be disallowed. If applicable, appropriate penalties will be assessed."* **All three conditions in that sentence are load-bearing** — pushed **by an attacking player**, **together with the puck**, **after making a stop** — so this is not a rule against every contact that moves a goaltender. It is the rule against scoring by driving a goaltender, and the puck they have just stopped, over their own goal line — and the penalty comes on top of the lost goal. **Play the puck, not the goaltender:** the permission covers contact you could not avoid while doing that, and nothing else.

  ⚠️ **Rule 69.7 does not run automatically against you, and the relief sits in the same paragraph as that disallowed goal — never carry the limit without it:** *"If, however, in the opinion of the Referee, the attacking player was pushed or otherwise fouled by a defending player causing the goalkeeper to be pushed into the net together with the puck, the goal can be permitted."* A defending player shoving you into their own goaltender need not cost your team the goal — but read the verb: the goal *"can be permitted"*, on the referee's opinion, not *shall be allowed*. ⚠️ **And read what 69.7 does not say: it puts no condition on that relief at all.** The *"provided the attacking player has made a reasonable effort to avoid such contact"* test is **69.1's**, and 69.1 uses it for a different question — whether a defender's shove makes the resulting contact yours. **On that question the effort decides the play outright**, and both books' reference tables say so in a group written for it: **NHL Table 14**, group **3** — *"A PLAYER PUSHES, SHOVES, OR FOULS ANOTHER PLAYER INTO THE GOALKEEPER, WHO IS IN OR OUT OF THE CREASE"* — allows the goal at **3 A** where the attacker acted *"after having made a reasonable effort to avoid contact"*, and disallows it at **3 C** where the contact was other than incidental and the attacker *"did not make a reasonable effort to avoid such contact"*; the IIHF prints the same group at **Table 16, Situation 3** in the 2025/26 book and **Table 14, Situation 3** in the 2026/27 one. **But neither table has a row for the goalkeeper pushed into the net with the puck, and neither book says whether 69.1's effort test feeds the referee's discretion under 69.7. That is not resolved here.** Make the effort regardless: it is what every neighbouring clause turns on.

  **A third paragraph covers the version where the puck is underneath somebody.** Where the puck is under a player in or around the crease area, *"(deliberately or otherwise)"*, *"a goal cannot be scored by pushing this player together with the puck into the goal"*, and *"[i]f applicable, the appropriate penalties will be assessed, including a penalty shot if deemed to be covered in the crease deliberately."* So digging at a covered puck is fair, and shovelling the pile into the net is not a goal. **That penalty shot runs your way, but only in one case:** **Rule 63.6**, which 69.7 points to, awards it where a defending player **other than the goalkeeper** falls on, holds, picks up or gathers the puck in the crease (**NHL 67.4**, which the IIHF prints at its own 67.4 as a pointer back to 63.6). A goaltender covering the puck in there is simply a whistle.

  **🇬🇧 The IIHF prints all four limbs at the same number**, in the 2025/26 (v1.1) and 2026/27 editions alike, differing in small things — capitalising *Player*, writing *"goal crease"* where the NHL writes *"crease"*, putting quotation marks of its own around *"incidental contact"*, dropping the *"that"* from *"In the event that a goalkeeper"*, capitalising *"Penalty Shot"*, and setting all four limbs off as separate paragraphs where the NHL prints three and keeps the relief inside the second. Both books also list the play among the goals that must be disallowed, at **NHL 78.5(ix)** and **IIHF 78.5(IX)** — but read those as cross-references rather than as the rule, because each is worded more loosely than 69.7 itself: both drop *"by an attacking player"* and write *"after making a save"* where 69.7 writes *"after making a stop"*. Take the conditions from 69.7.
- **Pushed into the goalie (69.1):** if a **defender** pushes, shoves or fouls you into the goalie, that is **not treated as contact initiated by you** — provided you made a reasonable effort to avoid it. Conversely, if **you** push a defender into their own goalie, that **counts as contact initiated by you** and the goal is disallowed.
- **Penalty (69.2):** intentional or deliberate contact with a goalie draws a penalty **whether or not a goal is scored**.
- ⚠️ **Under USA Hockey this play is not capped at two minutes, and which rule you are under is decided by what you did.** **Rule 607(d) Note 1** draws the line: *"any accidental or unavoidable contact that occurs with the goalkeeper shall be penalized under the Interference rule. Any deliberate body contact or check that is delivered to the goalkeeper shall be penalized as charging."* Interference is 625(a)(8)'s minor. **Charging is never a bare minor in that book:** **607(c)** assesses *"[a] minor plus a misconduct or a major plus a game misconduct penalty ... to a player who body checks or charges a goalkeeper while the goalkeeper is within the goal crease or privileged area"*, and **607(e)** adds that *"[a] match penalty for reckless endangerment may also be assessed for charging."* ⚠️ **The privileged area is not the blue paint, and it is where this section has told you to work.** **Rule 607(d) Note 2** draws it *"by connecting the end zone face-off spots with an imaginary line and imaginary lines from each face-off spot running perpendicular to the end boards"*, and the *Casebook* at **Rule 607, Situation 6** confirms it *"extends to the closer end boards"* — so it takes in the net front, the goal line and the ice behind the net. ⚠️ **And the Casebook decides this exact play in both directions, on one thing.** **Rule 607, Situation 4:** where *"a defending player has forced the attacking player into their own goalkeeper, no penalty should be assessed provided the attacking player has clearly made every attempt to avoid the contact with the goalkeeper"* — but *"if the player makes no attempt to avoid the goalkeeper and recklessly endangers the goalkeeper by accelerating through the contact, or by extending the arms or dropping the shoulder to deliver the hit, the major plus game misconduct, or match penalty option would be the proper call."* **Both halves of that relief travel together — the defender's push *and* your effort.** ⚠️ **And there is a rung between the two just quoted, reached by effort alone:** the same Situation puts an attacker *"going hard to the goal"* who *"makes an honest attempt to avoid the goalkeeper once it is realized contact is unavoidable"* at **a minor plus misconduct**. **So an honest effort is not a complete defence** — the relief that costs nothing is the one with a defender's foul behind it. ⚠️ **Read the question that Casebook Situation 4 answers, because it fixes the scope of the whole ladder:** it asks what decides *"whether to assess a minor plus misconduct, major plus game misconduct or match penalty for charging a goalkeeper while in their crease or privileged area"* — **so it is the ladder inside charging, and USA Hockey 607(d) Note 1 keeps accidental or unavoidable contact out of it altogether, in the interference rule instead.** [Rules Primer](../foundation/rules_primer.md) sets the privileged area out in full.

### The same play under the other three books

```facts
Rule: USA Hockey 625(b) flatly bars the goal with an attacker in the crease whoever scored, unless the puck got there first, the goalkeeper is out of the crease area, or a defender put them there — none of which licenses interference: a minor under 625(a)(8), and the goal off (Casebook Situation 9)
Rule: USA Hockey 625(b): with the puck in the attacking zone, their team in possession and the goalkeeper in contact with the crease, an attacker standing, holding their stick or skating through the crease is a neutral-zone faceoff; Casebook Situation 10 spares a momentary stick the whistle, not the goal
Rule: Hockey Canada 8.5 is not flat — where a teammate outside the crease scores, the goal stands "provided that the player who was in the goal crease does not attempt to play the puck, interfere with the play or obstruct the Goaltender's view or movements"
Rule: Hockey Canada has an impairment ground too, and it names vision — Interpretation 3 to 8.5(a) disallows the goal where a player standing in the crease impedes "the goaltender's vision or movements"; it answers to NHL 69.3, not to 69.1
Rule: Hockey Canada 8.5 puts the onus in the rule — attackers "who do not make an effort to avoid the goaltender must be penalized" — but keeps the questions apart: a referee may disallow a goal "without assessing an attacking player a penalty"
Rule: Hockey Canada's Interpretation 2 to 8.5(a) makes the penalty on an attacker fouled into the goaltender mandatory — "must be penalized" — where you make no effort to avoid the contact; USA Hockey asks the same effort in its Casebook, at Rule 607 Situation 4, not in its rules
Rule: Interpretation 2 also directs the referee to penalise the defender who fouled you; Hockey Canada's Interpretation 1 to 8.5(a) is no relief from that minor — it excepts the goal, and only for an attacker whom the foul left unable to avoid the contact
Rule: On a screen from outside the crease the books split — the IIHF prints the NHL's Rule 69, so a British reader is under it; USA Hockey writes no goaltender-vision provision in its rules or its Casebook, and Hockey Canada's two references reach only a player in the crease
Rule: Hockey Canada is not capped at a minor, nor is USA Hockey (607(c) and 607(e)) — 8.5(b) reaches a major plus game misconduct on degree of violence with nobody hurt, and mandatorily for charging the goaltender and for any such foul that injures; 8.5(c) is a match penalty, and 8.5(d) bars a misconduct
Rule: CARHA Rule 66(b) — CARHA-affiliated leagues only — is stricter still: no standing in the goal crease "unless the puck is in the goal crease area", the goal off if you do, and no incidental-contact allowance. Its push-in relief saves position only — contact impeding the goalkeeper is still a minor
Never: Plant your feet in the blue paint — a foot in there risks the goal under all four books, and the zone too under USA Hockey 625(b), a shall on three conditions, or IIHF 69.1, a referee's discretion
```

> **Rule-set note — USA Hockey is materially stricter, and it changes how you play the net front.** Everything above is the NHL standard. **USA Hockey Rule 625(b)** does not run the NHL's "position alone is not determinative" test at all. It reads: *"No goal may be scored with an attacking player in the goal crease unless the puck has preceded the player(s) into the goal crease or the goalkeeper is out of the goal crease area."* That is a flat position rule — no contact and no impairment required — and **that second exception runs out the moment you are what stops the goalkeeper getting back to the crease.** The same clause also stops play before you get that far: *"A face-off shall be conducted at the nearest neutral zone face-off spot any time an attacking player stands, holds their stick, or skates through the goal crease provided the puck is in the attacking zone, the attacking team has possession of the puck and the goalkeeper is in contact with the crease."* So under USA Hockey rules a foot in the paint does not just risk the goal — it costs you the zone. **Read the three provisos as limits, not decoration** — no stoppage unless the puck is in the attacking zone, your team has it, and the goalkeeper is touching the crease — and note that the book's own *Casebook* declines to make the trigger a toe over the line. **Rule 625, Situation 10** asks whether the referee must stop play *"every time an attacking player places their stick and/or skates in the opposing team's goal crease"* and answers ***"No"***: *"Play should not be stopped because a player momentarily places their stick and/or skates in the goal crease, unless they interfere with the goalkeeper in any manner."* ⚠️ **Take the rest of that Situation with it, because its second half runs the other way and is stricter than the printed rule:** where a goal is scored while an attacking player has their stick and/or skates in the crease and the goalkeeper is in it, the Referee *"shall have no alternative but to disallow the goal."* **So a stick through the paint will not stop play, and will still take the goal off the board.** The crease for this purpose includes the crease lines themselves and the space above, up to the height of the goal frame. The one relief the printed rule writes: if a **defender** physically interferes with you and that is what puts you in the crease, play continues and a legal goal counts.
>
> ⚠️ **And do not stop reading 625(b) at *"the goalkeeper is out of the goal crease area."*** That is the clause a net-front forward hears as a licence — the goalie has come out, so get to the paint ahead of the puck — and the *Casebook* answers exactly that play at **Rule 625, Situation 9**. The goal counts: *"If the goalkeeper is completely out of their crease, an attacking player may precede the puck into the crease and any legally scored goal in this situation shall be allowed."* **And then the same situation takes it back if you are what stopped them getting home:** *"However, if the goalkeeper is attempting to return to the goal crease and there is any interference by the attacking player who prevents the goalkeeper from making a play, then the goal must be disallowed and an interference penalty shall be assessed."* So an empty crease is open only while the goaltender is genuinely out of the play. Get there first if you can — but do not be the body between a scrambling goaltender and their net, because that costs the goal **and** two minutes.
>
> **The IIHF standard is the NHL's, with one addition.** **IIHF Rule 69** carries the same number and substantially the same text as the NHL's: the same "position alone should not by itself determine whether a goal is allowed" premise, the same two disallowing conditions, the same incidental-contact allowance outside the crease, the same "significant position within the goal crease" test in 69.3 (body or a substantial part of it in the crease for "more than an instantaneous period of time"), and the same rebound exception in 69.7. Two differences worth knowing. The IIHF text says *"relevant contact"* where the NHL says *"contact"*, and tells the referee to weigh *"the degree and nature of the relevant contact with the goalkeeper"* over the goalie's exact location. And IIHF Rule 69.1 adds a provision the NHL does not have: *"If an attacking Player is in the goal crease and does not leave immediately, the Referee has the possibility to stop the game and the subsequent 'face-off' shall take place at the nearest neutral zone face-off spot outside the attacking zone of the offending team."* So internationally, as under USA Hockey, loitering in the paint can cost you the zone without anyone needing to score first.

> **Hockey Canada is the fourth answer, and it does not run 69.1's premise either.** Goaltender interference is **Rule 8.5**, filed under *Restraining Fouls* rather than in a rule of its own, and it opens with a position bar that reads like USA Hockey's — **but, unlike USA Hockey's, it is not a flat one, because the same paragraph carves the case straight back out again**: *"Unless the puck is in the goal crease area, a player of the attacking team may not stand in the goal crease. If the puck should enter the net while such conditions prevail, the goal will NOT BE ALLOWED. However, if an attacking player is in the goal crease but does not interfere with the Goaltender and another attacking player (who is outside the goal crease) scores, the goal WILL BE ALLOWED provided that the player who was in the goal crease does not attempt to play the puck, interfere with the play or obstruct the Goaltender's view or movements."* **USA Hockey's 625(b) has no equivalent of that "However" sentence** — its only relief is for a player a defender pushed in — and that carve-out is the whole of the difference between the two books at the net front. And the book states an onus none of the other three does: *"The onus is always on the attacking player and players who do not make an effort to avoid the goaltender must be penalized."*
>
> **That carve-out is not a technicality — it is a situation you will meet, and the two books answer it in opposite directions.** A body in the blue paint and a teammate scoring from outside it: **allowed** under Hockey Canada, provided the player in the crease stays passive and clear of the goaltender's view and movements; **disallowed** under USA Hockey, where it makes no difference who scored. If you play under both books in a season, that is the one situation to know cold.
>
> **And Hockey Canada does have an impairment test — it names vision, and it is confined to the crease.** Interpretation 3 to Rule 8.5(a): an attacker is *"NOT committing a foul by simply standing in the goal crease. However, if while standing in the crease, the attacking player attempts to play the puck, interfere with the play, or impede the goaltender's vision or movements, then no goal may be scored."* That answers to **NHL 69.3**, which is the rule that names vision — 69.1 does not — and what Hockey Canada writes no counterpart to is 69.1's reach to positioning **outside** the crease. Hockey Canada also keeps the goal and the penalty apart, and says so in terms: it would be *"reasonable for a Referee to judge that a situation may warrant disallowing a goal under this rule without assessing an attacking player a penalty."* So there you can cost your team the goal without going to the box.
>
> **On being put into the goalie, Hockey Canada is the strictest of the four, and the only one that makes a penalty on you mandatory.** Interpretation 2 to Rule 8.5(a): where an attacker is *"tripped, hooked, cross-checked, or otherwise interfered with, falls, and makes contact with the goaltender, there must be an effort by the attacking player to avoid making contact with the goaltender. If the player does not make an effort to avoid contact with the goaltender, then they must be penalized for interference with the goaltender"* — and *"The referee should also penalize the defending player who committed the initial foul under the appropriate rule."* **Interpretation 1 is no relief from that minor — it governs the *goal*.** Its exception is the narrowest of the four, and it excepts the goal only for an attacker *"fouled by a defending player and, as a result, is unable to avoid contact with the goaltender."* So the two Interpretations answer different questions: 2 decides whether you are penalised, and asks only that you made an **effort**; 1 decides whether the goal counts, and asks that the foul left you **unable** to avoid the contact. Compare USA Hockey, whose relief is the **broadest** — *"play shall not be stopped and any legal goal scored shall be allowed"*, no effort proviso **in the printed rule** — but reaches your **position** only, since **617(c)(4)** disallows the goal for an attacker *"positioned in the crease"* **or** *"interfering with the goalkeeper"* and the push-in parenthetical sits in the first limb alone, while **625(a)(8)** stays a minor for *"physical contact, using their stick or body, in a manner that interferes with the movement of the goalkeeper, unless otherwise specified in the rules."* ⚠️ **Do not read that minor as USA Hockey's ceiling, though, because the book routes the deliberate half of this contact to a different rule altogether.** **Rule 607(d) Note 1:** *"any accidental or unavoidable contact that occurs with the goalkeeper shall be penalized under the Interference rule. Any deliberate body contact or check that is delivered to the goalkeeper shall be penalized as charging."* **And charging is never a bare minor there** — **607(c)** assesses *"[a] minor plus a misconduct or a major plus a game misconduct penalty ... to a player who body checks or charges a goalkeeper while the goalkeeper is within the goal crease or privileged area"*, and **607(e)** that *"[a] match penalty for reckless endangerment may also be assessed for charging."* **That area is far larger than the crease:** **Rule 607(d) Note 2** runs it between the end-zone face-off spots and *"perpendicular to the end boards"*, and the *Casebook* at **Rule 607, Situation 6** confirms it *"extends to the closer end boards"* — see [Rules Primer](../foundation/rules_primer.md). ⚠️ **And USA Hockey does ask you for the effort — it writes the test in its *Casebook* rather than in its rules, so a reader of the playing rules alone will not meet it.** Rule 607, **Situation 4**: where *"a defending player has forced the attacking player into their own goalkeeper, no penalty should be assessed provided the attacking player has clearly made every attempt to avoid the contact with the goalkeeper"* — and *"if the player makes no attempt to avoid the goalkeeper and recklessly endangers the goalkeeper by accelerating through the contact, or by extending the arms or dropping the shoulder to deliver the hit"*, the major plus game misconduct or match penalty *"would be the proper call."* ⚠️ **And a rung sits between those two: an attacker *"going hard to the goal"* who makes *"an honest attempt to avoid the goalkeeper once it is realized contact is unavoidable"* takes a minor plus misconduct — so effort alone does not reach the no-penalty rung, which needs the defender's foul as well.** **So all four books ask for the effort; what differs is where each writes it and what making it buys you** — the goal, the penalty, or both.
>
> **⚠️ And Hockey Canada does not cap this rule at two minutes — everything above decides the goal, and 8.5's lettered clauses decide whether you are still in the game.** **8.5(a)** is the minor. **8.5(b)** reaches *"A Major penalty and Game Misconduct penalty"* at the referee's discretion *"based on the degree of violence of impact"* — and **mandatorily**, *"will be assessed"*, in two named cases: *"to any player who **charges the goaltender**"* (its Note 1 refers the definition of charging to Rule 7.4(b) Interpretation 2), and *"to any player who **injures an opponent** by an Interference with the Goaltender infraction that would otherwise call for a Minor penalty."* **8.5(c)** is a **match penalty** *"to any player who attempts to injure or deliberately injures a goaltender by Interference."* **8.5(e)** makes the game misconduct automatic on any 8.5(b) major, and **8.5(d)** rules a misconduct out entirely, so there is no ten-minute step between the two minutes and the ejection. **The practical consequence for a net-front forward: arriving into the goaltender is an ejection under this book, and a foul that would have been two minutes becomes one the moment it injures someone.** *(Those tiers are Hockey Canada's. USA Hockey's are set out above, at 607(c) and 607(e); the NHL's and the IIHF's are not stated here.)*
>
> **⚠️ One divergence runs the opposite way to all of the above, and it is the one this section's screening advice rests on.** The only clause in any of the four that reaches an attacker's positioning **outside** the crease is NHL and IIHF Rule 69.1's first limb — and, as set out above, it sits under a *"should be disallowed only if"* ceiling, with both books' own tables answering that screen *"Goal is allowed"* at Situations 5C and 5E. USA Hockey's playing rules contain **no provision about screening a goaltender or obstructing a goaltender's vision at all.** Hockey Canada mentions the goaltender's view in exactly two places, the crease paragraph of 8.5 and Interpretation 3, and **both reach only a player who is in the crease**; Interpretation 3 says an attacker is *"NOT committing a foul by simply standing in the goal crease"*, with *"No penalty ... assessed unless the attacking player's body or stick makes actual physical contact with the goaltender."* So outside the paint no book of the four gives a goaltender a screening remedy, and those two do not even write a clause to argue from — while inside it they are far stricter than the NHL. **Whichever book you are under, the instruction is the same: screen from outside the blue.**
>
> **The practical consequence is the same instruction under all four, only harder as you go: stay out of the blue paint.** A foot in there risks the goal under all four books, and under USA Hockey rules it costs you the zone as well, and under the IIHF's it can — 69.1 lets the referee stop play where you are in the crease and *"do not leave immediately"*, before anyone has shot.
>
> Rec and beer leagues are often said to impose a stricter house rule still — commonly "no contact with the goaltender at all, ever, and any goal with a player in the crease is waved off." **How many do is a question about leagues rather than about books, and this document has no count for it.** What is checkable is that one rec book writes exactly that: the **CARHA Hockey Official Rule Book** puts it in **Rule 66(b)**: *"Unless the puck is in the goal crease area, a player of the attacking side may not stand in the goal crease. If the puck should enter the net while such conditions prevail, the goal shall not be allowed."* Same clause makes any physical contact that impedes the goalkeeper's movement a minor, with no incidental-contact allowance of the kind the NHL and IIHF give you. The one relief reads like USA Hockey's: if a defender physically interfered with you *"so as to cause them to be in the goal crease"*, a goal scored while you are still in there *"shall be allowed"*. ⚠️ **But the same clause then writes a separate minor for the very player that relief describes:** a Minor goes to an attacking player whose team is in possession who *"is interfered with by a defending player, but fails to attempt to avoid making contact with the goalkeeper"*, and *"In addition, if a goal is scored it shall be disallowed."* **CARHA prints the two as unconnected paragraphs and never says which governs where both fit** — but that minor stands on its own words either way, so the instruction is the same: make the effort to avoid the goaltender, or lose the goal and take two minutes as well. **That is not a CARHA peculiarity.** Being shoved in saves your **position** under USA Hockey too, and not goalkeeper interference: USA Hockey's **617(c)(4)** disallows the goal for an attacker positioned in the crease **or** interfering with the goalkeeper, and the push-in relief sits in the first limb alone. ⚠️ **Read that as one rec book and not as the rec position — CARHA governs CARHA-affiliated leagues only and is not a general rec-hockey standard. Read your own league's rule before you plant yourself in the goalmouth.**

## 7. Working the Half-Wall

The **half-wall** is the boards area level with the faceoff dot, between the corner and the point. It is the winger's home base in the offensive zone (see [Winger](../positions/winger.md)) and the most common place from which offensive-zone possessions are organised.

It is a good place to hold the puck because you have the boards protecting one side, you can see the whole zone, and you are equidistant from four different plays.

### The four options from the half-wall

```facts
Action: Know all four before the puck arrives, and know which one is on
Options: Low — to the corner or behind the net; the safe continuation that feeds the cycle and resets the defence's problem
Options: High — to the point; the low-to-high shot generator, best when the defence has collapsed and left the point unpressured
Options: Middle — the seam pass into the slot or the backdoor; highest value, highest risk, shortest window
Options: Yourself — drive the net or cut to the middle, best when your check is flat-footed or has turned their hips
Never: Arrive on the half-wall with only one option in mind — you will play it regardless of whether the slot was open
```

Know all four before the puck arrives, and know which is on:

1. **Low — to the corner or behind the net.** The safe continuation. Feeds the cycle, keeps possession, resets the defence's problem.
2. **High — to the point.** The low-to-high shot generator. Best when the defence has collapsed low and left the point unpressured.
3. **Middle — the seam pass into the slot or the backdoor.** The royal road pass. Highest value, highest risk, shortest window.
4. **Yourself — drive the net or cut to the middle.** Attack the inside off the wall. Best when your check is flat-footed, has turned their hips, or has over-committed to taking away a pass.

The failure is having only one of these in mind. A player who arrives on the half-wall intending to pass to the point will do that regardless of whether the slot was open.

### Buying time

```facts
Technique: Cut back — skate a few feet toward the corner and cut back up the wall; the defender's momentum has to reverse, yours does not
Technique: Delay and curl — turn away from pressure, protect the puck and curl toward the middle into open ice, coming out facing the ice
Technique: Receive with your body between checker and puck, then spin off — turning into pressure immediately is how you get pinned
Never: Put your back to the boards, or duck. Get your skates parallel to the wall instead, forearm and hip to the wall, head up and chin off your chest; this outranks everything else here
Options: Bump it to the level-support player and take an immediate return pass — it resets the defender's read entirely
Action: Feet moving, always — a stationary player on the wall gets pinned; one with momentum can go three directions
```

You often need two more seconds than you have. Ways to manufacture them:

- **Cut back.** Skate a few feet toward the corner and cut back up the wall. The defender's momentum has to be reversed; yours does not.
- **Delay and curl.** Turn away from the pressure, protect the puck, and curl toward the middle — into open ice, never with your back against the wall; the safety qualifier two bullets down governs this one too. Coming out of the curl you will be facing the ice with your options refreshed.
- **Don't turn into pressure first.** When you receive the puck under pressure, take it with your body between checker and puck, then spin off. Turning into pressure immediately is how you get pinned. **Safety qualifier that outranks all of this:** the orientation to aim for at the wall is skates parallel to the boards, forearm and hip to the wall, head up and chin off your chest — never your back to the boards, never duck. Chest-square to the wall is the fallback, not the target. See [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md).
- **Bump it and get it back.** A short pass to the level-support player and an immediate return pass re-sets the defender's read entirely, and often you get the puck back in more space than you gave it away in.
- **Feet moving, always.** A stationary player on the wall gets pinned. A player with momentum can go three directions.

## 8. Behind the Net as an Attacking Position

Behind the net is not where the puck goes to die. It is one of the best attacking positions on the ice, and most amateur teams treat it as a place you end up rather than a place you go on purpose.

### Why it is so good

```facts
Key: From behind the goal line you look up at the whole zone with all five defenders and the goalie in front of you
Key: Nobody can come at you from directly behind, because the net and the end boards are there — that is geometry, not immunity
Risk: You can still be driven into the goal frame behind the net. Keep your head up and know who is arriving
Rule: Checking from behind is a major "anywhere on the playing surface" (NHL Rule 43.3), with no minor written for it (43.2) and a mandatory game misconduct (43.5); USA Hockey 608(b) puts a major plus game misconduct on one that sends an opponent head first into the goal frame
Read: Every time the puck crosses behind the net the goalie must pick a post and rotate — every rotation is a moment they are not square
Key: Passes from below the goal line finished at 6.73%, second only to royal road passes — volunteer-tracked Passing Project data, 51,308 shots at 5v5, all attempts
```

- **You can see everything and nobody can see you the same way.** From behind the goal line you are looking *up* at the whole zone with all five defenders and the goalie in front of you. You have the best view on the ice and the defence has to defend with their backs to their own net.
- **Nobody can come at you from directly behind** — there is nothing there but the net and the end boards, so a defender can only pressure you from one of two sides and you can see them coming. **That is geometry, not immunity.** Checking from behind is a **major** *"anywhere on the playing surface"* under **NHL Rule 43.3** — 43.2 writes no minor for it at all, and 43.5 makes the game misconduct mandatory — and **USA Hockey 608(b)** puts a major plus a game misconduct on a check from behind that sends an opponent head first into the **goal frame**, which it names alongside the boards, precisely because players get driven into it back here. Keep your head up and know who is arriving.
- **The goalie has to turn their head.** Every time the puck crosses behind the net, the goalie must decide which post to seal and rotate to track you. Every rotation is a moment when they are not square to a shot.
- **Passes out to the slot are exceptionally dangerous.** Passes originating below the goal line finished at **6.73%** — the second-most dangerous pass type measured, behind only royal road passes (Alan Wells' analysis of **Ryan Stimson's** Passing Project data, [via NHL.com](https://www.nhl.com/kraken/news/analytics-with-alison-dangerous-passes-327627898); **volunteer-tracked, 51,308 shots at 5v5, counting all attempts rather than shots on goal**).

### What to do from there

```facts
Action: Pass out to the slot — the primary play, not the last resort; look for the high-slot forward or the backdoor player
Options: The wrap-around — carry round the post and stuff it far side before the goalie seals; best as a threat, because sealing hard opens the slot pass
Options: The walkout — come out into the slot yourself on your forehand, crossing the royal road with the puck
Options: The bank off the goalie — from a tight angle behind the goal line, off the back of their pads into the net; legal, and occasionally free
Options: Reverse or rim to restart the cycle if nothing is on — possession retained is a perfectly good outcome
Rule: The goalkeeper's restricted area restricts only the goaltender (geometry NHL Rule 1.8, penalty 27.8 / 63.2(viii)) — skaters may go anywhere behind the net at any time
Read: USA Hockey and Hockey Canada write no trapezoid at all, so under those books their goalie may retrieve a dumped puck anywhere behind the net — it changes the calculus of dumping it in
Rule: No trapezoid is not no restriction on their goalie — USA Hockey Rule 614(c) still makes it a minor to freeze the puck behind the goal line, and Hockey Canada's Interpretation 3 to Rule 10.1(a), clause v, gives a minor with "No warning" for freezing it outside the crease
Rule: Both books write a clause for your dump-in exactly — Hockey Canada's clause iv for a goalie who comes out "to beat an attacking player to the puck" and jumps on it, no warning, and USA Hockey's Casebook Situation 6, which answers the identical facts a minor
Read: Those are retrieval clauses, not save clauses — a cover taken after stopping a shot is a different case in both books, and Goaltender owns it
Read: In England and Wales the restricted area's status is unsettled (Rules & Regulations 22.3) — plan as though their goalie may retrieve a dumped puck
```

- **Pass out to the slot.** This is the primary play, not the last resort. Look for the high-slot forward or the backdoor player. Because the pass comes from below the goal line, it crosses the royal road and arrives at a player already inside the home-plate area — both levers at once: it moves the goalie and it puts the puck in a dangerous place.
- **The wrap-around.** Carry the puck around the post and try to stuff it in on the far side before the goalie can push across and seal. It works when the goalie has committed to the near post, and it is at its best as a *threat*: a goalie who fears the wrap seals hard to the post, which opens the pass to the slot.
- **The walkout.** Come out from behind the net into the slot yourself, on your forehand, with the puck. You cross the royal road carrying the puck and you arrive in the most dangerous area on the ice.
- **The bank off the goalie.** Legal, and occasionally free. From a tight angle behind the goal line you can bank the puck off the back of the goalie's pads into the net.
- **Reverse or rim to restart the cycle.** If nothing is on, feed it back out to the corner you came from or rim it to the far corner and rotate. Possession retained is a perfectly good outcome.

![](diagram:oz-behind-the-net)

> **Rules note — the trapezoid does not apply to you.** The **goalkeeper's restricted area** (the trapezoid behind the net — geometry from NHL **Rule 1.8**, penalty from **Rules 27.8 / 63.2(viii)**; never take dimensions from 27.8, whose "six feet from either goal post" is stale pre-2014 wording) restricts only the **goaltender's** ability to play the puck behind the goal line. Skaters may go anywhere behind the net at any time. Also note that the trapezoid exists in NHL and IIHF play — and reportedly the KHL, though no KHL rulebook was available to check that one — but **not under USA Hockey, Hockey Canada or CARHA**. The words *trapezoid* and *restricted area* appear nowhere in any of those three books, nor in USA Hockey’s separate *Casebook*; *designated area* appears once, in Hockey Canada’s **Rule 5.4(b)** — *"Each Goal Judge will be stationed in the designated area behind each goal"* — which is about where the **Goal Judge** stands and has nothing to do with the goaltender. That changes the calculus of dumping the puck in, because under those books their goalie may retrieve it anywhere behind the net. How much rec, beer-league and youth hockey that adds up to is a question about leagues rather than about books, and this document has no count for it. **And no trapezoid is not no restriction on their goalie** — it frees them to fetch the puck out there, not to sit on it. USA Hockey **Rule 614(c)** makes it a delay-of-game minor for a goalkeeper to fall on or gather the puck causing a stoppage where *"(1) The puck is behind the goal line and their body is entirely outside of the goal crease"*, and Hockey Canada's **Interpretation 3 to Rule 10.1(a)**, clause v, reads *"When a goaltender leaves their crease, they will not be allowed to freeze the puck. If they do, they will be assessed a Minor penalty. No warning will be issued."* ⚠️ **Both books also write a clause for the exact play you just made, and both run your way.** Hockey Canada's **clause iv** of the same Interpretation reaches a goaltender who comes out *"to beat an attacking player to the puck and simply jumps on the puck, causing a stoppage of play"*, with *"[n]o warning… in this instance"*. USA Hockey's *Casebook* answers the identical facts at **Rule 614(c), Situation 6** — a goalkeeper rushing a loose puck inside their own privileged area to beat a lone attacker to it — with **Yes**, a minor, because *"[t]he goalkeeper has an obligation to play the puck with their stick, just like any other player."* ⚠️ **Do not stretch that to a goaltender who came out to make a *save*.** A cover taken after stopping a shot is a different case in both books and the exceptions there run the other way; [Goaltender](../positions/goaltender.md) owns all of it. So dump it in expecting them to move it, and chase as though they cannot smother it. **🇬🇧 In England and Wales, treat this as unsettled.** The IIHF book carries the rule (Rule 27.7, not the NHL's 27.8), but England Ice Hockey's *Rules & Regulations* 22.3 say the restricted area is *"not currently enforced"* and the IHUK In-House Rules do not mention it in either the 2025-26 edition or the current 2026-27 one — though their Introduction says *"unless otherwise stated, all rules will be enforced in line with the IIHF Rule Book"*, which points the other way. For you as the attacking team the safe assumption runs the other way: plan as though their goalie **may** retrieve the puck, ask your league, and look behind the net to see whether the lines are painted at all. Treat an unmarked sheet as a hint rather than a ruling: IIHF 27.7 turns on *"the position of the puck"*, not on the markings.

## 9. Attacking Off the Rush vs Attacking Off a Set Possession

These are two different games and most amateur teams have only one of them.

### Off the rush

```facts
Read: You enter with speed and attack before the defence is set — the chance comes from numbers and momentum
Key: Chances appear in the first three or four seconds and then vanish; speed and the moment of entry are the resource
Options: The late-arriving trailer into the high slot, the drive to the net that occupies a defender, and the delay — curling back to let a second wave arrive
Key: Shots off the rush are more dangerous than equivalent shots from a set possession, because the goalie is also moving and re-establishing depth
```

You enter the zone with speed and attack before the defence is set. The chance comes from **numbers and momentum** — a 2-on-1, a 3-on-2, a trailer arriving late into space.

- The defining resource is **speed and the moment of entry**. Chances appear in the first three or four seconds and then vanish.
- The high-value plays are the **late-arriving trailer** into the high slot, the **drive to the net** that occupies a defender, and the **delay** — curling back up toward the blue line to let a second wave arrive.
- Shots off the rush are more dangerous than equivalent shots from a set possession, because the goalie is also moving and re-establishing depth.
- Full treatment of getting into the zone in the first place is in [Zone Entries](zone_entries.md).

### Off a set possession

```facts
Read: The defence is established, everyone is back and nothing is open — the chance comes from movement and patience
Key: The defining resource is time on the puck and the willingness to hold it
Options: Cycle, low-to-high with a screen, seam pass, net-front chaos, behind-the-net feed
Read: Coverage lapses arrive after a long shift of defending — a defence that has been chasing for forty seconds is about to make a mistake
```

The defence is established, everyone is back, and nothing is open. The chance comes from **movement and patience** — cycling, rotating, forcing the defence to move until coverage breaks.

- The defining resource is **time on the puck and the willingness to hold it**.
- The high-value plays are the ones in sections 4 to 8: cycle, low-to-high with a screen, seam pass, net-front chaos, behind-the-net feed.
- Coverage lapses tend to arrive after a long shift of defending — a defence that has been chasing for forty seconds is a defence about to make a mistake.

### Why having only one is a problem

```facts
Risk: A rush-only team has nothing once the rush is denied at the blue line — they enter, lose it, and go back the other way
Risk: A cycle-only team never threatens quickly, so the defence gets set every time and the cycle only ever produces perimeter shots
Action: Have a plan for the second wave — a chip past the defenceman, a soft area dump, or a controlled retreat and re-attack
Action: When a cycle produces a neutral-zone turnover, somebody must be anticipating and going the other way with speed
```

- **A rush-only team** dies against any structured defensive team, because when the rush is denied at the blue line they have nothing. They enter, lose the puck, and go back the other way. Their offensive-zone time is measured in seconds.
- **A cycle-only team** is easy to defend because it never threatens quickly. The defence gets set every single time, and the cycle only ever produces perimeter shots.

**How to have both:** the practical test is what your team does on the *second* wave. When the rush is stopped at the blue line, do you have a plan to establish possession — a chip past the defenceman, a soft area dump, a controlled retreat and re-attack? And when a cycle produces a turnover in the neutral zone, is anyone anticipating and going the other way with speed? See [Risk Management](../hockey-iq/risk_management.md) and [Scanning and Anticipation](../hockey-iq/scanning_and_anticipation.md).

## 10. Regaining Possession After a Shot

**Treat the seconds immediately after a shot as the most dangerous moment in a possession — and this one is measured.** The NHL's public play-by-play feed timestamps every shot and every goal, so the hazard can be read straight off it. Across games 2024020001–2024020400 of the 2024-25 regular season (`api-web.nhle.com/v1/gamecenter/{id}/play-by-play`), a team that put a shot on goal scored again within the next **three seconds 1.37%** of the time, against roughly **0.26%** for a three-second window picked at random — about **five times** the base rate. Put the other way round: **11.4% of all goals came within three seconds of a preceding shot on goal by the same team**, and 19.7% within ten. These are figures derived from the raw feed rather than published statistics, but the direction is not in doubt, and it agrees with the rebound-recovery figure quoted earlier in this document and with the shot-location values [Shooting](../technique/shooting.md) covers. The goalie has committed, is often down, and is out of position. The puck is loose in the most dangerous area on the ice. Every defender has just been pulled toward the shot.

This is also the moment most amateur teams stop playing — the shot goes in, everyone watches, and the defence walks out with it.

### F3's job

```facts
Convention: With three forwards rotating and both defencemen holding the blue line — the default shape this section describes — F3 is the high forward; a coaching choice, not a law
Position: Be the safety — at or near the top of the circle, so a rebound straight out is yours and a counter-attack has to go through you
Action: Cover the vacated point if a defenceman activates — F3 takes the blue line in the house-default shape, which wants two on the line
Options: Under a five-man rotation whichever player is highest covers; a third variant slides the weak-side defenceman across to the middle instead
Read: If the rebound squirts to the high slot, jump — you are the closest player with forward momentum
Position: The other two forwards go to the net — one to the front for the screen and tip, one hunting the strong-side rebound
```

In the house-default structure, **F3 is the high forward** and their responsibilities on a shot are, in order:

1. **Be the safety.** Sit at or near the top of the circle so a rebound that comes straight out is yours, and so a counter-attack has to go through you.
2. **Cover the vacated point if a defenceman activates.** If the strong-side defenceman pinches or dives to the middle, **F3 takes the blue line** — in the house-default five-man shape described earlier, which wants two players on the line at all times. This is a coaching choice, and a common alternative is the **five-man rotation**, where nobody has a fixed line job and whichever player is highest at that moment covers, defenceman or forward. A third variant simply accepts one player on the line while the D is down and asks the *weak-side* defenceman to slide across to the middle instead. Find out which your team plays — the five-man shape described earlier in this document is a default, not a law.
3. **Read the rebound and jump.** If the puck squirts to the high slot, you are the closest player with forward momentum. That is a high-danger chance handed to you.

The other two forwards go **to the net**: one to the front for the screen and tip, one hunting the strong-side rebound.

### The defenceman's pinch

```facts
Read: Pinch when you can arrive on contact — "if you can't arrive on contact, don't go"; on an uncontested race for a loose puck or a rim, arrive clearly first
Read: Pinch when the winger is flat-footed or facing the glass, when a forward can rotate high to cover your line, or on a loose puck, a bobble or a tired line
Never: Pinch when your forwards are already below you, when the opponent has turned up ice with speed, when protecting a narrow lead late, or when your partner has not seen you go
Technique: Stick in the passing lane before your feet commit; angle the winger into the boards, never toward the middle; close with short explosive steps
Priority: Take the player or take the puck — achieve neither and you have created an odd-man rush against your own team
Rule: A player facing the glass is defenceless — NHL Rule 41.1 puts the onus on you to avoid or minimise contact, and driving into their back is a major with no minor option (NHL and IIHF Rule 43.2)
Rule: Body checking is banned at USA Hockey 12U and below, in all girls'/women's and non-check adult classifications (604(a)), and at Hockey Canada U13 and below, in female hockey and any Member-approved division (7.3) — and both are floors: a local governing body may bar it in any classification
Rule: The IIHF book and the In-House Rules set no body-checking age — ask your league. IIHF 101.1 restricts it in women's hockey: a hit needs a clear intention of playing the puck, subject to the exception the rule itself then sets out, and the boards may not be used to eliminate, push or pin an opponent
Rule: Checking a player who no longer has the puck is a minor under NHL 56.2(iii), not capped there — IIHF 56.5 and Hockey Canada 8.3(b) reach a major plus game misconduct with nobody hurt; USA Hockey 640(b) catches every avoidable one, and though its minor excepts adult men, 640 Note 2 reaches them
Action: If the pinch fails, retreat toward the slot, not along the boards — following the puck down the wall abandons the middle
Convention: How aggressively defencemen pinch is a coaching choice — some green-light both, some only the strong side, some forbid going below the circles; ask
```

A **pinch** is stepping down from the offensive blue line to keep the puck in the zone or to win a puck on the wall. **Do not confuse it with a step-up**, which is challenging an attacker in the neutral zone or at your own line — different play, different zone. ⚠️ **And before the criteria: USA Hockey's late-hit window is the narrowest of the four.** Rule 640(b) makes a minor of every *avoidable* check on a player who has released a shot or pass — the rule says they *"are not eligible to be body checked"*, and the Declaration of Player Safety calls *"finishing the check"* an *"unacceptable action"*. **That named minor is written *"except Adult Male Classifications"*, and adult men should not read that as a free hit:** 640 Note 2 directs a roughing penalty for *"an avoidable body check to an opponent … who is no longer in control of the puck"* with no classification exception. Its separate Casebook gives a rule of thumb under which a check may be finished, and it wants **all four** of these: you first made an effort to gain possession of the puck, you were within *"an arm's length plus stick length"* when the puck was dumped, the check was already *"in the process of being delivered when control of the puck was relinquished"*, and you minimise the force used (Standard of Play, Situation 10). **Lateness is an express test there** — *"It is not acceptable to skate one or two more strides after the puck is gone and then decide to deliver the check"* — so everything below is about whether to go at all, not about how late you may arrive.

The decision criteria, which are consistent across coaching sources:

**Pinch when:**
- You can **arrive on contact** — you and the puck get there at the same time. "If you can't arrive on contact, don't go." That is the test when an opposing player is arriving too: getting there level lets you play the puck or seal them against the wall, and arriving a beat late is the failure, because once the puck is gone you may not hit the man (Rule 56.2, below). On an **uncontested race for a loose puck or a rim**, the bar is higher — you need to arrive **clearly first**, since there is nobody to seal and second place just means your back is to the play. [Forechecking Systems](forechecking_systems.md) applies the same two-part test.
- The opposing winger is **flat-footed or facing the glass**, not already turned up ice with speed.
- A forward is **able to rotate high** and cover your line.
- It is a **loose puck, a bobble or a tired line** — the best windows.

**Do not pinch when:**
- Your forwards are already below you and nobody can cover the line;
- The opponent has already turned up ice with speed and their head up;
- You are protecting a narrow lead late (see [Game Management](game_management.md));
- Your partner has not seen you go.

**Technique:** stick in the passing lane *before* your feet commit; angle the winger into the boards, never toward the middle; use the boards to box them out; close with short explosive steps. The governing principle is **"take the player or take the puck"** — if you achieve neither, you have created an odd-man rush against your own team.

> **⚠️ "Take the player" has a second limit, and it is the more important one.**
> A winger *facing the glass* is a defenceless player. That posture appears in
> the read above because it tells you they cannot escape with the puck — it is
> not permission to hit them. NHL Rule 41.1 puts the onus on you: *"The onus is
> on the player applying the check to ensure his opponent is not in a defenseless
> position and if so, he must avoid or minimize contact."* Driving into their
> back is checking from behind, which under NHL 43.2 and IIHF 43.2 has **no minor
> option at all** — it is a major with an automatic game misconduct, and Hockey
> Canada's rule names this exact scenario, a player digging a puck out along the
> boards, driven head-first into them. Seal the wall, take the puck, or angle
> them off it. [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md)

>
> **And check that you may hit at all.** Body checking is prohibited under USA
> Hockey Rule 604(a) at 12U and below, in every girls'/women's classification and
> in every non-check adult classification, and under Hockey Canada at U13 and
> below, throughout female hockey and in any Member-approved division. ⚠️ **Those
> lists are floors, not ceilings, so finding yourself outside them does not end
> the question.** The same USA Hockey sub-section adds that *"[a] local governing
> body may prohibit body checking in any classification"*, and **604(b)**
> prohibits it outright *"[a]ny time a team from a Competitive Contact category
> ... plays a team from a Body Checking category"*. Most adult rec leagues do not

> [UK and England Rules](../foundation/uk_rules.md). **🇬🇧 One case here is

> there is a clear intention of playing the puck or attempting to 'gain
> possession' of the puck with the exception from the situation described in
> this rule"*, forbids players competing for the puck *"to use the boards to
> make contact with an opponent to eliminate her from the play, push her into
> the boards, or pin her along the boards"*, and makes an illegal hit a minor or

> **"Take the player" has a legal limit.** You may finish a check on the player who *has* the puck, and you may use your body to seal them against the wall while contesting a puck you can both reach. You may **not** hit a player who has already moved the puck: NHL Rule 56.2(iii) makes it a minor *"on any player who deliberately checks an opponent, including the goalkeeper, who is not in possession of the puck."* ⚠️ **And a minor is not the ceiling, under the NHL book either.** NHL **56.4** lets the Referee assess a major *"based on the degree of violence"*, and **56.5** adds a game misconduct — but only *"when a major penalty is imposed under this rule for an infraction resulting in an injury of an opponent"*, so under the NHL book the game misconduct waits on an injury. **The other three books, set out next, do not make it wait.** IIHF 56.1 calls a late hit reckless endangerment of a player who no longer has the puck, and 56.5 reaches a major plus a game misconduct with nobody hurt; Hockey Canada 8.3(b) reaches that tier on *"the degree of violence of the impact"* with nobody hurt, and makes it mandatory where a late hit injures. What keeps a pinch legal is **immediacy**, not possession: NHL 56.1 permits a check *"rendered immediately following his loss of possession"*, and Hockey Canada 8.3 says immediate contact *"is best described as"* contact within an arm and stick length of the carrier at the instant the puck is released. **So the safe habit is to arrive while they still have it**, because you cannot judge a beat at speed. ⚠️ **And USA Hockey's window is the narrowest of the four** — 640(b) makes a minor of every *avoidable* check on a player who has released a shot or pass — the rule says they *"are not eligible to be body checked"*, and the Declaration of Player Safety calls *"finishing the check"* an *"unacceptable action"*. **That minor is written *"except Adult Male Classifications"*, but 640 Note 2 reaches adult men anyway, as roughing.** ⚠️ **Nor is the minor USA Hockey's ceiling — the tier is in Rule 640 itself:** 640(g) is a major plus a game misconduct where an action under 640(b, c, d, e or f) *"recklessly endangers an opponent"*, and 640(h) a match penalty. Both reach a late hit only through **(b)**, the sub-section excepting adult men, so **whether they reach adult men's hockey the book does not say, and this document does not know** — while 602(a), a match penalty for a player who *"recklessly endangers or attempts to injure any opposing player"*, is tied to no named foul at all and does not depend on the answer. USA Hockey's separate Casebook does put a measure on when a finish is unavoidable, and it is Hockey Canada's shape — a distance — but hedged four ways at once: you must have first made an effort to gain possession, been within *"an arm's length plus stick length"* when the puck was dumped, had the check already *"in the process of being delivered"*, and minimised the force (Standard of Play, Situation 10). USA Hockey's Casebook rules lateness out in terms too: *"It is not acceptable to skate one or two more strides after the puck is gone and then decide to deliver the check."* **Treat USA Hockey as the stricter standard, not a window the other books grant you** — the IIHF puts no measurable distance on it — proximity is a condition there, but a judged one; 56.1 sets a second condition instead, that you were still inside an *"objectively reasonable window of opportunity"* and, separately, *"in the immediate vicinity of the skater with the puck"*. On a pinch this happens constantly — the winger chips it past you and you arrive a beat late. Once the puck is gone, so is your check. Play the puck instead.

**If the pinch fails:** retreat toward the **slot**, not along the boards. Skating back down the wall follows the puck and abandons the middle; cutting to the slot re-establishes your gap and delays the attack until support arrives.

> **How aggressively defencemen pinch is a coaching choice.** Some teams give both defencemen a green light in the offensive zone; some allow the strong-side defenceman only; some instruct defencemen never to pinch below the top of the circles. **Ask.** A defenceman pinching in a system that does not expect it is, in coaching experience, among the commonest sources of odd-man rushes against amateur teams — coaching emphasis, not a counted one.

## 11. When to Shoot and When Not To

### Shoot when

```facts
Read: Inside the home-plate area with any kind of look — almost any shot from there beats almost any pass from there
Read: The goalie is unset, moving or screened
Read: You have a lane — a genuine one, not the memory of one
Read: There is someone at the net front — a tip threat and a rebound hunter make it a different play
Read: A rebound is available and the goalie is down — shoot immediately, do not settle it
```

- You are **inside the home-plate area** with any kind of look. Almost any shot from there beats almost any pass from there.
- The goalie is **unset, moving, or screened**.
- You have **a lane** — a genuine one, not the memory of one.
- There is **someone at the net front**. A shot with a tip threat and a rebound hunter is a different play from the same shot with nobody there.
- **A rebound is available and the goalie is down.** Shoot immediately. Do not settle it.

### Do not shoot when

```facts
Never: Fire into a shin pad from the point with the lane blocked — a blocked point shot usually goes the other way with your team spread out
Never: Shoot when a better shot is one pass away — specifically, a pass across the royal road
Never: Shoot with nobody at the net front — you are giving the puck to the goalie and starting their breakout
Never: Shoot from a sharp angle with no traffic — goalies seal the post, and there is nothing to hit and no rebound to chase
Never: Treat the cross-slot finishing rate and the point-shot conversion rate as a before-and-after pair — different studies, measuring different things
Priority: Act on the direction — a shot after the goalie has had to move across is worth materially more, so the half-second is usually worth it
```

- The lane is blocked and you are firing into a shin pad from the point. A blocked point shot is worse than no shot: it usually goes the other way, and your team is spread out.
- A **better shot is one pass away** — specifically, a pass across the royal road. **Do not put the two numbers side by side, though**, however tempting it is: the **15.50%** is a finishing rate on shots following a cross-slot pass, measured at 5-on-5 in the Passing Project, while the **3%** is a Clear Sight Analytics figure — whether you read it as Boyle's *distant shot from above the circles with no lateral movement* or as this document's point-shot-with-traffic rate in Section 1, it comes from the proprietary tracking that Sections 1 and 2 flag as attributed-but-uncheckable. They are different studies measuring different things, and Section 2 explains at length why treating them as a before-and-after pair overstates what was measured — on the Boyle reading, most of that gap is shot distance rather than the crossing. The direction is what you should act on: **a shot taken after the goalie has had to move across is worth materially more than the same-ish shot taken before**, so the half-second is usually worth it.
- **Nobody is at the net front.** You are giving the puck to the goalie and starting their breakout.
- You are on a **sharp angle with no traffic**. Goalies seal the post; there is nothing to hit and no rebound to chase.

### What to shoot *for*

```facts
Mindset: From the perimeter you are not trying to score — you are trying to manufacture a second chance
Technique: Shoot for a rebound — low, to the far pad, or off the pads; chest and glove get swallowed and the whistle goes
Technique: Shoot for a tip — low, hard, along the ice, through the screen, at the tipper's stick, and say who you are shooting at
Options: Shoot to be missed — a hard shot off the end boards to a spot for a teammate is a legitimate pass; say it first
Never: Pick corners against a set, sighted goalie — the lowest-percentage thing you can do with a puck. Aim for a corner instead only when they cannot see it or cannot get there
```

This is the part most players never think about. From the perimeter, **you are not trying to score. You are trying to manufacture a second chance.**

- **Shoot for a rebound.** Pucks aimed low, to the far pad, or off the pads generally produce controllable rebounds. Pucks aimed at the goalie's chest and glove do not — they get swallowed and the whistle goes.
- **Shoot for a tip.** Low, hard, along the ice, through the screen, at the tipper's stick. Say who you are shooting at. The tipper's job is to present a blade; yours is to hit it.
- **Shoot to be missed.** A shot deliberately aimed to miss wide can be a play — a hard shot off the end boards to a specific spot for a teammate is a legitimate pass. Say it first.
- **Aim for a corner only when the goalie cannot see it or cannot get there.** Picking corners against a set, sighted goalie is the lowest-percentage thing you can do with a puck.

### Getting pucks through from the point

```facts
Key: Your job is not to shoot harder, it is to shoot through — point shots are blocked more than shots from anywhere else on the ice
Technique: Walk the line first — two or three strides toward the middle changes the angle and moves the shot-blocker's feet
Technique: Change the release point — a quick snap from a different spot beats a blocker who has already dropped
Technique: Take something off it — a slower, lower shot that gets through and gets tipped beats a 95 mph slapshot into a shin pad
Options: Fake the shot — a blocker on the ice opens the lane for the real one, and often opens the seam pass instead
Options: Take the D-to-D first — it drags both the blocker and the goalie, and you shoot into a lane just vacated
```

Point shots are blocked more than shots from anywhere else on the ice. If you are a defenceman, your job is not to shoot harder — it is to shoot *through*:

- **Walk the line first.** Two or three strides toward the middle changes the angle and moves the shot-blocker's feet. A defenceman who receives and fires from the same spot is shooting at a stationary wall.
- **Change the release point.** A quick snap shot from a different spot than the one they set up for beats a blocker who has already dropped.
- **Take something off it.** A deliberately slower, lower shot that gets through and gets tipped beats a 95 mph slapshot into a shin pad, every time.
- **Fake the shot.** A shot fake that puts a blocker on the ice opens the lane for the real one, and often opens the seam pass instead.
- **Take the D-to-D first.** Moving it across the top drags both the blocker and the goalie, and you shoot into a lane that has just been vacated.

More detail on mechanics is in [Shooting](../technique/shooting.md).

## 12. Offensive-Zone Faceoffs

An offensive-zone faceoff is the only moment in the game where you get to arrange all five players exactly where you want them, with the puck about to arrive at a known place. It is the closest hockey gets to a free kick, and most amateur teams waste it entirely.

Full treatment of the draw itself — technique, stick placement, the violations that get your centre ejected — is in [Faceoffs](faceoffs.md). What matters here is what happens in the second *after* the draw.

### Set plays off a win

```facts
Key: The point of a set play is that four players start moving on the drop, before anyone knows who won it
Options: Win back to the point and screen immediately — inside winger straight to the net front, outside winger to the strong-side rebound spot
Options: Win back to a winger set up for a one-timer — they must be aligned as a shooter before the drop, not as a defender
Options: Win it forward — attack directly; high reward, but it puts your centre below the puck and fails badly if the draw is lost
Options: Tie up the opposing centre's stick and let a winger sweep in for the loose puck — useful when their centre is dominant on draws
Options: Delay for the screen — the defenceman skates laterally for a beat first; a shot half a second late with a screen beats one on time without
Risk: A winger creeping into the circle before the drop is encroachment, and it gets your centre thrown out of the draw
Never: Reach the whistle without everyone knowing the play — the common failures are a net-front player arriving after the shot and a shot into the first shin pad
```

The point of a set play is that four players start moving on the drop, before anyone knows who won it. Common families:

- **Win back to the point, screen immediately.** The centre draws the puck back to the strong-side defenceman; the inside winger goes straight to the net front to screen; the outside winger takes the strong-side rebound position. Simplest, most reliable, and it converts because everyone was already moving.
- **Win back to the winger for a one-timer.** The centre draws it back to a winger set up in the shooting position (usually a left shot on the right circle, or vice versa) who shoots first-time. Requires the winger to be aligned as a shooter before the drop, not as a defender.
- **Win it forward / "quick strike."** The centre draws the puck forward or to the inside and attacks directly. High reward if the opposing centre is slow to react, but it puts your centre below the puck and it fails badly if the draw is lost.
- **Tie up and let a winger take it.** The centre does not try to win the puck cleanly but locks up the opposing centre's stick, and a winger sweeps in for the loose puck. Useful when the opposing centre is dominant on draws. Watch the **encroachment** rule — a winger creeping into the circle before the drop gets your *centre* thrown out of the draw.
- **Delay for the screen.** The defenceman receives the win but skates laterally for a beat before shooting, giving the net-front player time to establish. A shot half a second late with a screen beats a shot on time without one.

Whatever your team runs, **everyone must know it before the whistle**, and the two most common failures are a net-front player who arrives after the shot and a defenceman who fires it into the first shin pad.

### Structure off a loss

```facts
Mindset: Assume you will lose roughly half the draws — structuring for the loss is the other half of the plan, not defeatism
Action: One forward pressures immediately, usually the winger nearest the puck, to deny the clean breakout pass and force a rim or a chip
Action: The other two forwards turn and skate rather than watch — a forward who is stationary when the puck is lost is beaten
Read: The defencemen hold the line and read — is this a rim to be intercepted, or a clean exit to be retreated against?
Convention: The strong-side defenceman is the one most likely to pinch on a rim; the weak-side one covers the middle
Key: Having a defined assignment for the loss is what lets you be aggressive on the win
```

Assume you will lose roughly half the draws. Structure for the loss is not defeatism, it is the other half of the plan.

- **One forward pressures immediately** — usually the winger nearest the puck — to deny the clean breakout pass and force a rim or a chip.
- **The other two forwards get defensive momentum going**, turning and skating rather than watching. A forward who is stationary when the puck is lost is beaten.
- **The defencemen hold the line and read**: is this a rim to be intercepted, or a clean exit to be retreated against? The strong-side defenceman is the one most likely to pinch on a rim; the weak-side one covers the middle.
- **Nobody is a spectator.** The most common goal against off an offensive-zone draw is not a great play by the other team; it is four players still processing the fact that they lost the puck.

The general principle is worth stating plainly: **having a defined assignment for the loss is what lets you be aggressive on the win.** Teams play tentatively on offensive-zone draws precisely because they have no plan for losing them.

## Common Mistakes

- **Treating "get pucks to the net" as a strategy.** At even strength, volume from the perimeter returns under 5% goals per unblocked shot — a rate counting misses as well as saves, so it is not shooting percentage — and about 1% from the point without traffic (that point-shot figure is attributed but uncheckable — see section 1; [Shooting](../technique/shooting.md) covers shot-location value). **On the power play the same source puts the whole zone at or above 5% — a contour read off a small image, so approximate in where the line falls but not in its direction — so this is an even-strength caution and not an argument against a power-play point shot.** Shot volume without shot quality is busywork.
- **Cycling with nobody in front of the net.** Very commonly the failure behind a possession that looks busy and produces nothing — **a coaching observation rather than a count, because nobody has published a tally of offensive-zone failures by cause.** A cycle that never produces a net-front presence or a shot from the home-plate area has accomplished nothing except tiring out your own line.
- **Scanning up the wall before scanning across the middle.** By the time you have looked at the safe option, the seam has closed. Look across first, every time — of the two options, only the seam expires.
- **Passing to a teammate's current position instead of where they are going.** In the slot, the extra touch is the difference between a goal and a save.
- **Standing still on the half-wall.** A stationary player against the boards gets pinned and stripped. Cutbacks and curls are what buy the two extra seconds you need. And when the contact arrives, never your back to the boards, and never duck. Get your skates parallel to the boards instead, forearm and hip to the wall, head up and chin off your chest.
- **Watching your own shot from the net front.** Turning to follow the flight un-screens the goalie and takes you out of the rebound. Look at the puck, then get your eyes back down.
- **Standing in the blue paint to screen.** Legal screening happens *outside* the crease, under every book — but check which reason is yours. **NHL Rule 69.3** voids the goal where an attacker establishes a significant position inside the crease that obstructs the goalie's vision **and** impairs their ability to defend, with no contact at all — and it defines significant as a substantial part of your body in there for *"more than an instantaneous period of time"*, so both limbs and the duration have to be met. ⚠️ **Do not take that as room to work in:** the IIHF's own Situation Handbook applies none of it, answering a player *"in the crease screening the goalkeeper"* with a flat *"The goal shall be disallowed"* (Situation 69.1) — guidance that ranks below the rulebook, but guidance an official may be working from. **Hockey Canada's** Interpretation 3 to 8.5(a) reaches the same result where a player standing in the crease impedes *"the goaltender's vision or movements"*. **USA Hockey 625(b)** needs no impairment to judge: your position in the crease is enough on its own — unless the puck preceded you into the crease, the goalkeeper is out of the crease area, or a defending player's interference is what put you there, in which case the goal stands. **The middle one runs out the moment you are what stops the goalie getting back:** Casebook Rule 625, Situation 9 disallows the goal and adds an interference minor where the goalkeeper was *"attempting to return to the goal crease"* and the attacker prevented them from making a play. ⚠️ **And under Hockey Canada the price of arriving into the goalie is not two minutes:** 8.5(b) reaches a major plus game misconduct at the referee's discretion *"based on the degree of violence of impact"* **with nobody hurt**, and makes it **mandatory** for charging the goaltender and **mandatory** for any interference-with-goaltender foul that injures an opponent; 8.5(c) reaches a match penalty. ⚠️ **Nor is it two minutes under USA Hockey, which routes the deliberate half of this to charging:** 607(d) Note 1 sends *"[a]ny deliberate body contact or check that is delivered to the goalkeeper"* there, 607(c) makes that *"[a] minor plus a misconduct or a major plus a game misconduct penalty"* where the goalkeeper is *"within the goal crease or privileged area"* — an area 607(d) Note 2 runs from the end-zone face-off spots back to the end boards — and 607(e) reaches a match penalty. **Its Casebook turns the whole thing on one thing, in both directions.** **USA Hockey's Casebook, Rule 607, Situation 4:** forced into the goalkeeper by a defender, *"no penalty should be assessed provided the attacking player has clearly made every attempt to avoid the contact with the goalkeeper"* — but *"if the player makes no attempt to avoid the goalkeeper and recklessly endangers the goalkeeper by accelerating through the contact, or by extending the arms or dropping the shoulder to deliver the hit, the major plus game misconduct, or match penalty option would be the proper call."* **Make the effort to avoid, visibly, every time — but do not read it as a complete defence.** ⚠️ **The same Situation puts a player *"going hard to the goal"* who makes *"an honest attempt to avoid the goalkeeper once it is realized contact is unavoidable"* at a minor plus misconduct**, and the no-penalty rung needs a defender's foul behind you as well as your effort.
- **Holding your position when the goalkeeper leans into you to establish theirs.** Under **NHL and IIHF Rule 69.3**, once a goalkeeper attempting to establish position in the crease has made contact with you, you must *"immediately vacate"* — *"give ground to the goalkeeper"* — or a goal scored on the play is disallowed. The rule adds a penalty *"whether or not a goal is scored"*, though both books' own reference tables assess none in the goal-scored case (**Situation 6 D** in the NHL's Table 14 and in the IIHF's Appendix IV: *"loss of goal only"*) and put the minor in the no-goal case instead. Neither reading pays you for standing there. Give ground, then re-establish the screen outside the paint.
- **Treating the rebound carve-out as a licence to move the goaltender.** **NHL and IIHF Rule 69.7** do permit incidental contact inside or outside the crease in a rebound or a loose-puck race you are both making, and allow the goal — but the paragraph after that disallows one *"[i]n the event that a goalkeeper has been pushed into the net together with the puck by an attacking player after making a stop"*, and adds *"appropriate penalties"* if applicable. ⚠️ **All three of those conditions have to be met**, so this is not a rule against every contact that moves a goaltender. ⚠️ **And carry the relief with the limit:** where the referee judges that a defending player pushed or fouled you into causing that push, *"the goal can be permitted"* — **read the verb: permitted, on the referee's opinion, not allowed as of right**, and 69.7 attaches no effort condition to it. **The *"reasonable effort to avoid such contact"* test is 69.1's, on the separate question of whether a defender's shove makes the contact yours** — which is the question the reference tables decide, and they decide it on the effort. Neither USA Hockey nor Hockey Canada writes the carve-out at all.
- **Tipping with a high stick — and assuming the ceiling is the crossbar wherever you play.** A puck contacting your stick above the height of the crossbar is not a goal under **NHL and IIHF Rule 80.3** and **Hockey Canada 6.9(b)**, all three of which make *where the puck meets the stick* the test. ⚠️ **USA Hockey does neither.** **Rule 621(d)** sets the ceiling at the **shoulder** and prints no crossbar test at all, and its *Casebook* at **Rule 621, Situation 7** rejects the contact point in terms — *"When any part of the stick is carried above the shoulders, the entire stick is considered to be high"* — so a blade held high loses the goal even when the puck struck the shaft low. **A tip between your shoulders and the crossbar is a goal in three books and no goal in the fourth.** ⚠️ **The whistle is closer between the books than their printed rules look.** The NHL, the IIHF and Hockey Canada stop play only once *your own* team gains possession of the high-batted puck (NHL and IIHF 80.1, Hockey Canada 6.9(a)). **USA Hockey 621(d) prints an immediate stoppage** unless an opponent gains possession — **but its Casebook applies the same offending-team-possession test.** Rule 621, Situation 15 permits the play to continue and states that *"an offending team player must be the first to gain possession of the puck"*, and that *"[a] deflection off of a player from either team would not cause the play to be completed."* **So do not quit on your own tip under any of the four.** ⚠️ **And all of that is about the puck — the moment the blade reaches a person it is Rule 60, or USA Hockey 621(a), 621(b) and 621(c), or Hockey Canada 9.5, or Rule 7.6 Head Contact in its minor and female divisions via 6.9(c). None of those is capped at a minor, and CARHA 62(b) — CARHA-affiliated adult leagues only — has no minor rung above the shoulders at all, being a major whether or not it injures, with a game misconduct added by its Note where the contact was intentional or where an accidental one injures; its minor, 62(a), is a different offence and not a lower rung, needing no contact at all.** A puck nobody claims is stopped in all four as well — USA Hockey by Casebook Situations 8, 10 and 12, the NHL and IIHF by **Rule 72.3** and Hockey Canada by its Interpretation 2 to 6.9(a)(i) — and what actually differs there is the face-off, which USA Hockey alone sends to the offending team's defending zone. **A tip that goes straight in does end the play at once:** the Casebook makes a high-sticked puck entering the goal *"an immediate stoppage of play"*, and under 621(d) it is no goal anyway. Keep the blade on the ice, which is where most tippable pucks are anyway.
- **Two defencemen leaving the blue line at once, when nobody called for it.** Or one leaving and no forward filling in. [Defending the Rush](defending_the_rush.md) lists this among the five ways a team manufactures an odd-man rush against itself, and calls it the subtlest, because nothing looks wrong until the puck turns over and there is nobody above it. **Both going is not always the error:** teams running a five-man rotation, or giving both defencemen a standing green light, break the default on purpose and cover the line with whichever player is highest, and a full-ice press, a delayed penalty and a 6-on-5 are called from the bench rather than decided individually. Unless one of those is on, assume the default and look for your partner before you go.
- **Pinching without arriving on contact.** "Take the player or take the puck." Half-hearted pinches create 3-on-2s going the other way. And arriving a beat late is not capped at a minor: **USA Hockey's window is the narrowest of the four** — 640(b) makes a minor of every *avoidable* check on a player who has released a shot or pass, and its Casebook allows one to be finished only where you first made an effort to gain possession, were an arm's length plus stick length away when the puck was dumped, had the check already in the process of being delivered, and minimise the force, with *"one or two more strides after the puck is gone"* ruled out in terms (Standard of Play, Situation 10). That named minor is written *"except Adult Male Classifications"* — but it is not a free hit there either: 640 Note 2 directs a roughing penalty for *"an avoidable body check to an opponent … who is no longer in control of the puck"* with no classification exception. **And the tier above it is in Rule 640 too:** 640(g) is a major plus a game misconduct where an action under (b, c, d, e or f) *"recklessly endangers an opponent"* and 640(h) a match — both reaching a late hit only through (b), so whether they reach adult men's hockey the book does not say — while 602(a)'s match penalty for a player who *"recklessly endangers or attempts to injure any opposing player"* is tied to no named foul at all.
- **Pinching to hit a winger who is facing the glass.** They cannot see you and cannot protect themselves, and the onus is on you to avoid or minimise the contact. Take the puck — **and no book lets this one cost you two minutes and nothing else**: driving into a defenceless player's back is checking from behind, and **under the NHL and the IIHF there is no minor option at all** — IIHF 43.2 says *"there is no option to award a minor penalty"* and 43.3 is a major plus an **automatic** game misconduct. Hockey Canada 7.5(a) and USA Hockey 608(a) do write a minor, but never a bare one: 7.5(a) is a minor **and** a game misconduct, 608(a) a minor **plus a misconduct**. ⚠️ **And before any of that, check that you may hit at all.** Body checking is banned at USA Hockey 12U and below and in every girls'/women's and non-check adult classification (Rule 604(a)), and at Hockey Canada U13 and below, throughout female hockey and in any Member-approved division (Rule 7.3) — and those lists are floors, not ceilings, because 604(a) lets a local governing body bar it in any classification and 604(b) bars it whenever a Competitive Contact team meets a Body Checking team. Britain publishes no age, so ask your league; British women's hockey runs on IIHF Rule 101.1, where a hit needs a clear intention of playing the puck, subject to the exception the rule itself then sets out, and the boards may not be used to eliminate, push or pin an opponent. Where checking is not permitted, all of this is angling, body position and playing the puck.
- **Retreating along the boards after a failed pinch.** Cut to the slot instead. Following the puck down the wall abandons the middle of the ice.
- **Quitting after the shot.** The three seconds after a shot are the most dangerous moment of your entire possession. Most teams spend them watching.
- **Having exactly one way to attack.** Rush-only teams are shut down by any structured defence; cycle-only teams never threaten before the defence is set.
- **Not knowing your team's rules for defenceman activation.** Whether your defencemen pinch, and how deep they may go, is a coaching decision that varies enormously. Ask before your first shift.

## Key Takeaways

1. **You are not trying to shoot — you are trying to shoot from a dangerous place with the goalie out of position.** Distance matters most: at even strength, goals per unblocked shot from outside home plate have been under 5% across a decade of NHL data. That metric counts misses in the denominator, so it is a different and lower number from shooting percentage. **On the power play the same map puts effectively the whole zone at or above 5% — approximate in where the line falls, not in its direction — so it is no argument against a point shot there.** [Shooting](../technique/shooting.md) covers shot-location value.
2. **The royal road pass is the highest-value pass type measured, roughly double the next one** — crossing the centre line before a shot finished at 15.50%, because the goalie cannot re-square in time; that ranks pass types against each other, and no measurement here weighs a pass against a zone entry, a forecheck recovery or a shot. That is volunteer-tracked Passing Project data, 51,308 shot attempts at 5-on-5 — which is not the same as even strength, and excludes the power play — so it is not comparable with shooting percentages, and it is not a power-play number.
3. **Look across the middle before you look up the wall.** The seam opens briefly and closes permanently; the safe option up the wall does not. Scan them in that order and you can still have both — scan them the other way round and the seam has gone.
4. **A cycle is a set-up, not an outcome.** If it does not end in a shot from the home-plate area, a royal road pass, a screened point shot or a net drive, it did nothing — and a point shot needs a screen and two rebound hunters. **And where the cycle puts you on the wall, one qualifier outranks all of it: never your back to the boards, and never duck. Get your skates parallel to the boards instead, forearm and hip to the wall, head up and chin off your chest.**
5. **Screen from outside the blue paint, without contact — that is normally legal and worth doing constantly.** Inside the crease, what voids the goal with no contact at all is a **significant** position — a substantial part of your body in the paint for more than an instantaneous period, obstructing the goalie's vision **and** impairing their ability to defend (NHL Rule 69.3). **Outside the crease, expect nothing from 69.1** — its positioning limb sits under a *"should be disallowed only if"* ceiling, and both books' own tables answer *"Goal is allowed"* for an outside-the-crease screen that does impair the goalie (NHL Reference Table 14 and IIHF 2025/26 Appendix IV Table 16, Situations 5C and 5E). **The permission stops at the paint, and under USA Hockey and Hockey Canada the painted line counts as crease, so keep your skates clear of it too.** **And once a goalkeeper makes contact establishing position in the crease, 69.3 requires you to give ground immediately** — a goal scored while you hold your position is disallowed, and the rule adds a penalty whether or not one is, though both books' own tables assess none in the goal-scored case. Under USA Hockey Rule 625(b) and the IIHF book, standing in the crease can also cost you the zone. **And the two amateur books differ from the NHL and from each other.** USA Hockey 625(b) voids the goal on your **position in the crease alone**, whoever scored — unless the puck preceded you into the crease, the goalkeeper is out of the crease area, or a defending player's interference is what put you there. **Do not hear that middle exception as a free run at an empty crease:** Casebook Rule 625, Situation 9 disallows the goal *and* adds an interference minor where the goalkeeper was *"attempting to return to the goal crease"* and you prevented them from making a play. Hockey Canada 8.5 does not run any of this: it allows a teammate's goal from outside the crease provided the player in the crease stays completely passive, and disallows the goal under Interpretation 3 to 8.5(a) where that player impedes *"the goaltender's vision or movements"*. **What neither book reaches is a screen set from outside the crease**, so out there they are the more permissive books — and Hockey Canada's Interpretation 2 to 8.5(a) penalises you personally if you are fouled into the goaltender and make no effort to avoid them, though its Interpretation 1 excepts only a player whom the foul left unable to avoid the contact. ⚠️ **And Hockey Canada's ladder for this rule does not stop at a minor:** 8.5(b) reaches a major plus game misconduct — an ejection — at the referee's discretion on the degree of violence of impact **with nobody hurt**, and makes it **mandatory** for charging the goaltender and **mandatory** for any interference-with-goaltender foul that injures an opponent; 8.5(c) reaches a match penalty. ⚠️ **And USA Hockey's ladder does not stop at a minor either:** 607(d) Note 1 routes any *"deliberate body contact or check"* on a goalkeeper to **charging**, which is never a bare minor there — 607(c) is *"[a] minor plus a misconduct or a major plus a game misconduct penalty"* where the goalkeeper is *"within the goal crease or privileged area"*, an area reaching from the end-zone face-off spots back to the end boards, and 607(e) reaches a match penalty. Its Casebook at **Rule 607, Situation 4** runs three rungs: forced into the goalkeeper by a defender, there is **no penalty** where you *"clearly made every attempt to avoid the contact"*; **a minor plus misconduct** where you were *"going hard to the goal"* and made *"an honest attempt to avoid the goalkeeper"*; and the **major plus game misconduct or match penalty** where you made none and *"recklessly endanger[ed] the goalkeeper by accelerating through the contact, or by extending the arms or dropping the shoulder to deliver the hit."* Screen with your position; do not arrive into the goalie.
6. **Behind the net is an attacking position, not a dead end** — nobody can come at you from directly behind, though you can still be driven into the goal frame. Passes from below the goal line finished at 6.73% in that same volunteer-tracked 5-on-5 sample of all attempts.
7. **The seconds right after a shot are the most dangerous moment in a possession** — a team that has just put a shot on goal scores again within three seconds about five times as often as in a three-second window picked at random, read off the NHL's raw play-by-play feed rather than taken from published statistics — **and the defending team recovers roughly 60% of rebounds**. That 60% is Greg Revak's, worked off a chart by Alex Novet that puts recovery by the attacking team at 38–42% wherever the shot came from; no league, season or sample size is stated for it anywhere. You win that race by deciding to be there before the shot.
8. **Keep the shape: one player below the puck, one level with it, one above it, two on the blue line.** That two-on-the-line rule is a default, not a law — other teams rotate all five, or slide the weak-side defenceman across. Find out which yours plays.
9. **Build both attacks.** Rush offence lives on speed and numbers in three seconds, set offence on patience over thirty. Teams with only one get shut down.
10. **Almost everything here is a coaching choice, not a law** — triangle rotation versus overload, whether defencemen pinch, who takes the net front. Find out what your team plays before deciding someone else is out of position. **How aggressively you pinch is a choice; whether a late pinch is legal is not** — once the puck is gone, so is your check, and it is not capped at a minor in any book. NHL 56.4 gives the Referee a discretionary major on the degree of violence, though its game misconduct, 56.5, arrives only *"for an infraction resulting in an injury of an opponent"*; IIHF 56.5 reaches a major plus a game misconduct with nobody hurt. **And USA Hockey's window is the narrowest of the four** — 640(b) makes a minor of every *avoidable* check on a player who has released a shot or pass, and its Casebook allows one to be finished only on four conditions at once, ruling out in terms *"one or two more strides after the puck is gone"* (Standard of Play, Situation 10). Its named minor reads *"except Adult Male Classifications"*, and 640 Note 2 catches it there as roughing regardless. **USA Hockey does not cap it at a minor either:** 602(a) is a match penalty for a player who *"recklessly endangers or attempts to injure any opposing player"*, tied to no named foul at all, and 640(g) and 640(h) reach a major plus a game misconduct and a match penalty — though both get to a late hit only through the sub-section that excepts adult men, so whether they apply in adult men's hockey the book does not say. ⚠️ **And before any of it, check that you may hit at all.** Body checking is banned at USA Hockey 12U and below and in every girls'/women's and non-check adult classification (Rule 604(a)), and at Hockey Canada U13 and below, throughout female hockey and in any Member-approved division (Rule 7.3) — and both lists are floors rather than ceilings, since a local governing body may bar it in any USA Hockey classification. Britain publishes no age, so ask your league — and British women's hockey runs on IIHF Rule 101.1, which allows a hit only where there is a clear intention of playing the puck, and subject to the exception the rule itself then sets out. Where checking is not permitted, everything above is angling, body position and playing the puck.
