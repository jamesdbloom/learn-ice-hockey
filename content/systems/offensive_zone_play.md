## Overview

```facts
Position: You already have the puck in the offensive zone — getting there is Zone Entries, winning it back is Forechecking Systems
Goal: You are not trying to take a shot — you are trying to take a shot from a dangerous place, with the goalie out of position
Key: The cycle, the low-to-high and the net-front battle are machinery for producing that one moment
Risk: Almost every amateur team can get the puck into the offensive zone; far fewer turn possession there into a dangerous shot
```

This document is about what to do **once you already have the puck in the offensive zone** — the zone at the far end of the ice, where the other team's goal is. Getting there is covered in [Zone Entries](zone_entries.md); winning it back is covered in [Forechecking Systems](forechecking_systems.md).

Almost every amateur team can get the puck into the offensive zone. Far fewer can turn possession there into a *dangerous* shot. The gap between those two things is what this document is about: how five players move the puck, and move the goalie, so that when a shot finally happens it comes from somewhere the goalie cannot cover.

The organising idea is simple and it runs through every section below: **you are not trying to take a shot. You are trying to take a shot from a dangerous place, with the goalie out of position.** Everything else — the cycle, the low-to-high, the net-front battle — is machinery for producing that one moment.

## 1. The Goal: Shot Quality, Not Shot Quantity

### The scoring area — "home plate"

```facts
Position: Home plate runs from the tops of the two faceoff circles, in along the tops, down to the dots, then angles in to the goalposts
Key: Shots inside home plate are counted as scoring chances; those from the inner part, close to the net and between the dots, as high-danger chances
Position: The slot is straight out in front of the net between the circles; the high slot runs from the dots up to the tops of the circles
Position: The goalmouth is the immediate net front; the point is up at the blue line; the half-wall is the boards level with the faceoff dot
Key: The faceoff circles and the goal crease are your on-ice ruler for everything below
```

Analysts describe the dangerous part of the offensive zone as a **home-plate shaped area** (as in the baseball plate). It runs from the tops of the two faceoff circles, in along the tops of the circles, down to the faceoff dots, and then angles in to the two goalposts. Shots inside it are counted as **scoring chances**; shots from the inner part of it, close to the net and between the dots, are counted as **high-danger chances**.

![](diagram:oz-home-plate)

Some geography, in plain terms:

- **The slot** is the area straight out in front of the net, between the two faceoff circles.
- **The high slot** is the upper part of it, roughly from the level of the faceoff dots up to the tops of the circles.
- **The goalmouth** is the immediate net front — the few feet either side of the blue paint.
- **The point** is up at the blue line, where defencemen usually stand.
- **The half-wall** is the boards area level with the faceoff dot, between the corner and the point.

For reference, the end-zone faceoff circles have a **15-foot radius** (NHL Rule 1.9) and the goal crease — the blue paint — extends **six feet out from the goal line** at its deepest, with straight side lines one foot outside each post (Rule 1.7). Those two markings are your on-ice ruler for everything below.

### Why location matters so much

The numbers are not close:

- Distance from the net is **"the single most important feature by a wide margin"** in expected-goals models — models that assign every shot a probability of becoming a goal based on where and how it was taken ([Hockey Graphs](https://hockey-graphs.com/2019/08/15/expected-goals-model-with-pre-shot-movement-part-4-variable-importance/)).
- Shooting percentage from **outside** the dangerous area has been **under 5%** across a decade of NHL data (Micah Blake McCurdy / HockeyViz, 2007–2017, [via The Coaches Site](https://members.thecoachessite.com/article/the-problem-with-point-shots-according-to-analytics)).
- Point shots specifically convert at roughly **3% with traffic in front and about 1% without** (Clear Sight Analytics, same source). **⚠️ Attributed but uncheckable** — this comes from the same proprietary tracking as the Valiquette figures above and has never been independently replicated, so treat it as illustrative. **[Shooting](../technique/shooting.md) owns shot-location value**, and gives a separately sourced point-shot conversion of roughly 1–3%; take the number from there rather than from here.
- Shots taken from the middle of the ice are more dangerous than shots from sharp angles, and danger climbs steeply as you close the distance: the NHL.com summary of Sportlogiq data charts *"the percentage of goals that have been scored from the past three seasons by distance in 10-foot increments from the net"*, and says of it that *"even the tiniest bit further in can make a 5% improvement in your chances to score"* ([NHL.com](https://www.nhl.com/kraken/news/seattle-kraken-analytics-with-alison-high-danger-chances-328378484), Alison Lukan, 27 November 2021). **Be careful with that sentence: the underlying chart is a distribution of where goals came from, so the 5% is a step between ten-foot bands of that distribution, not five percentage points added to your own shooting percentage.** The article's own phrasing blurs the two; the figure tells you the direction and steepness of the effect, not its per-shot size.

So a clean look from the slot is worth several times an unscreened shot from the blue line — not a bit more, several times. This is why "just get pucks to the net" is incomplete advice. Volume from the perimeter is not a plan; it is what a team does when it has no plan.

### The two levers you actually have

```facts
Key: There are only two ways to make a shot dangerous — be able to name which one you are pulling at any moment
Action: Move the puck to a dangerous place — carry it, pass it or drive it into the home-plate area before you shoot
Action: Move the goalie — take away their eyes with a screen, make them move laterally with a pass across the middle, or make them commit with a rebound
Priority: The best chances do both at once — a pass across the slot to a player already in the home-plate area
```

There are only two ways to make a shot dangerous, and you should be able to name which one you are pulling at any moment:

1. **Move the puck to a dangerous place.** Get it into the home-plate area before you shoot — by carrying it there, passing it there, or driving it there off a rebound.
2. **Move the goalie so a shot from anywhere becomes dangerous.** Take away their eyes with a screen, make them move laterally with a pass across the middle, or make them commit with a rebound.

The best chances do both at once: a pass across the slot to a player already in the home-plate area. That is the subject of the next section, and it is the most valuable thing you can do with a puck.

## 2. The Royal Road — The Most Valuable Action in Offensive Hockey

### What it is

```facts
Key: The royal road is an imaginary line down the centre of the ice, lengthwise, from the middle of one net to the middle of the other
Key: A royal road pass — also called a slot-line, seam or cross-slot pass — crosses that line in the offensive zone immediately before a shot
Key: Valiquette's split: green shots follow movement across the road, deflections, one-timers, screens and broken plays; red shots are clean looks with none of that
Risk: His percentages are attributed but uncheckable — treat them as illustrative, not as data; the effect itself has been replicated on public data
Risk: Published descriptions of where the line runs disagree — the net-to-net version used here is kept for being the more conservative instruction, not the better supported one
```

The **royal road** is an imaginary line running down the centre of the ice, lengthwise, from the middle of one net to the middle of the other. The term was popularised by former NHL goaltender **Steve Valiquette**, who separated shots into "green" (high-probability — preceded by movement across the road, plus deflections, one-timers, screens and broken plays) and "red" (low-probability — clean looks with no such movement).

> **⚠️ Attributed but uncheckable — treat as illustrative, not as data.** Green shots are reported as accounting for roughly **76% of NHL goals**, with red shots the other 24%. The figure does have a named source, contemporaneous with the data: NHL.com's Kevin Woodley reported in December 2014 that *"Valiquette said 76 percent of goals in the NHL this season have been scored off Green Shots, including 30 percent on plays that involved either a pass or possession moving across the Royal Road,"* and Ken Campbell repeated it in *The Hockey News* in February 2015. Chris Boyle, writing for the OMHA, gives the sample — Valiquette *"reviewed 100 games during the 2014/15 NHL season"* — and breaks the 76% into seven categories.
>
> **What is missing is not the attribution but the data.** The shot-by-shot tracking, the classification rules and the counts have never been published; Clear Sight Analytics' public glossary carries none of it. Woodley's own 2014 article concedes the point: *"There is no database, at least not one available to the public, tracking every shot in the NHL with that kind of detail."* Two further cautions. The **24% is a residual**, not a measured quantity — nobody reports it directly. And the retellings do not reconcile: Boyle's seven green categories sum to 74%, not 76%, and he puts direct red-shot goals at 18% rather than 24%. The direction of the finding is coaching consensus and survives independent testing on public data (see below); the specific number cannot be checked. [Shooting](../technique/shooting.md) carries the same figure with the same label.
>
> **The effect itself, unlike the number, has been replicated on public data.** Alan Wells' analysis of Ryan Stimson's Passing Project — volunteer-tracked and publicly released, *"51,308 shots at 5v5"* counting all attempts rather than shots on goal — puts shooting percentage on attempts preceded by a royal road pass at **15.50%**, far above every other pass type tracked. More importantly, Alex Novet's public expected-goals model built on Corey Sznajder's tracking (~72,000 shots) tested whether the royal road effect is just shot location in disguise, and found it is not: *"there is a big jump in Shapley value when the pass is royal road."* So the mechanism is real and independently supported. It is Valiquette's particular percentages that are not checkable.

> **⚠️ Published descriptions of where the line actually runs do not agree.** This document uses the **net-centre-to-net-centre** version above, because it is the more common description and gives the more conservative coaching instruction. But **both** of the sources closest to Valiquette's own figures describe something narrower and zone-limited: Blue Seat Blogs has it running only from the centre bar of the net to the top of the circles, and NHL.com describes *"a line that halves the offensive zone in two below the tops of the faceoff circles"*. On that reading the road stops at the top of the circles rather than spanning the rink — and it is **two-to-one against the popular version**, so the net-to-net geometry used here is kept for being the more conservative coaching instruction, not for being better supported. The framework is proprietary and the exact geometry has never been published, so the disagreement cannot be resolved from public sources. [Shooting](../technique/shooting.md) sets this out in full.

A **royal road pass** — also called a **slot-line pass**, **seam pass** or **cross-slot pass** — is a pass that crosses that centre line in the offensive zone immediately before a shot.

![](diagram:oz-royal-road)

### Why it beats everything else

```facts
Key: A goaltender's save is mostly positioning, not reflexes — a set goalie is already covering most of the net before the shot leaves
Action: Make them move sideways — crossing the road forces a lateral push across the crease to re-square to the new puck position
Read: They must also re-establish depth and angle, re-find the puck visually, and reset their stick and pads — which is where the five-hole opens
Goal: Get the shot away before they finish that sequence — then it is a desperation save, not a positioning save
```

Because a goaltender's save is mostly *positioning*, not reflexes. A set goalie has already squared up to the puck, cut down the angle, and sealed the ice. They are, in effect, already covering most of the net before the shot leaves.

Now make them move sideways. Crossing the royal road forces the goalie to:

- **Push laterally across the crease** to re-square to the new puck position;
- **Re-establish depth and angle** on the new line, which takes time they may not have;
- **Re-find the puck visually** — their sightline was set to one side of the ice and the puck is now on the other;
- **Reset their stick and pads**, which is where technical errors creep in and the five-hole opens.

If the shot comes before they finish that sequence, they are not making a positioning save — they are making a desperation save. That is a completely different, and much worse, proposition for them.

### The evidence

- Shots following a **royal road / cross-slot pass** finished at **15.50%** — the highest-value pass type measured, roughly double the next one (Alan Wells' analysis of **Ryan Stimson's** Passing Project data, [via NHL.com](https://www.nhl.com/kraken/news/analytics-with-alison-dangerous-passes-327627898); **volunteer-tracked, 51,308 shots at 5v5, counting all attempts rather than shots on goal** — see the sourcing note above, and carry that qualification wherever you quote the figure).
- Royal road passes are the **most important pre-shot movement variable** in expected-goals modelling — shots are much more likely to score when preceded by a pass moving across the ice in front of the goalie ([Hockey Graphs](https://hockey-graphs.com/2019/08/15/expected-goals-model-with-pre-shot-movement-part-4-variable-importance/)).

> **A note on numbers you will hear quoted.** The "33% versus 3%" figure does have a traceable origin, and it is not what most people who quote it think. It comes from Chris Boyle, writing for the OMHA, reporting Valiquette's study: *"Valiquette's study identifies a shot from above the face-off circles and no lateral movement will results in around a 3% chance to score. If the attacking forward is able to cross the Royal Road through the slot, his chances increase to 33%."* Read that carefully. Both numbers are shooting percentages, and the 3% baseline is **a distant shot from above the circles**, not the same shot taken without the crossing. It is not a clean crossed-versus-not-crossed comparison — shot distance is doing a great deal of the work, and quoting it as "cross the road and you triple your chances" overstates what was measured. Beyond that the underlying study is unpublished, and the **20–30%** range you will see in coaching material — which [Special Teams](special_teams.md) does not quote but flags for exactly this reason — has no published primary source behind it at all, so treat both as illustrative rather than exact. **The 15.50% figure above is the best-sourced number here and is the one to quote.** It is an all-situations measurement of one pass type in one tracking project; nobody has published a separate power-play-only conversion rate, so if you see a higher number attached to power-play seam passes, it is not a different measurement of the same thing — it is an unsourced one.

### How to actually create one

```facts
Action: Look across before you look along — your first scan off the wall is the far side of the slot, the backdoor and the weak-side dot
Never: Scan up the wall first — most players do, and never see the seam
Technique: Pass to where the shooter is going, not where they are — a pass into the feet costs the extra touch the goalie needed
Options: Carry it across yourself — the half-wall into the high slot, or curling out from behind the net into the slot, both cross the line
Options: Use the player behind the net — a pass out from below the goal line crosses the road almost by definition
Read: Expect the seam to be defended, and expect it to open only briefly
```

Knowing the concept is worthless without the habits that produce it. Four practical ones:

- **Look across before you look along.** When you receive the puck on the wall, your first scan is the far side of the slot — the **backdoor** (the far post, behind the defence) and the weak-side dot. Only when that is dead do you look at the safe option up the wall. Most players scan in the opposite order and never see the seam.
- **Pass to where the shooter is going, not where they are.** A seam pass into a player's feet takes an extra touch, and the extra touch is exactly the time the goalie needed.
- **Carry it across yourself.** A puck carrier who cuts through the middle drags the goalie laterally the same way a pass does. Driving from the half-wall into the high slot, or curling out from behind the net into the slot, both cross the line.
- **Use the player behind the net.** A pass out from below the goal line to the slot crosses the royal road almost by definition, and the goalie has to turn their head to track it. See section 8.

**The corresponding defensive truth:** taking away the middle of the ice is the whole point of most defensive-zone systems. Expect the seam to be defended, and expect it to open only briefly. See [Defensive Zone Coverage](defensive_zone_coverage.md).

## 3. Offensive Zone Structure — The Five-Man Shape

> **This section describes a coaching choice, not a law of hockey.** The structure below — three forwards rotating in an **attack triangle**, both defencemen holding the blue line, the strong-side defenceman free to pinch — is the **house default** assumed here. It is the most widely taught amateur structure. Realistic alternatives are named at the end of the section. **Find out which one your team plays.**

### F1, F2, F3 are roles, not people

```facts
Convention: F1, F2 and F3 are roles defined by order of arrival — first, second and third to the puck — not by whether you are a centre or a winger
Key: Where arrival order and depth in the zone do not coincide, arrival order is what decides the label
Convention: "Hunt, help, high" is the forecheck shorthand — F1 hunts the puck, F2 helps F1, F3 stays high
Position: One player below the puck — the outlet that lets it go backwards to safety, and the one who can attack the net from behind
Position: One player level with the puck — close support a short pass away, in the inside lane rather than flat on the boards
Position: One player above the puck — the shooting option on a low-to-high and the first line of defence if you lose it
Goal: Give the puck carrier at least two options at all times; players without the puck keep moving to provide them
```

**F1, F2 and F3 are roles defined by order of arrival — who gets to the puck first, second and third — not by whether you are a centre or a winger.** They rotate constantly, and they are reassigned every time the sequence of arrival changes. If the left winger is first in on the puck below the goal line, they are F1; if the centre is the last of the three to arrive and ends up at the top of the circle, they are F3. A common shorthand for the forecheck version is **"hunt, help, high"** — F1 hunts the puck, F2 helps F1, F3 stays high.

In an established possession, order of arrival and depth in the zone usually coincide — the first forward in is the deepest — which is why the roles map so neatly onto the below / level / above shape described next. When they do not coincide, arrival order is what decides the label.

In an established offensive-zone possession, the three forwards should always be arranged so there is:

- **One player below the puck** — deeper toward the goal line than the puck carrier. This is the outlet that lets the puck go backwards to safety, and the player who can attack the net from behind. Without them, every loss of the puck is a clean escape for the defence.
- **One player level with the puck** — close support, usually a short pass away, in the inside lane rather than flat on the boards. This is the player who receives the bump and keeps the possession alive.
- **One player above the puck** — higher in the zone, toward the top of the circles or the point. This player is the shooting option on a low-to-high pass *and* the first line of defence if you lose it.

That "below / level / above" arrangement is what forms the **attack triangle**, and it is the actual content of the advice "keep the triangle." The triangle gives you **width and depth at once**, which means the puck carrier always has at least two passing options at different angles. Coaching material states the requirement plainly: the puck carrier should have **at least two options** at all times, and players without the puck should keep moving to provide them ([Creating Offense with a 5-Man Unit](https://cdn1.sportngin.com/attachments/document/dfc7-2294372/Creating_Offense_5_man_unit_Oct_20.pdf)).

### Rotation: the triangle moves, the shape does not

```facts
Key: The three forwards hold no fixed spots — as the puck moves, everyone rotates so the shape is preserved
Read: Puck from the half-wall down to the corner → the corner player is now F1, the old carrier rotates up, the high player holds
Read: Puck low-to-high to the point → the low forwards attack the net and the slot; the high forward screens or gets to the top of the circle
Read: Puck behind the net → the net-front player becomes level support at the far post; the far-side forward slides into the slot
Never: Let the triangle sit entirely on the perimeter — at least one forward is always in or attacking the home-plate area
```

The three forwards do not hold fixed spots. As the puck moves, everyone rotates so the *shape* is preserved:

- Puck goes from the half-wall down to the corner → the corner player is now F1 (below/level), the old carrier rotates up, the high player holds.
- Puck goes low-to-high to the point → the low forwards attack the net and the slot; the high forward screens or gets to the top of the circle.
- Puck goes behind the net → the player who was net-front becomes the level support at the far post; the far-side forward slides into the slot.

![](diagram:oz-rotation-one-pass-later)

The one non-negotiable: **at least one forward is always in or attacking the home-plate area.** A triangle that is entirely on the perimeter is just three players passing the puck around the outside of a defence that is perfectly happy about it.

### The defencemen's three depths

```facts
Position: On the blue line is the default — the low-to-high outlet, the shooting threat that stops the defence collapsing, and the safety net
Technique: Stay a stride inside the line, not on it — if the puck completely crosses out, the zone is gone and everyone has to re-enter
Rule: A puck leaving with attackers still inside is a delayed off-side, cleared by tagging up — offside under NHL Rule 83.1 is about skates preceding the puck into the zone
Position: Down to the top of the circle is the activated position — shoot from a better angle, walk the line, or receive in the high slot, and still recover
Risk: Below the circles is always a commitment — below the hash marks you are effectively a fourth forward
Never: Go that deep before someone has taken your job at the blue line — before you go, not after
```

An offensive-zone defenceman is always at one of three depths, and the difference between them is the difference between a good defenceman and a liability:

1. **On the blue line (the point).** The default. You are the outlet for low-to-high, the shooting threat that stops the defence collapsing entirely, and the safety net against a counter-attack. Stay a stride *inside* the line, not on it — if the puck completely crosses out, the zone is cleared and everyone has to re-enter. (To be precise, that is not "offside": offside under Rule 83.1 is about attackers' skates preceding the puck *into* the zone. A puck leaving with attackers still inside creates a **delayed off-side**, which your teammates clear by tagging up — see [Rules Primer](../foundation/rules_primer.md). The practical cost is the same, though: you have lost the zone.)
2. **Down to the top of the circle.** The activated position. You step down to shoot from a better angle, to walk the line toward the middle, or to receive a pass in the high slot. You are still recoverable: you can turn and get back to the line before most counter-attacks develop.
3. **Below the circles — too deep.** Not always wrong, but always a commitment. If you go below the hash marks (the short lines at the sides of the faceoff circles), you are effectively a fourth forward and someone must take your job at the blue line *before* you go, not after.

### Who covers for whom

```facts
Convention: If one defenceman leaves the blue line, the other stays — two on the line at all times is the house default, not a law
Options: Teams running a five-man rotation, or green-lighting both defencemen, cover the line with whichever player is highest instead
Action: If a defenceman goes below the line and gets the puck, they play as a forward until the play ends — F3 fills in at the blue line
Action: If a defenceman goes down and does not get the puck, recover defensive position first, then look to attack again
Never: Chase after a failed activation — that is how odd-man rushes against you start
Action: Say it out loud — "I'm going", "I've got you", "hold"; silent rotations are the ones that leave the blue line empty
```

These are the house default's rules, not laws of hockey, and they are short and worth memorising:

- **If one defenceman leaves the blue line, the other stays.** You want two players on the line at all times. This is the near-universal amateur default and the assumption to play on if nobody has told you otherwise — but teams running a **five-man rotation**, or giving both defencemen a standing green light, deliberately break it and cover the line with whichever player is highest. See the pinch note at the end of section 10 and [Forechecking Systems](forechecking_systems.md).
- **If a defenceman goes below the line and gets the puck**, they play as a forward until the play ends — and **F3 must fill in at the blue line for them**.
- **If a defenceman goes down and does *not* get the puck**, their first job is to recover defensive position, then look to attack again. Chasing after a failed activation is how odd-man rushes against you start.
- **Say it out loud.** "I'm going," "I've got you," "hold." Silent rotations are the ones that leave the blue line empty. See [On-Ice Communication](../foundation/on_ice_communication.md).

### Realistic alternatives

```facts
Options: Overload / strong-side load — four players on the puck side, one weak-side outlet; wins board battles, gives up the weak-side seam
Options: Five-man rotation — defencemen rotate into forward spots and forwards cover the line as routine rather than emergency
Options: 1-3-1 spread — one at the point, two half-walls, one bumper in the middle, one net front; common on the power play
Risk: The five-man rotation requires everyone to be comfortable at the blue line
Never: Confuse the bumper with the net-front player — two different jobs at two different depths
```

- **Overload / strong-side load.** Four players are loaded onto the puck side, with only one weak-side outlet. Wins board battles and grinds teams down; gives up the weak-side seam and the quick change of point.
- **Five-man rotation ("position-less" or high cycle).** Defencemen rotate into forward spots and forwards cover the line as a matter of routine rather than emergency. This is how modern NHL teams with skating defencemen play. It requires everyone to be comfortable at the blue line.
- **1-3-1 spread.** One at the point, two on the half-walls, one **bumper** in the middle of the ice between them, one net front. Common on the power play (see [Special Teams](special_teams.md)) and occasionally used at even strength. **The bumper is not the net-front player** — they are two different jobs at two different depths.

## 4. Cycling

### What it is

```facts
Key: A cycle rotates the forwards around the low offensive zone — corner, half-wall, behind the net — protecting the puck from the defence
Action: Move the puck short distances, a few feet at a time, and rotate through the positions
Goal: Make the defence keep re-solving the same problem
```

A **cycle** is a possession tactic in which the forwards rotate around the low offensive zone — corner, half-wall, behind the net — passing the puck short distances to each other while protecting it from the defence. Think of a wheel: the puck moves a few feet at a time, the players rotate through the positions, and the defence has to keep re-solving the same problem.

### Why it works

```facts
Goal: Force defenders to move and decide repeatedly — every rotation asks "collapse low or hold the slot?", and over 30 or 40 seconds someone answers wrong
Key: It is a low-turnover way to hold the puck — board passes that fail do not become odd-man rushes; passes through the middle do
Goal: Tire the defenders — they skate without the puck and cannot change, and tired coverage shrinks toward the puck, which opens the slot
Goal: Buy time for your defencemen and late forwards to join and get set
```

- **It forces defenders to move and decide, repeatedly.** Each rotation asks the defence "do you collapse low or hold the slot?" Over 30 or 40 seconds of this, someone answers wrong.
- **It is a low-turnover way to hold the puck.** Passes along the boards, away from the middle of the ice, do not become odd-man rushes when they fail. Passes through the middle do.
- **It tires the defenders.** They are skating without the puck, which is far more exhausting than skating with it, and they cannot change lines. A tired defender's coverage shrinks toward the puck, which opens the slot.
- **It buys time for your defencemen and late forwards to join.** A cycle that lasts eight seconds is often just a cycle that lasted long enough for your point men to get set.

### The mechanics

```facts
Technique: Low-to-low — pass backwards and down to a teammate arriving behind you, then rotate up into the space they just left
Read: If the checker is still in front of you, rim it — onward around the boards, past the player sealing you to the wall
Read: If the checker has skated past you, reverse it — back against the flow, into the space they have just vacated
Risk: Picking the wrong one of those two is the most common cycle turnover
Technique: Bump pass — fire the puck hard at a spot on the wall, not at your teammate, and call it; a bump nobody expects is a turnover
Technique: Pivot / cutback — cut back up the wall you came down; a defender committed toward the corner cannot turn as fast as you
Options: Scissor / switch — carrier and support skate at each other and cross, support passing on the outside; the carrier may drop it or keep it
```

**Low-to-low (the true cycle).** The puck carrier on the half-wall or in the corner passes the puck **backwards and down** to a teammate arriving behind them, then rotates up into the space their teammate just left. Two variants matter and they are not the same play:

- **Rim** — the puck goes *onward* around the boards, following the curve of the rink, past the checker who is sealing you against the wall. Beats pressure that has you pinned.
- **Reverse** — the puck goes *back against the flow*, into the space a checker has just skated out of. Beats pressure that has over-committed and gone past you.

Picking the wrong one is the most common cycle turnover. The rule of thumb: **if the checker is still in front of you, rim it; if they have skated past you, reverse it.**

**The bump pass off the wall.** Rather than passing to a teammate directly, you fire the puck hard into the boards so it caroms to a spot. It is unblockable by a stick in the passing lane and it does not care about a defender standing between you. Aim at a *spot on the wall*, not at your teammate, and call it — a bump nobody expects is a turnover.

**The pivot / cutback.** You skate down the boards, run out of space, and cut back to skate back up the wall where you came from. This is the single most effective individual cycle move, because a defender who has committed their momentum toward the corner cannot change direction as fast as you can. It buys you two or three seconds and it usually opens the low-to-high pass.

**Low-to-high.** The puck goes up to the point or the top of the circle. Covered in full in section 5.

**The scissor / switch.** The puck carrier and a supporting teammate skate towards each other and cross, with the support player passing on the outside. The carrier can drop the puck or keep it. Defenders who are switching assignments during a scissor are defenders who are briefly covering nobody.

### When to cycle and when to attack the net

```facts
Read: Cycle when the defence is set, structured and between you and the net, and no dangerous shot exists
Read: Cycle when your defencemen are not yet established at the line, or you are protecting a lead and burning clock with possession
Read: Attack immediately when a defender turns their back to the puck, or a defenceman pinches and the ice behind them opens
Read: Attack immediately when the slot is unoccupied by a defender, or the goalie is moving or unset
Read: Attack immediately when your net-front player has beaten their check to the inside
```

**Cycle when:**
- The defence is set, structured and between you and the net;
- No dangerous shot exists and forcing one means a low-percentage attempt or a turnover;
- Your defencemen are not yet established at the line;
- You are protecting a lead or trying to burn clock with possession (see [Game Management](game_management.md)).

**Stop cycling and attack immediately when:**
- A defender turns their back to the puck;
- A defenceman pinches or over-commits and the ice behind them opens;
- The slot is unoccupied by a defender;
- A goalie is moving or unset;
- Your net-front player has beaten their check to the inside.

### The failure: cycling for its own sake

```facts
Key: A cycle is a set-up, not an outcome
Never: Cycle for its own sake — three or four rotations that never threaten the net burn thirty seconds at zero risk to the defence
Goal: End the cycle in one of four things — a shot from home plate, a royal road pass, a low-to-high with a screen, or a net drive
Risk: Nobody in front of the net means even a good shot has no tip, no screen and no rebound support
Read: If the puck never goes above the goal line and never goes to the middle, you are passing around the outside of a box with no reason to move
```

This is the defining amateur error in the offensive zone. A team gets the puck low, cycles it around three or four times, looks organised and busy, and never once threatens the net. The defence is entirely content — you have used up thirty seconds of your own shift, in their zone, at zero risk to them.

**A cycle is a set-up, not an outcome.** If a cycle does not end in one of: a shot from the home-plate area, a royal road pass, a low-to-high with a screen, or a net drive, it did not accomplish anything. Two clear symptoms:

- **Nobody is in front of the net.** Cycling with an empty goalmouth means even a good shot has no tip, no screen and no rebound support.
- **The puck never goes above the goal line and never goes to the middle.** You are passing along the outside of a box that has no reason to move.

## 5. Low-to-High

### The play

```facts
Action: Pass from a low position — corner, half-wall, behind the net — up to a defenceman at the point or the top of the circle, who shoots
Read: The standard way to generate a shot when the defence has collapsed low and taken away everything in tight
Action: The passer feeds the point then supports toward the strong-side hash marks — bail-out outlet and rebound position
Technique: The defenceman walks the puck toward the middle before shooting — it changes the angle, moves the goalie laterally and opens a lane
Position: One forward to the net front to screen and tip; one supporting in the high slot to form a triangle with the point
Read: The weak-side defenceman reads for a rebound squirting to their side and is ready to jump on it
Options: D-to-D across the top first — the goalie has to slide and the shot comes from the other side
```

**Low-to-high** means passing the puck from a low position (corner, half-wall, behind the net) up to a defenceman at the point or the top of the circle, who shoots. It is the standard way to generate a shot when the defence has collapsed low and taken away everything in tight.

The sequence, with everyone's job named:

1. **The passer** feeds the point and then **supports** — skating toward the strong-side hash marks, both to be an outlet if the defenceman needs a bail-out and to be in position for a rebound.
2. **The defenceman** receives it and **walks the puck toward the middle** before shooting. This matters enormously: walking the line changes the angle, forces the goalie to track laterally, and often opens a lane through the shot-blockers.
3. **One forward gets to the net front** to screen and tip.
4. **One forward supports in the high slot**, giving the defenceman a second option and forming a triangle with the point — meaning the puck can be moved again quickly instead of being fired into a shin pad.
5. **The weak-side defenceman** reads for a rebound squirting to their side and is ready to jump on it.

![](diagram:oz-low-to-high)

A common addition is **D-to-D across the top** before the shot: the strong-side defenceman moves it to their partner, the goalie has to slide across, and the shot comes from the other side. On the net-centre-to-net-centre geometry this document uses, that is a royal road crossing at the top of the zone — less valuable than one in the slot, but far better than a static point shot. *(Under the narrower, zone-limited description of the road noted in Section 2, a lateral pass out at the blue line would not cross it at all. The coaching point survives either way: the goalie still has to slide, and a shot arriving before they are set is worth more than one arriving after.)*

### Why it beats a cycle that never shoots

```facts
Goal: Release the puck — a screened point shot produces a rebound, a tip, a scramble, a whistle in your favour and a line change for you
Key: Even at a low conversion rate with traffic, the shot generates chaos in the home-plate area — and that is where goals live
Never: Cycle without ever releasing — it produces none of that
```

Because a shot from the point with a screen and a net-front presence produces things a cycle does not: a **rebound**, a **tip**, a **scramble**, a **whistle in your favour**, a **line change for you** and none for them. Even at a 3% conversion rate with traffic, the shot generates chaos in the home-plate area, and chaos in the home-plate area is where goals live. A cycle that never releases produces none of that.

### The honest caveat

```facts
Key: Low-to-high is a means, not an end — point shots are the lowest-percentage shots on the ice and are blocked more often than any others
Risk: They also generate fewer rebounds than shots from lower in the zone
Priority: Low-to-high is only worth doing when the net front is occupied — a screen, a tip threat and two players hunting the rebound
Never: Fire it from the point with nobody in front — the identical shot is a giveaway with extra steps
```

Low-to-high is a *means*, not an end. Remember the numbers from section 1: point shots are the **lowest-percentage shots on the ice**, they are **blocked more often** than shots from anywhere else, and analysis by Alex Novet found they **generate fewer rebounds** than shots from lower in the zone ([via The Coaches Site](https://members.thecoachessite.com/article/the-problem-with-point-shots-according-to-analytics)).

There is a real-world cautionary tale here. The Seattle Kraken under Dave Hakstol built an offensive-zone scheme heavily around low-to-high and generated the third-most point shots per sixty minutes in the league — while producing shots with the **lowest expected value per shot of any team** ([Sound of Hockey](https://soundofhockey.com/2022/03/30/kraken-coach-dave-hakstols-scheme-and-philosophies-offensive-zone-plays-part-7/), 2021-22 season). **Both quoted phrases were checked against that page verbatim on 28 July 2026 — but neither number is Sound of Hockey's own.** The article credits the point-shot rate to **Corey Sznajder's** manual tracking and the expected-value figure to **TopDownHockey/JFreshHockey**, which it labels "paid access". Neither underlying dataset is public, so this is a secondary source restating two private ones; treat it as a reported anecdote about one team in one season, not as a checkable statistic.

The lesson is not "don't go low-to-high." It is: **low-to-high is only worth doing when the net front is occupied.** A point shot with a screen, a tip threat and two players hunting the rebound is a good play. The identical shot with nobody in front is a giveaway with extra steps.

## 6. Net-Front Play

The net front is the least glamorous and most productive real estate in hockey. It is also the place where players most often do the job badly while believing they are doing it.

### Screening

```facts
Position: At the edge of the blue paint, inside the faceoff dots — roughly six to eight feet out, living just beyond the arc
Technique: Stand between the goalie's eyes and the puck, not between the goalie and the net — slide with the point man as they walk the line
Technique: Chest up and square to the shooter, hips low, feet wide, knees bent — the posture of a squat, not of a bow
Action: Be still at the moment of release — constant shuffling gives the goalie flashes of the puck through the gaps
Never: Watch the shot — look at the puck through your legs or over your shoulder, then get your eyes back to the ice in front of you
Rule: A screen from outside the crease without contact is normally legal, but NHL Rule 69.1 disallows a goal where positioning alone impairs the goalie's ability to move or defend
```

A **screen** is standing in the goaltender's line of sight so they cannot see the puck. **A screen from outside the crease, without contact, is normally legal**, and referees allow it constantly. The one qualification — see the rules section below — is that Rule 69.1 lets a goal be disallowed where an attacker impairs the goalie's ability to move freely or defend the goal *"either by his positioning or by contact"*, so positioning alone can cost you the goal if you crowd him tightly enough. Short of that, standing in front of a goaltender is a legitimate play.

The mechanics of doing it well:

- **Stand between the goalie's eyes and the puck, not between the goalie and the net.** These are different lines, and they change as the puck moves. If the point man walks the line, you must slide with them to stay on the line. A screen that was correct three seconds ago is now just a player standing near the net.
- **Tall chest, low hips — the two halves do different jobs.** Keep your **chest up and square to the shooter** so you present the biggest possible obstruction to the goalie's sightline; crouching, turning sideways or ducking away from the shot all shrink the surface you are blocking and let the goalie find the puck around you. At the same time keep your **hips low and your feet wide, knees bent** — that is what stops a defenceman moving you. Standing tall *and* narrow, with straight legs, is why players get shoved out of the paint; folding at the waist is why goalies see the shot. You want the posture of a squat, not of a bow.
- **Be still at the moment of release.** Plant and obstruct. Constant shuffling gives the goalie flashes of the puck through the gaps.
- **Do not watch the shot.** Look at the puck through your legs or over your shoulder, then get your eyes back to the ice in front of you. Turning your body to follow the flight of the shot is how you both un-screen the goalie and miss the rebound.
- **Position: at the edge of the blue paint, inside the faceoff dots — roughly six to eight feet out from the goal line.** Deep enough to obstruct the goalie's view and to reach rebounds, far enough out to stay clear of the crease. Note the geometry, because it is easy to get wrong: the crease is **8 feet wide at the goal line and 6 feet deep**, because the arc that closes it is struck with a **six-foot radius from the centre of the goal line** (Rule 1.7) — the six feet is the *depth*, not the half-width. So "three feet out from the goal line" is *inside* the paint, not clear of it. Live just beyond the arc.

![](diagram:oz-net-front-screen)

### Tips and deflections

```facts
Technique: Blade on the ice, always — the overwhelming majority of tippable pucks come in low
Key: You are redirecting, not shooting — ten to thirty degrees beats a set goalie, and a big swing usually misses the puck entirely
Technique: Angle the blade, absorb slightly, do not follow through — hard hands send it into the glass
Action: Present a target — hold the blade out in the lane where you want the puck, and shout for it
Rule: A puck contacting an attacking player's stick above the height of the crossbar is not a goal (NHL Rules 80.3 and 78.5(vi)) — where it hits the stick is what counts, so tip low
Rule: A puck deflected in off your skate or body is a good goal with no distinct kicking motion (NHL Rules 78.4 and 78.5(ii)) and no deliberate hand or body bat (78.5(i))
```

A **tip** or **deflection** is changing the direction of an incoming shot with your stick blade. It is devastating because it destroys the goalie's read: they have tracked and set for a puck travelling one way, and it is now travelling another way from six feet out.

- **Blade on the ice, always.** The overwhelming majority of tippable pucks come in low. A stick held off the ice at the net front is a stick doing nothing.
- **You are redirecting, not shooting.** A small change of angle — ten to thirty degrees — is enough to beat a set goalie. A big swing usually misses the puck entirely.
- **Angle the blade, absorb slightly, do not follow through.** Soft hands change direction; hard hands send it into the glass.
- **Present a target.** Shooters at the point aim at the tipper's stick. Hold the blade out in the lane where you want the puck, and shout for it.

**Two rules to know before you tip.** Both are in the NHL rulebook and both cost teams goals every season:

- **A puck that enters the net after contacting an attacking player's stick above the height of the crossbar is not a goal** (Rules 80.3 and 78.5(vi)). Rule 80.3 is the fuller statement: *"When an attacking player causes the puck to enter the opponent's goal by contacting the puck above the height of the crossbar, either directly or deflected off any player or official, the goal shall not be allowed. The determining factor is where the puck makes contact with the stick. If the puck makes contact with the stick at or below the level of the crossbar and enters the goal, this goal shall be allowed."* It is where the puck contacts the stick that matters, not where your hands are. Tip low.
- **A puck deflected into the net off your skate or body is a good goal** — provided there is **no "distinct kicking motion"** (Rules 78.4 and 78.5(ii)), and provided you did not deliberately bat it in with your hand or body (Rule 78.5(i)). So you may absolutely turn your skate to redirect a puck; you may not kick it.

### Hunting rebounds

```facts
Read: Anticipate where it goes — a pad tends to squirt back toward the corner it came from, a blocker to the shooter's off side, a chest to drop in the paint
Risk: Treat those as tendencies, not rules — a goalie angling their surfaces deliberately will break all three on purpose
Action: Shoot it back immediately — do not settle it, do not look up; the window is one or two seconds while the goalie is down
Options: Go to the far post when you get tied up — nobody is covering it and it opens the weak-side chance
Key: The defending team recovers roughly 60% of rebounds (Alex Novet, via The Coaches Site) — you win that race by being there before the shot
```

- **Anticipate where it goes, don't react to where it went.** The rough tendencies: a puck hitting a pad tends to squirt back toward the corner it came from, a puck hitting the blocker toward the shooter's off side, a puck hitting the chest to drop in the paint. **Treat these as tendencies, not rules** — they are coaching craft, not measured rebound distributions, and a goalie who is angling their surfaces deliberately (which is what [Goaltender](../positions/goaltender.md) teaches them to do) will break all three on purpose. Know where the shot is coming from, pre-load your feet that way, and be ready to be wrong.
- **Shoot it back immediately.** Do not settle it, do not look up. The window is one or two seconds, while the goalie is down and out of position.
- **Go to the far post when you get tied up.** If your check has sealed you off from the near side, the far post is a rebound location nobody is covering and it opens the weak-side chance.
- **Understand the odds.** The defending team recovers roughly **60% of rebounds** (Alex Novet, via The Coaches Site). That is exactly why the net-front job is a *job* and not a bonus — you have to be there, deliberately, before the shot, or you lose that race by default.

### Goaltender interference — the actual standard

```facts
Rule: NHL Rule 69.1 — an attacker's position, inside or outside the crease, should not by itself determine whether a goal is allowed
Rule: NHL Rule 69.1 — a goal is disallowed where you impair the goalie's ability to move freely within the crease or defend the goal, by positioning or by contact
Rule: NHL Rule 69.1 — a goal is disallowed where you initiate intentional or deliberate contact with the goalie, in or out of the crease; 69.2 penalises it whether or not a goal is scored
Rule: Outside the crease (NHL 69.1, 69.4), incidental contact is permitted and the goal stands, provided you made a reasonable effort to avoid it
Rule: Inside the crease (NHL 69.3), contact you initiate voids the goal, and a significant vision-obstructing position voids it with no contact at all
Rule: NHL Rule 69.7 — on a rebound, or a simultaneous race for a loose puck, incidental contact is permitted in or out of the crease and the goal is allowed
Rule: USA Hockey Rule 625(b) is a flat position rule — no goal with an attacking player in the crease unless the puck preceded them in, and standing in it costs you the zone
Never: Plant your feet in the blue paint — staying out keeps your goal under NHL rules, and your possession under IIHF and USA Hockey rules
```

This is the most misunderstood rule in the offensive zone, so here is what **NHL Rule 69** actually says.

**The governing premise (69.1):** an attacking player's *position*, whether inside or outside the crease, **should not by itself** determine whether a goal is allowed. Goals are disallowed only if:

1. an attacking player, **by positioning or by contact, impairs the goalkeeper's ability to move freely within the crease or defend the goal**; or
2. an attacking player **initiates intentional or deliberate contact** with the goalkeeper, inside or outside the crease.

"Contact" means any contact between goalie and attacker, **by stick or by any part of the body**.

**Outside the crease (69.1, 69.4):** **incidental contact is permitted and the goal stands**, provided you made a **reasonable effort to avoid** the contact. Contact that is more than incidental disallows the goal. A goalie outside the crease is explicitly **not "fair game"** — but incidental contact is permitted when they are playing the puck outside the crease and you tried to avoid it.

**Inside the crease (69.3):** this is much stricter. If you **initiate contact with a goalie who is in the crease — incidental or otherwise — and a goal is scored, the goal is disallowed.** Further, if you **establish a significant position within the crease so as to obstruct the goalie's vision and impair their ability to defend the goal**, the goal is disallowed even with no contact at all. "Significant position" means your body, or a substantial part of it, in the crease for **more than an instantaneous period**.

> **This is the practical line to remember: screening from outside the blue paint, without contact, is normally legal and you should do it constantly. Screening from inside the blue paint is not** — and even outside it, clause (1) above means positioning alone can void a goal if it stops the goalie moving or defending. Some coaching resources say you may stand in the crease as long as you are "playing the puck" — that overstates it, and Rule 69.3 is explicit that a sustained vision-obstructing position inside the crease will void a goal. Keep your feet out of the paint.

**Three more provisions worth knowing:**

- **Rebounds and loose pucks (69.7):** in a rebound situation, or where you and the goalie are simultaneously going for a loose puck, **incidental contact is permitted inside or outside the crease and the goal is allowed.** This is the exception that lets net-front players do their job in a scramble.
- **Pushed into the goalie (69.1):** if a **defender** pushes, shoves or fouls you into the goalie, that is **not treated as contact initiated by you** — provided you made a reasonable effort to avoid it. Conversely, if **you** push a defender into their own goalie, that **counts as contact initiated by you** and the goal is disallowed.
- **Penalty (69.2):** intentional or deliberate contact with a goalie draws a penalty **whether or not a goal is scored**.

> **Rule-set note — USA Hockey is materially stricter, and it changes how you play the net front.** Everything above is the NHL standard. **USA Hockey Rule 625(b)** does not run the NHL's "position alone is not determinative" test at all. It reads: *"No goal may be scored with an attacking player in the goal crease unless the puck has preceded the player(s) into the goal crease or the goalkeeper is out of the goal crease area."* That is a flat position rule — no contact and no impairment required. The same clause also stops play before you get that far: *"A face-off shall be conducted at the nearest neutral zone face-off spot any time an attacking player stands, holds their stick, or skates through the goal crease provided the puck is in the attacking zone, the attacking team has possession of the puck and the goalkeeper is in contact with the crease."* So under USA Hockey rules a foot in the paint does not just risk the goal — it costs you the zone. The crease for this purpose includes the crease lines themselves and the space above, up to the height of the goal frame. The one relief: if a **defender** physically interferes with you and that is what puts you in the crease, play continues and a legal goal counts.
>
> **The IIHF standard is the NHL's, with one addition.** **IIHF Rule 69** carries the same number and substantially the same text as the NHL's: the same "position alone should not by itself determine whether a goal is allowed" premise, the same two disallowing conditions, the same incidental-contact allowance outside the crease, the same "significant position within the goal crease" test in 69.3 (body or a substantial part of it in the crease for "more than an instantaneous period of time"), and the same rebound exception in 69.7. Two differences worth knowing. The IIHF text says *"relevant contact"* where the NHL says *"contact"*, and tells the referee to weigh *"the degree and nature of the relevant contact with the goalkeeper"* over the goalie's exact location. And IIHF Rule 69.1 adds a provision the NHL does not have: *"If an attacking Player is in the goal crease and does not leave immediately, the Referee has the possibility to stop the game and the subsequent 'face-off' shall take place at the nearest neutral zone face-off spot outside the attacking zone of the offending team."* So internationally, as under USA Hockey, loitering in the paint can cost you the zone without anyone needing to score first.
>
> **The practical consequence is the same instruction under all three, only harder as you go: stay out of the blue paint.** Under NHL rules that keeps your goal; under IIHF and USA Hockey rules it also keeps your possession.
>
> Many rec and beer leagues impose a stricter house rule still — commonly "no contact with the goaltender at all, ever, and any goal with a player in the crease is waved off." That is not just hearsay: the **CARHA Hockey Official Rule Book**, which a large number of Canadian adult leagues play under, writes it into **Rule 66(b)**: *"Unless the puck is in the goal crease area, a player of the attacking side may not stand in the goal crease. If the puck should enter the net while such conditions prevail, the goal shall not be allowed."* Same clause makes any physical contact that impedes the goalkeeper's movement a minor, with no incidental-contact allowance of the kind the NHL and IIHF give you. The one relief is the same as USA Hockey's: if a defender put you there, the goal counts. **Read your own league's rule before you plant yourself in the goalmouth.**

## 7. Working the Half-Wall

The **half-wall** is the boards area level with the faceoff dot, between the corner and the point. It is the winger's home base in the offensive zone (see [Winger](../positions/winger.md)) and the most common place from which offensive-zone possessions are organised.

It is a good place to hold the puck because you have the boards protecting one side, you can see the whole zone, and you are equidistant from four different plays.

### The four options from the half-wall

```facts
Action: Know all four before the puck arrives, and know which one is on
Options: Low — to the corner or behind the net; the safe continuation that feeds the cycle and resets the defence's problem
Options: High — to the point; the low-to-high shot generator, best when the defence has collapsed and left the point unpressured
Options: Middle — the seam pass into the slot or the backdoor; highest value, highest risk, shortest window
Options: Yourself — drive the net or cut to the middle, best when your check is flat-footed or has turned their hips
Never: Arrive on the half-wall with only one option in mind — you will play it regardless of whether the slot was open
```

Know all four before the puck arrives, and know which is on:

1. **Low — to the corner or behind the net.** The safe continuation. Feeds the cycle, keeps possession, resets the defence's problem.
2. **High — to the point.** The low-to-high shot generator. Best when the defence has collapsed low and left the point unpressured.
3. **Middle — the seam pass into the slot or the backdoor.** The royal road pass. Highest value, highest risk, shortest window.
4. **Yourself — drive the net or cut to the middle.** Attack the inside off the wall. Best when your check is flat-footed, has turned their hips, or has over-committed to taking away a pass.

The failure is having only one of these in mind. A player who arrives on the half-wall intending to pass to the point will do that regardless of whether the slot was open.

![](diagram:oz-half-wall-options)

### Buying time

```facts
Technique: Cut back — skate a few feet toward the corner and cut back up the wall; the defender's momentum has to reverse, yours does not
Technique: Delay and curl — turn away from pressure, protect the puck and curl toward the middle into open ice, coming out facing the ice
Technique: Receive with your body between checker and puck, then spin off — turning into pressure immediately is how you get pinned
Never: Put your back to the boards, and never duck — skates parallel to the wall, forearm and hip to the wall, head up; this outranks everything else here
Options: Bump it to the level-support player and take an immediate return pass — it resets the defender's read entirely
Action: Feet moving, always — a stationary player on the wall gets pinned; one with momentum can go three directions
```

You often need two more seconds than you have. Ways to manufacture them:

- **Cut back.** Skate a few feet toward the corner and cut back up the wall. The defender's momentum has to be reversed; yours does not.
- **Delay and curl.** Turn away from the pressure, protect the puck, and curl toward the middle — into open ice, never with your back against the wall; the safety qualifier two bullets down governs this one too. Coming out of the curl you will be facing the ice with your options refreshed.
- **Don't turn into pressure first.** When you receive the puck under pressure, take it with your body between checker and puck, then spin off. Turning into pressure immediately is how you get pinned. **Safety qualifier that outranks all of this:** the orientation to aim for at the wall is skates parallel to the boards, forearm and hip to the wall, head up — never your back to the boards, never duck. Chest-square to the wall is the fallback, not the target. See [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md).
- **Bump it and get it back.** A short pass to the level-support player and an immediate return pass re-sets the defender's read entirely, and often you get the puck back in more space than you gave it away in.
- **Feet moving, always.** A stationary player on the wall gets pinned. A player with momentum can go three directions.

## 8. Behind the Net as an Attacking Position

Behind the net is not where the puck goes to die. It is one of the best attacking positions on the ice, and most amateur teams treat it as a place you end up rather than a place you go on purpose.

### Why it is so good

```facts
Key: From behind the goal line you look up at the whole zone with all five defenders and the goalie in front of you
Key: Nobody can come at you from directly behind, because the net and the end boards are there — that is geometry, not a rule, and you can still be driven into the goal frame
Read: Every time the puck crosses behind the net the goalie must pick a post and rotate — every rotation is a moment they are not square
Key: Passes from below the goal line finished at 6.73%, second only to royal road passes — volunteer-tracked Passing Project data, 51,308 shots at 5v5, all attempts
```

- **You can see everything and nobody can see you the same way.** From behind the goal line you are looking *up* at the whole zone with all five defenders and the goalie in front of you. You have the best view on the ice and the defence has to defend with their backs to their own net.
- **Nobody can come at you from directly behind** — there is nothing there but the net and the end boards, so a defender can only pressure you from one of two sides and you can see them coming. **That is geometry, not immunity.** Checking from behind is a penalty *"anywhere on the playing surface"* (NHL Rule 43.3), and USA Hockey 608(b) names the **goal frame** alongside the boards precisely because players get driven into it back here. Keep your head up and know who is arriving.
- **The goalie has to turn their head.** Every time the puck crosses behind the net, the goalie must decide which post to seal and rotate to track you. Every rotation is a moment when they are not square to a shot.
- **Passes out to the slot are exceptionally dangerous.** Passes originating below the goal line finished at **6.73%** — the second-most dangerous pass type measured, behind only royal road passes (Alan Wells' analysis of **Ryan Stimson's** Passing Project data, [via NHL.com](https://www.nhl.com/kraken/news/analytics-with-alison-dangerous-passes-327627898); **volunteer-tracked, 51,308 shots at 5v5, counting all attempts rather than shots on goal**).

### What to do from there

```facts
Action: Pass out to the slot — the primary play, not the last resort; look for the high-slot forward or the backdoor player
Options: The wrap-around — carry round the post and stuff it far side before the goalie seals; best as a threat, because sealing hard opens the slot pass
Options: The walkout — come out into the slot yourself on your forehand, crossing the royal road with the puck
Options: The bank off the goalie — from a tight angle behind the goal line, off the back of their pads into the net; legal, and occasionally free
Options: Reverse or rim to restart the cycle if nothing is on — possession retained is a perfectly good outcome
Rule: The goalkeeper's restricted area restricts only the goaltender (geometry NHL Rule 1.8, penalty 27.8 / 63.2(viii)) — skaters may go anywhere behind the net at any time
Read: In England and Wales the restricted area's status is unsettled (Rules & Regulations 22.3) — plan as though their goalie may retrieve a dumped puck
```

- **Pass out to the slot.** This is the primary play, not the last resort. Look for the high-slot forward or the backdoor player. Because the pass comes from below the goal line, it crosses the royal road and arrives at a player already inside the home-plate area — the two most valuable properties a pass can have.
- **The wrap-around.** Carry the puck around the post and try to stuff it in on the far side before the goalie can push across and seal. It works when the goalie has committed to the near post, and it is at its best as a *threat*: a goalie who fears the wrap seals hard to the post, which opens the pass to the slot.
- **The walkout.** Come out from behind the net into the slot yourself, on your forehand, with the puck. You cross the royal road carrying the puck and you arrive in the most dangerous area on the ice.
- **The bank off the goalie.** Legal, and occasionally free. From a tight angle behind the goal line you can bank the puck off the back of the goalie's pads into the net.
- **Reverse or rim to restart the cycle.** If nothing is on, feed it back out to the corner you came from or rim it to the far corner and rotate. Possession retained is a perfectly good outcome.

![](diagram:oz-behind-the-net)

> **Rules note — the trapezoid does not apply to you.** The **goalkeeper's restricted area** (the trapezoid behind the net — geometry from NHL **Rule 1.8**, penalty from **Rules 27.8 / 63.2(viii)**; never take dimensions from 27.8, whose "six feet from either goal post" is stale pre-2014 wording) restricts only the **goaltender's** ability to play the puck behind the goal line. Skaters may go anywhere behind the net at any time. Also note that the trapezoid exists in the NHL, KHL and IIHF play but **not** in most rec, beer-league and youth associations — which changes the calculus of dumping the puck in, because their goalie may be allowed to retrieve it anywhere. **🇬🇧 In England and Wales, treat this as unsettled.** The IIHF book carries the rule (Rule 27.7, not the NHL's 27.8), but England Ice Hockey's *Rules & Regulations* 22.3 say the restricted area is *"not currently enforced"* and the 2025-26 In-House Rules do not mention it — though their Introduction says *"unless otherwise stated, all rules will be enforced in line with the IIHF Rule Book"*, which points the other way. For you as the attacking team the safe assumption runs the other way: plan as though their goalie **may** retrieve the puck, ask your league, and look behind the net to see whether the lines are painted at all. Treat an unmarked sheet as a hint rather than a ruling: IIHF 27.7 turns on *"the position of the puck"*, not on the markings.

## 9. Attacking Off the Rush vs Attacking Off a Set Possession

These are two different games and most amateur teams have only one of them.

### Off the rush

```facts
Read: You enter with speed and attack before the defence is set — the chance comes from numbers and momentum
Key: Chances appear in the first three or four seconds and then vanish; speed and the moment of entry are the resource
Options: The late-arriving trailer into the high slot, the drive to the net that occupies a defender, and the delay — curling back to let a second wave arrive
Key: Shots off the rush are more dangerous than equivalent shots from a set possession, because the goalie is also moving and re-establishing depth
```

You enter the zone with speed and attack before the defence is set. The chance comes from **numbers and momentum** — a 2-on-1, a 3-on-2, a trailer arriving late into space.

- The defining resource is **speed and the moment of entry**. Chances appear in the first three or four seconds and then vanish.
- The high-value plays are the **late-arriving trailer** into the high slot, the **drive to the net** that occupies a defender, and the **delay** — curling back up toward the blue line to let a second wave arrive.
- Shots off the rush are more dangerous than equivalent shots from a set possession, because the goalie is also moving and re-establishing depth.
- Full treatment of getting into the zone in the first place is in [Zone Entries](zone_entries.md).

### Off a set possession

```facts
Read: The defence is established, everyone is back and nothing is open — the chance comes from movement and patience
Key: The defining resource is time on the puck and the willingness to hold it
Options: Cycle, low-to-high with a screen, seam pass, net-front chaos, behind-the-net feed
Read: Coverage lapses arrive after a long shift of defending — a defence that has been chasing for forty seconds is about to make a mistake
```

The defence is established, everyone is back, and nothing is open. The chance comes from **movement and patience** — cycling, rotating, forcing the defence to move until coverage breaks.

- The defining resource is **time on the puck and the willingness to hold it**.
- The high-value plays are the ones in sections 4 to 8: cycle, low-to-high with a screen, seam pass, net-front chaos, behind-the-net feed.
- Coverage lapses tend to arrive after a long shift of defending — a defence that has been chasing for forty seconds is a defence about to make a mistake.

### Why having only one is a problem

```facts
Risk: A rush-only team has nothing once the rush is denied at the blue line — they enter, lose it, and go back the other way
Risk: A cycle-only team never threatens quickly, so the defence gets set every time and the cycle only ever produces perimeter shots
Action: Have a plan for the second wave — a chip past the defenceman, a soft area dump, or a controlled retreat and re-attack
Action: When a cycle produces a neutral-zone turnover, somebody must be anticipating and going the other way with speed
```

- **A rush-only team** dies against any structured defensive team, because when the rush is denied at the blue line they have nothing. They enter, lose the puck, and go back the other way. Their offensive-zone time is measured in seconds.
- **A cycle-only team** is easy to defend because it never threatens quickly. The defence gets set every single time, and the cycle only ever produces perimeter shots.

**How to have both:** the practical test is what your team does on the *second* wave. When the rush is stopped at the blue line, do you have a plan to establish possession — a chip past the defenceman, a soft area dump, a controlled retreat and re-attack? And when a cycle produces a turnover in the neutral zone, is anyone anticipating and going the other way with speed? See [Risk Management](../hockey-iq/risk_management.md) and [Scanning and Anticipation](../hockey-iq/scanning_and_anticipation.md).

## 10. Regaining Possession After a Shot

**Treat the seconds immediately after a shot as the most dangerous moment in a possession — and this one is measured.** The NHL's public play-by-play feed timestamps every shot and every goal, so the hazard can be read straight off it. Across games 2024020001–2024020400 of the 2024-25 regular season (`api-web.nhle.com/v1/gamecenter/{id}/play-by-play`), a team that put a shot on goal scored again within the next **three seconds 1.37%** of the time, against roughly **0.26%** for a three-second window picked at random — about **five times** the base rate. Put the other way round: **11.4% of all goals came within three seconds of a preceding shot on goal by the same team**, and 19.7% within ten. These are figures derived from the raw feed rather than published statistics, but the direction is not in doubt, and it agrees with the rebound-recovery figure quoted earlier in this document and with the shot-location values [Shooting](../technique/shooting.md) owns. The goalie has committed, is often down, and is out of position. The puck is loose in the most dangerous area on the ice. Every defender has just been pulled toward the shot.

This is also the moment most amateur teams stop playing — the shot goes in, everyone watches, and the defence walks out with it.

### F3's job

```facts
Convention: In the house-default structure F3 is the high forward — a coaching choice, not a law
Position: Be the safety — at or near the top of the circle, so a rebound straight out is yours and a counter-attack has to go through you
Action: Cover the vacated point if a defenceman activates — F3 takes the blue line in the house-default shape, which wants two on the line
Options: Under a five-man rotation whichever player is highest covers; a third variant slides the weak-side defenceman across to the middle instead
Read: If the rebound squirts to the high slot, jump — you are the closest player with forward momentum
Position: The other two forwards go to the net — one to the front for the screen and tip, one hunting the strong-side rebound
```

In the house-default structure, **F3 is the high forward** and their responsibilities on a shot are, in order:

1. **Be the safety.** Sit at or near the top of the circle so a rebound that comes straight out is yours, and so a counter-attack has to go through you.
2. **Cover the vacated point if a defenceman activates.** If the strong-side defenceman pinches or dives to the middle, **F3 takes the blue line** — in the house-default five-man shape described earlier, which wants two players on the line at all times. This is a coaching choice, and a common alternative is the **five-man rotation**, where nobody has a fixed line job and whichever player is highest at that moment covers, defenceman or forward. A third variant simply accepts one player on the line while the D is down and asks the *weak-side* defenceman to slide across to the middle instead. Find out which your team plays — the five-man shape described earlier in this document is a default, not a law.
3. **Read the rebound and jump.** If the puck squirts to the high slot, you are the closest player with forward momentum. That is a high-danger chance handed to you.

The other two forwards go **to the net**: one to the front for the screen and tip, one hunting the strong-side rebound.

### The defenceman's pinch

```facts
Read: Pinch when you can arrive on contact — "if you can't arrive on contact, don't go"; on an uncontested race for a loose puck or a rim, arrive clearly first
Read: Pinch when the winger is flat-footed or facing the glass, when a forward can rotate high to cover your line, or on a loose puck, a bobble or a tired line
Never: Pinch when your forwards are already below you, when the opponent has turned up ice with speed, when protecting a narrow lead late, or when your partner has not seen you go
Technique: Stick in the passing lane before your feet commit; angle the winger into the boards, never toward the middle; close with short explosive steps
Priority: Take the player or take the puck — achieve neither and you have created an odd-man rush against your own team
Rule: A player facing the glass is defenceless — NHL Rule 41.1 puts the onus on you to avoid or minimise contact, and driving into their back is a major with no minor option (NHL and IIHF Rule 43.2)
Rule: Body checking is banned at USA Hockey 12U and below, in all girls'/women's and all non-check adult classifications (Rule 604(a)), and at Hockey Canada U13 and below and throughout female hockey (Rule 7.3)
Rule: Neither the IIHF Rule Book nor the In-House Rules state an age at which body checking becomes legal — ask your league (UK and England Rules owns this)
Rule: NHL Rule 56.2(iii) is a minor on any player who deliberately checks an opponent not in possession of the puck — once the puck is gone, so is your check
Action: If the pinch fails, retreat toward the slot, not along the boards — following the puck down the wall abandons the middle
Convention: How aggressively defencemen pinch is a coaching choice — some green-light both, some only the strong side, some forbid going below the circles; ask
```

A **pinch** is stepping down from the offensive blue line to keep the puck in the zone or to win a puck on the wall. **Do not confuse it with a step-up**, which is challenging an attacker in the neutral zone or at your own line — different play, different zone.

The decision criteria, which are consistent across coaching sources:

**Pinch when:**
- You can **arrive on contact** — you and the puck get there at the same time. "If you can't arrive on contact, don't go." That is the test when an opposing player is arriving too: getting there level lets you play the puck or seal them against the wall, and arriving a beat late is the failure, because once the puck is gone you may not hit the man (Rule 56.2, below). On an **uncontested race for a loose puck or a rim**, the bar is higher — you need to arrive **clearly first**, since there is nobody to seal and second place just means your back is to the play. [Forechecking Systems](forechecking_systems.md) applies the same two-part test.
- The opposing winger is **flat-footed or facing the glass**, not already turned up ice with speed.
- A forward is **able to rotate high** and cover your line.
- It is a **loose puck, a bobble or a tired line** — the best windows.

**Do not pinch when:**
- Your forwards are already below you and nobody can cover the line;
- The opponent has already turned up ice with speed and their head up;
- You are protecting a narrow lead late (see [Game Management](game_management.md));
- Your partner has not seen you go.

**Technique:** stick in the passing lane *before* your feet commit; angle the winger into the boards, never toward the middle; use the boards to box them out; close with short explosive steps. The governing principle is **"take the player or take the puck"** — if you achieve neither, you have created an odd-man rush against your own team.

> **⚠️ "Take the player" has a second limit, and it is the more important one.**
> A winger *facing the glass* is a defenceless player. That posture appears in
> the read above because it tells you they cannot escape with the puck — it is
> not permission to hit them. NHL Rule 41.1 puts the onus on you: *"The onus is
> on the player applying the check to ensure his opponent is not in a defenseless
> position and if so, he must avoid or minimize contact."* Driving into their
> back is checking from behind, which under NHL 43.2 and IIHF 43.2 has **no minor
> option at all** — it is a major with an automatic game misconduct, and Hockey
> Canada's rule names this exact scenario, a player digging a puck out along the
> boards, driven head-first into them. Seal the wall, take the puck, or angle
> them off it. [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md)
> §5 and §6 own this and outrank anything in this section.
>
> **And check that you may hit at all.** Body checking is prohibited under USA
> Hockey Rule 604(a) at 12U and below, in every girls'/women's classification and
> in every non-check adult classification, and under Hockey Canada at U13 and
> below and throughout female hockey. Most adult rec leagues do not permit it
> either, and Britain publishes no age — so ask your league. Where checking is
> not permitted, everything here is angling, body position and playing the puck.

> **"Take the player" has a legal limit.** You may finish a check on the player who *has* the puck, and you may use your body to seal them against the wall while contesting a puck you can both reach. You may **not** hit a player who has already moved the puck: NHL Rule 56.2(iii) makes it a minor *"on any player who deliberately checks an opponent, including the goalkeeper, who is not in possession of the puck."* On a pinch this happens constantly — the winger chips it past you and you arrive a beat late. Once the puck is gone, so is your check. Play the puck instead.

**If the pinch fails:** retreat toward the **slot**, not along the boards. Skating back down the wall follows the puck and abandons the middle; cutting to the slot re-establishes your gap and delays the attack until support arrives.

> **How aggressively defencemen pinch is a coaching choice.** Some teams give both defencemen a green light in the offensive zone; some allow the strong-side defenceman only; some instruct defencemen never to pinch below the top of the circles. **Ask.** A defenceman pinching in a system that does not expect it is the single most common source of odd-man rushes against amateur teams.

## 11. When to Shoot and When Not To

### Shoot when

```facts
Read: Inside the home-plate area with any kind of look — almost any shot from there beats almost any pass from there
Read: The goalie is unset, moving or screened
Read: You have a lane — a genuine one, not the memory of one
Read: There is someone at the net front — a tip threat and a rebound hunter make it a different play
Read: A rebound is available and the goalie is down — shoot immediately, do not settle it
```

- You are **inside the home-plate area** with any kind of look. Almost any shot from there beats almost any pass from there.
- The goalie is **unset, moving, or screened**.
- You have **a lane** — a genuine one, not the memory of one.
- There is **someone at the net front**. A shot with a tip threat and a rebound hunter is a different play from the same shot with nobody there.
- **A rebound is available and the goalie is down.** Shoot immediately. Do not settle it.

### Do not shoot when

```facts
Never: Fire into a shin pad from the point with the lane blocked — a blocked point shot usually goes the other way with your team spread out
Never: Shoot when a better shot is one pass away — specifically, a pass across the royal road
Never: Shoot with nobody at the net front — you are giving the puck to the goalie and starting their breakout
Never: Shoot from a sharp angle with no traffic — goalies seal the post, and there is nothing to hit and no rebound to chase
Never: Treat the cross-slot finishing rate and the point-shot conversion rate as a before-and-after pair — different studies, measuring different things
Priority: Act on the direction — a shot after the goalie has had to move across is worth materially more, so the half-second is usually worth it
```

- The lane is blocked and you are firing into a shin pad from the point. A blocked point shot is worse than no shot: it usually goes the other way, and your team is spread out.
- A **better shot is one pass away** — specifically, a pass across the royal road. **Do not put the two numbers side by side, though**, however tempting it is: the **15.50%** is a finishing rate on shots following a cross-slot pass, measured all-situations in the Passing Project, while the **3%** is a Clear Sight Analytics figure — whether you read it as Boyle's *distant shot from above the circles with no lateral movement* or as this document's point-shot-with-traffic rate in Section 1, it comes from the proprietary tracking that Sections 1 and 2 flag as attributed-but-uncheckable. They are different studies measuring different things, and Section 2 explains at length why treating them as a before-and-after pair overstates what was measured — on the Boyle reading, most of that gap is shot distance rather than the crossing. The direction is what you should act on: **a shot taken after the goalie has had to move across is worth materially more than the same-ish shot taken before**, so the half-second is usually worth it.
- **Nobody is at the net front.** You are giving the puck to the goalie and starting their breakout.
- You are on a **sharp angle with no traffic**. Goalies seal the post; there is nothing to hit and no rebound to chase.

### What to shoot *for*

```facts
Mindset: From the perimeter you are not trying to score — you are trying to manufacture a second chance
Technique: Shoot for a rebound — low, to the far pad, or off the pads; chest and glove get swallowed and the whistle goes
Technique: Shoot for a tip — low, hard, along the ice, through the screen, at the tipper's stick, and say who you are shooting at
Options: Shoot to be missed — a hard shot off the end boards to a spot for a teammate is a legitimate pass; say it first
Never: Pick corners against a set, sighted goalie — aim for a corner only when they cannot see it or cannot get there
```

This is the part most players never think about. From the perimeter, **you are not trying to score. You are trying to manufacture a second chance.**

- **Shoot for a rebound.** Pucks aimed low, to the far pad, or off the pads generally produce controllable rebounds. Pucks aimed at the goalie's chest and glove do not — they get swallowed and the whistle goes.
- **Shoot for a tip.** Low, hard, along the ice, through the screen, at the tipper's stick. Say who you are shooting at. The tipper's job is to present a blade; yours is to hit it.
- **Shoot to be missed.** A shot deliberately aimed to miss wide can be a play — a hard shot off the end boards to a specific spot for a teammate is a legitimate pass. Say it first.
- **Aim for a corner only when the goalie cannot see it or cannot get there.** Picking corners against a set, sighted goalie is the lowest-percentage thing you can do with a puck.

### Getting pucks through from the point

```facts
Key: Your job is not to shoot harder, it is to shoot through — point shots are blocked more than shots from anywhere else on the ice
Technique: Walk the line first — two or three strides toward the middle changes the angle and moves the shot-blocker's feet
Technique: Change the release point — a quick snap from a different spot beats a blocker who has already dropped
Technique: Take something off it — a slower, lower shot that gets through and gets tipped beats a 95 mph slapshot into a shin pad
Options: Fake the shot — a blocker on the ice opens the lane for the real one, and often opens the seam pass instead
Options: Take the D-to-D first — it drags both the blocker and the goalie, and you shoot into a lane just vacated
```

Point shots are blocked more than shots from anywhere else on the ice. If you are a defenceman, your job is not to shoot harder — it is to shoot *through*:

- **Walk the line first.** Two or three strides toward the middle changes the angle and moves the shot-blocker's feet. A defenceman who receives and fires from the same spot is shooting at a stationary wall.
- **Change the release point.** A quick snap shot from a different spot than the one they set up for beats a blocker who has already dropped.
- **Take something off it.** A deliberately slower, lower shot that gets through and gets tipped beats a 95 mph slapshot into a shin pad, every time.
- **Fake the shot.** A shot fake that puts a blocker on the ice opens the lane for the real one, and often opens the seam pass instead.
- **Take the D-to-D first.** Moving it across the top drags both the blocker and the goalie, and you shoot into a lane that has just been vacated.

More detail on mechanics is in [Shooting](../technique/shooting.md).

## 12. Offensive-Zone Faceoffs

An offensive-zone faceoff is the only moment in the game where you get to arrange all five players exactly where you want them, with the puck about to arrive at a known place. It is the closest hockey gets to a free kick, and most amateur teams waste it entirely.

Full treatment of the draw itself — technique, stick placement, the violations that get your centre ejected — is in [Faceoffs](faceoffs.md). What matters here is what happens in the second *after* the draw.

### Set plays off a win

```facts
Key: The point of a set play is that four players start moving on the drop, before anyone knows who won it
Options: Win back to the point and screen immediately — inside winger straight to the net front, outside winger to the strong-side rebound spot
Options: Win back to a winger set up for a one-timer — they must be aligned as a shooter before the drop, not as a defender
Options: Win it forward — attack directly; high reward, but it puts your centre below the puck and fails badly if the draw is lost
Options: Tie up the opposing centre's stick and let a winger sweep in for the loose puck — useful when their centre is dominant on draws
Options: Delay for the screen — the defenceman skates laterally for a beat first; a shot half a second late with a screen beats one on time without
Risk: A winger creeping into the circle before the drop is encroachment, and it gets your centre thrown out of the draw
Never: Reach the whistle without everyone knowing the play — the common failures are a net-front player arriving after the shot and a shot into the first shin pad
```

The point of a set play is that four players start moving on the drop, before anyone knows who won it. Common families:

- **Win back to the point, screen immediately.** The centre draws the puck back to the strong-side defenceman; the inside winger goes straight to the net front to screen; the outside winger takes the strong-side rebound position. Simplest, most reliable, and it converts because everyone was already moving.
- **Win back to the winger for a one-timer.** The centre draws it back to a winger set up in the shooting position (usually a left shot on the right circle, or vice versa) who shoots first-time. Requires the winger to be aligned as a shooter before the drop, not as a defender.
- **Win it forward / "quick strike."** The centre draws the puck forward or to the inside and attacks directly. High reward if the opposing centre is slow to react, but it puts your centre below the puck and it fails badly if the draw is lost.
- **Tie up and let a winger take it.** The centre does not try to win the puck cleanly but locks up the opposing centre's stick, and a winger sweeps in for the loose puck. Useful when the opposing centre is dominant on draws. Watch the **encroachment** rule — a winger creeping into the circle before the drop gets your *centre* thrown out of the draw.
- **Delay for the screen.** The defenceman receives the win but skates laterally for a beat before shooting, giving the net-front player time to establish. A shot half a second late with a screen beats a shot on time without one.

Whatever your team runs, **everyone must know it before the whistle**, and the two most common failures are a net-front player who arrives after the shot and a defenceman who fires it into the first shin pad.

### Structure off a loss

```facts
Mindset: Assume you will lose roughly half the draws — structuring for the loss is the other half of the plan, not defeatism
Action: One forward pressures immediately, usually the winger nearest the puck, to deny the clean breakout pass and force a rim or a chip
Action: The other two forwards turn and skate rather than watch — a forward who is stationary when the puck is lost is beaten
Read: The defencemen hold the line and read — is this a rim to be intercepted, or a clean exit to be retreated against?
Convention: The strong-side defenceman is the one most likely to pinch on a rim; the weak-side one covers the middle
Key: Having a defined assignment for the loss is what lets you be aggressive on the win
```

Assume you will lose roughly half the draws. Structure for the loss is not defeatism, it is the other half of the plan.

- **One forward pressures immediately** — usually the winger nearest the puck — to deny the clean breakout pass and force a rim or a chip.
- **The other two forwards get defensive momentum going**, turning and skating rather than watching. A forward who is stationary when the puck is lost is beaten.
- **The defencemen hold the line and read**: is this a rim to be intercepted, or a clean exit to be retreated against? The strong-side defenceman is the one most likely to pinch on a rim; the weak-side one covers the middle.
- **Nobody is a spectator.** The most common goal against off an offensive-zone draw is not a great play by the other team; it is four players still processing the fact that they lost the puck.

The general principle is worth stating plainly: **having a defined assignment for the loss is what lets you be aggressive on the win.** Teams play tentatively on offensive-zone draws precisely because they have no plan for losing them.

## Common Mistakes

- **Treating "get pucks to the net" as a strategy.** Volume from the perimeter converts at under 5%, and about 1% from the point without traffic (that point-shot figure is attributed but uncheckable — see section 1; [Shooting](../technique/shooting.md) owns shot-location value). Shot volume without shot quality is busywork.
- **Cycling with nobody in front of the net.** The single most common offensive-zone failure. A cycle that never produces a net-front presence or a shot from the home-plate area has accomplished nothing except tiring out your own line.
- **Scanning up the wall before scanning across the middle.** By the time you have looked at the safe option, the seam has closed. Look across first, always.
- **Passing to a teammate's current position instead of where they are going.** In the slot, the extra touch is the difference between a goal and a save.
- **Standing still on the half-wall.** A stationary player against the boards gets pinned and stripped. Cutbacks and curls are what buy the two extra seconds you need.
- **Watching your own shot from the net front.** Turning to follow the flight un-screens the goalie and takes you out of the rebound. Look at the puck, then get your eyes back down.
- **Standing in the blue paint to screen.** Legal screening happens *outside* the crease. Rule 69.3 will void a goal when an attacker establishes a significant vision-obstructing position inside the crease, even with no contact.
- **Tipping with a high stick.** A puck contacting your stick above the height of the crossbar is not a goal (Rule 80.3). Keep the blade on the ice — which is where most tippable pucks are anyway.
- **Two defencemen leaving the blue line at once.** Or one leaving and no forward filling in. This is the mechanism behind most odd-man rushes against attacking teams.
- **Pinching without arriving on contact.** "Take the player or take the puck." Half-hearted pinches create 3-on-2s going the other way.
- **Pinching to hit a winger who is facing the glass.** They cannot see you and cannot protect themselves, and the onus is on you to avoid or minimise the contact. Take the puck.
- **Retreating along the boards after a failed pinch.** Cut to the slot instead. Following the puck down the wall abandons the middle of the ice.
- **Quitting after the shot.** The two seconds after a shot are the most dangerous moment of your entire possession. Most teams spend them watching.
- **Having exactly one way to attack.** Rush-only teams are shut down by any structured defence; cycle-only teams never threaten before the defence is set.
- **Not knowing your team's rules for defenceman activation.** Whether your defencemen pinch, and how deep they may go, is a coaching decision that varies enormously. Ask before your first shift.

## Key Takeaways

1. **You are not trying to shoot — you are trying to shoot from a dangerous place with the goalie out of position, and location is worth more than anything else.** Distance from the net is the single biggest factor in whether a shot becomes a goal; shooting percentage from outside the home-plate area is under 5% (a decade of NHL data), and roughly 1% from the point with no traffic — that point-shot figure being **attributed but uncheckable** proprietary tracking, so treat it as illustrative, and take shot-location value from [Shooting](../technique/shooting.md), which owns it.
2. **The royal road pass is the most valuable action in offensive hockey.** A pass crossing the centre line of the ice before a shot finished at 15.50% — far ahead of any other pass type — because the goalie cannot re-square, re-find the puck and reset their stick in the time available. **That figure carries its qualification wherever it goes:** it is volunteer-tracked Passing Project data, 51,308 shots at 5-on-5, counting all attempts rather than shots on goal, so it is **not** comparable with the shooting percentages in point 1.
3. **Look across the middle before you look up the wall.** The seam is open briefly and closes permanently. Scanning in the wrong order is why most players never see it.
4. **A cycle is a set-up, not an outcome, and low-to-high is only worth doing when the net front is occupied.** If the cycle doesn't end in a shot from the slot, a seam pass, a screened point shot or a net drive, it did nothing — and the same point shot is a good play with a screen and two rebound hunters, a giveaway without them.
5. **Screening the goalie without contact, from outside the blue paint, is normally legal — and you should do it constantly.** Two limits: inside the paint, a significant vision-obstructing position voids the goal; and anywhere, positioning that impairs the goalie's ability to move freely or defend the goal can void it too, contact or not (Rule 69.1).
6. **Behind the net is an attacking position, not a dead end.** You have the best view on the ice, nobody can come at you from directly behind because the net and the end boards are there — geometry, not a rule, and you can still be driven into the goal frame — and passes from below the goal line finished at 6.73% — second only to royal road passes, and measured the same way, in the same volunteer-tracked 5-on-5 Passing Project sample of all shot attempts.
7. **The seconds immediately after a shot are the most likely time to score, and the defending team recovers about 60% of rebounds.** You win that race by deciding to be there before the shot, not by reacting after it.
8. **Keep the shape: one player below the puck, one level with it, one above it, and two on the blue line.** That two-on-the-line rule is the house default in this document, and under it a forward fills in when a defenceman leaves — without being asked. Other teams run a five-man rotation with no fixed line job, or slide the weak-side defenceman across instead; find out which yours plays.
9. **Build both attacks.** Rush offence lives on speed and numbers in the first three seconds; set offence lives on patience and rotation over thirty. Teams that only have one get shut down by anyone competent.
10. **Almost everything in this document is a coaching choice, not a law.** Triangle rotation versus overload, whether defencemen pinch, who takes the net front — all vary by team. Find out what your team plays before you decide someone else is out of position.
