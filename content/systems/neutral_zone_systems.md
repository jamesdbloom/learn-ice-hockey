## Key focus

**Know which structure your team is playing this shift, and play the same one as the other four.** It is a coaching choice, so find out the default and the call to switch. Half a unit pressuring while half holds back opens a seam through your own team.

**Read the first opponent who comes at you.** Hard and straight means pressure — beat it with one early pass. Arcing across means they are steering you into a trap — move the puck rather than carrying it where they send you.

**When your team loses the puck, get on the defensive side of your check and track home through the middle, head up.** Do not hang at the red line for a breakaway pass unless your coach has made you the stretch option. Your defencemen can only hold a tight gap if you are coming back.

**One forward in each lane — left wall, middle, right wall — and hold your lane when the puck is in another.** Two in one lane lets one defender cover both. Support here means being somewhere different, not being close. Call the lane you are in.

**Receive the puck moving.** On a regroup, curl back with the puck and turn up into your lane as it moves across, so you take the pass at speed. Do not stand and call for it, and do not leave before your defenceman has it.

**With no good way forward, turn the puck back and come again rather than forcing the entry.** A forced entry into a set structure usually ends your possession. If you put it in deep, get it over the red line first so it is not icing (some CARHA leagues use the blue lines — ask yours), and chase it unless you are changing.

**At your own blue line, stand up only when you have help behind you — otherwise give ground, and call which one you are doing.** **Standing up is refusing ground with your feet, not building speed into a hit** — a head-down carrier is not an invitation to arrive harder, and that is the picture the charging rules reach.

## Overview

```facts
Key: The team that controls the neutral zone decides what kind of game gets played
Risk: A turnover in the middle third does the most damage relative to the risk you thought you were taking — coaching consensus rather than measurement, and Risk Management owns the grading
Goal: Control it and you choose whether the night is a track meet of rushes or a grinding slog — lose it and your opponent chooses for you
```

The **neutral zone** is the middle third of the ice, between the two blue lines — fifty feet of it, and about three seconds for a strong skater.

**Whoever controls it decides what kind of game gets played:** end-to-end rushes, or pucks shot in and chased. It is also where a turnover costs most, because it catches players moving the wrong way at speed. Coaching material calls it the place possession changes hands most often — **consensus rather than a tracked figure**, since no published count of turnovers by location was found.

**The structures are coaching choices, and each only works when all five play it.** That is why knowing your team's system matters more than which system it is.

**The first forechecker tells you what you face.** Pressure is beaten by an early pass. A trap is beaten by moving the puck, or by shooting it deep on purpose and chasing it.

**Away from the puck you are either an option or coming back.** Separate lanes give the carrier three choices. Tracking back is what lets your defencemen stand up. A regroup keeps the puck and lets your forwards arrive with speed instead of from a standstill.

**The vocabulary is on one page.** [Language and Glossary](../foundation/language_and_glossary.md) defines the words used here, and [Rink Map](../foundation/rink_map.md) draws every line and area to scale. Three matter constantly below. **F1, F2 and F3** are the forwards, numbered by order of arrival at the puck. **D1 and D2** are the strong-side and weak-side defencemen for that sequence, not left and right. **The wall** usually means the boards, but also a line of skaters strung across the ice — "the flat wall", "a wall of four". You play a puck *along* the boards; you get *behind* a line of skaters.

## What the neutral zone actually is

**In short:** Get the puck past the red line before shooting it deep; it is the icing line except in some CARHA leagues.

```facts
Key: A strong skater crosses the neutral zone in about three seconds — that is the entire window for every decision in this document
Rule: Offside needs both skates completely over the line before the puck; an airborne trail skate stays onside under NHL, IIHF and Professional Women's Hockey League rules, but not under USA Hockey 630(a) or Hockey Canada 6.11, which require contact, and CARHA never defines its "own side" either way
Rule: The centre red line is still the icing line, though some CARHA leagues use the blue lines (65) — the shorthanded exemption is conditional and not universal (NHL and IIHF 81.6, USA Hockey 624(b)(1)); Special Teams has the conditions
Rule: The two-line pass is gone — NHL Rule 83 (Off-side) has no two-line provision, and neither do the IIHF, USA Hockey, Hockey Canada or CARHA books (CARHA Rule 73(d))
Never: Rebuild your attack around a local two-line pass rule unless somebody shows it to you in writing
Read: On a shorter sheet every structure here collapses faster — passive traps work better on small ice, aggressive pressure on big ice
```

**The neutral zone** is the area between the two blue lines. On a regulation NHL sheet (200 by 85 feet) that leaves **50 feet** — worked out from the rink's dimensions rather than stated in the NHL rule text; [Rink Map](../foundation/rink_map.md) owns it, along with the IIHF's stated 15.0 m. A strong skater covers it in about three seconds. That is the whole window for every decision in this document.

Three lines govern what happens there:

- **Your defensive blue line** — attackers must not precede the puck across it. **Offside** needs **both** skates completely over the line before the puck completely crosses. One skate on or behind the line keeps a player onside, and **dragging the blade in contact with the line is onside in every book**, which is why this document teaches it. ⚠️ **A skate in the air over the line is where the books split:** NHL, IIHF and PWHL count it onside; USA Hockey and Hockey Canada require ice contact; CARHA's wording answers it neither way. [Rules Primer](../foundation/rules_primer.md) sets the six side by side.
- **The centre red line** — no longer relevant to passing, but still the **icing line**, so **get the puck past it before you shoot it deep**. Shoot from your own half and have it cross their goal line untouched, and that is icing — unless you are shorthanded or another wave-off applies. Some CARHA leagues divide the ice at the blue lines instead (CARHA Rule 65), so ask your league. ⚠️ **The shorthanded exemption is conditional, not blanket.** In every book it holds only while you are *below the numerical strength of the opposing team*: an expiring penalty ends it only if that leaves you level, so on a 5-on-3 the first expiry still leaves you short and exempt. A puck released before the penalty expired draws no icing however late it lands. Some books add carve-outs: the NHL withdraws it where a major expires with nobody on the penalty box to come out, and USA Hockey grants it only at Youth 15-Only and above, Girls 16U and above, High School and Adult. [Special Teams](special_teams.md) quotes the rule in full. That is why "get it past the red line first" is still shouted on every bench.
- **Their blue line** — where your entry either works or dies. Covered in full in [Zone Entries](zone_entries.md).

**The two-line pass rule is gone, so the long pass is yours under every published rulebook checked here.** A pass once could not cross both your own blue line and the red line untouched. **NHL Rule 83 — Off-side** is now the only limit on where a pass may go, and it has no two-line provision. The IIHF, USA Hockey and Hockey Canada books have none either. The **CARHA Hockey Official Rule Book**, which governs CARHA-affiliated adult leagues only, abolishes it at **Rule 73(d)**: *"For the purpose of 2-line passes, the red line will not be in play. It will still be in effect for icing."* Every stretch pass in this document exists because of that change.

The NHL is commonly said to have removed it after the 2004-05 lockout, alongside the obstruction crackdown and the trapezoid — **but that date is in no rulebook held here**, so treat it as received history rather than something checked.

The folklore persists for a reason. That same CARHA book still prints a **"Red Line Off-Side Pass"** referee signal (*"arm extended at shoulder level pointing toward the centre red line"*) and still mentions *"an off-side pass over the centre red line"* in its delayed-penalty rule (39(b)) — dead text left behind when the rule went. If your league's book reads like that, the signal is a fossil, not a rule. **Flagged as unverified:** a few older recreational-association rulebooks are often said to keep a local two-line rule, but no published rulebook confirms it. If a teammate insists yours does, ask to see it in writing before you rebuild your attack around it.

> **Rec and youth note:** on a shorter sheet the neutral zone is proportionally smaller and the closing speed is the same, so every structure in this document collapses faster. Passive traps work better on small ice; aggressive pressure works better on big ice.

## Why the neutral zone decides games

**In short:** A turnover in the middle is the costliest kind, and the team that wins this third chooses what kind of game it is.

```facts
Risk: A turnover at centre ice costs more than one in either end — it catches two or three of your players moving the wrong way at speed and puts the puck in open ice with your defence flat-footed
Risk: Open ice in front of a flat-footed defence is how a centre-ice turnover becomes a 2-on-1
Key: Coaching material puts the making of odd-man rushes in the middle third — consensus rather than a tracked figure, because no public data classifies an odd-man rush by the play that created it
Key: Not every odd-man rush starts in the middle third — one of the five causes Defending the Rush names is both defencemen caught below the puck in the offensive zone
Key: The repeatable, player-to-player part of shot differential shows up almost entirely in neutral zone play, not in shots per offensive-zone possession (NHL tracking, 2011-12)
Goal: Win the neutral zone and you choose whether the game is rushes or a grind — neither is better, but the choice must be yours
```

Four reasons, and they compound.

**1. The middle third is the handover.** In either end, one team usually just has the puck. Every breakout that works and every entry that fails resolves in the middle. ⚠️ **That more possessions change hands here than anywhere else is not a counted figure** — public tracking counts zone exits and entries, and no published count of turnovers by location was found. Take it as why coaches stress this zone, not as a measurement.

**2. A turnover there is the most expensive kind.** Lose it deep in their end and they still have 180 feet to skate. Lose it in your own corner and your whole team is home. Lose it at centre ice and two or three of you are moving the wrong way at speed, with the puck in open ice and your defence flat-footed. **That is the turnover that becomes a 2-on-1.** The gradient is coaching consensus rather than measurement, and [Risk Management](../hockey-iq/risk_management.md) owns it.

**3. Most of the ways to make an odd-man rush live here.** A forward caught on the wrong side of the puck, a defenceman who stepped up and missed, a winger who left the zone early — all happen in the middle third. ⚠️ **That is coaching consensus, and not every odd-man rush starts here.** [Defending the Rush](defending_the_rush.md) owns the subject and lists five causes, one of them in the offensive zone — both defencemen caught below the puck. **No public data classifies an odd-man rush by the play that created it**, so nothing counts which cause is commonest.

**4. It is where the skill part of shot differential comes from.** Public tracking of NHL zone entries — Eric Tulsky and colleagues' paper at the 2013 MIT Sloan conference, which hand-tracked **330 games from the 2011-12 season**, and Corey Sznajder's later tracking (**2014-16**, reported second-hand and flagged as indicative in [Zone Entries](zone_entries.md)) — found that **carrying the puck in produces roughly twice the unblocked shot attempts per entry of dumping it in, at 5-on-5**.

The paper's authors drew a further conclusion from their own data. Over a season, shots per offensive-zone possession did not separate players reliably — the paper puts that down to luck. Entry differential and the share of entries made with possession did, *"clearly show[ing] reproducible differences from player to player"*. So the talent component of shot differential *"seems to come almost entirely from the neutral zone play."* The structure you play there is one of your biggest levers on chances at both ends. Both data sets are NHL, the figures vary between studies, and "roughly double" is the honest number rather than any decimal.

**The strategic consequence.** Win the neutral zone and you choose the game. Force the other side to dump it in every time and the night becomes a grind of forechecks and retrievals; move through with control and it becomes rushes and broken play. Neither is better in the abstract. **What matters is that the choice is yours, not theirs.** [Game Management](game_management.md) covers when to want which.

## How to read the numbers

```facts
Convention: Structures are named from the puck outward — the first number is the layer closest to the opponent's net
Key: A 1-2-2 is one pressuring, two across the middle, two at the back; a 1-4 is one pressuring and four in a line behind
Never: Read a neutral zone number as its offensive-zone twin — a 2-1-2 forecheck inside their end is a separate animal
Never: Confuse the neutral zone 1-3-1 with the power play 1-3-1 — different zone, different shape, same three digits
```

Structures are named from the **puck outward**: the first number is the layer nearest the opponent's net, the last is nearest your own. A **1-2-2** is one pressuring, two across the middle, two at the back. A **1-4** is one pressuring and four in a line behind.

Two traps:

- **The offensive-zone forecheck uses the same numbers for different things.** A 2-1-2 forecheck inside their end is not a 2-1-2 neutral-zone structure. This document covers the neutral-zone version of each shared name — the wall your five hold across the middle third. [Forechecking Systems](forechecking_systems.md) covers the offensive-zone version, and the history of the left-wing lock.
- **1-3-1 also names a power-play formation** — one point, two half-walls, one bumper, one net front. A different shape in a different zone; context tells you which. See [Special Teams](special_teams.md).

## Neutral zone defensive structures

**Everything in this section is a coaching choice, not a law of hockey.** Good teams play all of these, and one team often plays two or three depending on the score, the shift and who is on the ice. **Find out which one your team plays and what the switch call is** — most teams have one for "we're pressuring this shift" versus "we're holding".

### 1-2-2 — the classic containment structure

**In short:** F1 takes away the middle and forces a side, F2 meets the puck on that wall, and D2 stays home as the safety.

```facts
Position: F1 pressures near their goal line or hash marks, F2 and F3 sit across the middle, both defencemen hold the back layer around your own blue line
Goal: F1 takes the middle away and forces the carrier to one side — the turnover is a bonus, dictating the side is the job
Action: F2 steps up to play the puck as it reaches the strong-side winger — the puck, not the player; F3 holds the middle and weak side; D2 stays home as the safety valve
Risk: D1 stepping up on the wall is the decision with the least cover behind it — if D1 is beaten, only D2 stands between the puck and your net
Countered by: Quick D-to-D behind the net to change the side, a middle-lane forward supporting low under F2 and F3, and simple patience
Convention: Coaching material calls it the most widely played neutral zone structure, but nobody publishes a count — consensus, not a verified fact
```

**The shape.** F1 pressures near the opponent's goal line or hash marks. F2 and F3 sit across the middle of the neutral zone, usually between their blue line and the red line. The two defencemen hold the back layer, around your own blue line.

**The roles.**
- **F1** is not mainly trying to steal the puck. **F1's job is to take away the middle and force the carrier to one side.** Skate a curved route that shows the carrier the boards and takes away the D-to-D pass behind the net. A turnover is a bonus; dictating the side is the job.
- **F2** covers the strong-side wall. Once F1 has forced a side, step up on the winger receiving on the boards, timed to meet the puck — the puck, not the player. **Even where checking is allowed, the step up is not a hit:** arrive with your feet and stick on the puck, not with speed built up into the player. Contact before the puck arrives is interference. Where body checking is barred — and in women's hockey under the IIHF or the PWHL, which penalise any step or glide into a player — going into them when it arrives is a penalty too. Speed into the player is the picture the charging rules reach (see [Gap control as a team concept](#gap-control-as-a-team-concept)), and driving them dangerously into the wall is boarding.
- **F3** holds the middle and weak side, reading for the cross-ice pass or the seam.
- **D1** (strong side) steps up on the wall to play the puck if it gets past F2 — the decision with the least cover behind it, because if D1 is beaten, only D2 stands between the puck and your net.
- **D2** (weak side) stays home, holds the middle, and is the safety valve against everything going wrong.

![](diagram:nz-1-2-2-containment)

**Strengths.** Balanced. It never gives up numbers behind the play, it forgives one player's mistake, and it asks no exceptional skating of anyone. It is the safest structure to teach a new team. Coaching material calls it the most widely played — *though nobody publishes a count, so treat that as consensus rather than a verified fact.*

**Weaknesses.** It concedes the first pass: F1 alone cannot create real pressure, so a calm defence will usually break out cleanly. And it depends entirely on F1 taking the middle away — if F1 chases the puck instead of steering it, the whole structure is on the wrong side.

**How it is beaten.** A quick D-to-D behind the net to change sides before F1 re-sets the angle; a middle-lane forward supporting low under F2 and F3; and patience — the 1-2-2 rewards panic and barely punishes an extra second spent making the right play.

### The 1-2-2 trap ("the neutral zone trap")

**In short:** A trap lets the carrier skate and steers them to a wall at the red line, where the turnover starts your counterattack.

```facts
Position: A 1-2-2 with the layers dropped back — F1 high and splitting the ice, F2 and F3 at or just inside the red line, the defence on your own blue line
Goal: Do not stop the attack. Funnel it to a wall and kill it at the red line, with their forwards ahead of the puck and behind the play
Read: Layers up near their blue line with F1 actually closing is containment; layers back at the red line with F1 gliding is a trap
Convention: The pure passive trap is no longer a viable full-time system at high levels — it survives as a situational tool and as DNA inside newer structures
Countered by: Quick puck movement, the boards, the stretch pass behind the wall, and a willingness to dump it in rather than force a low-percentage entry
```

**What makes it a trap rather than containment.** The trap is a 1-2-2 with the layers dropped back and the intent changed. F1 sits high, at or near their blue line, still splitting the ice. **The whole structure retreats to defend the centre red line rather than their blue line.** F2 and F3 sit at or just inside the red line on the walls, and the defence holds **your own** blue line.

**Not every 1-2-2 is a trap.** Containment still contests the exit; a trap has given that up and is funnelling you. The tell is F2's depth and F1's speed: layers up near their blue line with F1 closing is containment; layers back at the red line with F1 gliding is a trap.

The result is a funnel. The carrier is allowed to skate and is steered to a wall, arriving at the red line into F2 stepping up, D1 behind F2, and no lane through the middle because F1 took it away 40 feet earlier. The turnover happens there, with their forwards ahead of the puck. **You do not stop the attack — you invite it to a place where killing it gives you an immediate counterattack.**

![](diagram:nz-1-2-2-trap)

**History and why it dominated.** The trap's famous modern version is Jacques Lemaire's New Jersey Devils, who won the 1995 Stanley Cup with it and were then blamed for a decade of low-scoring hockey. They did not invent it; they perfected it and proved it could win a Cup without the league's most talented roster — which is why everyone copied it.

**The two older lineages, and why only one holds up.** The trap is said to have roots both in European international hockey and in the 1970s Montreal Canadiens Lemaire played for. Those claims are not equally supported.

**The Montreal one is documented, at the time.** Michael Farber's *Sports Illustrated* report from the middle of the 1995 final says Lemaire *"hardly grew the trap in a petri dish. The forechecking scheme, which can be used whenever an opponent has to clear from deep within its own zone, has been around for decades."* Farber quotes **Pierre Page**, the former Nordiques coach then scouting for Toronto: *"Montreal's been trapping for 40 years. Montreal always forced you to the boards and locked the middle. But people forgave them because they had great offensive players and they won 24 Stanley Cups."*

Lemaire has since confirmed his part, with a qualifier: *"We didn't use it as a whole team, but I was always talking with my linemates [Steve Shutt and Guy Lafleur mostly], trying to think of ways we could be better defensively. And so our line would use that [trap] system."* So the 1970s Canadiens did not play it as a coached team system. Montreal had a long habit of forcing wide and locking the middle, and Lemaire's line trapped on its own initiative — a better explanation of the Devils' version than "he invented it".

***(The European lineage stays flagged as unverified.** The claim that Czechoslovak and Swedish teams of the 1970s and 80s trapped traces to a single self-disclaiming sentence — *"Much of it is anecdotal evidence, but many old-timers from Europe will tell you..."* — with no primary source behind it, and Farber's piece never links the trap to Europe. What Czechoslovak hockey demonstrably contributed is the **left wing lock**, a related but distinct system brought to the NHL by Barry Smith in Detroit — after the Devils, so it cannot be the trap's ancestor. Conflating the two looks like where the story comes from.)*

The Devils were not even the first NHL team to run it. **Roger Neilson used it with the 1992-93 Rangers**, and it was unpopular enough with his players — captain Mark Messier in particular — that he lost the job over it. *(The secondary source says Neilson "was fired after the season"; that is wrong. Hockey Reference's 1992-93 Rangers page records two coaches that season — Neilson at 19-17-4 and Ron Smith at 15-22-7 — so he went partway through it. The trap's folk history travels through blogs.)* Treat the Devils as the team that proved the trap, not the one that invented it.

**Why it worked so well then.** Three things, all of which have since changed:
1. **The two-line pass rule.** You could not pass from your own end past the red line. That made the long ball illegal and forced every attack to skate through the trap's teeth. (Nothing of it survives in the current Rule 83.)
2. **Obstruction was tolerated.** A hook, a hold or a subtle interference on a forward cutting through the middle was very often not called. The trap's four-man wall could be four-man in practice as well as on the whiteboard.
3. **Less speed and less skill throughout the lineup.** A wall of four is much easier to hold when nobody on the other side can beat one of the four in a footrace.

**What changed.** Coming out of the 2004-05 lockout the NHL dropped the two-line pass, ordered obstruction called, and added the trapezoid limiting where goalies may play the puck. **That date is the commonly given one and is in no rulebook held here**, so take it as received history. Stretch passes became legal, the wall could no longer be held with sticks and bodies, and the goalie stopped being a free third defenceman on retrievals. Add players who can really skate, and **the pure passive trap is no longer a viable full-time system at high levels.** It survives as a situational tool — protecting a lead, the last two minutes, a shift with your best defenceman off — and as DNA inside the newer structures below.

**How it is beaten.** See [beating a trap](#beating-a-trap). In short: quick puck movement, the boards, the stretch pass behind the wall, and a willingness to dump it in rather than force a low-percentage entry.

### 1-3-1 — widely described as the modern standard

**In short:** Three across slide as one wall and leave no lane through the middle, so nobody chases the puck out of their lane.

```facts
Position: F1 pressures, three players stand spread in a line across the ice, one defenceman sits behind them as the last man
Convention: Conventionally F2 and F3 on the walls with a defenceman in the middle — but never three forwards across, because F1 is already one of your three
Key: A 1-3-1 does not try to take the puck from you — it offers you the puck on terms you will not like
Action: The three across hold their lanes and slide as a unit; their job is not to attack the puck but to leave no lane through them
Goal: Deny the middle lane and almost never concede an odd-man rush — the back defenceman stays uncommitted and retrieves every dumped puck
Risk: One of the three leaving his lane opens a seam instantly, and the single back defenceman cannot cover two lanes
Countered by: Getting behind the flat wall with a stretch pass, chip-and-chase past it, or quick lateral movement — a sliding wall has gaps
```

**The shape.** F1 pressures. **Three players stand spread across the ice in a line** — conventionally **F2 and F3 on the walls with a defenceman in the middle**, and the middle man decides which way the line slides. One defenceman sits behind them as the last man. (Some coaches put a forward in the middle and a defenceman on a wall; never three forwards across, because F1 is already one of your three.)

**The roles.**
- **F1** splits the ice, takes the middle away and forces a side. In the most passive versions F1 barely leaves the red line.
- **The three across** are a wall. Each holds a lane, and they slide as a unit toward the puck. Their job is not to attack the puck but to leave no lane through them.
- **The single back defenceman** is the safety: handles anything that gets through and retrieves every dumped puck to start the breakout.

![](diagram:nz-1-3-1)

**Strengths.** It denies the middle better than anything else. The three-man line spans the full 85 feet with F1 already taking the middle away, so a controlled entry has no seam to go through. It protects a slower defence pair, and with the back defenceman uncommitted it almost never gives up an odd-man rush. That is why so many teams switch to it with a one-goal lead in the third.

**The famous demonstration.** In November 2011, Guy Boucher's Tampa Bay Lightning held their 1-3-1 and Peter Laviolette's Philadelphia Flyers simply refused to advance — a Flyers defenceman stood still with the puck in his own zone, daring Tampa to come and get it. Neither coach blinked, Tampa won 2-1, and the league argued about it for a week. **A 1-3-1 does not try to take the puck from you. It offers you the puck on terms you will not like.**

**Weaknesses.** It gives the opponent's defencemen complete freedom in their own end, which costs you against a defence that can pass. It needs strict lane discipline — one of the three chasing the puck opens a seam at once, and one back defenceman cannot cover two lanes. And it is passive: trailing, a 1-3-1 will not get you the puck back.

**How it is beaten.** Attack it wide and behind. A forward who gets **behind** the flat line is in a footrace with one defenceman — that is what the stretch pass is for. Chip-and-chase past the wall works for the same reason. Above all, quick lateral puck movement makes the wall slide, and a sliding wall has gaps.

### Pressure — the neutral zone under an aggressive forecheck

**In short:** Behind a 2-1-2 forecheck, F3 holds the middle and the weak-side defenceman stays home while the strong-side one steps up.

```facts
Position: Two forwards are gone hunting the puck in their end, leaving F3 as the only layer between the puck and your defence
Action: F3 holds the middle, level with or slightly ahead of the puck, and does not drift to a wall — the pass that hurts you comes up the middle
Action: The strong-side defenceman steps up hard on a pass to the wall — hard on the gap, not into a hit; the weak-side defenceman stays home as your only genuine safety
Key: Where the defencemen stand depends on the phase — up at the offensive blue line while the forecheck is live, back toward your own blue line once the puck is out
Goal: The only approach here that reliably takes the puck back rather than waiting for a mistake — the right choice when you are chasing a goal
Risk: One pass past the two forecheckers leaves F3 alone against three with your defence stepping up — that is how a 3-on-2 becomes a 3-on-1
Countered by: A crisp first pass past the two pressuring forwards, a reverse or rim behind an over-committed F1, and the stretch pass
```

The aggressive option is not really a neutral-zone structure. It is what the middle third looks like when your team runs a **2-1-2 forecheck inside the opponent's end**. **[Forechecking Systems](forechecking_systems.md) covers that system**; this section covers what the other three do behind it.

**The shape from the neutral zone's point of view.** Two of your forwards are in their end hunting the puck. That leaves **one forward holding the middle as the only layer between the puck and your defence**, and two defencemen holding a deliberately tight gap, both ready to step up.

> **Where the defencemen stand depends on the phase, and both answers are right.** **While the forecheck is live**, they are up at the **offensive** blue line, pinching selectively — the shape [Forechecking Systems](forechecking_systems.md) sets out. **Once the puck comes out**, the same pair has retreated and holds its tight gap back toward **your own** blue line, which is the shape here. If you read the two documents side by side, note which blue line each names.

![](diagram:nz-pressure)

**What the other three do** once the puck is coming out.
- **F3 holds the middle**, level with or slightly ahead of the puck — the first protection if the two-man pressure is beaten. Do not drift to a wall; the pass that hurts you comes up the middle. *(Where F3 sits while the forecheck is still live in their end is a separate coaching split — weak-side high by default, puck-side in the stacked variant. [Forechecking Systems](forechecking_systems.md) covers it; either way, F3's job on the way back is the middle.)*
- **The strong-side defenceman** steps up hard on a pass to the wall. That aggressive gap makes the system work, and it has the least cover behind it — the weak-side defenceman is the only safety left. ⚠️ **"Hard" is about the gap, not the contact.** Close the space fast; speed built into a hit is charging — see [Gap control as a team concept](#gap-control-as-a-team-concept).
- **The weak-side defenceman stays home** — with two forwards deep, the only real safety you have.

**Strengths.** It is the only approach here that reliably *takes* the puck back rather than waiting for a mistake. It tires defences that cannot handle pressure, and it is the right choice when you are chasing a goal.

**Weaknesses.** One pass past the two forecheckers leaves F3 alone against three, with your defence stepping up into them — that is how a 3-on-2 becomes a 3-on-1. It takes enormous fitness; nobody pressures like this for 60 minutes. And a team with a good breakout and one skilled defenceman punishes it badly.

**How the opposition beats it.** A crisp first pass past the two forecheckers; a reverse or rim behind an over-committed F1; and the stretch pass, which turns your aggression into their odd-man rush. See [Breakouts](breakouts.md).

### 1-4 — the full retreat

```facts
Position: One forechecker and four skaters in a flat line across your own blue line — the most conservative structure in hockey
Read: Use it protecting a lead in the last minute or two, killing time with an exhausted stranded unit, or defending a 6-on-5 against a pulled goalie
Goal: Let almost nothing through — controlled entries against a disciplined 1-4 are close to impossible and odd-man rushes almost never happen
Risk: It concedes the whole neutral zone and generates no offence, so you spend the shift in your own end and clearing becomes dumping
Countered by: A hard chip past the flat line and a race for it — nobody in a flat line is deeper than anybody else
Never: Play it as your default — some youth teams do and should not
```

**The shape.** One forechecker and **four skaters in a flat line across your own blue line** — the most conservative structure in hockey.

**When it is used.** Protecting a lead in the last minute or two; killing time with an exhausted, stranded unit; defending a 6-on-5 with their goalie pulled. Some youth teams play it as a default and should not.

![](diagram:nz-1-4)

**Strengths.** Almost nothing goes through it. Controlled entries are close to impossible, and odd-man rushes almost never happen because everyone is home.

**Weaknesses.** It concedes the whole neutral zone. They enter with speed where they like, again and again, because a 1-4 creates no offence and so no relief. **You will spend the shift in your own end.** When you do win the puck, all five of you are at or inside your own blue line with nobody ahead to pass to — so clearing becomes dumping, and they come again.

**How it is beaten.** Chip the puck hard past the flat line and hunt it — nobody in a flat line is deeper than anybody else, so it is a genuine race. Failing that, gain the line, set up, and make them defend; a 1-4 has no counterpunch.

### The left-wing lock

```facts
Position: Two forwards forecheck aggressively in their end; the moment possession is lost the left wing drops into line with the two defencemen
Goal: Forecheck without paying the odd-man price — three across the back, each responsible for roughly a third of the width of the ice
Convention: Conventionally the centre and right wing forecheck, steering the puck to the opponent's right side — a coaching choice, not a law
Risk: It takes one player out of the counterattack and asks him to do a job most wingers are not trained for — costly if he is your best attacker
Read: A left wing who reads transitions early makes it work; one who reacts late gives you the worst of both systems
Options: Many teams now lock whichever winger is highest in the zone instead, which needs a loud and immediate in-game call
```

**The shape.** A hybrid of forecheck and neutral-zone structure. Two forwards — conventionally the centre and right wing — forecheck hard in their end, often steering the puck to the opponent's right side. **The moment possession is lost, the left wing drops into line with the two defencemen**, making three across, each covering about a third of the ice's width.

The effect is a 2-3: aggressive up front, three across the back. You get real forechecking pressure without the usual odd-man risk, because the third man back is already there.

![](diagram:nz-left-wing-lock)

**History.** Detroit did not invent it. It was **invented in Czechoslovakia in the 1970s** to counter the dominant Soviet side, then **adapted for the narrower North American rink by Barry Smith**, Scotty Bowman's assistant, at Detroit. The Red Wings played it heavily from 1994-95, won the Presidents' Trophy with it, ran into Lemaire's trap in the 1995 Final, and won a Cup with it in 1997. The two stories are about twenty years apart and both true. [Forechecking Systems](forechecking_systems.md) carries the same account.

**Strengths.** You forecheck without being punished for it. It is simple to teach — "left wing, you're the third D" — and works at every level, which is why youth hockey still uses it.

**Weaknesses.** It asks one player to do a job most wingers are not trained for, and takes him out of the counterattack. If your left wing is your best attacker, it costs more than it saves. And a left wing who reads transitions late gives you the worst of both systems.

**Modern variant.** Many teams now **lock whichever winger is highest in the zone** instead. That removes the personnel problem but needs a clear call — loud and immediate. See [On-Ice Communication](../foundation/on_ice_communication.md).

### Which one should you be playing?

```facts
Convention: Which structure you play is your coach's call, and the honest answer is usually more than one
Read: Trailing and needing the puck, or with a fast, fit forward group → pressure behind a 2-1-2 forecheck
Read: Protecting a one-goal lead → a 1-3-1 or a 1-2-2 trap; last 90 seconds against a pulled goalie → a 1-4
Read: A slow defence pairing on the ice → 1-3-1; facing fast wingers who stretch → something with two men back
Options: Facing a team with one elite puck-moving defenceman is a genuine split — pressuring and containing are both defensible
```

That is your coach's call, and usually the answer is "more than one". Rough guidance:

| Situation | Tends toward |
|---|---|
| Trailing, need the puck | Pressure (behind a 2-1-2 forecheck) |
| Protecting a one-goal lead | 1-3-1 or 1-2-2 trap |
| Last 90 seconds, opponent's goalie pulled | 1-4 |
| Slow defence pairing on the ice | 1-3-1 |
| Fast, fit forward group | Pressure (behind a 2-1-2 forecheck) |
| Facing a team with one elite puck-moving defenceman | **Genuine split — see below** |
| Facing a team with fast wingers who stretch | Something with two men back |

#### The one row coaches genuinely disagree on: facing an elite puck-mover

You will hear both answers from different coaches, and both are defensible. [Forechecking Systems](forechecking_systems.md) sets out the same split one zone earlier.

- **The case for pressuring (2-1-2).** A good passer is dangerous because of the *time* they get. Deny the time and the skill never gets used. This suits fast, fit forwards and a team chasing the game.
- **The case for containing (1-2-2 or 1-3-1).** Pressure gives an elite puck-mover what they want: a committed forechecker and space behind him. One pass past F1 and you are worse off than if you had never gone. This suits a team that cannot be sure of arriving first, is tired, or is protecting a lead.

**The tiebreaker is you, not the opponent.** Pressure only beats a skilled passer if you arrive before the pass. If you cannot, containment is not the cowardly option; it is the only one that works. Ask which your bench wants — the two produce opposite F1 routes.

**Ask your coach two questions:** which structure is the default, and what is the signal to change it. Most neutral zone breakdowns are not effort failures — they are two players on one shift with different ideas about whether the team is pressuring or holding. ⚠️ **That is coaching consensus rather than a tracked figure** — nothing found classifies breakdowns by cause.

## Gap control as a team concept

**In short:** Gap is a five-man outcome: forwards tracking back above the puck are what let your defencemen step up.

```facts
Key: Gap is a five-man outcome, not a two-man one — the single biggest thing amateur teams get wrong about the neutral zone
Convention: A tight gap is roughly one to one and a half stick lengths, close enough to touch their stick with yours if you reach — coaching convention rather than a measured figure, and coaching material does not agree on it
Read: Three forwards above the puck and tracking back → your D can step up and squeeze; one forward gliding and two coasting → your D must back off
Action: As a forward, get on the defensive side of your check immediately and track home through the middle, not along the wall
Never: Stop at the red line to wait for the counterattack unless your coach has set you as the stretch man — one forward hanging there forces your D to back off and concedes a controlled entry
Convention: In the default this guide assumes the late man belongs to the backchecking forwards, not the defencemen — an assignment, not a law; ask which your team plays
Convention: Both defencemen taking a step up inside the dots is an aggressive-gap cue, not universal — a step up is not a pinch, not a licence to attack the puck, and not a licence to finish it as a hit. Your feet keep moving backwards and the attacker stays in front of you
Rule: Where a step up is finished as a hit, the charging rules are what it meets — USA Hockey's Casebook rules the head-down case charging (607, Situation 2) and reaches a check accelerated through without the two-stride run-up (Situation 3)
Rule: Charging binds every division, checking league or not, and no book caps it at a minor: one that injures is a major and game misconduct (Hockey Canada 7.4(b), CARHA 52(a)); USA Hockey 607(a) has no bare minor; a reckless charge is a major and game misconduct (607(b)) — plan on a match for it (602(a))
Rule: Under NHL and PWHL 42.5 and EIHL Casebook Rule 42, a charging major that injures the face or head adds a mandatory game misconduct; an IIHF charge to the head goes to Rule 48, where a major always carries one (48.3); every Hockey Canada (7.4(e)) and CARHA (30(a)) charging major ejects
Rule: Charge a player "in such a way that they are unable to defend themselves", or charge to injure, and Hockey Canada 7.4(c) makes it a mandatory match penalty; a deliberate attempt to injure is a mandatory match under CARHA 48(a), USA Hockey 602(a) and NHL and PWHL 21.1 too
```

Gap control is usually taught as a defenceman's skill, and [Defending the Rush](defending_the_rush.md) covers the technique. But **gap is a five-man outcome, not a two-man one** — the single biggest thing amateur teams get wrong about the neutral zone.

**What gap means.** The distance between you and the attacker you are responsible for. A *tight* gap here is roughly **one to one and a half stick lengths** — close enough to touch their stick with yours if you reach. The ordinary neutral-zone gap used throughout is **two to three stick lengths**.

Both are coaching convention, and coaching material does not agree. Some material calls two to three stick lengths tight; some puts one to two, or one and a half, at the ordinary gap; and the only page found that anchors a distance to the red line prints **two** stick lengths there. [Defending the Rush](defending_the_rush.md) cites those pages and sets out the rest of the ladder (a stick and a half at the red line, one at your own blue line). The distance matters more than the label.

**Why forwards determine the gap.** A defenceman can only hold a tight gap if being beaten is not fatal. It is not fatal when a forward is arriving from behind — **backpressure** — because the attacker who beats the D skates into a second defender. So:

- **Three forwards above the puck and tracking back → your D can step up and squeeze.** The attacker has no time and usually chips it in.
- **One forward gliding and two coasting → your D must back off.** With nobody behind them, they must respect being beaten clean. The attacker builds speed, picks a lane and enters with control.

**The same defencemen will look elite or hopeless depending on what the forwards did 40 feet earlier.** If you keep getting entered on with speed, the fix is usually not the defence.

**What this means for you as a forward.** When your team loses the puck in the offensive or neutral zone:

1. **Get on the defensive side of your check immediately** — between them and your net. A backchecker level with the attacker is worth more than a faster one on the wrong side.
2. **Track through the middle, not along the wall.** The dangerous pass goes there, and it is the shorter route home.
3. **Take the late man.** A trailer arriving unmarked into the high slot is one of the cheapest goals off a rush — coaching consensus: rush goals are counted publicly, but **no published breakdown classifies a rush goal by which defensive assignment failed**. In the system this guide assumes — a low zone collapse behind a 2-1-2 forecheck — the late man belongs to the **backchecking forwards**, not the defencemen, who are on the first wave; [Defending the Rush](defending_the_rush.md) says the same. Some coaches give the trailer to the weak-side defenceman instead, so know which you play.
4. **Do not stop at the red line to wait for the counterattack.** One forward hanging there makes the D back off, which means a controlled entry against you and 40 seconds in your own zone instead of the counterattack you were fishing for.

**What this means for you as a defenceman.** On a change of possession in the offensive zone, a common cue — **in aggressive-gap systems like the pressure structure above, not everywhere** — is for both defencemen to **step up and inside the dots**, closing the gap and taking the middle to buy your forwards time to get back. Backing off too early turns a manageable rush into a 3-on-2 with speed.

**Four limits on that cue, because other documents here seem to say the opposite and are describing different things:**
- **A step up is not a pinch.** Your feet keep moving backwards and the attacker stays in front of you. It is *not* a licence to attack the puck. [Defender](../positions/defender.md) is right that you should only step up to *challenge* in the neutral zone when you are **confident** you will win it — how aggressive to be is a coaching choice — because if you miss, your partner is alone in a 2-on-1.
- **A step up is not a licence to finish it as a hit.** Close the space with your feet; ⚠️ **do not build speed into the player.** A carrier who has not seen you coming is exactly the picture the charging rules name — **USA Hockey's Casebook rules it charging** when a player skates *"at full speed to deliver a hard body check"* on *"an unsuspecting opponent skating up the ice with their head down"* (Rule 607, Situation 2). ⚠️ **A step up needs no run-up to reach it:** Situation 3 also answers *"Yes"* for a check delivered *"without taking the two fast strides, but instead accelerat[ing] through the check."* The full price is in *What it costs* at the end of this section.
- **Stepping up together is not pinching together.** [Forechecking Systems](forechecking_systems.md) says only one defenceman pinches at a time, and that holds: a pinch is at the *offensive* blue line with the puck behind you if you miss. Both tightening a neutral-zone gap by ten feet is a much smaller bet.
- **It needs backpressure to be safe.** With one forward gliding at the red line, one defenceman holds the middle and the other stays home. **Ask which your team wants — the cue is not universal.**

**What it costs.** ⚠️ **Charging binds every division, checking league or not, and no book here caps it at a minor.** The charging rules carry no division clause, where the body-checking rules that restrict themselves say so (USA Hockey 604(a), Hockey Canada 7.3).
- **Stride count runs the other way for anyone who learned the American number.** Hockey Canada 7.4(ii) makes *"two or more strides"* the offence; USA Hockey 607's Note and CARHA 52's Note (CARHA-affiliated adult leagues only) need *"more than two"*; IIHF 42.1 (2026/27) says only *"multiple strides"*; NHL 42.1 judges it *"as a result of distance traveled"*.
- **USA Hockey:** no minor on its own — 607(a) reads *"A minor plus a misconduct or a major plus a game misconduct penalty shall be assessed"*. A reckless charge is a mandatory major plus game misconduct (607(b)), and plan on a match for it: 602(a) says one *"shall be assessed"* to any player who *"recklessly endangers or attempts to injure"*, though 607(e) says only *"may also"*.
- **Hockey Canada and CARHA:** a charge that injures is a mandatory major plus game misconduct (7.4(b), 52(a)), and every charging major ends your game (7.4(e), 30(a)). Hockey Canada 7.4(c) makes a charge *"in such a way that they are unable to defend themselves"*, or a deliberate attempt to injure, a mandatory match penalty; CARHA 48(a) makes one mandatory for any player who *"deliberately injures or attempts to injure an opponent… in any manner"*.
- **NHL and PWHL:** a charging major that injures the face or head carries a mandatory game misconduct (42.5). 21.1 makes a match mandatory for any player who *"deliberately attempts to injure or who deliberately injures an opponent in any manner"*; their charging rule's own match (42.4) is the referee's call, so plan on 21.1's.
- **EIHL:** the Casebook adds a mandatory game misconduct when a charging major injures the face or head (Rule 42); otherwise the IIHF book below applies.
- **IIHF:** a charge to the head goes to the head-check rule, where any major carries an automatic game misconduct (48.3); 42.4 leaves a major and game misconduct for any other reckless charge to the referee.

[Defending the Rush](defending_the_rush.md) owns the step-up and carries this in full; [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md) owns whether you may check at all.

## Denying the blue line: stand up or back off

At your own blue line you have two broad options, and they are a genuine trade-off.

![](diagram:nz-stand-up-at-the-line)

![](diagram:nz-back-off-at-the-line)

### Standing up at the line

**In short:** Meet the carrier at your line and refuse ground with your feet, but never build speed into a hit.

```facts
Action: Meet the attacker at or just inside your blue line, refuse to give ground, and force a dump, a pass or a turnover
Goal: Turn carries into dump-ins, keep the play 60 feet from your net rather than 20, and let your forwards recover
Read: Right when you have backpressure, when the attacker is wide rather than in the middle, or when they are already fighting the puck
Risk: Beaten standing up is beaten completely — your feet are stopped, theirs are not, and you have surrendered the middle
Countered by: The chip-and-chase — the attacker puts the puck past your hip and runs around you; stopped feet cannot win that race, so go for the puck, never into them
Never: Meet the attacker by building speed into a hit — standing up is refusing ground with your feet, and a head-down carrier tells you only that they cannot change direction
Rule: A stand-up finished as a hit meets the charging rules in every division — USA Hockey's Casebook rules the head-down case charging (607, Situation 2), and a check accelerated through without the two-stride run-up too (Situation 3) — and no rulebook here caps charging at a minor
Rule: Charge a player "in such a way that they are unable to defend themselves" and Hockey Canada 7.4(c) makes it a mandatory match, as CARHA 48(a), NHL and PWHL 21.1 and USA Hockey 602(a) do a charge meant to injure; a reckless USA Hockey charge is a major and game misconduct — plan on a match (602(a))
```

**What it is.** Meet the attacker at or just inside your blue line, refuse to give ground, and force a dump, a pass or a turnover.

**Why it works.** A carried entry is worth roughly twice a dumped one in unblocked shot attempts at 5-on-5, on NHL tracking more than a decade old — see [Zone Entries](zone_entries.md) for the limits. Turn carries into dump-ins and your night gets easier. It also keeps the play 60 feet from your net rather than 20, and lets your forwards recover.

**What it risks.** Beaten standing up, you are beaten completely — your feet are stopped, theirs are not, and you have given up the middle. It also invites the **chip-and-chase**: the attacker puts the puck past your hip and runs around you, and with stopped feet you cannot win that race. **Once the puck is past you, go for the puck, not the player: turn and skate to it, and never step into them or hit them** — they no longer have the puck, and blocking them with your body is interference.

**When it is right.** When you have backpressure; when the attacker is wide rather than in the middle; when they are at a bad angle or already fighting the puck; when protecting a lead means forcing dump-ins.

**What standing up is not.** It is refusing ground with your feet, so the carrier has to solve you. ⚠️ **It is not building speed into a hit, and a carrier fighting the puck or looking down at it is not an invitation to arrive harder** — that only tells you they cannot change direction. **And keep your skates on the ice until contact** — leave your feet to deliver a check and every book here calls it charging, even shoulder to shoulder.

**What it costs.** USA Hockey's Casebook rules the head-down case charging (Rule 607, Situation 2), and Situation 3 reaches a check *"accelerat[ing] through"* it without the two strides. Charging binds every division, and **no rulebook here caps it at a minor**. ⚠️ **A charge made *"in such a way that they are unable to defend themselves"* — on a head-down carrier who has not seen you, for one — is a mandatory match penalty under Hockey Canada (7.4(c)).** Any charging major ends your game under Hockey Canada and CARHA. Under USA Hockey a reckless charge is a mandatory major plus a game misconduct (607(b)), and plan on a match for it (602(a)). A charge meant to injure is a mandatory match under Hockey Canada and CARHA (48(a)), as is any attempt to injure under USA Hockey 602(a) and any deliberate one under NHL and PWHL 21.1. The stride counts and the rest are set out under [Gap control as a team concept](#gap-control-as-a-team-concept) and owned by [Defending the Rush](defending_the_rush.md).

### Backing off

```facts
Action: Skate backwards matching their speed, keeping the attacker in front of you and never giving up the middle or your body position
Goal: Funnel the attacker outside where the shot is low-percentage — you cannot be beaten wide by pure speed
Read: Right when you have no backpressure, when the attacker has a clear speed advantage, on a 2-on-1 or worse, or when a mistake is a breakaway
Risk: You concede the entry — they come in with speed and possession and you defend with your feet moving toward your own net
```

**What it is.** Skate backwards matching their speed, keeping the attacker in front of you — give up the blue line, never the middle or your body position.

**Why it works.** You cannot be beaten wide by pure speed, and you funnel the attacker outside, where the shot is low-percentage. It is very hard to make a catastrophic error doing it.

**What it risks.** You concede the entry. They come in with speed and possession, and you defend with your feet moving toward your own net — exactly what they wanted.

**When it is right.** When you have no backpressure; when the attacker has a clear speed advantage; on a 2-on-1 or worse, where the middle matters far more than the line; when you are the last defender and a mistake is a breakaway.

### The honest summary

**Standing up is a bet that you have help. Backing off is the play when you do not.** The skill is reading, in the second before you commit, whether your forwards got back. Check over your shoulder as you retreat, and talk: "I'm up!" or "I've got nothing, back!" is one of the most valuable calls in the game.

**This is a coaching choice too.** Some coaches want the line held aggressively; others want everything funnelled outside and defended in the zone. Find out which yours wants before you try to be a hero at the line.

## Regroups — declining a bad entry to buy a better one

### What a regroup is

```facts
Goal: In possession with no good way forward, turn the puck back toward your own end, reset, and come again
Key: You are not retreating — you still have the puck, and you are declining a bad entry to buy a better one
Never: Treat a backwards pass as a failure, or feel obliged to go forward every time you receive in the neutral zone
Risk: Forcing it forward instead produces low-percentage entries, dumped pucks nobody chases, and turnovers that become rushes the other way
Convention: Professionals regroup two or three times a shift and amateurs barely do — coaching observation, not a tracked figure
```

A **regroup** is when your team, in possession but with no good way forward, **turns the puck back toward your own end, resets, and comes again**. You still have the puck. You are declining a bad entry to buy a better one.

Professionals regroup two or three times a shift without thinking twice. Amateurs barely do, because every player who receives in the neutral zone feels obliged to go forward. **The result is low-percentage entries, dumped pucks nobody chases, and turnovers at their blue line that become rushes the other way.**

*(That comparison is coaching observation, not a measurement — **no count of amateur regroups has been published**. The only professional count is a public analysis of 198 AHL games that tagged **826 neutral-zone regroup situations in 2,217 minutes of five-on-four power-play time — about one every three minutes**, **315** of them full regroups back to the defensive zone and **511** immediate re-entry attempts. That is one league, half a season, **power play only**, with a wider definition than this one, so it neither confirms nor refutes anything about even strength. Take the direction, not the number.)*

### Why a regroup beats a forced entry

```facts
Risk: A forced entry into a set structure ends your possession — a turnover at the line, a dump-in you do not recover, or an offside
Goal: Keep the puck and make their forecheck reset, which takes time and skating
Read: The opposition's forwards were coming toward you and now have to turn — turning is the slowest thing anybody does on skates
Key: Your forwards arrive at the line with speed instead of receiving standing still — that is the real prize
```

A forced entry into a set structure usually ends in a turnover at the line, a dump-in you do not recover, or an offside. A regroup keeps the puck, and while you hold it:

- Their forecheck has to reset, which takes time and skating.
- Their forwards, who were coming at you, have to turn — the slowest thing anybody does on skates.
- **Your forwards build speed toward the line instead of receiving standing still.** That is the real prize: speed with the puck already moving is what beats structure.

### The D-to-D regroup

```facts
Action: A forward who cannot advance turns and passes back to a defenceman skating backwards or opened up to receive
Technique: Receive with your hips open up the ice, not facing your own net, so you can see the whole sheet
Action: If the strong side is covered, move it D-to-D — the pass across changes the point of attack and forces the whole structure to slide
Action: As the puck travels D-to-D, all three forwards change lanes and build speed, curling back toward it and then turning up ice
Technique: Keep the D-to-D flat and hard, and hit whichever forward has the most speed and the most ice
Never: Make it blind across the middle with a forechecker between you — an interception is an instant 2-on-1 with both defencemen wrong-sided
```

The standard form:

1. A forward who cannot advance turns and passes back to a defenceman skating backwards or opened up to receive.
2. The defenceman receives with **hips open up the ice**, not facing their own net, to see the whole sheet.
3. If the strong side is covered, they move it **D-to-D**. That pass changes the point of attack and makes the whole opposing structure slide.
4. As it travels, **all three forwards change lanes and build speed**, curling back toward the puck and then turning up ice.
5. The receiving defenceman hits whichever forward has the most speed and ice.

![](diagram:nz-regroup-d-to-d)

**The one hard rule.** Make the neutral-zone D-to-D flat and hard, and never blind across the middle with a forechecker between you — an interception is an instant 2-on-1 with both defencemen wrong-sided. If the lane is not clean, hold it or take it back yourself.

### The hinge, and the skating hinge

```facts
Action: After passing D-to-D, sink back into mid-ice behind your partner instead of following the puck up the boards
Key: Your partner moves up, finds nothing, and returns it to you — you are now behind the play, in the middle, facing up ice with the whole sheet in front of you
Goal: The puck comes back to a player with time and vision, instead of being forced up the boards by a partner who had no option
Technique: The skating hinge — drop deeper as you make the first pass, so you arrive into the return pass with speed rather than standing still
Read: A forecheck that takes the D-to-D pass away kills the hinge; one that takes the cross-ice pass away concedes it
```

The D-to-D regroup assumes the second defenceman can do something with it. **The hinge is what you run when they cannot.** Coaching material calls it a habit amateur teams often lack entirely — an observation, not a counted difference.

You pass D-to-D, and instead of following the puck up the ice, **sink back into the middle behind your partner**. Your partner carries it up, finds everything covered, and gives it back. **You are now the best-placed player on the ice**: behind the play, in the middle, facing up ice with nobody near you, because the forecheck has committed to the other side. First look the far winger, then the centre.

![](diagram:nz-hinge)

**The skating hinge is the version worth learning.** Drop deeper *as you make the first pass*, so you take the return already moving. That gives you room to accelerate, an extra second to read, and the chance to draw a forechecker before moving the puck — which is what opens the pass.

**Whether it is available depends on their forecheck.** If their first forechecker takes away the **D-to-D pass**, the hinge is dead — look at the quick-up or the boards. If they take away the **cross-ice pass to the far forward** instead, they have conceded the hinge. That one read tells you which regroup options exist tonight.

### The low regroup

**In short:** Take it back below your own blue line and restart as a breakout; it is a possession play, not a panic play.

```facts
Action: Take the puck all the way back below your own blue line, sometimes behind your own net, and restart as if from a breakout
Read: Use it when a neutral zone reset is not enough — their forecheck has re-formed across the middle, or your forwards need time to sort themselves out
Risk: A turnover below your own goal line is a scoring chance — the low regroup is a possession play, not a panic play
Never: Use it when you are panicking — off the glass and out of your zone is the correct answer instead
Rule: Under NHL and IIHF rules your goalie may not play the puck behind the goal line outside the trapezoid (NHL Rule 1.8; penalty 27.8 / 63.2(viii)) — the KHL reportedly too, but unverified here — the sole exception is a goalie keeping skate contact with the crease
Rule: USA Hockey and Hockey Canada write no trapezoid, and neither frees your goalie out there — USA Hockey 614(c) makes a goalie’s freeze a minor on four triggers, among them a puck "behind the goal line and their body is entirely outside of the goal crease" and a puck outside the privileged area
Rule: Hockey Canada's Interpretation 3 to Rule 10.1(a), clause v: "When a goaltender leaves their crease, they will not be allowed to freeze the puck. If they do, they will be assessed a Minor penalty. No warning will be issued."
Rule: Interpretation 3 to Hockey Canada's Rule 10.1(a), clause ii, lets a goalie come out to cut down the angle and, after stopping the shot, cover the puck. That clause ii is the exception to clause v is a reading here, not a Hockey Canada ruling; the NHL and IIHF 63.2(vi) Notes write it in
Rule: The goalkeeper's restricted area is IIHF Rule 27.7, not the NHL's 27.8; nothing the Elite League publishes switches it off, and England Ice Hockey's Rules & Regulations 22.3 call it "not currently enforced" in its own competitions — in Britain keep your goalie inside it
Rule: In a CARHA-affiliated league keep the puck moving up ice once it is in your own zone — CARHA 74(a): a team in possession in its defending zone "shall always advance the puck towards the opposing goal" unless opponents prevent it; if you do not, play stops for a faceoff in that zone
```

A deeper version: the puck goes all the way back below your own blue line, sometimes behind your net, and you restart as if from a breakout.

**Use it when** a neutral-zone reset is not enough — their forecheck has re-formed across the middle, or your forwards need time to sort themselves out. It costs territory but buys time, and turns the problem into a breakout, which you already have a system for. See [Breakouts](breakouts.md).

**Be aware of the trade.** Below your goal line their forecheck comes with you, and a turnover there is a scoring chance. The low regroup is a possession play, not a panic play — if you are panicking, **off the glass and out of your zone** is the answer. ⚠️ **In a CARHA-affiliated adult league, keep the regroup in the neutral zone, or keep the puck moving up ice once it is in your end:** CARHA Rule 74(a) says *"A team in possession of the puck in its defending zone shall always advance the puck towards the opposing goal, except if prevented from doing so by players of the opposing team"*, and an infraction stops play for a faceoff in your zone.

> **Trapezoid note: know your book before you count on your goalie on a low regroup.** Under NHL and IIHF rules your goalie cannot handle the puck behind the goal line outside the trapezoid — a delay-of-game minor — unless keeping skate contact with the goal crease. So a puck rimmed into the corner gets less help. The KHL is commonly said to have the same rule; that is unverified here, because no KHL rulebook was available. ⚠️ **The PWHL switches its trapezoid off (Rules 1.8 and 27.8) but its delay-of-game rule still lists the minor (65.2(viii)), so a PWHL team should play as if it binds.**
>
> **USA Hockey, Hockey Canada and the CARHA Hockey Official Rule Book write no trapezoid**, so under them your goalie may fetch a rimmed puck anywhere behind the net. How much rec, beer-league and youth hockey that covers is a question about leagues, and this document has no count. ⚠️ **But no trapezoid is not no restriction: all three let the goalie play the puck out there and not sit on it.** USA Hockey **Rule 614(c)** makes it a delay-of-game minor to fall on or gather the puck on four triggers, two of which reach a goalie who has gone to fetch: *"(1) The puck is behind the goal line and their body is entirely outside of the goal crease"* and *"(3) The puck is outside the boundaries of the ”goalkeeper’s privileged” area"* — and the privileged area does not include the corners. CARHA **Rule 58(b)** makes a minor of a goalkeeper who *“deliberately falls on or gathers the puck into their body… when the puck is behind the goal line and the goalkeeper’s body is entirely outside the boundaries of the goal crease area”*, and **Rule 61(b)** reaches anywhere — a minor for one who *“deliberately holds the puck and in the opinion of the Referee, is causing an unnecessary stoppage of play”*. Hockey Canada's **Interpretation 3 to Rule 10.1(a)**, clause v, is blunter: *"When a goaltender leaves their crease, they will not be allowed to freeze the puck. If they do, they will be assessed a Minor penalty. No warning will be issued."*
>
> **Do not hand your goalie clause v on its own — it is one of five.** Clause **ii** says *"[t]he goaltender may come out of their crease to cut down the angle and catch the shot or, after stopping the shot, cover the puck"*. **Hockey Canada publishes nothing saying which clause governs**, and no Hockey Canada casebook was available here, so treating clause ii as the exception is a reading of the text, not a ruling. It is supported by the Interpretation's stated intent, *"to eliminate unnecessary stoppages caused by the goaltender"*; by clause ii's second half having nothing to permit otherwise; and by the other two books writing the exception in — the **NOTE to NHL 63.2(vi)**, *"If a goalkeeper comes out of his crease to 'cut down the angle' on a shot and after making the save covers the puck, this shall be legal"*, matched by **IIHF 63.2(VI)**. On a low regroup clause v is the one that bites, because your goalie is out there to **retrieve**, not to save. A goalie out to make a *save* is a different case in every book, and [Goaltender](../positions/goaltender.md) owns it. So under these books ⚠️ **your goalie is an extra option for moving the puck, not a place to park it.**
>
> **🇬🇧 In Britain, play as though it binds.** Nothing the Elite League publishes switches it off — its Casebook adopts the 2026-27 IIHF book (Rule 27.7, not the NHL's 27.8). In England Ice Hockey's own competitions it is unsettled: its *Rules & Regulations* 22.3 say the restricted area is *"not currently enforced"*, and the In-House Rules do not mention it in the 2025-26 or 2026-27 edition — though their Introduction says *"unless otherwise stated, all rules will be enforced in line with the IIHF Rule Book"*, which points the other way. Assume it binds, ask your league, and look behind the net for painted lines. Treat an unmarked sheet as a hint, not a ruling: IIHF 27.7 turns on *"the position of the puck"*, not the markings.

### Timing the forwards' swing

```facts
Never: Stand still and call for it — a forward receiving a regroup pass at a standstill has wasted the regroup
Never: Leave too early. Turning up ice before your D controls the puck leaves you offside or leaves the puck behind you
Technique: Curl back in the same direction the puck is going, staying below the puck, then turn up into your lane as the D-to-D pass is made
Goal: Receive the puck already moving forward at close to top speed, facing the play
Position: Three forwards at three depths — one close for the short outlet, one wide for the outside lane, one high as the stretch option
```

The regroup is a defenceman's pass but a forward's tactic, and forwards get it wrong two ways.

**Wrong way one — standing still and calling for it.** You are a static target with no speed, and you will be closed down at once. A forward receiving a regroup pass at a standstill has wasted the regroup.

**Wrong way two — leaving too early.** Turn up ice before the D has the puck under control and the pass has to be perfect; if it is not, you are offside or the puck is behind you.

**The right way — swing with the puck, turn up as it moves across.** As your defenceman receives, curl back **the same way the puck is going**, staying below it so you are always a legal, supportable option. As the D-to-D pass is made, turn and drive up ice into your lane, so you receive **already moving at close to top speed**, facing the play. Elite Hockey Canada's coaching material puts it in one line: *"forwards should move in the same direction as the defensemen… move down the ice to support the defensemen and to then up the ice again."* **(Elite Hockey Canada is a private coaching site, not Hockey Canada the governing body — decent coaching material, nothing more.)**

**Three forwards, three depths.** Do not all curl to the same place at the same time. One supports close for the short outlet, one swings wide for the outside lane, one stays high as the stretch option — which is lane discipline, next.

### When not to regroup

```facts
Read: You have numbers — take a developing 3-on-2 rather than throwing it away
Read: Your change is due — a hard dump past the red line and a line change is a legitimate tactic, not a failure
Read: The pass back is not clean — a regroup into pressure is just a turnover in a worse place
```

- **When you have numbers.** Take a developing 3-on-2. A regroup that throws away an odd-man rush is not clever hockey.
- **When your change is due.** A regroup on tired legs means entering the zone with nothing left. Sometimes the right play is a hard dump past the red line and a change — a legitimate tactic, not a failure; see [Game Management](game_management.md). In a CARHA league keep the backswing short: past fifteen inches that book's Rule 79 makes it a slap shot — a minor, and a major if it injures, which ends your game (Rule 30(a)).
- **When the pass back is not clean.** A regroup into pressure is a turnover in a worse place — [Risk Management](../hockey-iq/risk_management.md) maps where that costs most.

## Attacking through the neutral zone

### Lane discipline: three lanes, three forwards

```facts
Position: Divide the ice lengthways into left wall, middle and right wall, and put one forward in each lane
Goal: Three separated forwards are three separate passing options — defenders cannot cover three lanes with one flat line of skaters unless they spread thin
Never: Put two forwards in the same lane — one defender then covers both and you have made it 5-on-4 against yourself
Action: When the puck is in a lane that is not yours, hold your lane and stay level or ahead of the puck instead of going to help
Key: Support in the neutral zone means being available in a different place, not being close
Convention: Lanes are positions, not people — you will swap constantly, so call it: "I've got middle"
```

Divide the ice lengthways into three lanes: **left wall, middle, right wall.** The base rule is **one forward in each lane**.

**Why.** Three separated forwards are three passing options, and one flat line of defenders cannot cover three lanes without spreading thin. Two forwards in one lane lets one defender cover both — you have made it 5-on-4 against yourselves.

**Why it goes wrong.** Everybody chases the puck. It goes to the left wall, your centre follows, and your right wing drifts to the middle to "support". Now the right side is empty, the defence has slid over, and there is nowhere to go.

![](diagram:neutral-zone-lanes)

**What to do.** When the puck is in a lane that is not yours, **hold your lane and stay level with or ahead of the puck**. Support here means being available somewhere else, not being close. See [Puck Support and Spacing](../hockey-iq/puck_support_and_spacing.md).

**Lanes are positions, not people.** You will swap constantly — a winger who ends up in the middle stays there and somebody else takes the wall. Call it: "I've got middle."

### The stretch pass

```facts
Position: A forward stays high — near or beyond the far blue line — and the pass bypasses the neutral zone entirely
Rule: The stretch pass is legal only because the two-line pass rule went — commonly dated to the end of the 2004-05 lockout, a date in no rulebook held here; what is checked is that NHL Rule 83 now contains no two-line provision
Read: It counters a flat line still holding out in the neutral zone — a trap at the red line or a 1-3-1 — but not a 1-4, which leaves no legal ice behind it
Rule: One skate in contact with the far blue line, or on your own side of it, keeps the stretch forward onside at the instant the puck completely crosses (NHL Rule 83.1)
Rule: A skate in the air over the neutral zone counts as onside under NHL, IIHF and PWHL rules, but not under USA Hockey 630(a) or Hockey Canada 6.11, which require skate contact; CARHA never defines its "own side", so drag the blade in contact and you are onside in every book
Technique: Flat and hard, into the space they are skating into — a stretch pass to a stationary player is a turnover
Risk: Leaving a forward high means defending 4-on-5 while you do it — a coaching decision, so ask
```

**What it is.** A long pass from your own end or the neutral zone to a forward who has stayed high — near or beyond the far blue line — skipping the neutral zone entirely.

**Why it exists.** It is legal only because the two-line pass rule went (see [What the neutral zone actually is](#what-the-neutral-zone-actually-is)). It counters a flat line **still holding out in the neutral zone** — a 1-2-2 trap at the red line, or the three-man line of a 1-3-1 — because there is real ice behind such a line for a receiver to stand in legally.

> **It is not the counter to a 1-4.** A 1-4 holds its line on **its own blue line**, so there is no legal ice behind it: a receiver already behind it is offside the moment the puck crosses. Against a 1-4, **chip it hard past the flat line and race for it** — see [the 1-4 section](#1-4--the-full-retreat) and [Forechecking Systems](forechecking_systems.md). The same goes for any structure that has retreated to its own blue line, whatever it is called.

**How to do it well.**
- **Get behind the wall, not level with it.** Level is covered; behind is a footrace.
- **Stay onside.** Time it rather than camping. One skate on the far blue line, or on your own side of it, keeps you onside as the puck crosses. **Drag the blade in contact with the line** — a skate in the air counts as onside under NHL, IIHF and PWHL rules but not under USA Hockey or Hockey Canada, and CARHA never says, so contact is onside under every book checked.
- **Pass to space, not to feet.** A stretch pass to a stationary player is a turnover; into the space they are skating into, it is a breakaway.
- **Flat and hard.** A saucer over 60 feet is very hard to take at speed. See [Passing and Receiving](../technique/passing_and_receiving.md).

**The cost.** Leaving a forward high means defending 4-on-5 while you do it. That is a coaching decision: some coaches want a stretch option every possession, others forbid it in some score situations. **Ask.**

### The late man

```facts
Position: Delay deliberately, staying 10 to 15 feet behind the play, and arrive into the space the first two attackers have vacated
Goal: Arrive into the high slot behind a two-man rush — defenders track the puck, so the second man in is the one nobody picked up
Convention: Coaching material treats it as one of the most reliable scoring looks in hockey — consensus rather than a tracked figure
Never: Drift five feet behind — being the late man means a full gap back, so the defence has to choose between you and the puck
Action: Be the first man back if it turns over — that is the deal
```

Also called the **trailer** or **third man high**. One forward deliberately stays 10 to 15 feet behind the play and arrives into the space the first two have vacated.

The picture below is drawn from the defending bench, because that side has to solve it. Read "the defencemen" and "the backcheckers" as the trailer's opponents.

![](diagram:rush-trailer)

**Why it works.** Defenders track the puck and the immediate threats, so the player arriving second is the one nobody picked up. Coaching material treats the late man into the high slot behind a two-man rush as one of the most reliable scoring looks in hockey — **coaching consensus rather than a tracked figure**: published goal breakdowns stop at the shot and the pass before it, and **none classifies a rush goal by the coverage that broke down**.

**The discipline.** Be genuinely late — a full gap back, not five feet, so the defence has to choose between you and the puck. And be first back if it turns over; that is the deal.

### Chip-and-chase

```facts
Action: Rather than carrying through a tight gap, chip the puck past the defender into space — off the boards, off the glass, or softly behind them — and skate onto it
Read: Right against a defenceman standing up at the line, against a 1-3-1 with only one man behind the wall, or when you have a speed advantage
Technique: The chip is a placement, not a clearance — soft off the wall into the corner you are already skating to, or flat past the defender's stick-side hip
Never: Chip without chasing — the difference from dump-and-pray is entirely whether the second and third forwards go
Never: Chip from your own side of the red line at equal strength — untouched over their goal line it is icing, and you lose the zone and the faceoff
Risk: After an even-strength icing, NHL, IIHF, PWHL and Hockey Canada U18AAA, Junior and opted-in Senior rules bar a line change before the faceoff — narrow exceptions, an injured player among them
```

**What it is.** Instead of carrying through a defender with a tight gap, **chip the puck past them into space** — off the boards, off the glass, or softly behind them — and skate onto it.

**When it is right.** Against a defenceman standing up at the line; against a 1-3-1, with only one player behind the wall; when you have a speed advantage; when you are changing and want the puck deep and safe.

**How to do it well.** The chip is a **placement**, not a clearance. Aim for a spot you can win — soft off the wall so it dies in the corner you are skating to, or flat past the defender's stick-side hip so you run around them on your forehand. **The difference from dump-and-pray is whether the second and third forwards go.**

> **Icing note:** from your own side of the red line at equal strength, a hard chip that crosses their goal line untouched is icing — you lose the zone and the faceoff position. **Under NHL, IIHF and PWHL rules you also cannot change** (NHL and IIHF Rule 81.4, PWHL 83.4), **though there are narrow exceptions and replacing an injured player is one**. That half is not universal: USA Hockey and CARHA write no such bar, and Hockey Canada's reaches U18AAA and Junior, and Senior only where the Member opts in (Rule 6.7(d)). Get it over the red line first, or chip it soft enough to stay in.

### Using the middle to change the point of attack

```facts
Goal: A puck touched in the middle forces every defender to reset — the structure has to slide twice, and sliding structures have seams
Action: Play wall to middle to far wall — a two-pass sequence that takes about a second and is the most reliable way to enter with control against organised defence
Technique: Make the middle-lane pass a one-touch relay, not a reception and a look
Risk: Losing the puck in the middle costs the most relative to the risk you thought you were taking — coaching consensus rather than measurement — and a player who turns to survey there gets hit
Never: Take a middle-lane puck with your head down over it — you cannot brace for a hit you never saw, and the middle is where coaching material puts the contact
```

A forward in the middle lane is not usually there to shoot. **A puck touched in the middle forces every defender to reset.**

A defensive structure defends one side at a time — the whole thing slides strong side. One quick pass into the middle and out to the far wall makes it slide twice, and a sliding structure has seams. Wall to middle to far wall takes about a second and is the most reliable way to enter with control against organised defence.

**The catch.** The middle is also where losing the puck costs most relative to the risk you thought you were taking — **coaching consensus rather than measurement**; [Risk Management](../hockey-iq/risk_management.md) owns that grading and puts your own slot higher still. So make the middle-lane pass a **one-touch relay**, not a reception and a look. A middle forward who turns to survey gets hit, and the puck goes the other way with your team spread out.

**Plan around the hit, not just the puck.** A one-touch relay is a puck instruction; it does not stop you putting your head down to make it, and coaching material puts open-ice contact in the middle of the neutral zone — **consensus rather than a counted ranking**. ⚠️ **Take it with your head up.** [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md#keep-your-head-up-always--you-cannot-brace-for-a-hit-you-cannot-see) owns that rule; it applies equally when you track home through the middle and when you skate onto a stretch pass. Scan **before** the puck arrives — [Scanning and Anticipation](../hockey-iq/scanning_and_anticipation.md) buys you the look you would otherwise take with the puck on your stick.

### Taking a pass on the wall with a defender stepping up

**The puck comes to you on the boards and their defenceman is stepping up to meet you.** One of the commonest situations a winger faces, and you have about a second. Three good answers:

- **Bump it to the middle on one touch** if your centre is there and moving — the defender who stepped up is now behind the play.
- **Chip it off the boards past the defender's outside and skate around them** — the [chip-and-chase](#chip-and-chase), and only if a second forward is coming to support it.
- **Turn it back to a defenceman and regroup** if neither of those is there.

Do not stop on the wall and try to beat the stepping defender one-on-one with nobody behind you — a turnover there is a rush the other way with you on the wrong side. Which answer comes first is a coaching choice (some teams chip every wall puck, others play it to the middle), so ask your bench. Take the pass with your skates parallel to the boards and your head up, and never turn your back to the checker — a defender stepping up on you is exactly when that matters.

## Beating a trap

**In short:** Against a trap, move the puck faster than they can slide, use the boards, and be patient rather than forcing the seam.

```facts
Action: Move the puck faster than they can slide — two quick passes, D-to-D or wall-middle-wall, beat five skaters sliding; a trap beats teams that carry the puck, not teams that pass it
Action: Use the boards deliberately — a rim or a chip off the wall to a forward arriving behind the pressure, especially against F2 stepping up
Never: Turn your back to the checker on a rim or a chip along the wall, and never duck — take the contact with your skates parallel to the boards, forearm and hip to the wall, head up and chin off your chest
Read: Stretch a forward behind the wall against a 1-3-1 or a trap, but not against a 1-4. Chip past a 1-4's flat line and race for it instead
Never: Attempt the seam unless it is obviously there — the highest-risk answer, the one amateur players attempt first, and the one to try last
Options: Dump it past the red line on purpose and hunt it with three forwards when the entry is not on — worth more than a turnover at the blue line
Key: Dumping past the red line is a read on a trap, not a general preference — the one-third threshold is modelled from one team's games, and that NHL tracking found players give the puck up too readily
Mindset: Be patient, and keep the puck moving — a trap gives you the puck and waits for you to do something stupid with it
```

A trap hands you the puck and defends the ice instead, so possession alone tells you nothing about what to do. Everything below assumes a passive structure — a 1-2-2 trap, a 1-3-1 or a 1-4 — that refuses to come and get the puck.

**1. Move the puck faster than they can slide.** A wall of four defends one side at a time and cannot re-form faster than a hard pass travels. Two quick passes, D-to-D or wall-middle-wall, beat five skaters sliding. **A trap beats teams that carry the puck; it does not beat teams that pass it.**

**2. Use the boards deliberately.** A hard rim or a chip off the wall goes past a defender playing the ice, not the boards. Chipping it along the wall to a forward arriving behind the pressure is unglamorous and very effective, especially against F2 stepping up on your winger. **In a CARHA league, rim it with a short backswing:** CARHA's Rule 79 makes any backswing past fifteen inches a slap shot — a minor, and a major if it injures, which ejects you (Rule 30(a)).

**That play sends the receiver onto a puck on the boards with a defender closing** — in most structures above the strong-side defenceman's job is to step up hard on a pass to the wall, so expect to be met. ⚠️ **Never turn your back to the checker, and never duck.** Get your **skates parallel to the boards**, forearm and hip to the wall, head up and chin off your chest — **not the point of your shoulder, and not the whole shoulder if you can help it.** **This holds in a non-checking league too:** the wall does not need an opponent to hurt you, and a legal angle-off puts you into it just as a check does. [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md) owns the full rule and the evidence.

**3. Stretch a forward behind the wall — against a 1-3-1 or a trap, not a 1-4.** A 1-3-1 lives ahead of one defenceman, so a forward behind the three-man line taking a legal stretch pass is instantly 1-on-1 or better. Even the *threat* makes the wall drop back and opens ice in front of it. **This only works while the wall is out in the neutral zone.** Against a 1-4 on its own blue line a receiver behind it is offside, so **chip hard past the flat line and race for it** — nobody in a flat line is deeper than anybody else, and only the goalie is beyond. **If the goalie comes out and gets there first, pull up and take away the outlet rather than finish on them** — a goalie outside the crease is not fair game in any rulebook here, and [Forechecking Systems](forechecking_systems.md#why-this-matters-so-much-to-a-forechecker) covers what contact with one costs.

**4. Skate it through the seam — only when it is real.** A trap committed to one side has a seam behind the pressure, and a skilled carrier cutting back through the middle behind a stepped-up forechecker breaks it completely. It is the highest-reward, highest-risk answer — the one amateurs try first and should try last. **If the seam is not obviously there, it is not there.**

**5. Dump it and forecheck — on purpose.** If the entry is not on, **put the puck deep past the red line and hunt it with three forwards.** A team that has put five players in the neutral zone has built itself to defend rushes, not forechecks, so a grind is often the right choice. A controlled entry is worth roughly twice a dump-in, but **a dump-in you chase is worth more than a turnover at the blue line.**

Published analytics suggests a carry is only the better choice when you are around a third confident of completing it. That threshold is modelled from one team's tracked games and an assumed shooting percentage, so it is a shape to think with, not a precise number. And that NHL work found players give the puck up at the line **too readily**, not too rarely — so this is a read on a trap, not a reason to dump by default. **Against five players committed to the neutral zone, your chance of completing a carry is low by design.**

**6. Be patient, and make them come.** A trap punishes impatience: it gives you the puck and waits for a mistake. Keeping the puck in your end, regrouping twice, and making them come or stand and watch is a legitimate answer — and if they stand, you have 200 feet to build speed into. That is what the Flyers showed, crudely, in 2011. **Patience means moving the puck, not standing on it:** every rulebook lets the referee penalise a team for delaying the game, and Hockey Canada's Rule 10.1(a) and CARHA's Rule 55(a) make deliberate team delay a bench minor, as USA Hockey's 610(g) does after a warning. ⚠️ **In a CARHA-affiliated league, do your regrouping in the neutral zone instead:** CARHA Rule 74(a) makes a team with the puck in its own zone advance it unless opponents prevent it, and stops play for a faceoff in that zone if it does not.

## Neutral zone faceoffs

**In short:** Treat it as a set play: when defending, take away the middle; when attacking, win it clean; and never creep before the drop.

```facts
Goal: Defending — stop a clean win back to their point, take the middle away first, and get the puck out so you can change
Position: Defending wingers commonly line up between the opposing wingers and the middle of the ice rather than directly on their man
Position: Your centre sets up on the defensive side of the opposing centre, so a lost draw still leaves him between the puck and his net
Goal: Attacking — win it clean to a defenceman for an immediate entry or shot, or to the wall to chip it in and forecheck
Action: Know before the drop whether you are winning it forward or back, and who your man is if you lose it
Rule: A teammate's encroachment gets your centre ejected, and a second team violation on the same draw is a bench minor under NHL and IIHF 76.7 and PWHL 78.7 — an individual minor on the offender under USA Hockey 613(d), and discretionary under Hockey Canada 6.2(e) and CARHA 57(d)
Rule: The IIHF 2025/26 book warned the team and ejected nobody, but its 2026/27 book ejects as the NHL does (IIHF 76.6, both editions) — Faceoffs carries the five-book split
Rule: Across the Home Countries the centre is replaced for every violation except on a faceoff following an icing, whichever IIHF edition your league runs (IHUK In-House Rules 2026-27, Section 10, Rule 76); an Elite League draw, which those rules never name, runs IIHF 2026/27 76.6
Convention: The specific alignments are a coaching choice and vary hugely — ask your coach for yours
```

A regulation sheet has **four neutral zone faceoff dots** — two just inside each blue line, **five feet from the line and forty-four feet apart** (NHL Rule 1.9), so each sits about twenty and a half feet from its boards — plus the centre dot. Both teams treat a neutral zone draw as a set play.

![](diagram:faceoff-neutral-zone)

**What each team is trying to do**, in general — the specific alignments are a coaching choice and vary hugely:

**The defending team** (dot near its own blue line) generally wants to:
- **Stop a clean win back to the opposition defenceman**, which gives an immediate shot or controlled entry.
- **Take away the middle first.** Wingers commonly line up between the opposing wingers and the middle rather than directly on their man.
- **Get the puck out and change.** A defensive-side draw is often about relief as much as offence.
- Put the **centre on the defensive side of the opposing centre**, so a lost draw still leaves them between the puck and their net.

**The attacking team** (dot near the opponent's blue line) generally wants to:
- **Win it clean to a defenceman for an immediate entry or shot** before the defence sets.
- **Create confusion with pre-draw movement** — wingers switching sides, or swinging behind the circle to receive in an unexpected lane. Coaching resources on NHL faceoff plays describe wingers taking different lanes to open a passing option.
- **Win it to the wall and chip it in**, turning the draw straight into a forecheck.

**What you need to know before the puck drops:**
1. **Are we winning it forward or back?** Your centre should say. If you do not know, you are guessing.
2. **Who is your man if we lose it?** Neutral zone draws turn straight into rushes, because everybody is already facing the right way.
3. **Do not creep.** ⚠️ **A teammate drifting in early gets your centre thrown out of the draw.** A neutral-zone dot has no circle, but every book still keeps the other players about fifteen feet from the two centres (NHL 76.6, USA Hockey 613(b), CARHA 57(a)). Hold your line until the puck drops.

**What it costs.** Encroachment gets the centre ejected from the draw, and a second violation by your team on the same draw is a bench minor under NHL 76.7, PWHL 78.7 and IIHF 76.7, and an individual minor on the offender under USA Hockey 613(d). ⚠️ **That second penalty is automatic in four books of six and a judgement call in two, so plan on the strictest.** Hockey Canada 6.2(e) and CARHA 57(d) say a team *"may be assessed a Minor penalty"* — Hockey Canada for second violations of its provisions (a) to (d), CARHA only of its own section (a). ⚠️ **Hockey Canada's discretion vanishes in one case a frustrated player can produce:** the minor is mandatory — *"will be assessed"* — where the offender *"subsequently contacts the Linesperson before the Linesperson has had reasonable time to vacate the face-off location"*.

**What changed.** ⚠️ **The IIHF no longer spares the centre.** Its 2025/26 edition only warned the team; its 2026/27 edition adopts the NHL's sentence — *"the offending team's player taking the face-off shall be ejected from the face-off circle"* (Rule 76.6). **In Britain the edition does not matter:** the IHUK In-House Rules 2026-27 say *"with the exception of face-offs following icings, the linesperson shall immediately replace the centre for all violations"*, applied *"in England, Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland (the Home Countries)"*. They do not name the Elite League, but its Casebook leaves Rule 76 to the 2026/27 IIHF book, which ejects the centre too. [Faceoffs](faceoffs.md) covers the split, the In-House scope, and the technique.

## Reading the neutral zone while you carry the puck

**In short:** Read F1's route, count the bodies across the middle and check your support before you commit with the puck.

```facts
Read: F1 skating straight at you, hard → they are pressuring; the answer is one quick pass past him
Read: F1 arcing across, showing you the boards, not really closing → containment or a trap; do not go where they are steering you
Read: F1 standing near the red line doing nothing → 1-3-1 or 1-4; take your time, look up and start a regroup
Read: Count the bodies across the middle — two means space to attack, three means a 1-3-1, four in a line means a 1-4 or a full trap
Read: All their forwards on one side means the ice on the other side is real — that is what the D-to-D and the middle-lane relay are for
Read: Their defencemen's feet stopped or squared up at the line → chip past them; feet moving backwards → carry it, you have the line
Action: Check your own support before you commit — if both your forwards are in your lane, slow down and let them separate
Key: Know the clock and the score — a regroup at 2-2 in the second is smart hockey, the same regroup 40 seconds down a goal is a mistake
```

You are coming out of your own end with the puck and about 1.5 seconds to decide. Here is the read, in the order you get it.

**1. Look at F1's route, not F1's speed.** It is the biggest tell.
- **F1 skating straight at you, hard** → pressure (2-1-2, or an aggressive 1-2-2). **Beat it with one quick pass past them** — beat the first man and you are attacking with numbers.
- **F1 arcing across, showing you the boards, not really closing** → containment or a trap. **Do not go where they are steering you.** Change sides with a D-to-D, or accept the wall and be ready to chip past the man stepping up.
- **F1 standing near the red line doing nothing** → 1-3-1 or 1-4. **Take your time, look up, and start a regroup.** Against a **1-3-1** the stretch pass is live, because there is ice behind the line. Against a **1-4** it is not — a receiver behind a wall on its own blue line is offside — so chip past and race instead. Skating straight into the wall is what they want.

**2. Count the bodies across the middle.** Lift your head at your own blue line and count the opponents between you and their blue line.
- **Two** → there is space. Attack it.
- **Three across** → 1-3-1. Look behind the wall before you look through it.
- **Four in a line** → 1-4 or a full trap. There is nothing there. Regroup or chip and change.

**3. Check the far side.** If all their forwards are on one side of the ice, the ice on the other side is real. This is what the D-to-D and the middle-lane relay are for.

**4. Check your own support before you commit.** If both your other forwards are in your lane, there is no pass and you are about to be alone against three. Slow down, hold the puck, and let them separate.

**5. Watch their defencemen's feet.** Stopped or squared up at the line means standing up — **chip past them**. Moving backwards means backing off — **carry it, you have the line**. Decide around the red line, not at their blue line.

**6. Know the clock and the score.** A regroup at 2-2 in the second is smart hockey. The same regroup with 40 seconds left, down a goal, is a mistake.

## Common Mistakes

- **Hanging at the red line waiting for a breakaway.** **When the puck turns over, get on the defensive side of your check and track home through the middle.** Without your backpressure your defencemen must back off and concede controlled entries all night. A forward told to stay up as the stretch option is running a plan, not making this mistake.
- **Forcing the entry every time.** **If it is not open, do not go — regroup, or put it in deep and chase it.** Losing the puck at their blue line is worse than either.
- **Never regrouping — treating a backwards pass as a failure.** **When nothing is on, give it back to a defenceman, swing, and come again with speed** — unless you have numbers, your change is due, or the pass back is not clean.
- **All three forwards in the same lane.** **When the puck is in a lane that is not yours, hold your lane, stay level with or ahead of it, and call your lane.** Chasing as a group lets one defender cover three of you.
- **Receiving a regroup pass standing still.** **Curl back the way the puck is going, stay below it, and turn up into your lane as it goes D-to-D** — but not before your defenceman has it, or you end up offside or with the puck behind you.
- **Following the puck up the boards after a D-to-D pass.** **Sink back into mid-ice behind your partner instead**, so a puck given back reaches somebody facing up ice with the whole sheet in front of them.
- **Blind D-to-D through the middle of the neutral zone.** **If the lane is not clean, hold the puck or take it back yourself.** An interception there is an odd-man rush with both defencemen wrong-sided.
- **Chipping the puck in and not chasing it.** **Chip it to a spot you can win, and send the second and third forwards after it.** Without the chase it is a giveaway.
- **Stretching a forward behind a 1-4.** **Chip past the flat line and race for it instead**, and if the goalie gets there first, pull up and take away the outlet. A receiver behind a line on its own blue line is offside; keep the stretch pass for a line still out in the neutral zone.
- **Trying the seam first against a trap.** **Try it last: move the puck faster than they can slide, use the boards, and if the seam is not obviously there, it is not there.**
- **Dumping from your own side of the red line at equal strength.** **Get the puck across the red line first, or chip it soft enough to stay in.** Otherwise it is icing: you lose the zone and the draw location, and under NHL, IIHF and PWHL rules — and Hockey Canada's in U18AAA, Junior and opted-in Senior — your tired line cannot change either, though an injured player can still be replaced. USA Hockey and CARHA have no such bar.
- **Standing up at the blue line with no backpressure.** **Check over your shoulder first; with nobody coming back, back off and keep the attacker in front of you and outside.** Standing up alone turns a rush into a breakaway.
- **Finishing a step up as a hit, or reading a head-down carrier as a licence to arrive harder.** **Close the space with your feet and keep the attacker in front of you — take their time and the puck, do not punish a carrier who has not seen you.** Miss and the rush is past you; land it and it is charging, in every division, checking league or not. ⚠️ **No book here caps charging at a minor**: Hockey Canada makes a charge on a player who cannot defend themselves a mandatory match penalty, and in several books a charge that injures ends your game. [Gap control as a team concept](#gap-control-as-a-team-concept) sets out each book.
- **Taking a middle-lane relay with your head down over the puck.** **Scan before the puck arrives, then take it with your head up.** Coaching material puts the contact in the middle — **consensus rather than a counted ranking**. [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md) owns the head-up rule.
- **Chasing a rim or a chip along the wall with your back to the checker.** **Never turn your back to the checker, and never duck** — skates parallel to the boards, forearm and hip to the wall, head up and chin off your chest, never the point of your shoulder. A wall hurts you without an opponent, so this holds in a non-checking league too.
- **Assuming everyone is playing the same structure.** **Confirm the plan on the bench, not during the shift.** Half your unit pressuring and half trapping opens a seam through your own team.
- **Confusing the neutral zone 1-3-1 with the power-play 1-3-1.** **When a coach calls a 1-3-1, check which zone they mean.** Same digits, different shape and job.

## Key Takeaways

1. **Know which structure your team is playing, and play the same one as the other four.** 1-2-2, trap, 1-3-1, pressure, 1-4, left-wing lock — each is a coaching choice, not a law. Learn your default and the signal to change it. You have about three seconds across the middle third, so decide before the puck reaches you.
2. **Read their F1.** Coming hard means pressure — beat it with one pass. Arcing across means a trap — do not go where you are steered. Then read their defencemen's feet: stopped or squared up at the line, chip past them; moving backwards, carry it — you have the line.
3. **When the puck is lost, get on the defensive side of your check and track home through the middle, head up.** Do not hang at the red line unless your coach has made you the stretch man. Your defencemen can only hold a tight gap if you come back, so when you keep getting entered on with speed, the fix is rarely the defence.
4. **At your own blue line, stand up only with help behind you; otherwise back off.** Check over your shoulder and call it. If the puck is chipped past you, go for the puck, never the player. ⚠️ **Stand up with your feet, not with a hit** — finished as a hit it is charging in every division, no book caps it at a minor, and Hockey Canada makes it a mandatory match penalty on a player who cannot defend themselves.
5. **Regroup instead of forcing a bad entry.** As a forward, curl back with the puck and turn up as it goes D-to-D — never stand still, never leave before your defenceman has it. As a defenceman, after a D-to-D sink into mid-ice so it can come back to you. Skip it when you have numbers, your change is due, or the pass back is not clean.
6. **Three forwards, three lanes.** Support means being somewhere else, not close. Use the middle as a one-touch relay — wall to middle to far wall makes their structure slide twice. **Take the middle-lane puck with your head up**: coaching material puts the contact there — **consensus rather than a counted ranking**.
7. **Be the late man, or take him.** Attacking, trail a full gap — 10 to 15 feet — behind a two-man rush and arrive into the high slot, and be first back if it turns over. Backchecking, the trailer is usually yours rather than your defencemen's — ask whether your team gives him to the weak-side defenceman.
8. **Beat a trap by moving the puck, not carrying it.** Quick passes, the boards, a stretch forward behind a wall still out in the neutral zone, or a dump you chase. Against a 1-4, chip past the flat line and race; if the goalie gets there first, pull up. Try the seam last. ⚠️ **On the boards, never turn your back to the checker and never duck — skates parallel, head up, even in a non-checking league.**
9. **Carry it in when the line is yours; when it is not, a dump you chase beats a turnover at the line.** Public NHL tracking puts a carry at roughly twice a dump in shot attempts — NHL-only, over a decade old, and it found players giving the puck up too readily, so take the direction, not the decimals. Get it over the red line first or it is icing, and in several books your tired line cannot change. Chase it unless you are changing.
10. **Before a neutral-zone draw, know whether you are winning it forward or back, and who your man is if you lose it.** These draws turn straight into rushes. Hold your line until the puck drops — creeping gets your centre thrown out.

## Notes on verification

- The two-line pass rule's removal for the 2005-06 season, alongside the obstruction crackdown and the trapezoid, is well attested but rests on secondary summaries, not the NHL rulebook. No form of the date — 2005, 2004-05 or 2005-06 — appears in the current NHL book; every four-digit year printed in it was checked. The book does date some rules — Rule 9.7 (*"Beginning with the 2013-2014 season"*), Rule 9.6 (*"beginning with the 2019-2020 season"*), Rule 64.3 (a memorandum *"distributed to all teams prior to the 2015-2016 season"*) — but not this one; Rule 83 carries no year. So the date is unchecked rather than contradicted, and the removal itself is not in doubt, because Rule 83 has no two-line provision.
- The **origin of the left-wing lock is settled once the two claims are read as complementary.** Wikipedia traces the invention to 1970s Czechoslovak hockey; The Coaches Site credits Barry Smith with adapting it for the NHL rink and bringing it to Detroit under Scotty Bowman in the mid-1990s. Those are different events twenty years apart, not rival attributions. Detroit's adoption from 1994-95 and their 1997 Cup are not in dispute.
- The **zone entry figures** (a carry-in producing roughly twice the shots of a dump-in; the rough one-third confidence threshold for attempting a carry) come from **public hockey analytics work on NHL data from the 2011-12 season** — Tulsky, Detweiler, Spencer and Sznajder, *Using Zone Entry Data To Separate Offensive, Neutral, And Defensive Zone Performance*, presented at the **2013** MIT Sloan Sports Analytics Conference (330 hand-tracked games from **2011-12**) — plus Corey Sznajder's separate later tracking (**2014-16**, reported second-hand). Note the two dates the paper carries: **2013 is when it was presented, 2011-12 is when the data was collected.** [Zone Entries](zone_entries.md) carries the full table. They are more than a decade old, the exact values differ between studies, and they describe NHL play, not your league. Use the direction of the finding, not the decimals.
- **The shot-differential finding is the paper's own, and it is quoted from the paper's own discussion**, not from any of the secondary write-ups listed below: *"Surprisingly, the talent component of shot differential seems to come almost entirely from the neutral zone play, as metrics like entry differential and fraction of entries with possession clearly show reproducible differences from player to player…"* The same discussion carries the conclusion it rests on — that the shots-per-possession component *"is heavily driven by luck at the single-season level"* — and the numbered conclusion *"the neutral zone play that sets a team up to enter the offensive zone with possession is a critical driver of success."* Located in the PDF text on 31 July 2026. It is an inference the authors drew from their own tracking, and it is about NHL players in one season.
- **The direction of that paper's own strategic conclusion is easy to get backwards, so it is worth stating.** It observed **NHL players only** and makes no claim of any kind about amateur, junior or recreational behaviour. Its conclusion runs *toward* carrying: *"it is worth questioning whether teams are pressing hard enough to carry the puck in"*, and *"in general NHL teams probably could benefit from being more aggressive at the blue line."* Where this document recommends putting the puck deep against a trap, that is a coaching argument about one specific picture — five opponents committed to the neutral zone — and not a finding of the paper. The one-third threshold is also narrower than it sounds: the authors calculated it from **average** outcomes and said plainly that it is *"still not sufficient data to say conclusively that teams should be trying harder to carry the puck in at every opportunity"*, and the turnover and net-value figures underneath it were tracked in the Washington games within the sample rather than in all 330. [Zone Entries](zone_entries.md) carries both scopes in full.
- Neutral zone faceoff **dot dimensions** come straight from **NHL Rule 1.9**: *"Two red spots two feet (2') in diameter shall be marked on the ice in the neutral zone five feet (5') from each blue line… The spots shall be forty-four feet (44') apart and each shall be a uniform distance from the adjacent boards."* The 85-foot sheet width gives the roughly twenty-and-a-half feet from the boards quoted in the text; the rulebook itself states only the uniform-distance requirement.
- The specific faceoff alignments described are **generalisations from coaching resources**. There is no standard neutral zone faceoff alignment; ask your coach for yours.
- **The regroup count quoted above is an AHL power-play figure and nothing more.** It comes from *Structure vs Speed: Evaluating the Power Play Neutral Zone Regroup Decision in the AHL*, a student presentation by Dani Chu, Kristen Bystrom, Matthew Reyers and Lucas Wu (Simon Fraser University) using event data supplied by HockeyData for **198 AHL games between 14 October 2016 and 28 January 2017, 14 Eastern Conference teams, 1,343 five-on-four power plays, 2,217 minutes**. It is a conference presentation rather than a peer-reviewed paper, it counts a regroup as a power-play recovery of the puck in the neutral zone — wider than this document's definition — and its authors list "gather more data for more reliable estimates" as future work. It is cited here to show that *a* professional regroup count exists, not to put a number on your shift.
- **On rush goals.** Public tracking does break goals down by how they were produced. Corey Sznajder's All Three Zones tracking splits five-on-five offence into rush and forecheck/cycle play and reports the share and shooting percentage of each, along with the type and lane of the pass before the shot — the article cited below is **playoff data**, not a full season. A separate public analysis of the NHL's own goal-tracking data counted rush goals by team for a full regular season, and defines a rush goal by three **puck-trajectory** tests and nothing else: how far the puck travelled along the length of the ice, how long it spent in the offensive zone, and how long it spent beyond the goal line. **Neither classifies a goal by the defensive breakdown that allowed it**, and that is the claim this document is careful about. Both pages were re-read on 29 August 2026 to check that. Both are NHL, and one is a single hand-tracker's data. **Nor does anything located for this document count where on the sheet possession changes hands.** The tracking cited above counts zone exits and zone entries as events; it does not partition a game's turnovers by zone, and the All Three Zones author lists splitting in-zone offence by whether it began "off a turnover, a dump-in or a cycle" as work still to do. So the description of the neutral zone as the place possession changes hands most often stays a coaching-material impression, and the middle third's ownership of odd-man rushes with it.
- **On what structure teams run.** The public tracking that exists for the neutral zone measures *events* — zone exits, zone entries, whether the puck carrier was pressured — not which shape a team lined up in. So the "most widely played" description above stays a coaching-material impression.
