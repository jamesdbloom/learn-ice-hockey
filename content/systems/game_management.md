## Key focus

**Protecting a lead, manage the puck, not the clock.** Nothing across the middle of your own end; chip it past the forechecker rather than forcing a breakout pass. Deny the middle, win your own-end draws and keep shifts short.

**Trailing, add risk in order.** Speed and support through the neutral zone first, then a harder forecheck, then pinches, then a defenceman into the rush, then the goalie. Scale it to the clock and the goals you need, and keep someone last back.

**Change on the way out.** Go when your team has the puck heading north, has put it deep or has cleared it. Never while they have it in your end, never as the last player back, and not in a period's last minute. Never ice it to get a change.

**After a goal against, play one simple shift.** Win a puck, get it deep, take your man, change. Don't chase the goal back, and don't go looking for a hit — a late hit can cost far more than two minutes. Say one thing: "reset, next shift."

**Pull the goalie earlier than feels natural.** Down two with four or five minutes left is normal. With six on, get set up, put pucks to the net with a body in front who stays off the goalie, and never pinch both point men.

**On a delayed penalty, get the extra attacker on at once.** Play stops when the other team controls the puck, so the goalie goes as the referee's arm rises. Make no blind pass across your own goal — you are the only team that can score on it. Not every league allows it, so agree it beforehand.

**Once your goalie is pulled, take no penalty in your own end.** A foul that would have cost a penalty shot becomes an awarded goal. With a loose puck in your crease, knock it straight down — holding it is what costs you.

**Defending a pulled goalie, hold a tight shape in the slot.** Don't chase. Lift sticks low on the shaft at the net front as the puck arrives, then let go, and shoot at the empty net only through a clean lane — a miss can be icing.

**In 3-on-3 overtime, keep the puck.** Without a good look, regroup rather than shoot: a miss is often a 3-on-2 the other way. Change only with the puck, and check first whether your league plays overtime at all.

**Keep your feet moving and give up the last word.** When your feet stop, your stick reaches, and that is hooking and tripping. A push back costs you a minor and often wipes out the power play you had just earned.

## Overview

**Game management** is the layer above systems: how the score and the clock change the risk you take. A 2-1 lead with four minutes left is a different game from a 2-1 lead in the first period. Anything here that is not explicitly a rule is a **coaching choice** — your team may want something different.

**It comes down to one question: how much risk do the score and the clock allow right now?** With a lead, the danger is a turnover in a bad place and tired legs, so you manage the puck and the bench. Trailing, the danger is the odd-man rush you give up while chasing, so you add risk in a known order. At the very end the goalie is the biggest lever on both sides.

**Two habits sit under all of it: changing at the right moment, and giving up the last word.** A bad change and a retaliation penalty both hand the other team something for nothing, at any score.

**Trailing teams shoot more and leading teams shoot less.** That is well documented, and it distorts every raw statistic. Much else here is coaching lore rather than measurement, and the document says so where it matters.

**Which book your league runs changes some of the costs** — what icing costs you, whether coincidental minors make it 4-on-4, and whether there is overtime at all. 🇬🇧 **In Britain the IIHF flags are yours**, and many fixtures have no overtime. Where a rule *is* the tactic, it is set out in full.

## Score Effects: How the Score Changes the Game

### The finding

This is a well-documented result in hockey analytics: **teams that are trailing take more shots, and teams that are leading take fewer.** The effect is large, it is consistent, and it grows as the deficit grows and as the clock runs down.

Analysts noticed this early enough that it reshaped hockey statistics. Shot-attempt measures — **Corsi** (all shot attempts for and against, including blocks and misses) and **Fenwick** (the same, minus blocked shots) — turned out to be badly distorted by score. The first fix was to count only attempts while the game is level (**Corsi Tied**) or within one goal (**Corsi Close**). In 2011 it was found that a team's shot differential *in a tied game state* predicted future wins better than its actual points in the standings.

Corsi Close was later criticised for throwing away too much data, and the modern approach is **score adjustment** — weighting each shot attempt according to the score situation it happened in, so an attempt at 3-0 down in the third period counts for less than an attempt at 1-1 in the first.

### Why leading teams retreat

There are honest reasons, and they matter:

- **A goal against costs a leading team more than a goal for gains them.** Going 2-1 up from 1-1 is worth less than going 1-1 from 2-1 down is worth to the opponent. Asymmetric stakes justify asymmetric risk.
- **Time is on your side.** If the clock runs out with the score unchanged, you win. The trailing team has to force the issue; you don't.
- **The trailing team over-commits.** When the other team pushes defencemen up into the rush and cheats for offence, the counter-attack is available. Defending deeper and countering is a legitimate way to *score*, not just to survive.
- **The trailing team pulls its goalie.** Late, a lead turns into an empty-net opportunity — a shot at an unguarded goal, which needs no ranking to be obviously the best look available.

So a leading team conceding shot attempts is not automatically playing badly. It is often trading *volume* of shots against for *quality* of shots against — more point shots from distance, fewer chances from the slot.

### Why it's often overdone

```facts
Key: The trade only works if you are actually trading volume of shots against for quality of shots against
Risk: You never get the puck back, so you never change, so your legs go — fatigue in your own zone is how one-goal leads die
Risk: Your defencemen play the whole shift below the hash marks — no exits, no relief, eventually a bad clear
Risk: Collapsing everyone to the net front concedes screens, rebounds and scrambles — the highest-danger chances there are
Risk: Four minutes of defending without the puck produces stick fouls, and a power play against is what a trailing team wants
```

The trade only works if you are actually trading volume for quality. Amateur and even professional teams routinely cross the line into pure retreat, and then:

- **You never get the puck back.** The other team keeps possession in your end, which means you never change, which means your legs go. Fatigue in your own zone is how one-goal leads die.
- **Your defencemen play the whole shift below the hash marks.** No exits, no relief, and eventually a bad clear.
- **Quality goes up, not down.** A team that collapses everyone to the net front concedes screened shots, rebounds and net-front scrambles — the highest-danger chances there are. "Fewer chances" is a claim you have to earn with structure, not just by standing near your own goal.
- **You invite penalties.** Defending for four straight minutes without the puck produces stick fouls. A power play against is exactly what a trailing team wants.

**Treat the attack-type argument with real suspicion.** You may hear that "analysis of shot quality by attack type has found" cycle attacks produce better shot quality for trailing teams and rush attacks for leading ones. ⚠️ **No study, dataset or author has been traced for it here**, and none of this document's sources carry it — treat it as **an untraced assertion, not evidence**, and do not build a plan on it. The conclusion it usually supports — that ahead, your best offence is the counter-attack off a turnover, which needs you organised and *fast* rather than passive — is coaching craft either way.

### What this means for reading statistics

If you look at a box score or a shot chart without knowing the score situation, you will systematically:

- **Overrate trailing teams.** They out-attempt everyone. Being outshot 40-22 while winning 4-1 tells you very little.
- **Underrate leading teams and the players who protect leads.** Defencemen and checking forwards who play the last ten minutes of one-goal games will look worse than they are.
- **Misjudge goalies.** A goalie facing lots of low-danger third-period shots from a team chasing the game will have a flattering save percentage; one facing fewer, better chances will not.

The fix when watching or analysing: **look at score-adjusted numbers, or at even-strength numbers with the game close.** See [How To Watch Hockey](../off-the-ice/how_to_watch_hockey.md).

## Protecting a Lead

### What actually works

**In short:** With a lead, ask whether they can get it back somewhere dangerous: manage the puck, chip it out and take away the middle.

```facts
Priority: With a lead the question is not "can I create a chance" but "can they get it back somewhere dangerous"
Never: Cross-ice passes through the middle, blind D-to-D across your own goalmouth, or stretch passes you are not sure about
Action: Chip the puck past the forechecker and out rather than forcing a breakout pass under pressure
Rule: Shooting the puck directly out of play from your own defending zone is a two-minute delay-of-game minor, with no pressure exception but three written carve-outs, under NHL Rule 63.2(iii) and IIHF Rule 63.2(III) alike — so it binds a British player too
Rule: The NHL penalises a puck put over the glass "behind" the players' bench (Rule 63.2(iii)); the IIHF's Rule 63.2(III) reaches glass "surrounding" the bench, which appears to include the side glass — so a British player has slightly less room, not more
Rule: USA Hockey writes no such minor for putting the puck out of play — Rule 610(c) penalises only a deliberate shoot-out, and 631(d) makes an accidental one a last-play faceoff
Rule: Hockey Canada penalises no skater for putting the puck out of play either — 10.1(ii) reaches only a deliberate shoot-out, from any zone; its direct, non-deliberate limb at 10.1(v) names the goalie alone
Rule: Never flip it out deliberately, wherever you are on the ice — that is a two-minute minor under NHL and IIHF 63.2(ii), USA Hockey 610(c), Hockey Canada 10.1(ii), PWHL 65.2(ii) and CARHA 75(b)
Action: Take away the middle — concede the outside, keep your stick in the passing lane, defend with your feet
Action: Shorten your shifts to 30–35 seconds when protecting a lead — a coaching prescription rather than a measured figure; fresh legs defend, tired legs reach
Priority: Win the defensive-zone draws, and have a plan for losing the draw as well as winning it
Convention: Which forecheck your team switches to with a lead — 2-1-2, 1-2-2 or 1-1-3 — is a system choice, so find out yours
```

**1. Manage the puck.**

![](diagram:the-risk-map)

![](diagram:breakout-d-to-d)

The biggest lever you have while protecting a lead. With a lead, the value of a possession is not "can I create a chance" but "can I make sure they don't get it back in a dangerous place." That means:

- No cross-ice passes through the middle of your own zone or the neutral zone. A turnover there becomes an **odd-man rush** — attackers outnumbering defenders coming back, a 2-on-1 or 3-on-2. Coaches and analysts treat these as the most dangerous chances in open play, and published tracking backs that; [Defending the Rush](defending_the_rush.md) has the figures and their limits.
- No blind D-to-D passes across your own goalmouth.
- No stretch passes you're not sure about. An interception at your own blue line is worse than a chip out.

**2. Chip out, don't force out.** A **chip** — a short, deliberate flip or wall-play that puts the puck past a forechecker and out of the zone — beats an attempted breakout pass under pressure. You will lose possession, but you'll lose it 150 feet from your net, with your team facing the right way. Getting the puck out and getting a change is worth more than a coin-flip at a controlled exit. *(You may see a "40%" break-even quoted for this. It is arithmetic with nothing published behind it, so use the direction, not a number.)*

**Keep the chip in play.** Lifting it straight out of the rink from your own end is a penalty, so put it along the boards or off the glass.

**What it costs.** Under the NHL, the IIHF and the PWHL — so in Britain too — a puck shot or batted **directly out of play from your own defending zone** is a two-minute minor (NHL 63.2(iii), IIHF 63.2(III), PWHL 65.2(iii)). There is no "I was under pressure" exception. Only three things escape it: a rink with no glass, a puck shot into the players' bench, and a puck out directly off a faceoff. Over the glass behind the bench is still a penalty, and the IIHF's wording — glass *"surrounding"* the bench — leaves a British player slightly less room, not more.

Aim nowhere near the bench either. ⚠️ **The bench is not a target:** the IIHF Situation Handbook (63.24) still calls the minor when a clear goes over the glass and lands in the bench, because *"the determining factor… is where the puck leaves the playing surface"*. Only a puck shot directly into a bench with no glass escapes.

**A *deliberate* flip out of the rink is a minor from anywhere on the ice**, in all six books. **And never aim it out off a draw:** in the NHL, the IIHF and the PWHL the faceoff exemption sits on the defending-zone minor, while the deliberate minor (63.2(ii)) names no faceoff exemption — so never aim it out. USA Hockey, Hockey Canada and CARHA have no draw exemption at all.

**USA Hockey and Hockey Canada write no defending-zone limb.** Both penalise only a deliberate shoot-out (USA Hockey 610(c), Hockey Canada 10.1(ii)). An accidental one in USA Hockey is a last-play faceoff, and Hockey Canada's direct, non-deliberate limb names the goalie alone. Plenty of rec leagues call all this loosely, but never assume. [Risk Management](../hockey-iq/risk_management.md) carries all six books in full.

**3. Take away the middle.**

![](diagram:stick-in-lane-body-net-side)

Concede the outside; deny the slot and the middle lane through the neutral zone. Practically: your stick lives in the passing lane, you defend with your feet rather than lunging, and you never chase a puck carrier to the wall and leave the middle open behind you.

**4. Short shifts.** 30–35 seconds rather than 50 — a coaching prescription rather than a measured figure; the measured range is 30–80 seconds, in [Conditioning and Recovery](../off-the-ice/conditioning_and_recovery.md). Fresh legs defend; tired legs reach. A team that changes cleanly every 35 seconds late in a game is much harder to score on than a team of tired heroes.

**5. Win the defensive-zone draws.**

![](diagram:faceoff-dzone-alignment)

![](diagram:faceoff-dzone-clean-loss)

Coaches treat a defensive-zone faceoff loss with three minutes left and a one-goal lead as the most dangerous single event in the game — coaching emphasis, not a counted one — because it hands the opposition immediate set-up possession in your end. Have your best faceoff man out there, have a clear plan for the loss as well as the win, and know your wall assignments. See [Faceoffs](faceoffs.md).

**6. Keep forechecking — just choose the *right* forecheck.**

![](diagram:forecheck-212)

This is a system choice, not a rule of hockey. The default assumed here is a **2-1-2 forecheck** (two forwards pressuring in the offensive zone, one high, two defencemen at the line). Many teams switch with a lead to a **1-2-2** (one forechecker steers the puck to one side; two forwards hold the neutral-zone line) or a **1-1-3** (one chaser, one mid-ice support, three players across the neutral zone — commonly called a trap). Under a 1-2-2 or 1-1-3, your job as the first forward in is not to win the puck, it's to take away one side of the ice so your teammates can intercept the exit. **Find out which one your team goes to with a lead, and when.** Turning up as an aggressive F1 while the other four are trapping is how leads get thrown away.

> **F1 / F2 / F3 are roles, not people.** They are defined by order of arrival at the puck, not by whether you are a centre or a winger.

### What doesn't work

```facts
Risk: Sitting back entirely is a siege you will eventually lose — you need a way to get out and a way to counter
Never: Ice the puck repeatedly — it is the classic self-inflicted wound
Never: Play keep-away on the perimeter with no plan; the blue-line turnover becomes the rush you must not concede
Never: Play the clock instead of the puck — watching the scoreboard makes you late to loose pucks
Never: Chase the extra body check with a lead; Hockey Canada 8.3 puts the window at an arm and a stick length, but USA Hockey asks whether the check was avoidable — that distance is one of four conditions
Rule: No book caps a late hit at a minor — with nobody hurt, IIHF 56.5 and Hockey Canada 8.3(b) reach a major plus game misconduct (automatic under 8.3(e)), CARHA 49(a) a major that ejects (30(a)); Hockey Canada 8.3(c) is a match for an attempt to injure
Rule: The NHL and PWHL may make a late hit a major (NHL 56.4, PWHL 57.4), which ejects only on injury — but a hit from behind ejects in both (43.5), and an attempt to injure is a match (21.1)
Rule: USA Hockey does not cap a late hit at a minor — a reckless one is a mandatory major plus a game misconduct (640(g)), and plan on a match (602(a)); 640(g) reaches only 640(b–f), unstated for adult men, and where it misses 602(a) still makes a reckless hit or an attempt to injure a match
Rule: USA Hockey's silence on whether 640(g) and 640(h) reach a late hit in adult men's hockey is not a free hit there — 640 Note 2 directs a roughing penalty with no classification exception; that 640(a) then carries the minor or double minor is a reading, since 640(a) names Note 1
```

- **Sitting back entirely.** Five players below the top of the circles for four minutes is not a system, it's a siege you will eventually lose. You need a way to *get out* and a way to *counter*.
- **Icing the puck repeatedly.** See below — this is the classic self-inflicted wound.
- **Playing keep-away in the offensive zone without a plan.** Pointless perimeter possession invites a turnover at the blue line and a rush the other way, which is the one thing you must not concede.
- **Playing the clock instead of the puck.** Watching the scoreboard makes you late to loose pucks. Play the puck; the clock takes care of itself.
- **Chasing the extra body check.** With a lead you can't afford a hit that takes you out of position — **if they have already passed or shot, let them go and get back into position.** That is a late hit, and no book caps it at two minutes. In several books it can get you thrown out with nobody hurt, and USA Hockey makes a reckless one a mandatory major that throws you out of the game — plan on a match there. A hit from behind throws you out of the game in almost every book, and USA Hockey must throw you out when it is forceful along the boards, head first or reckless. ⚠️ **Adult men's hockey under USA Hockey is not a free hit:** a roughing penalty still applies, and a reckless hit is still a match. ⚠️ **A hit meant to hurt is a match penalty** in the NHL, the PWHL, Hockey Canada, USA Hockey and CARHA. The full prices are under [What a late hit costs in each book](#what-a-late-hit-costs-in-each-book).

### The icing trap

**In short:** With a tired line, chip it out rather than ice it; under the NHL, IIHF and PWHL, icing means no change for the draw.

```facts
Rule: Icing is sending the puck from your own side of the centre red line past the opposing goal line untouched (NHL Rule 81.1)
Rule: Under hybrid icing the linesperson judges who would reach the puck first, not who reaches the dot first (NHL Rule 81.1)
Rule: After your own icing you may make no player substitutions before the ensuing faceoff (NHL Rules 81.4 and 82.1)
Rule: The post-icing substitution bar has three narrow exceptions — a goalie who had been pulled for an extra attacker, an injured player, or a penalty affecting on-ice strength (NHL Rules 81.4 and 82.1)
Rule: The IIHF's post-icing substitution bar is Rule 81.4, and its exceptions run one longer — you may also replace "a Player with a broken skate", which the NHL does not allow; but IIHF 82.1 expressly covers icing too and lists only the NHL's three, and the book does not say which governs
Rule: A refused change sends the substitutes back to the bench and warns the team through its coach; a bench already warned takes a bench minor for delay of game (IIHF 82.2(V)) — the NHL contradicts itself, 82.2(v) warning either team, its 82.3 penalising the visitors with no warning
Rule: No time-out is granted to the defensive team following an icing (NHL Rule 87.1) — USA Hockey attaches no icing condition to its one time-out (636(f))
Risk: Under the NHL, IIHF and PWHL, a defensive-zone draw, your five most tired players, and no way to rest them
Rule: Hockey Canada applies its "no-change on icing" rule only "[i]n categories of U18AAA, Junior, and at the option of the Member in Senior" (6.7(d), with 6.1(f)) — below those an icing costs you the faceoff and nothing else
Rule: And where Hockey Canada's bar does bind, 6.7(d)(iii) still permits the time-out that NHL and IIHF 87.1 and PWHL 89.1 refuse — either team may take it; the team that iced simply may not change during it
Action: Ask before the game — many rec and youth leagues use no-touch icing, and under USA Hockey there is no no-change rule to enforce
```

**Icing** is sending the puck from your own side of the centre red line all the way past the opposing goal line untouched. Under NHL **hybrid icing** (adopted 2013-14) the linesperson judges who would reach **the puck** first — not who reaches the dot first. The end-zone faceoff dot is the **deadline for that decision**: a race too close to call when the first skate reaches it is icing.

**So with a tired line, chip it out — don't ice it.** Under the NHL the team that ices it may make no substitutions before the faceoff, and gets no time-out. You have handed the opposition a faceoff in your end, against your five most tired players, with no way to rest them. That is a goal waiting to happen, and the rules were written to make it so.

**What it costs, and where:**

- **The exceptions are narrow.** Under the NHL only three things let you change: putting back a goalie pulled for an extra attacker, replacing an injured player, or a penalty that changes on-ice strength (82.1). Who is on the ice is fixed **the moment the puck leaves the offending player's stick**.
- **The IIHF — the British base book — writes the same bar, with one more exception: *"a Player with a broken skate"* (81.4).** ⚠️ **Do not plan a change around that fourth exception.** The IIHF's own 82.1 also covers icing and lists only the NHL's three, and neither the 2025/26 nor the 2026/27 edition says which rule governs. The NHL has no fourth exception at all.
- **A refused change costs you.** ⚠️ **Under the IIHF the players are sent back and the team is warned through its coach** (82.2(V)). That warning is one per team per game and shared with other change offences, so a bench already warned takes a bench minor straight away. Stalling on the way to the faceoff is a bench minor with no warning. The NHL's book disagrees with itself here: 82.2(v) warns *"either team"* first, while 82.3 gives the **visiting team** a bench minor with no warning.
- **A shorthanded team may usually ice the puck — with two exceptions.** ⚠️ **Under the NHL, a team short through a major whose penalty box is empty when the major expires keeps playing short and may not ice the puck until the next stoppage** (81.6). That happens only when the penalised player was ejected or injured. What buys the exemption is the count, not the major: if a minor makes it 4-on-4, you are equal in strength and icing is called.
- **USA Hockey allows the shorthanded exemption only for Youth 15-Only and above, Girls 16U and above, High School and Adult** (624(b)(1)). Below those a shorthanded team cannot ice the puck at all, so length-of-the-ice clears just feed the power play own-end draws. [Special Teams](special_teams.md) covers this rule.
- **The whole trap is NHL, IIHF and PWHL; Hockey Canada is a third answer.** USA Hockey attaches no substitution bar to an icing, and the PWHL writes the NHL's two rules in the same words. **Hockey Canada applies its no-change rule only in U18AAA, Junior and, at the Member's option, Senior** (6.7(d)), with the NHL's three exceptions; below those an icing costs you the faceoff and nothing else. ⚠️ **Even where it binds, Hockey Canada lets either team take its time-out after an icing (6.7(d)(iii))** — but the team that iced still may not change during it.
- **Rec and youth leagues vary.** Many still use no-touch (automatic) icing, and plenty do not enforce the no-change rule at all. Ask before the game; it changes how hard you should work to get the puck over the red line.

## Playing From Behind

### Pressing without giving up odd-man rushes

**In short:** Add pressure in order of safety, starting with speed and support, and always keep someone as the last man back.

```facts
Action: Move the puck north faster and arrive in numbers through the neutral zone before you park extra players deep
Options: Aggressive forecheck, then pinching defencemen, then activating a defenceman, then pulling the goalie — roughly that order of safety
Read: Trailing, you pinch on anything close; leading, you pinch only when you are certain
Risk: A pinch that misses is a 2-on-1 the other way, and a fourth attacker deep is a genuine odd-man risk
Never: Leave the last-man-back spot empty — if a defenceman goes, a forward replaces them high
Action: Say that swap out loud
```

Trailing, you must generate more. The trap is that the easiest way to generate more — sending everybody — is also the fastest way to concede and end the game.

What to add, in roughly this order of safety:

1. **Speed and support, not bodies.** Get the puck moving north faster and arrive in numbers *through* the neutral zone, rather than parking extra players deep. This costs you nothing defensively.
2. **Aggressive forecheck.** Switch from a 1-2-2 to a 2-1-2, or to a full-ice press with both defencemen pinching, to create turnovers in their end. (Don't call it a "2-2-1" — that's basketball terminology, not a hockey forecheck; see [Forechecking Systems](forechecking_systems.md).) Cost: **the second forward is now deep** instead of in the middle band of the zone, so a clean exit against you is more dangerous — and in the full-ice press, so is a fourth body. **F3 stays high in both shapes**, above the puck, as the insurance; a failed forecheck is far worse when F3 has drifted low.
3. **Pinching defencemen.** A **pinch** is your defenceman stepping down from the *offensive* blue line to keep a puck in (a **step up** is challenging an opponent forward in the neutral zone). It keeps possession alive; a pinch that misses is a 2-on-1 the other way. Trailing, you pinch on anything close; leading, only when you are certain. **Who covers the point behind that defenceman is a coaching choice** — F3, the weak-side defenceman sliding across, or whoever is highest — so find out which your team plays. ⚠️ **And a pinch takes the defenceman to the wall, so in every league, checking or not, never turn your back to the checker there, and never duck.** [Forechecking Systems](forechecking_systems.md#when-to-pinch) has the full technique; the rule for a player driven hard into the wall is in [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md), §5.
4. **Activating a defenceman into the rush or the low zone.** Now you have four attackers and one back. This is a genuine 2-on-1-against risk and should be deliberate, not accidental.
5. **Pulling the goalie.** The biggest risk of all — its own section below.

![](diagram:pinch-centre-below-the-goal-line)

![](diagram:forecheck-pinch)

**The non-negotiable in all of this: someone is always the last man back.** If a defenceman goes, a forward replaces them high. That swap has to be spoken out loud. See [On-Ice Communication](../foundation/on_ice_communication.md).


### When to start taking risks

```facts
Priority: Scale risk to how much time is left against how many goals you need, not to how frustrated you feel
Action: Down one in the first or second period, play your normal game harder — no structural risk at all
Action: Down one early in the third, add forecheck pressure and pinch more, but keep the last man back honest
Action: Down two mid-third, activate defencemen and accept some rush chances against — trading one-for-one is no longer a loss
Action: Down three or more in the third, be extremely aggressive early rather than moderately aggressive late
```

Risk should scale with **how much time is left relative to how many goals you need**, not with how frustrated you feel. A rough ladder:

- **Down one, first or second period:** play your normal game harder. No structural risk at all. There is enormous time left, and a second goal against is what actually kills you.
- **Down one, early third:** add forecheck pressure and pinch more aggressively. Still keep the last man back honest.
- **Down two, mid-third:** activate defencemen; take chances at the offensive blue line; accept some rush chances against. You need two goals, so trading one-for-one is no longer a loss.
- **Down one or two, last few minutes:** goalie out (see below), all-in.
- **Down three or more, any time in the third:** the mathematics say be extremely aggressive early rather than moderately aggressive late — one of the clearest findings in the goalie-pull research this document draws on. A team down three with ten minutes left that plays "sensibly" is choosing to lose slowly.

### How to know it's time

```facts
Read: Lots of zone time and no slot chances means your entries are dying at the blue line — change how you enter, not how hard you skate
Read: Their goalie seeing everything means you need traffic in front of the goalie — not on the goalie — and pucks to the net, not better shots
Read: Losing races out of your own end means you are too slow starting breakouts — simplify and go north earlier
Action: Decide your clock thresholds before the third period, so nobody has to decide in the moment
```

Signs it's time to change something, rather than keep doing the same thing harder:

- **You've had the puck and produced nothing.** Lots of zone time, no slot chances, means your entries are dying at the blue line. Change *how* you enter (see [Zone Entries](zone_entries.md)), not how hard you skate.
- **Their goalie is seeing everything.** No screens, no rebounds, no second chances. You need traffic in front of the goalie — not on the goalie — and pucks to the net, not better shots.
- **You keep losing races out of your own end.** You're too slow starting breakouts; simplify and go north earlier.
- **The clock crosses a threshold.** Have thresholds decided *before* the third period — "if we're still down one at six minutes we go to the 2-1-2 and pinch everything" — so nobody has to decide in the moment.

## Pulling the Goalie

### When

**In short:** Expect the goalie to come out earlier than feels natural; every study cited here finds coaches pull too late.

The analytics consensus sits much earlier than tradition, and it has been moving that way for years. **Every study this document cites finds the same thing: coaches pull too late** — and Beaudoin and Swartz report that every earlier analysis they review reaches the same conclusion, back to Morrison's 1976 paper, which they call *"the first paper on the subject of pulling the goaltender"*.

**What teams historically did.** In 2007-08 NHL coaches typically pulled with **1:00 remaining when down one goal and 1:30 when down two** — and essentially never when shorthanded. The analyst Meghan Hall puts the average at **1:13 in 2013-14 rising to 1:37 by March 2021**, with a high of **1:46 in 2019-20** — real movement, but slow.

**What the numbers support.** The evidence comes at it from several directions:

- **The 6-on-5 is genuinely powerful.** With the extra attacker, the pulling team scored a goal every **8.5 minutes**, against sample means of **28.6 (road) and 26.2 (home) minutes** for a single team at 5-on-5 with both goalies in — using 2007-08 data. Put differently, 6-on-5 scores at rates comparable to a power play. (These are **per-team** rates; the 4-on-4 and 5-on-5 figures under Special Situations are **combined** rates — time until *either* team scores — so don't compare the two sets directly.)
- **The empty net is genuinely expensive.** In Beaudoin and Swartz's 2007-08 sample the defending team scored into the empty net every **3.1 minutes** when the road team had pulled its goalie, and every **2.6 minutes** when the home team had — which is, on this document's own arithmetic from their table, roughly **19 to 23 goals per 60 minutes**. This is a real cost, not a rounding error.
- **The trade is still worth it, and worth it earlier.** Simulation over four million games found that simply pulling whenever trailing with under three minutes left was worth about **one extra standings point over an 82-game season**, and a more aggressive policy about **1.5 points** (average points per season 83.7 → 84.7 → 85.2). One study of 2003–2019 pulls found the **highest success rate at a pull around the 3:00 mark, roughly 26% (±4%)** — although the same author cautions that data before about 3:45 is sparse and those estimates are uncertain.
- **A pulled goalie is the biggest lever there is.** Ian Ferer's survival analysis of 2,185 one-goal games (2014-15 to 2018-19) found the leading team was **3.275 times more likely to lose their lead** when the trailing team pulled its goalie.
- **You draw penalties.** In 652.4 minutes of pulled-goalie play in 2007-08, **44 penalties were called on the pulling team versus 84 on their opponents** — close to two against them for every one against you. Penalties came against the defending team every **7.4–7.9 minutes** at 6-on-5, versus every **12.2–13.3 minutes** at 5-on-5.

**And the honest limits.** Success rates are still low, and note what is being measured: Meghan Hall counts a pull as a success when the trailing team **either won in regulation or, far more often, tied the game to force overtime**. On that definition, pulling when down one succeeded roughly **15%** of the time in the most recent seasons of her data; down two, about **1%**, or 25 times in seven seasons; down three, not once. Whatever happens in the overtime that follows is a separate coin-flip on top. And it can go very badly — in their inaugural season the Seattle Kraken allowed **26 empty-net goals while scoring five**.

**Practical takeaway for a player:** expect to go earlier than feels natural, expect to be out there down two with four or five minutes left, and know that the goalie is coming out on the next stoppage in your favour — not on the next whistle of any kind.

> **A coaching constraint that gets ignored.** As Seattle's Dave Hakstol put it: *"The earlier you pull, the stronger your second group has to be."* Pulling with five minutes left means three or four different units play 6-on-5. If your second group can't handle it, pulling early isn't free.

### The 6-on-5 structure

**In short:** Get set up, screen the goalie from outside the crease without touching them, move the puck fast, keep one point man back and change with possession.

```facts
Position: Two at the points, two on the half-walls, one at the net front, one below the goal line or in the middle
Action: Get set up — a 6-on-5 that has to enter the zone repeatedly is wasting its advantage
Action: Put the puck to the net with a screen; the extra man is a body in front, not a sixth passer, and it is on that player to stay off the goalie
Technique: One-touch it — the advantage only shows up if the puck moves faster than they can shift
Never: Let both point men pinch — someone must be able to stop a clear, because there is no net behind you
Action: Change with possession and change fast; changing while chasing the puck concedes an empty-netter
Never: Foul in your own end once your net is empty — every trade that was worth taking with a goalie behind you stops being worth taking without one
Rule: With your net empty, fouling an attacker when nobody is between them and your goal is an awarded goal, not a power play or a penalty shot (NHL 25.1 and 57.4)
Rule: With your net empty, covering a puck in your own crease is an awarded goal — NHL and IIHF 67.5, PWHL 69.5, Hockey Canada 10.2(a)(vi), CARHA 58(c) Note 1 on its face though 36(b) conditions it, and USA Hockey 614(b) where it prevented an obvious and imminent goal
Convention: Umbrella, overload or your normal power-play look at 6-on-5 is a team choice — learn yours in practice
```

The extra attacker is almost always a forward — Meghan Hall's NHL numbers have the use of *five* forwards with the net empty nearly doubling across seven seasons — and the sixth skater usually comes on for the goalie at a stoppage with an offensive-zone faceoff, or on the fly once you have possession in their end.

![](diagram:faceoff-goalie-pulled)

The most common shape takes a standard power-play look and adds a second body high: **two players at the points (the area just inside the blue line, not the line itself), two on the half-walls (level with the faceoff dots), one at the net front, and one below the goal line or in the middle of the zone**.

![](diagram:six-on-five-shape)

The principles matter more than the diagram:

- **Get set up.** A 6-on-5 that has to enter the zone repeatedly is wasting its advantage. Win possession, gain the line, and get organised.
- **Puck to the net with a screen.** The extra man is most valuable as a body in front and a second-chance collector, not a sixth passer. Stand in the goalie's eyes, not on them and not in their way. Avoiding contact is your job in every book. Under the NHL, the IIHF and the PWHL a screen from outside the crease keeps the goal, but standing where the goalie can't move freely in their crease, or any contact, can cost it, and incidental contact is excused only when it starts outside the crease and you made *"a reasonable effort to avoid such contact"*. USA Hockey files even accidental contact with a goalie under interference, Hockey Canada says *"The onus is always on the attacking player"*, and CARHA penalises deliberate contact with a goalie *"whether in or out of the goal crease"*.
- **One-touch it.** The defenders can only cover so much; the advantage only shows up if the puck moves faster than they can shift.
- **The point men do not both pinch.** Someone must be able to stop a clear. There is no net behind you.
- **Change with possession, and change fast.** A 6-on-5 shift is exhausting. If you change while chasing the puck you will concede an empty-netter.
- **And remember your own net is empty.** A foul you would normally take back there stops being worth it. With your goalie out, the NHL lists seven infractions that give an **awarded goal** rather than a power play or a penalty shot (Reference Table 10), among them delaying the game, handling the puck and fouling from behind. ⚠️ **Covering a loose puck in your own crease is the sharpest of them.** It is an awarded goal under the NHL, the IIHF, the PWHL and Hockey Canada on the bare fact that your goalie is out; under CARHA on its face, though another of its rules adds conditions; and under USA Hockey wherever it *"prevented an obvious and imminent goal"*. **Knock a loose puck in your crease straight down where you stand:** every book prices holding or picking it up, not knocking it down. See [When a penalty is worth taking](#when-a-penalty-is-worth-taking) and [Catching a loose puck in your own crease](#catching-a-loose-puck-in-your-own-crease).

![](diagram:six-on-five-one-point-stays)

> **The shape you use at 6-on-5 is a system choice.** Some teams run an **umbrella** (three players high) there, some **overload** four players onto one side, some just add a net-front body to their normal power-play look. Learn yours in practice, because there is no time to work it out in the game. See [Special Teams](special_teams.md).

### The 6-on-4

If you are trailing *and* on a power play, pulling the goalie creates a 6-on-4. This is a much bigger edge than most coaches use. In 2007-08, teams that pulled the goalie on a power play to make it 6-on-4 **scored every 5.5 minutes and allowed an empty-net goal every 4.8 minutes** — nearly as likely to score as to concede, but you *need* the goal and they don't. The clearest single recommendation from the goalie-pull research is that **teams trailing should pull much earlier on a power play than at 5-on-5.**

**And don't assume the opposite when *you* take the penalty.** It feels right for a trailing team that has just been penalised to leave the goalie in until the kill is over. ⚠️ **Beaudoin and Swartz's simulations find the reverse, at least late.** In their scenario C — down one, 1:54 left, just penalised, 4-on-5 — the best strategy tested is *"pull goalie until the score is tied"*, worth **0.0761** expected points against **0.0409** for current NHL practice (which keeps the goalie in while shorthanded) and **0.0351** for never pulling. Their best season-long strategy, which pulls *"when shorthanded, even-strength and on power-plays with increasing time remaining and various goal deficits"*, is worth **85.2 points over an 82-game season against 83.7** for current NHL practice (their Table 8).

**The caveat is time, not strength.** With more clock left — down two with six minutes, down three with 12:22 — the same paper does not call for pulling while shorthanded. So late and down one, being shorthanded is not by itself a reason to keep the goalie in; earlier in a game it still is. This is a model of NHL scoring rates, not a rule — but if you are keeping the goalie in with under two minutes left because you are killing a penalty, the arithmetic does not support you.

### The delayed-penalty freebie

**In short:** If your team has agreed it, pull your goalie the moment the arm goes up against them, and keep the puck from them.

```facts
Action: Goalie goes to the bench immediately at the first sign of the referee's arm going up against the other team — every second is a free attacker
Action: The extra attacker jumps from the bench as the goalie arrives
Never: Blind D-to-D passes across your own crease during a delayed call
Rule: On a delayed penalty the referee raises an arm and play continues until the team to be penalised gains control of the puck (NHL Rule 15.1)
Rule: During a delayed penalty the offending team cannot score unless you shoot the puck into your own net (NHL Rule 78.5(xi))
Action: Keep the puck away from the offending team; do not force a low-percentage shot that could be smothered
Risk: A shot that misses and rims to an opponent ends the sequence
```

This is the one time you get an extra attacker for free, and amateur teams waste it constantly.

Under **NHL Rule 15.1**, when a player on the team *not* in control of the puck commits a penalty, the referee **raises an arm and delays the call**; play continues until **the team to be penalized gains control of the puck**. So the moment the arm goes up, your goalie can skate to the bench and you play 6-on-5 with **no empty-net risk** — because the offending team cannot score.

The one exception is a genuine own goal: under **Rule 78.5(xi)**, during a delayed penalty the offending team cannot score **unless the non-offending team shoots the puck into their own net.** A deflection off an opponent doesn't count; your own defenceman ringing it off your own goalie does. So: no blind D-to-D passes across your own crease during a delayed call.


> **"Delayed penalty" names two different things, and only one of them means
> pull the goalie.** What is described here is the signalled call under **Rule 15.1**
> — the referee's arm is up and play continues. The phrase is also used for the
> **delayed *clock*** under **Rule 26**: a third penalty assessed to a team already
> serving two, whose time waits rather than putting them three men short. ⚠️ **Nobody
> is about to be awarded anything and there is no free attacker.** Go on the
> referee's raised arm, not on the words. [Special Teams](special_teams.md) covers the
> distinction.

![](diagram:delayed-penalty-your-own-net)

**What to do when the arm goes up:**
- **Goalie:** go, immediately, at the first sign. Every second is a free attacker.
- **Bench:** the extra attacker jumps as the goalie arrives.
- **On-ice players:** the whistle comes when *they* gain control — so keep the puck away from them, don't force a low-percentage shot that could be smothered, and above all don't turn it over. A shot that misses and rims to an opponent ends the sequence.

> **Rec-league note:** not every recreational league allows, or practises, pulling the goalie on a delayed call, and some officials will stop play early. **How much rec, beer-league and youth hockey that adds up to is a question about leagues rather than about books, and this document has no count for it.** It also requires your goalie to be paying attention. Talk about it beforehand.

### Defending against 6-on-5

**In short:** Don't chase: hold a tight shape in the slot, treat every clear as a shot, and take no penalties.

![](diagram:five-on-six-clear-three-outcomes)

```facts
Never: Chase. Hold a tight shape in the slot, block lanes and let them have the perimeter instead
Convention: "Box" and "diamond" are borrowed penalty-kill names — there they are four players, a box two high and two low, a diamond a single apex; defending a 6-on-5 you have five skaters and this document does not settle where the fifth stands, so ask your coach
Position: Sticks in lanes, feet in the house; at the net front hold your ground without leaning or pushing, and lift sticks low on the shaft, well below their bottom hand, as the puck arrives, then let go
Action: Treat every clear as a shot — get your head up and put it at the empty net when the lane is clean
Rule: You can be iced against a pulled goalie; six players against six is equal strength, not below it (NHL Rules 81.1, 81.3, 84.1)
Rule: After icing it against a pulled goalie the attacking team picks the dot, you cannot change but for the narrow exceptions and you cannot take a time-out at all — NHL 81.2, 81.4, 87.1, the IIHF the same, and the PWHL at 83.2, 84.1, 89.1; USA Hockey imposes none of the three
Priority: Own-zone draws at 5-on-6 decide games — agree the plan before you line up
Never: Take a penalty; a 6-on-4 against is close to a goal
```

Your job as the leading team defending an empty net:

- **Do not chase.** Six attackers means someone is always open if you leave your area. Hold a tight shape in the slot, block lanes, and let them have the perimeter. **⚠️ "Box" and "diamond" are borrowed penalty-kill words, and the count does not come with them.** On the kill ([Special Teams](special_teams.md)) both are four-player shapes; defending a 6-on-5 you have **five** skaters, and this document does not settle where the fifth stands. [Defensive Zone Coverage](defensive_zone_coverage.md) names one answer — the **collapsing box**, all five of you inside the house with sticks in lanes — but it is one option, not the shape, so ask your coach before their goalie comes out. **Don't reach for Box+1 to fill the gap:** its fifth player chases the puck, which is the first thing this bullet tells you not to do.
- **Sticks in lanes, feet in the house.** At the net front, hold the inside beside the screener rather than trying to move them — they do not have the puck, so walking them off the spot is interference, and a shove with the shaft is a cross-check. Stay square with no lean and no push, and lift their stick low on the shaft, well below their bottom hand, as the puck arrives, then let go. [On-Ice Communication](../foundation/on_ice_communication.md) sets out what each book lets you do there.
- **Why the net front matters.** Coaches say most 6-on-5 goals come from tips, screens and rebounds. **The strong form does not hold up.** In the NHL's public play-by-play for the first 400 games of the 2024-25 regular season, **29.7% of goals with the extra attacker on were a tip, a deflection, or a shot within three seconds of a previous shot on goal**, against **23.4%** at 5-on-5. That points where the coaches point but is a long way from "most"; the sample is only **74 goals**, and the feed does not record screens at all. Separately sourced: the ice in front of the net converts several times better than the perimeter ([Shooting](../technique/shooting.md)).
- **Every clear is a shot.** Do not just chip it out. Get your head up and put it toward the empty net if the lane is clean. **A puck that goes in is a goal and there is no icing; a puck that misses and crosses the goal line is icing** (see the next point), so this is a shot to take when you can actually hit the net, not a blind heave. A blind clear that goes straight to their point man is the worst outcome of the three.
- **You *can* be iced against a pulled goalie, so get it out with a purpose or put it on net.** It is tempting to read the NHL's icing exemption for a side *"below the numerical strength of the opposing team"* as covering five skaters against six. **The rulebook does not support that:** it counts the goalie inside a team's strength, so six against six is equal, and it expressly keeps an icing called while the other goalie heads to the bench (NHL 81.1, 81.3, 84.1). ⚠️ **So the whistle comes.** You get a defensive-zone draw, **the attacking team picks the dot**, you **cannot change** but for the narrow exceptions above, and you **cannot take a time-out** at all (81.2, 81.4, 87.1). The shorthanded exemption is a different situation — see [Special Teams](special_teams.md).
- **Faceoffs are everything.** Draws in your own end at 5-on-6 decide games. Have the plan agreed before you line up.
- **Take no penalties.** A 6-on-4 against is close to a goal.

## Line Matching and Last Change

### The rule

**In short:** Where your league runs last change, the visitors change first and the home team answers; go straight to the dot.

```facts
Rule: After a stoppage the visiting team places its line-up on the ice first, then the home team may substitute (NHL Rule 82.1)
Rule: The referee allows the visitors up to five seconds and the home team up to eight, then no more changes (NHL Rule 82.2)
Rule: Only the team that iced the puck is frozen — if the visitors ice it, the home team changes normally and keeps last change (NHL Rules 82.1, 81.4)
Rule: The IIHF writes last change into Rule 82.3 instead, with a penalty the NHL's 82.1 does not carry — failure, tardiness or "deliberate error in complying" is a warning first, then a bench minor
Rule: The NHL, IIHF and PWHL also require players to "proceed directly" to the faceoff, with a bench minor and no warning first for stalling — announced as "Slow Proceeding to Face-off Location" (NHL and IIHF 82.3, PWHL 84.3)
Key: Last change is the main mechanical component of home-ice advantage, but travel, rest and rink familiarity are in there too
Action: In a rec league, treat matching as something you do on the fly — ask whether yours runs a formal change procedure at all; this document has no count of how many do
```

Under **NHL Rule 82.1**, following a stoppage the **visiting team places its line-up on the ice first**, and only then may the home team make any substitution it wants. **Rule 82.2** sets the clock: the referee gives the visiting team **up to five seconds**, raises their hand to close visitor changes, gives the home team **up to eight seconds**, then lowers their hand. After that, no more changes until play resumes.

**Icing is where this is often misread.** Rule 82.1 reads: *"The home team may then make any desired substitution, except in cases following an icing, which does not result in the delay of the game."* Read alone, that looks as if any icing switches last change off. It does not: later in the same rule the bar falls only on **the team in violation** of Rule 81 (Icing) or Rule 63.8 (Delaying the Game) — the team that iced the puck — and Rule 81.4 repeats it. So if the home team ices it, the home team is frozen; if the **visitors** ice it, the home team changes normally and keeps last change. Full treatment in [Faceoffs](faceoffs.md).

**🇬🇧 The IIHF puts last change in a different rule, and attaches a penalty to it.** IIHF **Rule 82.3** gives the home team *"last change"*, requires the visitors to put their players out first, and refuses a change not made promptly. Failure to comply brings a warning first and then a bench minor for delay of game. **Warning first, then the penalty.** ⚠️ **The NHL's 82.3 opens with something else, so don't read one book's 82.3 as the other's:** its first paragraph gives the **visiting team** a bench minor for any change after the referee's signal, with no warning — where its own 82.2(v) warns *"either team"* first.

**The second paragraph of 82.3 is the one most likely to catch an ordinary player, and it is the same in the NHL, the IIHF and the PWHL (84.3):** *"Players must proceed directly to the location of the face-off"*, and stalling is a bench minor for delaying the game, with no warning. **It reaches dawdling, not disagreeing** — so go straight to the dot, or straight to the bench.

This is what "**last change**" means, and it is the main mechanical part of home-ice advantage. Across 2021-22 to 2023-24, NHL home teams earned a **.585 points percentage against .524 for road teams** — a gap of **6.1 percentage points**, or about **11.6%** more points at home. (The source article calls it a 5.5% boost, which does not follow from the two numbers it prints, so the arithmetic is given instead.) The effect is real but modest, and only part of it is last change — travel, rest and rink familiarity are in there too.

> **Rec-league note:** plenty of recreational leagues run no formal line-change procedure at all — the referee just drops the puck — and this document has no count of how many. If that is your league, treat everything below as something you do *on the fly*, not at stoppages.

### What coaches do with it

```facts
Convention: The classic use is matching a checking line — picked for defensive reliability, forechecking and faceoffs — against a scoring line
Options: Shelter a young player with soft starts, get the trusted group out for late defensive-zone draws, or chase a favourable matchup
Risk: Last change can cost you the draw — coaches sometimes send out a worse faceoff man to win the matchup instead
Key: The road team's counter is the change on the fly, which is why change discipline matters more on the road than at home
```

The classic use is **matching a checking line against a scoring line**. A **checking line** (or shutdown line) is a forward line selected primarily for defensive reliability, forechecking and faceoffs rather than scoring. The coach with last change waits for the opponent's top line, then sends the checkers over the boards against them.

Coaches also use last change to:

- **Shelter players.** Give a young forward or a weak defence pair starts in the offensive zone against the opponent's fourth line.
- **Protect a lead.** Get the defensively trusted group out for every defensive-zone draw in the last five minutes.
- **Chase a matchup.** Get your best offensive line out against their weakest pair.
- **Manipulate the faceoff.** Last change can even cost you a draw: home teams won only **50.4% of 8,383 centre-ice faceoffs in 2023-24** (the Sound Of Hockey analysis cited below, not one of this guide's own faceoff computations, which [Faceoffs](faceoffs.md) covers) — partly because coaches sometimes send out a worse faceoff man to win the matchup instead.

**The road team's counter is the change on the fly.** Last change applies only at stoppages. On the road, coaches dictate matchups by timing changes during play — getting a line off as the opponent's top line comes on — which is why change discipline matters more on the road than at home.

### What you should do when you know you're being matched

```facts
Action: On a scoring line, change on the fly rather than at whistles — at a stoppage on the road you show your hand first
Never: Take the bait and beat your man one-on-one into traffic. Move the puck early instead and make them cover all five of you
Read: If three players are focused on you, two of your teammates are unmarked
Goal: A 0-0 shift with no penalties against a strong checking line is a win if your other lines are winning theirs
Priority: On the checking line, your first job is to not get scored on. Get the puck deep and change
Action: On the checking line, be first on faceoffs and first back; you were sent out for reliability, not creativity
```

If you're on a scoring line being checked:

- **Change on the fly, not at whistles.** At a stoppage on the road you have to show your hand first. In open play you don't. Get out during play, when the other team can't react cleanly.
- **Don't take the bait.** A good checking line wants you frustrated and beating your man one-on-one into traffic. Move the puck early, take the safe play, and make them cover all five of you.
- **Use their commitment.** If three players are focused on you, two of your teammates are unmarked. Score effects apply in miniature — an over-committed checker leaves space behind.
- **Win the shift, don't win the fight.** Grinding a strong checking line to a 0-0 shift with no penalties and no chances against is a *win* for you if your other lines are winning theirs.

If you're on the line doing the checking:

- **Your first job is to not get scored on.** A 0-0 shift is a success.
- **Get the puck deep and change.** Long shifts against a top line are how the goal happens.
- **Be first on faceoffs and first back.** You were sent out for reliability, not creativity.

## Defence Pair Logic

Almost everything in this section is a coaching choice; the point is to know why your coach might be doing it.

### Shutdown pair versus offensive pair

```facts
Convention: Most teams differentiate their three pairs by role rather than purely by quality
Priority: Shutdown pair — gap control, defending the rush, protecting the net front, clean simple exits; not expected to produce points
Priority: Offensive or transition pair — join the rush, activate from the point, move pucks, higher risk tolerance
Convention: The third pair is usually sheltered, with offensive-zone starts and weaker opposition
Never: Assume "first pair" means the hardest job. Many teams use their second pair to shut down, so ask
```

Most teams differentiate their three pairs by role rather than purely by quality:

- **The shutdown pair** takes the hard matchups and the defensive-zone starts. Priorities: gap control, defending the rush, protecting the net front, clean simple exits. Not expected to produce points.
- **The offensive (or transition) pair** gets favourable starts and matchups. Priorities: joining the rush, activating from the point, moving pucks. Higher risk tolerance.
- **The third pair** is usually sheltered — offensive-zone starts, weaker opposition.

Which pair does which varies. Many teams use their **second** pair as the shutdown pair, keeping the first pair free to play offence. Ask; don't assume that "first pair" means "hardest job."

### Left-shot / right-shot pairings

**In short:** Know which side you are playing: on-hand helps exits and retrievals, and off-hand, take your first touch into the middle.

```facts
Key: On your on-hand side the outlet up the wall and the D-to-D are on your forehand, and your body shields the puck from the middle
Technique: Retrieving on your on-hand side lets you turn away from the forecheck; off-hand you turn into the boards or the checker
Read: At the left point a left shot's blade sits toward the boards — the gain is the puck arriving on your forehand, released in one motion
Convention: Power-play units deliberately put off-hand defencemen at the points, because the blade is out in the middle for the one-timer
Key: Defending the rush, on-hand suits keeping a carrier on the wall and off-hand suits taking away the seam — neither is simply better
Technique: On your off side, take your first touch into the middle, open your hips earlier on retrievals, and treat the backhand D-to-D as normal
```

A **left shot** holds the stick with the **left hand at the top of the shaft and the right hand lower**, so the blade sits on their **left** side and their **forehand is on their left**. That is what makes the left side of the ice their natural side. **On-hand** means playing on your natural side — a left shot on the left, a right shot on the right; **off-hand** means the opposite.

Coaches overwhelmingly try to build pairs of one left shot and one right shot. Why it matters:

- **Zone exits and breakouts.** On your on-hand side, the outlet pass up the wall and the D-to-D pass are on your forehand, and your body shields the puck from the middle. On your off-hand, that same pass is on your backhand or requires a turn into pressure.
- **Retrievals.** Skating back on your on-hand side lets you take the puck off the wall and turn away from the forecheck. Off-hand, you turn into the boards or into the checker.
- **Blue-line play in the offensive zone.** The geometry is the opposite of what people assume: for a **left shot at the left point, the stick side *is* the boards side.** The on-hand advantage is that a puck coming up the wall **arrives on your forehand**, so you can take it and release it in one motion and **keep it low** for tips and rebounds. The off-hand point man takes it on the backhand and needs a turn, but their blade is out in the middle — which is exactly why power-play units put **off-hand** defencemen at the points for the one-timer.
- **Angling and defending the rush.** The same geometry runs the other way here. Facing the attack on your on-hand side, your stick is on the **boards** side and your body is toward the middle; on your off-hand side, your stick sits naturally in the **middle** passing lane. Neither is simply better — on-hand suits keeping a carrier on the wall, off-hand suits taking away the seam — but know which one you have, because it decides whether you defend with your stick or with your feet.

There is real evidence behind the preference. One study of NHL shot-location data found **64.2% of shots occurred with opposite-handed (i.e. both on-hand) pairings** and **32.1% with two left shots**, and that opposite-handed pairs out-performed same-handed pairs — **50.71% versus 49.32% Corsi For** — with the gap narrowing as blocked and missed shots are excluded, which suggests the problem is generating clean offensive plays rather than defending.

Two honest caveats. The effect is small — around one percentage point. And roughly 60% of NHL defencemen shoot left (a split reported by NHL.com, **not** by the puck++ studies above), so someone has to play their off side; some analysts argue teams now play weaker right shots ahead of better left shots just to preserve handedness. If you are asked to play your off side: **take your first touch into the middle rather than up the wall, open your hips earlier on retrievals, and accept the backhand D-to-D as a normal play.**

### Zone starts

```facts
Key: Zone starts describe where your shifts begin, but published definitions divide by different things — one by every shift you took, another by your offensive and defensive draws alone
Action: Read the glossary of whichever site you are on before you read anything into a zone-start figure, and never compare a figure from one site against one from another
Risk: Heavy defensive-zone starts push your raw shot and goal differentials down however well you play — a real effect, but a modest one
Action: Check both players' zone starts, and that both came from the same site, before you compare their numbers
Mindset: A defensive-zone start means your coach trusts you — and the first ten seconds are the most dangerous of your shift
```

**Zone starts** describe where your shifts begin. Before you read anything into one, find out **what the percentage is divided by**, because published definitions do not agree and they are not close.

- **Evolving Hockey** counts a change on the fly as a zone start, so its offensive-zone percentage divides by every shift you took — and since most shifts start on the fly, nobody's is large. It publishes a draws-only version separately, under a different name.
- **The formula in general reference works** divides offensive-zone starts by offensive *plus defensive* starts only, leaving out neutral-zone draws and every change on the fly — so the same player scores far higher.

So "60% zone starts" means nothing until you know the definition. **Read the glossary of the site you are on, and never compare figures across sites.** [How To Watch Hockey](../off-the-ice/how_to_watch_hockey.md) carries the measured share of shifts that begin on the fly, with its limits.

Whichever definition you are reading, zone starts are a coaching lever and a statistical distortion in one:

- A player with heavy **defensive-zone starts** begins most of the shifts that start on a draw 200 feet from the other net, under pressure. That pushes their raw shot and goal differentials down however well they play — a real effect, but a modest one, because it reaches only the shifts that began on a draw at all.
- A player with heavy **offensive-zone starts** looks better than they are, by the same modest amount running the other way.

When you compare two players' numbers, check their zone starts first — and take both figures from the same site, so that you know they were counted the same way. And when you get a defensive-zone start, understand what it means: your coach trusts you, and the first ten seconds are the most dangerous of your shift.

## Shift Length and Change Discipline

### How long a shift should be

```facts
Convention: 35–45 seconds for most players, 30–35 when protecting a lead or stuck in your own end — coaching prescription, not measurement
Read: The signal to change is a reduction in your ability to accelerate, not a stopwatch
Key: Gliding to a spot you would have skated to ten seconds earlier means you are already too tired to be useful
Action: Take the measured shift-length range and the physiology from Conditioning and Recovery, which covers them
```

Shifts in fast, high-level hockey are typically **under a minute**, because hockey is repeated maximal effort and effort quality drops well before you feel exhausted. **[Conditioning and Recovery](../off-the-ice/conditioning_and_recovery.md) owns the physiology and the measured range** (30–80 seconds). A reasonable target is **35–45 seconds**, shortened to **30–35** when protecting a lead or stuck in your own end — **both are coaching prescription, not measurement**, as is the "well-managed" 30–45 s figure that document gives.

The signal to change is not a stopwatch, it's a **reduction in your ability to accelerate**. The moment you find yourself gliding to a spot you would have skated to ten seconds earlier, you are already too tired to be useful.

### Tired players make bad decisions

```facts
Risk: Decision speed goes first — you see fewer options, later
Risk: Then your feet go: you stop skating and start reaching, which is how sticks end up around waists and hands
Risk: Then support goes, so a teammate loses a battle they should have won; backchecking goes last and costs most
Key: A 90-second shift is worth less in total than a 30-second one, and it costs your next shift too
Convention: Coaches put late-shift penalties down to fatigue — the mechanism is sound reasoning, but no published study in amateur hockey codes penalty motive against the preceding play
```

Fatigue does not just cost you a step. It costs you, in this order:

1. **Decision speed** — you see fewer options, later.
2. **Feet** — you stop skating and start reaching, which is how sticks end up around waists and hands. Coaches routinely blame late-shift penalties on fatigue; the mechanism is sound, but **no published study in amateur hockey codes penalty motive**, so the frequency is not measured — see *Frustration penalties* below.
3. **Support** — you stop arriving as the second man to the puck, so your teammate loses a battle they should have won.
4. **Backchecking** — the last thing to go, and the most expensive.

As coaches put it: **a 90-second shift is not three times as valuable as a 30-second shift — it is worth less in total, and it costs your next shift too.** *(Nobody has measured a shift's value against its length, so read that as the shape of the argument, not a quantity. What is measured is the decline in repeated-sprint quality — see [Conditioning and Recovery](../off-the-ice/conditioning_and_recovery.md).)*

### When NOT to change

**In short:** Change when your team has the puck heading north, never with the puck loose in your own zone.

```facts
Never: Change when the puck is in your defensive zone and your team does not have it
Never: Change while you are the last man back, until you are relieved. Say so out loud
Never: Change while the opposition is on a rush; finish the defensive play first
Rule: You cannot change after your own icing (NHL 82.1, IIHF 81.4, PWHL 84.1) — an injured player can still be replaced — so do not start drifting toward the bench — USA Hockey Rule 624 imposes no such restriction
Rule: You may change after your own icing to put back a pulled goalie, to replace an injured player, or when a penalty affects on-ice strength (NHL 82.1) — the IIHF adds a broken skate at 81.4, but not at its own 82.1, which covers icing too; do not plan a change around it
Rule: A change attempted after your own icing and refused sends those players back with a warning; a bench already warned takes a bench minor for delay of game (IIHF 82.2(V)); NHL 82.2(v) warns first too, but its 82.3 gives a visitor the bench minor with no warning, and the book does not say which governs
Rule: Knock your own net off by accident and you lose the change as after an icing, bar three exceptions, an injury among them — the draw in your end, at the dot the attackers pick (NHL and IIHF 63.8, PWHL 65.8); Hockey Canada 6.1(g) bars the change in U18 AAA, Junior and optionally Senior
Never: Change in the middle of a 6-on-5 possession. Change with the puck or not at all
Action: Change when your team has the puck heading north, has just dumped it in deep, or after a completed clear
```

This is the part that separates good game management from a good motor.

**Do not change when:**

- **The puck is in your defensive zone and not under your team's control.** A player leaves, a replacement arrives late, and for two seconds you are 4-on-5 in the most dangerous area of the rink. Coaches name this as one of the biggest sources of goals against in amateur hockey — an observation, not a measured ranking: public tracking does break goals down by how they were produced, but it is NHL data, and it classifies them by the offence's play rather than the defensive breakdown that let them in.
- **You are the last man back**, until you are relieved. Say so out loud.
- **The opposition is on a rush.** Finish the defensive play, then change.
- **You've just iced the puck.** You can't (NHL Rule 82.1; IIHF Rule 81.4; PWHL Rule 84.1), so don't drift toward the bench. The only ways off are the exceptions under [The icing trap](#the-icing-trap): a pulled goalie going back in, an injured player, or a penalty that changes on-ice strength — plus the IIHF's broken skate, which its own 82.1 leaves out, so don't plan a change around it. Try it and be refused and the players are sent back with a warning; a bench already warned takes a bench minor for delay of game (IIHF 82.2(V)).
- **You've just knocked your own net off.** Under **NHL Rule 63.8** (**PWHL 65.8** in the same terms), a net *"displaced accidentally by a defending skater or goalkeeper"* — **IIHF 63.8**'s *"a defending Player"* — means no substitutions before the faceoff, which is in your own end at whichever dot **the attacking team** chooses. Knock it off, by accident or not, while they have an imminent scoring chance and the goal is awarded (NHL 25.2); on purpose otherwise, it is a delay-of-game minor (63.2(iv)). USA Hockey 610(d) makes a deliberate one a minor, a penalty shot on a breakaway, where it takes away an immediate scoring chance, or in the last two minutes and overtime, and a goal with your net empty where it prevented an obvious and imminent goal. The exceptions are the same three as after icing, and **Rule 87.1 takes away the time-out as well** (PWHL 89.1 the same). **Hockey Canada writes this only in U18 AAA, Junior and, at the Member's option, Senior (Rule 6.1(g))**, and no USA Hockey counterpart to the lost change was found in its playing rules or its *Casebook*, searched by rule number and by concept. [Rules Primer](../foundation/rules_primer.md) covers the rule in full.
- **You're in the middle of a 6-on-5 possession.** Change with the puck or not at all.
- **You'd be changing into an offensive-zone faceoff you're needed for**, if you're the faceoff man.

**Do change when:**

- **Your team has the puck heading north, or has just dumped it in deep.** These are the safe windows.
- **After a completed clear from your own end** — the classic "get it out, get off."
- **You're beaten and a fresh teammate can take over safely.**

### Why a bad change costs goals

```facts
Risk: A bad change concedes a numbers disadvantage, a structural hole and a player joining the play blind, all at once
Key: Few single actions in hockey concede as much for free as a bad change — coaching judgement, not a measured ranking
Key: The fix is discipline and communication, not fitness
Action: Call it — "I'm changing", "stay on", "last man"
```

A bad change concedes three things at once: a numbers disadvantage, a structural hole (the player leaving usually has a specific job nobody is doing), and a player joining the play with no idea where anybody is. Few single actions in hockey concede as much for free — coaching judgement, not a measured ranking.

The fix is discipline and communication, not fitness. **Call it**: "I'm changing," "stay on," "last man." See [On-Ice Communication](../foundation/on_ice_communication.md).

### The rules you're working inside

**In short:** Leave at your bench and out of the play, touch nothing, and as the replacement join the play only once the player leaving is off.

```facts
Rule: You may change at any time during play, provided the player leaving is within five feet of the bench and out of the play (NHL Rule 74.1)
Rule: The five feet is the NHL's, the PWHL's (76.1) and, as 1.50 m, the IIHF's (NHL and IIHF 74.1). Hockey Canada 6.1(a) gives you 3.05 m (10 ft.), and USA Hockey 204(b) writes no distance at all — only "at the players' bench and out of the play"
Rule: If either player plays the puck, checks or makes contact while both are on the ice, it is too many men — a bench minor (NHL Rule 74.2)
Rule: The IIHF and USA Hockey set that trigger wider — contact or a played puck while "either" the arriving or the retiring player is on the ice, not both (IIHF 74.1; USA Hockey 204(b))
Rule: USA Hockey alone adds a third trigger — a bench minor where the change "causes their team to gain a competitive advantage" with no puck played and nobody hit (Rule 204(b); Casebook Rule 204 Situation 8 applies it to a 2-on-1 turned into a 2-on-2)
Rule: If the offending team scores before the whistle, the goal is disallowed and the penalty assessed (NHL Rule 74.2)
Never: Leave the ice anywhere but at the bench — a bench minor under NHL and IIHF 74.1 and PWHL 76.1, and if replaced under USA Hockey (Casebook 630, Sit. 12), Hockey Canada (6.1(a)) and CARHA (19(b))
```

Leave only through the bench door.

**What it costs.** Under the NHL you may change at any time if the player leaving is within five feet of the bench and out of the play (74.1). If either player plays the puck or makes contact while **both** are on the ice, it is a bench minor for too many men, and a goal the offending team scores before the whistle is disallowed (74.2). The PWHL writes the same five feet, and the IIHF writes it as 1.50 m.

🇬🇧 **The IIHF's trigger is wider, and that decides cases.** It calls too many men when the puck is played or contact made while **either** the player coming on or the player going off is on the ice. **USA Hockey sides with the IIHF.** ⚠️ **So under both of those books the player coming off can give the penalty away alone**, before the substitute steps on. *(The IIHF counts a player with one skate on the ice and one in the bench as off.)*

**Hockey Canada.** ⚠️ **Do not carry that carve-out into a Hockey Canada game, which answers the same facts the other way.** It calls a bench minor for a player with *"either one or both skates still on the bench"* who plays the puck — the player leaving included, *"whether or not the team has the correct number of players on the ice."* It then applies the NHL's narrow test to the goalie change, and never says which governs a skater change. Its distance is 3.05 m (10 ft.); USA Hockey sets none, only that the player leaving *"shall always be at the players' bench and out of the play before any change is made."*

USA Hockey adds a trigger no other book has. ⚠️ **A change that *"causes their team to gain a competitive advantage"* is a bench minor**, even with no puck played or contact made. Its Casebook applies it to a change that turns a 2-on-1 against into a 2-on-2. **So under the American book, the change that rescues you numerically is the one most likely to be called.** [Rules Primer](../foundation/rules_primer.md) owns the rule.

Leaving through any exit but the bench is a bench minor regardless under the NHL, the IIHF and the PWHL. If you are replaced, it is one under USA Hockey's Casebook (Rule 630, Situation 12), and under Hockey Canada and CARHA too, which want the player leaving at the bench before any change (Hockey Canada 6.1(a), priced as too many players at 10.7(a); CARHA 19(b), 19(e)).

## Overtime

### Regular-season 3-on-3 (NHL)

**In short:** Keep the puck: regroup back through the neutral zone rather than force a shot, and change only with possession.

```facts
Rule: Five minutes maximum, sudden death, three skaters and one goalie a side, teams changing ends (NHL Rule 84.1)
Priority: Possession over shooting — if you do not have a high-quality look, you do not shoot
Technique: With no good option, carry or pass back out through the neutral zone, reset and come again
Never: Force a low-percentage shot — a missed net in 3-on-3 is often a goal against
Never: Change without possession; you never surrender the puck to get fresh legs
Read: With the NHL's long change, the goal usually comes from isolating a defender who has been stuck out and cannot get off
Rule: A penalty makes it 4-on-3, coincidental minors leave it 3-on-3, and no team ever has fewer than three skaters (NHL Rule 84.3)
Rule: Lose with your goalie pulled in regular-season overtime and you forfeit the point you had already earned, unless you pulled them at a delayed penalty call against the other team (NHL Rule 84.2, PWHL 86.2)
```

**Three-on-three overtime is a possession game.** Early on it was chaos and end-to-end rushes; that has changed, and it is the most important thing to know. The core ideas, in coaches' and players' own words:

- **Possession over shooting.** "Overtime is all about puck possession and maintaining it at all costs." If you don't have a high-quality look, you don't shoot — because giving up the puck means facing a 3-on-2 the other way immediately.
- **The controlled regroup is the key skill.** With no good option in the offensive zone, you carry or pass the puck **back out through the neutral zone**, reset, and come again. Retreating 30–40 seconds without forcing anything is normal and correct. The whole ice is your working space; camping in the offensive zone doesn't work.
- **Never force a low-percentage shot.** A missed net in 3-on-3 is often a goal against. As one goalie put it, once you give up the puck you may not get it back. Turnovers, careless passes and shots that miss the net "invite quality scoring chances against you almost right away."
- **Only change with possession.** You never surrender the puck to get fresh legs.
- **The goal usually comes from a tired opponent.** The winning play is often not a great shot — it's isolating a defender who has been stuck out for 90 seconds and can't get off. That is a long-change play; where teams do not change ends, as under the IIHF book below, a tired player can reach the near bench and it is much less reliable.
- **Use your goalie as an outlet.** With the extra space, a pass back to the goalie is a legitimate reset. See [Goaltender](../positions/goaltender.md).


![](diagram:ot-3-on-3-regroup)

**The format.** NHL Rule 84.1: five minutes maximum, sudden death, **three skaters and one goalie** per side, teams change ends (they defend the same end as in the second period — the long change). Both points are secured before it starts: a tie after regulation guarantees each team one point.

The format arrived in 2015-16 to reduce shootouts, and the share of **overtime games** that went to a shootout fell from **over 55% to under 40%** in its first season. (Only around a quarter of NHL games go to overtime at all.)

**Penalties in 3-on-3 (Rule 84.3)** are unusual and worth knowing:
- A penalty makes it **4-on-3**, not 3-on-2 — the non-offending team *adds* a skater.
- **Coincidental minors at the same stoppage** (with nothing else on the clock) leave it at **3-on-3**.
- A two-man advantage is **5 against 3**.
- **No team ever has fewer than three skaters.**

**Pulling the goalie in overtime (Rule 84.2)** is possible but almost never sensible: if you lose while the goalie is out, **you forfeit the point you'd already earned** — unless the goalie was pulled for a delayed penalty. And once pulled in regular-season overtime, the goalie **must wait for a stoppage to return and cannot change on the fly** (Rules 84.2 and 74.5), on pain of a bench minor. **Both of those riders are NHL and PWHL (86.2) only** — IIHF 84.2 is a single permissive sentence, so if you play in Britain neither applies to you; see below.

One more detail: **in the last two minutes of regulation and at any time in overtime**, the five-second whistle forcing players to set up for a faceoff does not apply (Rule 82.2(viii)) — officials give teams reasonable time.

### Playoff overtime (NHL)

```facts
Rule: Fifteen-minute intermission, then full twenty-minute sudden-death periods at 5-on-5, changing ends, until someone scores (NHL Rule 84.5)
Action: Play simple and get pucks in behind their defence
Never: Be the one who makes the play that loses it
Action: Manage shifts ruthlessly — teams routinely shorten to the low 30s in double overtime
```

**Rule 84.5**: after a tie through regulation, a **fifteen-minute intermission**, then full **twenty-minute periods at 5-on-5**, sudden death, changing ends each period, repeating until someone scores. No 3-on-3, no shootout.

This is a completely different game-management problem: it's regulation hockey, played tired, with an infinite-cost mistake. The universal advice is **play simple, get pucks behind their defence, don't be the one to make the play that loses it**, and manage shifts ruthlessly — teams routinely shorten shifts to the low 30s in double overtime.

### Overtime elsewhere: IIHF, Britain and rec leagues

**In short:** Ask before the game: many British fixtures have no overtime, and where it is played the formats differ.

```facts
Rule: IIHF preliminary overtime is five minutes of 3-on-3, but teams do not change ends (IIHF Rule 84.1)
Rule: IIHF playoff overtime is ten minutes of 3-on-3, not the NHL's twenty-minute 5-on-5 periods (IIHF Rule 84.5)
Rule: The IIHF shootout is five shooters a side with a coin toss for order, not three with home-team choice (IIHF Rule 84.4)
Rule: IIHF 84.2 is one sentence permitting the pull; the NHL's forfeited point and its bar on returning on the fly have no IIHF counterpart
Rule: Under the In-House Rules overtime is played only in NIHL fixtures, and in SNL fixtures whose competition regulations provide for it (Rule 84)
Rule: NIHL league and group-stage playoff overtime is five minutes of 3-on-3 with no change of ends, then game-winning shots with three shooters a side (In-House Rules 2025-26 and 2026-27, Appendix 1)
Rule: In NIHL 1 and 2 the same player may now shoot again once the first three have gone — the 2025-26 rule that everyone had to shoot first is gone (In-House Rules 2026-27, Appendix 1)
Rule: NIHL Playoff Final Weekend is different — semi-finals go to 20 minutes of 5-on-5 then shots, and the Final to successive 20-minute 5-on-5 sudden-death periods with no shots at all (In-House Rules 2025-26 and 2026-27, Appendix 1)
Rule: The Elite League writes its own Rule 84 — a regular-season or Challenge Cup group game plays five minutes of 3-on-3 without changing ends, then a five-shooter shootout, home team choosing order; knockout formats vary, so check the Casebook (EIHL Casebook 84.1, 84.4)
Read: Under IIHF rules your bench is the near bench, so waiting for an exhausted defender is much less reliable
Action: Ask before the game — formats run from 4-on-4 to 3-on-3 to a straight shootout to no overtime at all
```

**IIHF overtime differs from the NHL in ways that change how you play it.** It is your base rule in Britain, amended by the In-House Rules and the Elite League below (*IIHF Official Rule Book 2025/26*, Rule 84):

- **Preliminary/round-robin:** five minutes, sudden death, **3-on-3**, after a three-minute intermission — but **teams do not change ends.** Your bench is the near bench, so a tired player can get off, and waiting for the exhausted defender is much less reliable.
- **Playoff, relegation, classification and bronze-medal games:** **ten minutes** of 3-on-3, no change of ends, then the shootout — not the NHL's 20-minute 5-on-5 periods.
- **Gold-medal game:** repeating sudden-death 3-on-3 periods with a **15-minute intermission** between each; ends change from the second. No shootout.
- **Pulling the goalie:** one sentence permitting it. **Neither NHL rider is in the IIHF book** — no lost point if you lose with the net empty, no bar on the goalie returning on the fly.
- **Shootout:** **five** shooters a side and a **coin toss** for order. More under [Shootouts](#shootouts).
- **Overtime penalties** work as in the NHL: a penalty makes it 4-on-3, coincidental minors with nothing else on the clock leave it 3-on-3, a two-man advantage is 5 against 3, and no team ever has fewer than three skaters.

**Britain — in many fixtures there is no overtime at all.** The IHUK In-House Rules cut IIHF Rule 84 to one line, the same in the 2025-26 and 2026-27 editions: overtime *"is only played in NIHL fixtures and in SNL fixtures where specifically provided for in competition regulations"*. ⚠️ **Recreational and junior games end level, so managing a tied third period ends with the horn, not with 3-on-3.** For 2026-27 that reaches England, Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland (the 2025-26 edition reached England and Scotland only), under Ice Hockey UK.

Where overtime *is* played, Appendix 1 of the In-House Rules sets the format:

- **NIHL National League, its group-stage playoffs, and NIHL 1 and 2:** **five minutes of 3-on-3**, no change of ends, then **game-winning shots (GWS)** with **three** shooters a side. Goalies may go to the bench in the rest period; penalised players stay in the box. No group-stage playoff game may end tied.
- **NIHL Playoff Final Weekend:** semi-finals go to **20 minutes of 5-on-5**, then shots. The **Final** plays successive **20-minute 5-on-5 sudden-death periods** with **no** shots, and teams change ends each period — the closest thing here to NHL playoff overtime.
- **SNL:** five minutes of 3-on-3 sudden death, then a **best-of-three penalty shot shootout**, then sudden death.
- **BUIHA:** fixtures marked *"VARSITY OT"* follow the NIHL procedure.
- **Elite League:** none of the above — the *EIHL Casebook* writes its own Rule 84. A regular-season or Challenge Cup group game plays **five minutes of 3-on-3**, no change of ends, then a **five-shooter** shootout. Knockout formats vary, so check the Casebook for your game.

The full list of British amendments lives in [UK and England Rules](../foundation/uk_rules.md).

Formats vary enormously below the professional level everywhere else too: some leagues use 4-on-4, some 3-on-3, some go straight to a shootout, many simply record a tie, and youth leagues often have no overtime at all. **Ask before the game.**

## Shootouts

### For the shooter

```facts
Action: Decide before you start skating — deciding at the hash marks is how you end up doing neither thing well
Technique: Have two moves and know which one you are using
Options: A shot in stride to the far side or five-hole, a deke to the backhand, or forehand-backhand-forehand
Read: Slower approaches give you more options and force the goalie to move first; faster ones beat passive, deep goalies
Goal: Get the goalie sliding, then go where they have just left
Never: Telegraph. Head up instead, puck out in front where both a shot and a deke are live
Key: A missed net is the worst outcome; a shot on target at least gives the goalie the chance to make an error
```

- **Decide before you start skating.** Deciding at the hash marks is how you end up doing neither thing well.
- **Have two moves and know which one you're using.** The commonest are a shot in stride (usually to the far side or five-hole), a deke to the backhand, and a forehand-backhand-forehand.
- **Speed is a choice, not a default.** Slower approaches give you more options and force the goalie to move first; faster approaches beat goalies who are passive and deep. Pick one deliberately.
- **Make the goalie move first if you can.** Get them sliding, then go where they have just left. [Shooting](../technique/shooting.md) has the shooter's side of this in full.
- **Don't telegraph.** Head up, puck out in front where both a shot and a deke are live.
- **You only need one good shot.** A missed net is the worst outcome; a shot on target at least gives the goalie the *chance* to make an error.

### For the goalie

```facts
Technique: Control your depth — coming out cuts the angle, staying deeper gives you more time against dekes
Never: Commit first. Stay square instead, stay patient, make them decide
Technique: Track the puck, not the hands
Read: Match your retreat to their approach — back in with a fast shooter, hold your depth and stay big against a slow one
Rule: You cannot be replaced once the shootout starts unless you are injured, and a substitute gets no warm-up (NHL Rule 84.4)
Rule: Under the IIHF book the goalies "may be changed after each shot" (IIHF Rule 84.4(VI)), so where a British fixture does go to shots a substitution is legal — but no warm-up is permitted for a substitute goalie in any game (IIHF Rule 8.2)
```

- **Control your depth.** Coming out cuts the angle and beats shooters; staying deeper gives you more time against dekes. Know which you're better at.
- **Don't commit first.** A goalie who moves before the shooter has decided has handed them a side to go to. Stay square, stay patient, make them decide.
- **Track the puck, not the hands.**
- **Match your retreat to their approach.** A shooter coming in fast means you back in with them; a slow shooter means you hold your depth and stay big.
- **Expect to stay in.** Under the NHL you cannot be replaced once the shootout starts unless you're injured (84.4). The IIHF book reverses that — *"[t]he goalkeepers from each team may be changed after each shot"* (84.4(VI)) — and the In-House Rules write nothing on it, so in Britain a coach genuinely can switch goalies mid-shootout. Either way a substitute gets no warm-up (NHL 84.4, IIHF 8.2), so nobody is getting shots first.

See [Goaltender](../positions/goaltender.md) for the technical detail.

### The format

**In short:** Find out your competition's shootout rules, because the number of shooters and who may shoot again differ between them.

NHL **Rule 84.4**: if the five-minute overtime settles nothing, each team gets **three shots**, alternating, and the **home team chooses whether to shoot first or second**. If still tied it goes to **sudden death**, and **no player may shoot twice until everyone eligible has shot**. The rules for each attempt are those of a penalty shot (Rule 24). The final score is recorded as one goal better than the opponent regardless of how many shootout goals were scored, and the winning shooter is not credited with a goal in personal statistics.

**The shootout you will actually take in Britain.** Your base book is the IIHF's, and **its Rule 84.4 differs from the NHL in four ways**:

- **Five different shooters a side, not three.**
- **A coin toss between the captains, not the home team,** decides who shoots first.
- **Goalies may be changed after each shot**; in the NHL a goalie cannot be replaced once the shootout begins unless injured.
- **Repeat shooters in the tie-break:** after the first five, *"[t]he same Player can be used for each shot"*, where the NHL makes everyone eligible shoot before anyone shoots twice.

**The In-House Rules replace the whole procedure in the leagues that use them** (Appendix 1). It is **game-winning shots (GWS)**: **three** shooters a side taking alternate shots, then one shot each until it is settled. **Eligibility changed for 2026-27**, so check which season you are reading:

- **NIHL National League and its group-stage playoffs — the same in both editions:** any eligible player may shoot, and the same player may go repeatedly. Eligible means not serving a penalty and not injured. Your best shooter can go every round.
- **NIHL 1 and 2 — reversed for 2026-27.** In **2025-26** every eligible player had to shoot before anyone took a second attempt. **The 2026-27 edition deletes that** and lets any eligible player go again *"(AFTER the initial 3 shots have been taken)"*. **The everyone-shoots-first rule is the NHL's, and the 2026-27 In-House Rules no longer write it for any competition they cover.**
- **SNL — the same in both editions:** a *"best-of-three penalty shot shootout"*, then sudden death, with the first three shooters repeatable *"after every full round of three."*
- **Elite League — none of the In-House procedure:** the *EIHL Casebook*'s Rule 84.4 keeps the IIHF's **five** shooters, but *"the Home Team shall have the choice to shoot first or second"* — no coin toss.

As in the NHL, the deciding goal is recorded as the game-winning goal and the winners' score goes up by one. Full amendment list: [UK and England Rules](../foundation/uk_rules.md).

## Momentum and How to Respond to It

Whether "momentum" is a real measurable force is genuinely disputed. What is not disputed is that **teams behave differently after a goal**, and that behaviour is controllable.

### After conceding

```facts
Mindset: Take the next shift as simply as possible — win a puck, get it deep, take your man, change
Mindset: Play the game in front of you — replaying the goal is a decision-making tax you cannot afford
Action: Reset your risk to baseline; chasing the goal back immediately is how one-goal deficits become three-goal deficits
Action: Say one short factual sentence — "reset, next shift". Silence after a goal against is corrosive
Convention: Coaches treat the minute after a goal against as the most dangerous stretch in hockey — coaching lore, not a measured hazard rate
```

Coaches everywhere treat the minute after a goal against as the most dangerous stretch in hockey, because the conceding team is either deflated or over-eager. **That is coaching lore, not a measured hazard rate** — there is no published count of goals by time since the last goal. The habits below are worth having either way.

**What to do:**

- **Take the next shift as simply as possible.** Win a puck, get it deep, take your man, change. Nothing clever. **And don't go looking for a hit to settle yourself** — *"finishing your check"* on a player who has already passed or shot is a late hit once a very short window has closed; the section below sets out the window and the cost.
- **Play the game in front of you.** Replaying the goal in your head is a decision-making tax you can't afford. See [Mental Game](../off-the-ice/mental_game.md).
- **Reset your risk to baseline.** The instinct is to chase the goal back immediately. That's how one-goal deficits become three-goal deficits.
- **Talk.** One short factual sentence — "reset, next shift" — from anyone. Silence after a goal against is corrosive.

### After you score

```facts
Action: Play the shift after your own goal like any other — win the draw back, get the puck deep, take your man
Risk: Coaches warn that a team switches off, or starts cheating for a second goal, straight after scoring — coaching lore, not a measured rate
Options: Some coaches send the scoring line straight back out to press; others send a checking line to settle the game. Ask which your bench does
```

A goal for changes behaviour as surely as a goal against, only in the other direction: the bench is loud, the scoring line is still celebrating, and somebody starts cheating up the ice for the next one. **Take the centre-ice draw that follows like a defensive-zone draw** — win it back, get the puck deep, take your man — and let the second goal come from the same structure that produced the first. Coaches commonly treat the shift after you score as a dangerous one; read that as coaching lore, the same class of claim as the minute after a goal against. **Whether the scoring line goes straight back out to press or a checking line goes out to settle the game is a coaching choice** — ask your bench which yours plays, and if you are on that shift, play it simply.

### What a late hit costs in each book

**In short:** Pull up on anyone who has passed, shot or turned away, keep every hit off the head, and hit only where checking is allowed.

```facts
Never: "Finish your check" on a player who has already passed or shot; USA Hockey's Declaration of Player Safety calls the concept "an unacceptable action"
Rule: The NHL does not cap a late hit at a minor — 56.2(iii) is a minor for a deliberate check on a player without the puck, 56.4 a major "based on the degree of violence", 21.1 a match for an attempt to injure; 56.5 ejects only on injury, 43.5 from behind, the harsher of 43 and 48 on the head
Rule: Under the IIHF book, the British one, a late hit is not capped at a minor — Rule 56.5 reaches a major plus a game misconduct with nobody hurt, on the Referee's judgement that the hit recklessly endangers AND that the player "would not be sufficiently sanctioned by imposing a major penalty"
Rule: A late hit on a player who has released the puck is not free in USA Hockey adult men's play — 640(b) exempts them, but 640 Note 2 directs a roughing penalty with no classification exception; that 640(a) carries it is a reading, since 640(a) names Note 1
Rule: USA Hockey does not cap a late hit at a minor — a reckless one is a mandatory major plus a game misconduct (640(g)), and plan on a match (602(a)); 640(g) reaches only 640(b–f), unstated for adult men, and where it misses 602(a) still makes a reckless hit or an attempt to injure a match
Rule: Hockey Canada makes a late hit interference; 8.3(b) reaches a major plus a game misconduct on "the degree of violence of the impact" with nobody hurt, mandatory on injury, and 8.3(c) is a match for an attempt to injure; two or more strides in is charging, a match if defenceless (7.4(c))
Rule: Hockey Canada: a late hit from behind that misses the head, or drives a player head first into the boards, is checking from behind — minor and game misconduct at least (7.5(a), (e)), major and game misconduct if hurt (7.5(b)), match into the boards or goal frame if defenceless (7.5(c))
Rule: Hockey Canada: a head hit from behind is head contact; head first into the boards, the harsher of that and checking from behind. Minor and female: match if defenceless or you meant harm; major and game misconduct a must if hurt or a cross-check above the shoulders, possible on violence (7.6(b), (c))
Rule: Hockey Canada Junior/Senior: a hit from behind to the head is a match if defenceless or you meant harm (7.7(c)); major and game misconduct at high violence, a head-high cross-check, or if the head hits glass, ice or boards and is hurt (7.7(b)); minor and misconduct if moderate (7.7(a))
Rule: USA Hockey has no bare minor for a check from behind (608(a)); a reckless one, one sending a player head first into the boards or goal frame, or a forceful one on a player at the boards is at least a major plus game misconduct (608(b), Casebook 608 Situation 1); reckless, plan on a match (602(a))
Rule: USA Hockey makes a check from behind a mandatory match where it "recklessly endangers" an opponent "in a vulnerable or defenseless position" with excessive force (608(c))
Rule: CARHA grants no window at all — it writes no late-hit rule, prices the body check itself at 49(a), puts a game misconduct in both limbs of 53(a) for a hit from behind "anywhere on the ice", and makes accidental head contact a minor of its own at 50(a)
Rule: The check is not late the instant the puck leaves, if you may check at all — Hockey Canada 8.3's whole test is an arm and a stick length — but under the IIHF a body check on a skater no longer in control of the puck and unaware or unprepared is "automatically considered reckless", window or not
Rule: USA Hockey’s test is avoidability, not distance — 640(b) bars an "avoidable" check on a player who has released a shot, and the Casebook’s arm-and-stick distance is one of four conditions, so an avoidable check inside it is still at least a minor
```

**What to do is the same in every book.** Let a player who has passed or shot go. If you could still pull out of the hit, pull out. If they have turned away, leave them, and keep any hit off their head. And first be sure you may body check in this game at all — often you may not ([Whether you may body check at all](#whether-you-may-body-check-at-all)). The rest of this section is what it costs when you don't.

**"Finishing your check"** — hitting a player who has already passed or shot — is a late hit. USA Hockey's *Declaration of Player Safety, Fair Play & Respect*, which covers body checking *"at all levels of play"*, calls the concept *"an unacceptable action"*.

**But the check is not late the instant the puck leaves.** The NHL and the PWHL allow a check on a player who has just lost the puck *"provided the check is rendered immediately following his loss of possession"* (NHL wording; the PWHL's says "her"). The IIHF allows it while the checker is *"in the immediate vicinity"* and the opponent is still within an *"objectively reasonable window of opportunity"*. **Hockey Canada alone puts a measurement in its rule text:** under **Rule 8.3**, contact must come *"within a maximum distance of an arm and stick length between the puck carrier and the player delivering the check, at the instant the puck has been released."*

**USA Hockey's Playing Rules write no window.** Under 640(b) a player who has released a shot or pass is *"considered to be vulnerable or defenseless"* and *"not eligible to be body checked"*. But its Casebook gives a *"general rule of thumb"* (**Standard of Play Situation 10**): the check may be finished if the defender first tried to win the puck, was *"an arm’s length plus stick length away from the opponent when the puck was dumped"*, and the check was already *"unavoidable"*. And *"[i]t is not acceptable to skate one or two more strides after the puck is gone and then decide to deliver the check."*

None of that window ⚠️ **reaches a player who is not ready for the hit.** Under the IIHF, *"A bodycheck to an opponent who is vulnerable is automatically considered reckless even if that bodycheck would be considered legal to a non-vulnerable opponent"*. Vulnerable means a skater without the puck who is *"either not aware of an impending hit or they are not prepared for the hit"*, and forceful contact on them *"risks turning a body check into a late hit"*.

USA Hockey's Situation 10 itself requires the checker *"to minimize the force used in this instance and not take advantage of a vulnerable or defenseless opponent"*.

And ⚠️ **the window is only yours if you may body check in this game at all** — under USA Hockey Rule 604 decides that, and at 12 & under and in the other classifications listed below there is no legal check to finish. **The Casebook's next situation settles it** (*Standard of Play* Situation 23): *"The concept of 'finishing the check' is one that is meant to intimidate or punish the opponent and has no place in youth hockey. Provided this check is avoidable, it must be penalized in every instance."* ⚠️ **Avoidability is the whole test: if you could have pulled out once the puck had gone, there was no window.** **Practically, the window is about the length of your arm and your stick, and going looking for it is how players get ejected.**

**CARHA Hockey has no window at all — do not go looking for one.** ⚠️ **The body check itself is the penalty (Rule 49(a))**, with no possession, timing or vulnerability condition. It is a minor or major *"at the discretion of the Referee, based upon the intent and the violence of the impact with the opposing player"*, for a player who *"intentionally bodies, pushes, shoves"* an opponent or *"does not avert body contact with an opponent"*. And *"[w]hen injury results from intentional body contact a Major penalty shall be assessed."* A major ejects you (Rule 30(a)). The only carve-out, **49(c)**, is *"accidental contact"*. ⚠️ **A hit meant to hurt is a match penalty, whatever rule it falls under** (**48(a)**). **66(a)(1)** (interference) does reach only an opponent *"who is not in possession of the puck"* — but CARHA leaves out the NHL's sentence allowing a check *"rendered immediately following his loss of possession"*. **CARHA omits the authorisation, not the limit** — "it isn't interference, so it's legal" gets the answer backwards.

**Other CARHA rules reach the hit finished after the puck has gone.** **52(a)**'s Note makes more than two strides a charge. **53(a)** is a minor or major **plus a game misconduct** for a hit from behind *"anywhere on the ice"*, the major mandatory *"[i]f a player is injured"* and *"[a] Match penalty could also be assessed"* — and a player who has just released a shot is very often turned away. **53(b)** makes it a match penalty where that player is driven from behind *"into the boards or goal net"* and is *"unable to protect or defend themselves"*. **49(b)** makes a hit into the boards a double minor at the floor, and its Note counts that as two of the three penalties that end your game under **32(a)**. And **27(b)** removes "play had stopped" as a shelter — penalties apply *"regardless of whether or not play is in progress"* — and **49(d)** is a minor or major for *"deliberate physical contact with an opponent after the whistle"*, a major ejecting you (30(a)).

And ⚠️ **head contact is its own rule, which is where 49(c) stops helping.** A check on a player who has just released the puck rides up far more often, because they are turned, unset and following their shot. **50(a)** makes head contact a minor even where it is *"accidental"*; **50(b)** makes intentional head contact a double minor, or a major plus a game misconduct, on the degree of violence; **50(c)** is a match penalty for deliberately injuring an opponent, or attempting to. (A stick in the face is Rule 62, by Rule 50's own Note.) ⚠️ **49(c)'s carve-out has no scoping words — *"no penalty shall be assessed"* — and CARHA does not reconcile it with 50(a)**, so treat 50(a) as governing once the contact catches the head. That is a reading rather than the book's words, and it is the one that does not cost you a game on a guess.

Checking from behind ends your game in CARHA with nobody hurt, whichever limb is called: ⚠️ **53(b)'s match penalty is mandatory and needs neither intent nor injury, and 53(a)'s game misconduct is in both of its limbs** — the Referee chooses only the tier, and an injury makes it the major. A match penalty keeps you out of every further game until the Chairman has dealt with your case (**34(a)**). 49(a) and 52(a) are the genuinely discretionary ones, each making the major mandatory on injury. **Either way a major ejects you (30(a)), with a suspension of at least one game (32(d)).** **Read CARHA as a book with no window — and it binds CARHA-affiliated adult hockey only, never rec hockey in general.**

**Under the IIHF book — the British one — a late hit is reckless endangerment by 56.1's own words, and 56.5 reaches a major plus a game misconduct with nobody hurt.** ⚠️ **That rule asks the Referee two questions, not one:** whether the player *"recklessly endangers their opponent by an 'interference or a late-hit'"*, **and** whether the player *"would not be sufficiently sanctioned by imposing a major penalty"* — judged on severity, violence and *"the general reprehensibility involved."*

**The NHL does not cap a late hit at a minor either.** There it is interference: the check is allowed only *"immediately following his loss of possession"* (56.1), a deliberate check on an opponent *"who is not in possession of the puck"* is a minor (**56.2(iii)**), and **56.4** allows a major *"based on the degree of violence"*. Under that rule the game misconduct comes only with an injury (**56.5**). ⚠️ **But a late hit on a player turned away from you is checking from behind, and that ejects you with nobody hurt** — a major on an opponent *"unable to protect or defend himself"* (**43.3**), and *"A game misconduct penalty must be assessed anytime a major penalty is applied for checking from behind"* (**43.5**). ⚠️ **And a hit meant to hurt is a match penalty, injury or not (21.1).** The PWHL writes all three the same, at 21.1, 43.3 and 43.5. What the IIHF adds is a game misconduct for a reckless hit with nobody hurt, from any angle and with no attempt to injure. ⚠️ **If a hit from behind reaches their head, the NHL and PWHL take the harsher of Rule 43 and Rule 48 (head contact); under the IIHF, plan on the major and game misconduct.**

**If they have turned away from you, leave them — USA Hockey prices that hit as checking from behind, and so does Hockey Canada while it stays off their head.** Hockey Canada puts a game misconduct on every checking-from-behind minor or major (**7.5(a)**, **7.5(e)**), a major plus a game misconduct mandatory if they are hurt (**7.5(b)**), and ⚠️ **a match penalty where they go into the boards or goal frame** *"unable to protect or defend themselves"* (**7.5(c)**). USA Hockey writes no bare minor (**608(a)**): a reckless one, or one that puts them head first into the boards or goal frame, is a major plus a game misconduct (**608(b)**). Its Casebook makes that or a match mandatory for every forceful check on a player standing along the boards with their back to the middle (Rule 608, Situation 1). For a reckless one plan on a match and a likely multi-game suspension (**602(a)**) — and it is a match wherever a player *"recklessly endangers an opponent from behind with excessive force while the opponent is in a vulnerable or defenseless position"* (**608(c)**).

**Keep the hit off their head, and never drive a turned player head first into the boards — if you can see their numbers, pull up and play the puck.** ⚠️ **Under Hockey Canada a hit from behind that reaches the head is priced as head contact**, because Rule 7.6 *"supersedes any other rules, with the exception of Rule 7.10 – Fighting, and any foul that results in contact to the head must be penalized as Head Contact."*

**In minor and female hockey (Rule 7.6):** ⚠️ **a match penalty if they could not protect or defend themselves, or you meant to injure them** (**7.6(c)**). ⚠️ **A major plus a game misconduct if they are hurt, or for any cross-check that strikes them above the shoulders, whether or not they are hurt** — and at the referee's discretion on violence alone (**7.6(b)**, with its Interpretation 2). Otherwise a minor if accidental and a double minor if intentional, with a third in one game an ejection (**7.6(a)**).

**In Junior and Senior (Rule 7.7):** ⚠️ **a match penalty if they could not protect or defend themselves, or you meant to injure them** (**7.7(c)**). ⚠️ **A major plus a game misconduct for high violence, a cross-check above the shoulders, or an illegal hit that puts their head into the glass, ice or boards and causes a head injury** (**7.7(b)**). **A minor plus a ten-minute misconduct for moderate violence** (**7.7(a)**).

Head contact with minimal violence may be called there as roughing, charging or another penalty instead. ⚠️ **But not when you raise or drive your shoulder, elbow, forearm, hands or stick into their head in any manner, or hit them late, after they have passed or lost the puck long enough that you could have pulled up.** Those are head contact whatever the violence: a minor plus a misconduct at the least, and a major plus a game misconduct or a match with high violence (Interpretation 1 to **7.7(a)**). The same Interpretation treats a blind-side hit, a cheap hit on a player down on the ice and a hit with both feet off the ice the same way.

If you drive them head first into the boards or goal frame, ⚠️ **the checking-from-behind price still applies — a minor and a game misconduct at the least, a major and a game misconduct if they are hurt, a match if they could not protect themselves (7.5(a), (b), (c), (e)) — and you take whichever is harsher.** **So if you can see their numbers, do not finish the hit.**

**One carve-out for adult men's hockey in the United States:** USA Hockey's named late-hit minor, **640(b)**, is written *"(except Adult Male Classifications)"*. That does not make the hit legal there. **640 Note 2** still directs a roughing penalty for a player who *"delivers an avoidable body check to an opponent after the whistle or who is no longer in control of the puck"*, with no classification exception. 640(a) carries the roughing minor or double minor — a reading rather than the book's words, since 640(a) writes its tier *"as outlined in Note 1 above"*. So in adult men's hockey the rule number changes and the advice does not.

**And under USA Hockey the minor is not the ceiling.** ⚠️ **640(g): *"A major plus a game misconduct penalty shall be assessed to any player who recklessly endangers an opponent as a result of any actions falling under Rule 640(b, c, d, e or f)."*** **640(h)** adds that a match penalty *"may also be assessed"* under those sub-sections. ⚠️ **Note which sub-sections those are:** 640(b) is the one excepted for adult men, while (d) — a body check *"with no effort to gain possession of the puck"* with the stick blade above the knees — and (e) — a check on an opponent already engaged for the puck — carry no such limit. **Whether 640(g) reaches a late hit in adult men's hockey, the book does not say.** Where 640 applies, the major plus game misconduct is mandatory, and plan on a match. **Rule 602(a) does not depend on that answer:** a match *"shall be assessed"* to any player who *"recklessly endangers or attempts to injure"* an opponent, so a reckless late hit is a match under it wherever 640 does not reach.

**Under Hockey Canada a late hit is interference, and 8.3(b) reaches a major plus a game misconduct on *"the degree of violence of the impact"* with nobody hurt, mandatory where it injures — with 8.3(c) a match penalty for a player who *"attempts to injure or deliberately injures an opponent by interference."*** **And don't skate a long way to make it:** two or more strides immediately before contact, a jump or a blind-side hit is also charging under **Rule 7.4** — the same major-plus-game-misconduct ladder (**7.4(b)**), and a match penalty where you charge them *"in such a way that they are unable to defend themselves"* (**7.4(c)**).

### Whether you may body check at all

**In short:** Find out whether body checking is allowed in this game; where it is barred, take the ice and play the puck.

```facts
Rule: No late-hit question arises where you may not body check at all — USA Hockey 604(a) bars it at 12 & under and below, in all Girls'/Women's and all non-check Adult classifications; Hockey Canada 7.3 at U13 and below, throughout female hockey and in any Member-approved division
Rule: USA Hockey 604(b) bars body checking outright any time a checking team plays a non-checking one — so whether you may check can change per fixture, not just per division
Rule: The IIHF book and the In-House Rules name no age — ask your league; the Women's NIHL is non-checking at U16 and senior. Outside it, in women's play IIHF 101.1 allows a check only to play or win the puck, never to pin or push a player into the boards, and a step or glide into one is at least a minor
Rule: CARHA's adult book writes no division where body checking is allowed — 49(a) penalises intentional body contact at a minor or a major, and a major ejects with at least a one-game suspension (30(a), 32(d))
Rule: The PWHL's 52.1 permits angling and puck-directed contact, but opposite-directional force, leaving your skating lane to make contact, or an arm, shoulder or hip extended after angling is an illegal hit
Action: Where body checking is permitted, take the body of the player who has the puck; where it is barred, take the ice they need and play the puck
```

**Every late-hit price above assumes you may body check in this game — and in a great many classifications you may not.** In a CARHA adult league, never (49(a)). ⚠️ **And under USA Hockey Rule 604(b) it can turn on the fixture rather than the classification: body checking is prohibited outright any time a checking team plays a non-checking one.**

**USA Hockey 604(a)** prohibits body checking *"in the 12 & under youth age classifications and below, all Girls'/Women's age classifications and all non-check Adult classifications"*, and lets a local governing body prohibit it in any classification. At those levels a body check is a minor (**604(c)**), a reckless one a major plus a game misconduct (**604(d)**), and plan on a match for that one (**602(a)**). **Hockey Canada Rule 7.3** *"applies only in divisions of U13 and below, in female hockey, and any other divisions approved by a Member of Hockey Canada"*: there an intentional body check, bump, shove or push is a minor (**7.3(a)**), though incidental contact between two players chasing the puck in the same direction is not penalised. ⚠️ **The minor is only the floor:** a violent check can be a major plus a game misconduct, one that hurts the player must be (**7.3(b)**), and one meant to injure is a match penalty (**7.3(c)**).

🇬🇧 **The IIHF book and the In-House Rules name no age, so ask your league.** ⚠️ **In Britain's Women's NIHL, do not body check at all** — its Rules of Competition make both U16 and senior *"non-checking"*. ⚠️ **Elsewhere in women's hockey, IIHF Rule 101.1 allows a body check only *"when there is a clear intention of playing the puck or attempting to 'gain possession' of the puck"*** — a condition, not a licence. The rule adds that *"[a]ny move by a Player to step or glide into an opposing Player will be assessed at least a minor penalty (2') for an 'illegal hit'"*, and an illegal hit is a minor, or a major plus an automatic game misconduct. **The PWHL** (**52.1**) allows contact when you angle in the same direction, but opposite-directional force, leaving your skating lane to make contact, or extending *"an arm, shoulder, or hip after angling an opponent"* is an illegal hit. [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md) sets out the clauses themselves.

So, **where body checking is permitted**, the instruction does not change with the book: take the body of the player who *has* the puck, or take the ice they need. **Where it is barred, only the second half is yours** — take the ice, play the puck, and the contact is not available to you at all. See [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md).

### The value of a simple shift

```facts
Key: A simple shift is a deliberate, low-ambition, high-execution 35 seconds
Never: Cross-ice passes, pinches or one-on-one attempts on a simple shift
Action: First man in on the forecheck, hard on the wall, change on the whistle or on the clear
Goal: Stop the bleeding and hand the game back to the bench in one piece
Mindset: It is not a lack of ambition — being able to deliver one reliably makes you very hard to leave out
```

A **simple shift** is a deliberate, low-ambition, high-execution 35 seconds: no cross-ice passes, no pinches, no one-on-one attempts, first man in on the forecheck, hard on the wall, change on the whistle or on the clear. Its purpose is to stop the bleeding and hand the game back to the bench in one piece. It is not a lack of ambition; it's the correct play in a specific situation, and being the player who can reliably deliver one makes you very hard to leave out of a line-up.

### Time-outs, icing, and what the evidence says

**In short:** Call your time-out for rest and instruction before a big moment: it is no momentum spell, but it is free.

```facts
Rule: Each team gets one thirty-second time-out per game, taken at a normal stoppage (NHL Rule 87.1)
Rule: No time-out is granted to the defensive team following an icing — you cannot rest your way out of your own icing (NHL Rule 87.1)
Rule: Under USA Hockey, Rule 636(f) gives one sixty-second time-out and attaches no icing condition to it, but “[f]or time curfew games, no time-outs shall be permitted” — and the ask must come before the line change procedure concludes, at any stoppage
Rule: The IIHF sets that same line-change clock on the ask (87.1) where the NHL asks only for the request "prior to the drop of the puck" — so in Britain the window shuts when the referee lowers their hand
Action: Spend it on rest and instruction at a moment you choose — before a critical defensive-zone draw, or before setting up 6-on-5
Rule: The two other stoppages this rule names also cost the defending team the line change — NHL and IIHF 63.8 and PWHL 65.8, whose goalie-freeze limb allows only two substitutions, an injured player and a strength-changing penalty, with no pulled-goalie one
Key: The measured effect on winning is nil in the NHL study behind this section, and across its 2014-15 to 2019-20 games most benches finished with the time-out still in the pocket
```

**Spend your time-out on rest and instruction at a moment you choose** — before a critical defensive-zone draw, before setting up 6-on-5, after a long shift you couldn't escape. It is not a momentum spell, but it is free — and the error most benches make is not spending it badly, it is never spending it at all.

What the evidence says about time-outs is humbling. A survival analysis of NHL games from 2014-15 to 2018-19 found **no statistically significant effect on win probability** from a time-out in the last five minutes of one-goal games. A leading team calling one was **1.339 times more likely to lose the lead** — not statistically significant, and possibly just because coaches call time-outs when they are already under siege. Time-outs bought about **0.25–0.50 extra shot attempts** and almost nothing in unblocked shots.

The same study found that **teams barely use them**: across 7,314 games from 2014-15 to 2019-20 only **30.1%** of available time-outs were called, and the rate *fell*, from **41.8% in 2014-15 to 22.8% in 2019-20**.

**What the rules say.** Under NHL **Rule 87.1** each team gets **one thirty-second time-out per game**, regular season or playoffs. It must be taken at a normal stoppage, and **only one time-out — commercial or team — is permitted at any one stoppage.**

Crucially, **no time-out shall be granted to the defensive team following an icing** (also: following the goalie causing a stoppage from a shoot-in from beyond the centre red line, or when a defending player accidentally dislodges the net). This is the rule that closed the loophole in 2017-18 — before it, a coach could ice the puck and then buy their tired players a rest with a time-out. **You cannot rest your way out of your own icing.**

**Those two other stoppages cost you the line change too, under a different rule.** Under NHL and IIHF Rule 63.8 (PWHL 65.8 in the same terms), ⚠️ **the defending team may not change before the draw** in two cases, and the attacking team picks the dot in both. One is a net that a defender or goalie accidentally displaces. The other is when *"the puck is shot into the end zone by the attacking team from their own side of the center red line, and the opposing goalkeeper freezes the puck resulting in a stoppage of play"*.

Note the difference: **the two limbs carry different exceptions.** The net-off limb has three: a pulled goalie, an injured player, a strength-changing penalty. ⚠️ **The goalie-freeze limb has only two — an injured player or a strength-changing penalty — and no pulled-goalie exception at all.** So a freeze on a routine dump-in from centre is a third route to a bench that can neither change nor rest, and your goalie decides it. **USA Hockey and Hockey Canada write no such freeze limb**: USA Hockey attaches no substitution bar to any of this, and Hockey Canada's **6.1(g)** covers only the dislodged net, and only *"[i]n categories of U18 AAA, Junior and at the option of the Member in Senior"*. [Goaltender](../positions/goaltender.md) covers the same rule from behind the net.

🇬🇧 **IIHF Rule 87.1 says the same three things in the same words** — one thirty-second time-out per team, none after a faceoff violation, and none to the defensive team after an icing, a goalie freeze on a shoot-in from beyond the red line, or a defender dislodging the net. **It adds one sentence a British bench needs:** the time-out *"must be requested by the team before the Referee has completed the Players change procedure, before the Referee lowers his hand and points to the respecting face-off location"*, where the NHL asks only *"(prior to the drop of the puck)"*. **So your window is the line change, not the drop.**

**USA Hockey goes the other way:** Rule 636(f) gives each team **one sixty-second time-out** with **no** icing condition, so under that book a coach can ice the puck and then buy the rest. **But the ask is on the line-change clock, after any stoppage:** *"[t]ime-outs must be requested during a stoppage of play prior to the conclusion of the line change procedure"*. ⚠️ **And in a curfew game there is no time-out at all:** *"[f]or time curfew games, no time-outs shall be permitted"*, a curfew game being *"one in which the game must end by a certain time of day"*, with both teams told the time before the start. **If your league buys ice in blocks and the game has a hard stop, nobody has a time-out to call — find out at the pre-game meeting.**

## Special Situations

### 4-on-4

```facts
Action: More ice means more time — hold the puck a beat longer and make the second option available
Read: The open middle cuts both ways: more clean entries for you, more odd-man rushes against
Convention: Two forwards and two defencemen is the common personnel choice, but many teams use three forwards — know yours
Priority: The defensive priority is the far-side rush; losing a puck at the offensive blue line is a 2-on-1 with nobody to help
```

Under **Rule 19.1**, when **one minor is assessed to a player on each team at the same stoppage** and no other penalties are on the clock, both are served **without substitution** and the teams play **four skaters against four**. 🇬🇧 **That trigger is the NHL's and the PWHL's (PWHL 19.1), not the IIHF's.** IIHF Rule 19.1 requires *"immediate substitution"* for equal minors, and its Situation Handbook confirms it at **Situation 84.10**: *"Since the penalties are coincidental, the penalties are not served on the clock and the teams are playing 5 – 5."* A British game still reaches 4-on-4 when one team takes a minor and the other a major at the same stoppage (IIHF Appendix IV Table 13, Table 15 in 2025/26, examples 18-20 and 32 the same in both), so the tactics below still apply. ⚠️ **Late in the game that route closes too** — in the last five minutes of regulation and in overtime, IIHF Rule 19.3 makes a minor against a major **5-on-4**; see [Coincidental penalties](#coincidental-penalties) below.

4-on-4 is a higher-scoring game state than 5-on-5 — in 2007-08 a goal was scored every **12.1 minutes** at 4-on-4 versus **13.7 minutes** at 5-on-5. Both are **combined** rates: the time until *either* team scores, not until one particular team does. How to play it:

- **More ice, so more time — use it.** Support is farther away, so hold the puck a beat longer and make the second option available.
- **The middle is open, which cuts both ways.** More space through the neutral zone means more clean entries and more odd-man rushes against.
- **Two forwards and two defencemen is the common personnel choice**, but many teams use three forwards and a defenceman to attack. Coaching choice — know yours.
- **The defensive priority is the far-side rush.** Losing a puck at the offensive blue line at 4-on-4 is a 2-on-1 the other way with nobody to help.

### Coincidental penalties

```facts
Rule: Coincidental minors of equal duration cancel; any difference in time is served normally (NHL Rule 19)
Rule: A second pair of coincidental minors while already 4-on-4 leaves strength unchanged, because the first pair is still on the clock (NHL Rule 19.1)
Rule: Under IIHF Rule 19.1 coincidental minors bring immediate substitution and the teams stay five a side — Britain reaches 4-on-4 by a minor against a major at the same stoppage (Appendix IV Table 13, Table 15 in 2025/26), just not by this one
Rule: But not late — a minor against a major at the same stoppage is 5-on-4 under IIHF 19.3 in the last five minutes of regulation and at any time in overtime, the differential served as a major. NHL 19.4 is the same rule
Rule: Penalised players in coincidental situations cannot leave the box until the first stoppage after their penalty expires (NHL Rule 19)
Never: Retaliate — you convert your opponent's mistake into no advantage at all
```

The principle of **Rule 19** is *cancel as many matching penalties as you can*. Coincidental minors of equal duration cancel; any difference is served normally. Once teams are 4-on-4, another pair of coincidental minors leaves them **at 4-on-4** — not 3-on-3, and not back to 5-on-5 — because Rule 19.1's four-skater provision applies only *"provided there are no other penalties in effect and visible on the penalty clocks"*. The second pair gets immediate substitution, and four players now sit in the boxes. Penalised players in coincidental situations **cannot leave the box until the first stoppage after their penalty expires**, which is why you sometimes see one sitting long after the clock hits zero.

🇬🇧 **The IIHF book answers coincidental minors the other way, and adds a rule for the end of the game.** Its Rule 19.1 requires *"immediate substitution"* for equal minors, so the teams stay five a side; a minor against a major at the same stoppage is what brings a British game to 4-on-4. ⚠️ **In the last five minutes of regulation and at any point in overtime, that route closes too.** **IIHF Rule 19.3**, *"Last Five Minutes and Overtime"*, serves the differential of a minor against a major *"immediately as a major penalty"*. The book's worked example (number 19 in the Appendix IV table, the same in both editions) says *"Team B will play short-handed for three minutes and it will be served in the same manner as a major penalty."* So it is **5-on-4 for three minutes**, and the team that took the major must place *"the penalized Player or replacement Player in the penalty box immediately."* **NHL Rule 19.4 is the same rule in materially the same words, so this is not a British departure** — though the NHL's also reaches a **match** penalty, and for a major wants the replacement in the box *"prior to expiration of the penalty"*.

The game-management point: **if you retaliate, you have converted your opponent's mistake into no advantage at all.** Your team earned a power play; you gave it straight back. That is one of the few things in hockey that is pure, unforced loss.

### The last minute of a period

```facts
Never: Get caught changing inside the last minute — a bad change concedes numbers, a hole and a blind arrival, at the moment there is no time to answer a goal
Never: Force an entry; a turnover at their blue line with 20 seconds left is a 2-on-1 you will not recover from
Action: Get the puck deep and finish the period there
Priority: Nobody's line is more important than the clock — tired with 15 seconds left, stay on and defend
Read: The exception is a genuine chance — possession with numbers, and you go
```

The first and second periods have their own risk profile. A goal against in the final minute of a period is disproportionately damaging — it's the last thing everyone carries into the dressing room, and there's no time to respond.

- **Do not get caught changing.** A change inside the last minute concedes the same three things any bad change does — a numbers disadvantage, a hole where somebody's job was, and a player joining the play blind — at the one moment in the period when there is no time to answer the goal.
- **Do not force an entry.** A turnover at their blue line with 20 seconds left is a 2-on-1 you will not recover from.
- **Get the puck deep and finish the period there.**
- **Nobody's line is more important than the clock.** If you're tired and there are 15 seconds left, stay on and defend rather than change.
- **The exception is a genuine chance.** If you have possession with numbers, go — just know the difference between a chance and a hope.

### The first shift after a long stoppage

```facts
Mindset: Expect your first stride to be slow, and expect both teams to play the shift badly
Action: Keep your feet moving during the stoppage rather than leaning on your stick
Action: Simplify the first 20 seconds — the pass you would normally complete may not connect
Priority: Win the faceoff; possession from a cold start is worth more than usual because they are disorganised too
Goal: Be first to the first loose puck — that race usually sets the tone of the next two minutes
```

After a TV time-out, an injury delay, a video review or an intermission, everybody is cold, the ice is different, and the first shift is played badly by both teams. That makes it an opportunity as much as a risk.

- **Expect your first stride to be slow.** Keep your feet moving during the stoppage rather than leaning on your stick.
- **Simplify the first 20 seconds.** Nobody's timing is right yet; the pass you'd normally complete may not connect.
- **Win the faceoff.** After a stoppage the game restarts from a draw, and possession from a cold start is worth more than usual because the other team is disorganised too.
- **Be first to the first loose puck.** Whoever wins the first race after a stoppage usually sets the tone of the next two minutes, and that is decided by intent, not fitness.

## Discipline as Game Management

### When a penalty is worth taking

**In short:** Take a penalty only to stop something likelier to score than their power play; keep it a minor, and never once your goalie is pulled.

```facts
Key: The test is whether the thing you are preventing is more likely to become a goal than the power play you give up
Options: Stopping an odd-man rush in the last minutes of a close game, or preventing a certain goal — with a foul that stays a minor
Rule: Two fouls are not priced as minors under the NHL — fouling a breakaway from behind is a penalty shot, not a power play (57.3), and so is a skater other than the goalie covering a puck that is itself in their own crease (67.4)
Rule: The trade assumes a minor — a reckless trip or hook is a mandatory major plus game misconduct in USA Hockey (639(b), 623(b)), and plan on a match (602(a)); the IIHF can make either a major plus game misconduct with nobody hurt (57.4, 55.3), CARHA any trip (86(a))
Rule: A trip or hook that injures ejects you in Hockey Canada (8.2(b), 8.6(b)); a hook that injures does in the NHL, the PWHL and CARHA (55.4, 56.4, 64(b) with 30(a))
Rule: USA Hockey prices covering a puck in your own crease as "a penalty shot/optional minor penalty" (614(b)), and 406(a) gives the election to the non-offending team — so it never makes the trade cheaper for you, and the minor still puts a man off
Priority: For a puck in the air there is usually no trade to price at all; knocking it down concedes nothing, and the next section is the whole of that rule
Never: Foul anything once your own goalie is pulled — every trade that was worth taking with a goalie in the net stops being worth taking without one
Rule: The empty-net awarded goal — NHL 25.1 and 57.4 for the breakaway; for the crease one NHL and IIHF 67.5, PWHL 69.5 and Hockey Canada 10.2(a)(vi) on the substitution, CARHA 58(c) Note 1 on its face though 36(b) conditions it, USA Hockey 614(b) where it prevented an obvious and imminent goal
Never: Take one for retaliation, for sending a message, after the whistle, or at any point in the first period
Rule: Trading a foul for a power play assumes the penalty is one, which the after-the-whistle ones are not always — USA Hockey writes a puck put out during a stoppage as a minor (610(c)) and a misconduct (601(c)(10)) without saying which governs, and a game misconduct in protest of a call (601(d)(7))
```

Rarely — but not never. The test is simple: **is the thing you are preventing more likely to become a goal than the power play you are giving up?**

**The arithmetic behind that test lives in [Risk Management, "Penalty risk as a decision"](../hockey-iq/risk_management.md#penalty-risk-as-a-decision)** — what a minor costs you, what a penalty shot is worth, and the full rule account of the fouls that are *not* priced as minors. This document covers the part that changes with the score and the clock:

- **Stopping an odd-man rush in the last minutes of a close game — but *not* a clean breakaway.** Fouling a breakaway from behind gives up a **penalty shot**, not a power play (Rule 57.3), and across the eleven NHL seasons 2015-16 to 2025-26 penalty shots converted at a *higher* rate than power plays — not by much, and not in every recent season, but higher. Hauling someone down from behind saves you little or nothing; the time to prevent the breakaway was four seconds earlier, with your gap — see [Defending the Rush](defending_the_rush.md).
- **Preventing a certain goal.** If it is a goal or a penalty, take the penalty — ⚠️ **and keep it a minor, because the trade only works while it is one.** A reckless trip or hook is a mandatory major plus game misconduct in USA Hockey (639(b), 623(b)), where you should also plan on a match (602(a)). The IIHF can make either a major plus game misconduct with nobody hurt (57.4, 55.3), and CARHA can make any trip one (86(a)). A hook that injures — in Hockey Canada a trip too — is a major that ends your game in the NHL, the PWHL, Hockey Canada and CARHA. ⚠️ **For a puck in the air there is usually no trade at all** — see [Catching a loose puck in your own crease](#catching-a-loose-puck-in-your-own-crease).
- **Sometimes: killing time you need killed.** A stoppage you badly need for a change or a rest can occasionally be worth two minutes — but this is a coach's decision, not yours.

Once your own goalie is out, ⚠️ **every one of those trades stops working.** This is the game state most of this document is about, so it matters here. With the net empty there is no shot for anyone to save, so the rulebook skips the penalty shot and simply gives them the goal:

- **A breakaway fouled from behind with your goalie pulled is an awarded goal** — NHL **Rules 25.1 and 57.4**, which the rulebook cites as a pair (25.1 is the general awarded-goal rule; 57.4 is the operative clause for tripping, hooking, holding and slashing).
- **Covering the puck in your own crease with your goalie pulled is an awarded goal too** — not the penalty shot it would be with the goalie in (NHL 67.5). ⚠️ **The other books reach the same place on slightly different terms, so treat any cover in your own crease as the goal.** Details in [Catching a loose puck in your own crease](#catching-a-loose-puck-in-your-own-crease).

So: **when you have pulled your goalie, there is no foul left that buys you anything.** The exact conditions and wording are set out in [Risk Management](../hockey-iq/risk_management.md#penalty-risk-as-a-decision).

Note what is *not* on the list: retaliation, sending a message, standing up for a teammate after the whistle, and anything in the first period. ⚠️ **And after-the-whistle penalties are not always priced as a power play** — under USA Hockey and Hockey Canada a puck fired out of the rink after a whistle can climb to a game misconduct. See [When it absolutely isn't](#when-it-absolutely-isnt).

### Catching a loose puck in your own crease

**In short:** Never cover or hold a loose puck in your own crease; put it straight down and play it.

```facts
Rule: Five books print the permission to catch a puck and put it straight down — NHL and IIHF 67.2, PWHL 69.2, Hockey Canada 10.2(a)(iii) and CARHA 61(a), the last two "without gaining or attempting to gain" an advantage; USA Hockey only in Casebook Situation 1 to Rule 618
Priority: Knock the puck straight down where you stand — that concedes nothing under any book, and no version of this trade beats it
Priority: Only where you cannot knock the puck down, and only while your own goalie is still on the ice, is the penalty shot better than the goal it prevented
Priority: ⚠️ **Once your own net is empty there is no trade left** — covering a puck in your crease concedes the goal, not a shot (USA Hockey: where it prevented an obvious and imminent goal), so knock it down
Rule: One thing that buys the penalty is the conduct after the catch — holding it, a stride, turning, throwing it (NHL and IIHF 67.2(I), PWHL 69.2(i); Hockey Canada Interpretation 4 to 10.2(a)(iii)); another is a hand laid over a puck on the ice to conceal it, the penalty shot in your crease (67.2(II))
Rule: Under USA Hockey a hand closed on a CAUGHT puck outside the crease and not opened at once is a stoppage, not a penalty; a hand covering a loose puck on the ice is a minor (614(a)), and so is picking it up; in the crease any of them is the penalty shot or optional minor (614(b), 618(a))
Rule: Every book here awards the crease penalty shot for a puck in your crease, wherever you are — NHL and IIHF 63.6, PWHL 65.6, CARHA 58(c), USA Hockey 614(b) and 618(a), Hockey Canada 4.11(a)(v), which its 10.2(a)(v) cross-refers to, for the hand and the body alike
Rule: ⚠️ **With your OWN net empty, covering a puck in your crease is an awarded goal, not a penalty shot** — NHL and IIHF 67.5, PWHL 69.5, Hockey Canada 10.2(a)(vi); CARHA 58(c) Note 1 on its face, though 36(b) conditions it; USA Hockey 614(b) only where it prevented an obvious and imminent goal
Rule: The converse — a minor where the puck is outside the paint and you are inside it — is rule text in the NHL and IIHF (63.6) and PWHL (65.6); USA Hockey's Casebook agrees (614 Situation 1); Hockey Canada's 10.2(a)(v)-(vi) also go by where you stand: price the shot, or the goal with your net empty
Rule: The crease is a volume, up to the top of the four-foot goal frame in all six books (NHL and PWHL 1.7, CARHA 5(c), USA Hockey 618(a) Note) — knee high in the paint is the penalty shot, above the crossbar a minor (IIHF Situations 63.15, 63.27): height sets which penalty you pay, not whether
Rule: USA Hockey alone lets the NON-OFFENDING team elect that minor in place of the shot (406(a)) — their election, not yours, so it never makes the trade cheaper for you
```

**The play is always the same: knock the puck straight down where you stand, and keep your hand off it.** No book charges you for that. What follows is the price of anything else.

**You may catch the puck if you put it straight back down.** NHL and IIHF **Rule 67.2** read *"A player shall be permitted to catch the puck out of the air but must immediately place it or knock it down to the ice"*, with **PWHL 69.2** the same; Hockey Canada 10.2(a)(iii) and **CARHA 61(a)** allow a player who *"immediately drops it to the ice"* without gaining or attempting to gain an advantage. ⚠️ **USA Hockey's playing rules print no such permission.** Under 618(a) a hand closed on a caught puck **outside** the crease and not opened at once is only a stoppage and a last-play faceoff — **but a hand closed on a puck *in* the crease is the penalty shot or optional minor** (614(b), 618(a)). The express permission is in the separate *Rules and Casebook*, Situation 1 to Rule 618: catch and drop in *"virtually one continuous motion"* and play continues — a casebook interpretation, not rule text.

Covering a loose puck on the ice, or picking one up, is a different act. ⚠️ **Under USA Hockey it is a minor with the puck outside the crease** (614(a) and its Casebook Situation 1; 618(a)'s second paragraph, matched by NHL and IIHF 67.2(III) and PWHL 69.2(iii)), **and the penalty shot or optional minor with it inside** (614(b), 618(a)).

And "put it down" means ⚠️ **down where you are, not somewhere better.** Moving the puck first is not *"immediately"* placing it. In IIHF **Situation 67.6** a defender with their own net empty grabs the puck at waist height and *"drops the puck outside the crease"* — and the ruling is *"an Awarded Goal to Team B"* (a Handbook application, which ranks below the Rulebook). Hockey Canada writes the same limit into the rule: *"**without gaining or attempting to gain an advantage**."*

**What costs you is the conduct after the catch, not its height.** NHL and IIHF **67.2(i)** and PWHL **69.2(i)** penalise catching the puck and skating with it to avoid a check or gain ground; Hockey Canada's **Interpretation 4 to 10.2(a)(iii)** lists throwing it, manoeuvring around a player while holding it, and taking a stride with it. **That is the variable you control. Knock it down and play it.**

**A hand laid over the puck also buys the penalty, and inside your own crease it is priced at the top.** NHL and IIHF **67.2(II)** give a minor for placing a hand over a puck on the ice *"to conceal it from or prevent an opponent from playing the puck"*, and the Note beneath sends it, in your goal crease area, to the penalty shot or the awarded goal. ⚠️ **So a hand flat over a loose puck in your own paint is priced like gathering it in**, and with your net empty that is the goal. The PWHL writes the same limb (69.2(ii)); USA Hockey, Hockey Canada and CARHA never use the word *conceal*. ⚠️ **Do not read that as a permission under those books.** USA Hockey penalises *"the use of hands to make the puck unplayable"* (614(a) Note) and a hand holding the puck in the crease (614(b)). Hockey Canada permits a hand only *"to stop, knock down, or push the puck"* (10.2 preamble). CARHA 58(c) gives the penalty shot where a defender *"holds or gathers the puck into their body in any manner, or picks up the puck with their hand"* in the crease — CARHA binds its affiliated leagues only. **Under every one of them, knocking it down is the play.**

**Now the price, if you do hold it — and only while your own goalie is in the net.** Holding a puck that is itself in your own crease is a penalty shot (**NHL Rule 67.4**), which is still better than the goal it prevented. ⚠️ **With your own net empty there is no such trade:** the same act is an **awarded goal** under the NHL, the IIHF, the PWHL, Hockey Canada and CARHA, and under USA Hockey wherever it prevented an obvious and imminent goal.

**In most books the puck's position decides, not yours; Hockey Canada adds yours, so under it standing in the paint can cost you the shot.** The NHL and IIHF write the puck test in **Rule 63.6**, the PWHL in 65.6, USA Hockey in 614(b) and 618(a), and Hockey Canada in **4.11(a)(v)**. Hockey Canada's gives the shot to a defender who *"closes their hand on the puck gaining an advantage, or deliberately falls on or gathers the puck into their body, while the puck is in the goal crease"*. **The NHL, the IIHF and the PWHL (65.6) also print the converse:** gather a puck lying outside the paint while you stand in it and it is a minor, not a shot.

**Neither Hockey Canada nor USA Hockey writes that into its rule text.** Hockey Canada's 10.2(a)(v) awards the shot *"when the infraction occurs inside the offending team's goal crease"*, which locates the infraction rather than the puck; 4.11(a)(v) makes the puck reading plausible but does not rule the other out; and **Interpretation 5** (a minor, because *"The puck was not actually covered in the crease"*) has the player outside the crease too.

**USA Hockey answers it in its Casebook, and your way:** in Rule 614, Situation 1, a defender covers the puck outside the crease and slides in — *"The Referee must assess a minor penalty"*, because *"[t]he location of the puck at the instant it is covered or held is the determining factor"*. That is not quite the NHL's permutation, but the reason is the same. **Hockey Canada publishes no ruling on it, so price the shot there.** None of this changes the play: knock the puck straight down where you stand and no book charges you anything.

**And the trade changes once your own goalie is pulled.** ⚠️ **With the net empty, covering a puck that is in your own crease is an awarded goal, not a penalty shot.** **NHL and IIHF 67.5**, **PWHL 69.5** and **Hockey Canada 10.2(a)(vi)** attach it to the bare fact of the substitution, as **CARHA 58(c) Note 1** does on its face; the NHL and IIHF reach a player who *"falls on the puck, holds the puck, picks up the puck, or gathers the puck into"* their body or hands in the crease. **USA Hockey conditions it**: 614(b) awards the goal only where the act *"prevented an obvious and imminent goal"*; otherwise it stays the penalty shot or optional minor. **Under Hockey Canada the goal, like the shot, turns on where the infraction is as well as where the puck is (10.2(a)(vi); and 4.12(a) gives it for any cover of a puck in the crease), so price a cover made from inside your crease as the goal even with the puck outside it.** Either way the free option is the same: knock the puck straight down where you stand.

**USA Hockey alone lets the other side elect the minor instead of the shot** (Rule 406(a)) — ⚠️ **their choice, not yours, so it never makes the trade cheaper for you.** The shot itself costs you no strength: under 406(a) *"the offending team shall not be reduced in on-ice strength as a result of the infraction"*, and Hockey Canada's 4.11 preamble makes the shot *"in lieu of a Minor Penalty"* the same way.

**And the crease is a volume, not a footprint** — in all six books it runs up to the top of the goal frame, four feet (NHL 1.7 and 2.1, IIHF 1.7, PWHL 1.7, Hockey Canada 1.5(c), CARHA 5(c), the Note to USA Hockey 618(a)). ⚠️ **Height sets which penalty you pay, never whether you pay one.** The IIHF Situation Handbook gives a minor for a puck caught above the crossbar (Situation 63.27) and the shot for one taken knee high in the paint (63.15 and 67.4). **Do not read that as an instruction to catch it higher:** in each of them the player turned away or threw the puck after the catch, and that conduct is what produced a penalty at all — the height only chose which one. Reaching above the crossbar means reaching up through a scramble, and knocking the puck down was available every time. (The Handbook ranks below the Rulebook, so these are worked applications, not rule text.)

### When it absolutely isn't

**In short:** Take no penalty when leading late, when already a man short or up, after the whistle, or at 6-on-5.

```facts
Never: Take a penalty with a lead in the last five minutes. If you are beaten, be beaten, and let them have the outside shot
Never: Take one while already killing a penalty; coaches rank a 5-on-3 against as the most dangerous game state in hockey, which is coaching emphasis and not a counted ranking
Never: Take one while on your own power play, or after the whistle
Rule: The after-the-whistle one can cost more than a power play — USA Hockey prices a puck put out during a stoppage at a minor (610(c)) or a misconduct (601(c)(10)) without saying which, and a game misconduct in protest of a call (601(d)(7))
Rule: Hockey Canada writes the same act at Rule 11.1, preamble item (v), laddering to a Misconduct at 11.1(d) and a Game Misconduct at 11.1(e) — Note 1 to each lets a referee assess either without the rungs below it, but both rungs still require that the player persists in the conduct
Rule: In all six books, intentionally knocking or shooting the puck out of the reach of an official who is retrieving it is a misconduct — NHL, PWHL and IIHF 39.4(ii), USA Hockey 601(c)(3), Hockey Canada 11.1(d)(iii), CARHA 46(b)(3)
Never: Take one at 6-on-5 or defending 6-on-5 — both directions are catastrophic
```

- **When you're leading late.** A penalty with a lead in the last five minutes hands them a power play in the window where you have the least time left to answer a goal. If you're beaten, be beaten — let them have the outside shot.
- **When you're already killing a penalty.** Coaches rank a 5-on-3 against as the most dangerous game state in hockey — coaching emphasis, not a counted one.
- **When you're already on a power play.** Nothing wastes an advantage faster.
- **After the whistle.** These penalties buy you nothing and are the easiest to call. **Skate away and leave the puck where it is** — ⚠️ **firing it away is not two minutes in every book.** The same act can be two minutes in an NHL-rules or British game and end your night under USA Hockey or Hockey Canada, so find out which book your league runs.
- **When you're 6-on-5 or defending 6-on-5.** Both directions are catastrophic — recall that on **2007-08** pulled-goalie data the defending team took **84 penalties against the pulling team's 44** — roughly twice as many. That is a single season, and it is the split *between the two teams*, not a comparison with normal play.

**What firing the puck away costs.** In all six books, knocking or shooting the puck away from an official who is retrieving it is a **misconduct** (NHL, PWHL and IIHF 39.4(ii), USA Hockey 601(c)(3), Hockey Canada 11.1(d)(iii), CARHA 46(b)(3)). Under the NHL and the IIHF a puck fired out of the rink after the whistle is otherwise a delay-of-game minor (63.2(ii)).

**Under USA Hockey** a puck put out during a stoppage is a minor (610(c)) or a **misconduct** (601(c)(10)), and the book does not say which governs. ⚠️ **Do it in protest of a call and it is a game misconduct** (601(d)(7)), which also suspends you for the team's next game (404(b)). A puck sent *"in the general direction of an on-ice official, or in the direction of a spectator"* is the same (601(d)(9)), and one shot directly at them recklessly is a **match penalty** (Note to 601(d)). A plain avoidable shot after the whistle is an unsportsmanlike minor, then a misconduct if you persist, then a game misconduct (601(a)(4)).

**Hockey Canada** ⚠️ **lists *"Shooting the puck out of the playing area, after the whistle"* as Unsportsmanlike Conduct** (11.1, preamble item (v)): a minor, a misconduct for persisting (11.1(d)(i)), and a game misconduct for persisting after either (11.1(e)). The referee may start at a higher rung, but persistence is what they must see.

### Frustration penalties

**In short:** Keep your feet moving, give up the last word and have a reset; frustration penalties buy you nothing.

```facts
Action: Keep your feet moving — when your feet stop your stick starts reaching, and a stick used as a substitute for skating is what hooking and tripping calls are made of
Action: Give up the last word — USA Hockey's casebook has both players given a minor for a push and a push back, so answering costs you a minor whatever else happens
Risk: The extra minor against whoever started it is the part the casebook says officials often skip, so retaliating can leave you at a straight wash
Key: The casebook condemns that practice rather than endorsing it — it says the approach discredits the officials and creates more problems later in the game
Action: Have a reset — a breath at the faceoff dot, a tap of the pads, a look at the clock
Convention: That most amateur penalties are frustration-driven is coaching experience, not data — no published study in amateur hockey codes penalty motive against the preceding play
```

*This section carries the full treatment of what is and is not known about why amateur players take penalties; [Risk Management](../hockey-iq/risk_management.md#the-penalties-that-are-not-trades-at-all) sends you here for it. The claim below should never be quoted without the qualification attached to it.*

The standard coaching observation is that **the great majority of penalties in amateur hockey are frustration penalties**: retaliation, slashing after losing a battle, cross-checking after a whistle, arguing with an official. There is published data on youth-hockey penalties, and it partly supports this and partly does not. It is worth separating the two, because the coaching advice below survives either way.

**What the data does support.** Most penalties in youth hockey really are aggression-type infractions rather than obstruction fouls. Gilbert and Trudel coded gamesheets and video from **55 Bantam (14-15) games across five leagues, 850 penalties**, and found **an average of 15.5 penalties per game with "most penalties classified as minor aggression penalties (62.4%)"** — roughing, cross-checking, elbowing, slashing, as against the hooking-and-holding category. They also found that "the number of penalties tended to increase from the first period to the third period", which is at least consistent with accumulating frustration.

**What it does not support is the motive.** No study codes *why* a penalty was taken. "Minor aggression" is a category of contact, not intent: a clean cross-check on a forechecker and a revenge slash both land in it, so the claim that most are *retaliation* has no measurement behind it. Two findings cut against it. McFaul and colleagues, video-coding 249 verified head impacts across 48 U15 and U18 AA/AAA games, found **"most HC events (~68%) were the outcome of nonaggressive play and within the rules of the sport (~67%)"**. Kirker, Tenenbaum and Mattson found **"aggression was instrumental in nature two-thirds of the time"** — goal-directed, not hot-blooded. And Gilbert and Trudel found **74.9% of penalties went to one player at a time**, whereas retaliation and post-whistle scrums tend to produce penalties in pairs.

There is also a nice piece of counter-evidence against the intuition that frustration needs a physical outlet: Régnier and colleagues, comparing 28 Pee Wee teams in a body-checking league against 21 in a league that banned it, reported that **"contrary to popular belief, playing without body-checking resulted in fewer 'hostile aggression penalties.'"**

**Where that leaves it.** "Most amateur penalties are aggression-type infractions" is measured; "most are frustration-driven" is coaching experience, not data. No published study codes penalty motive in amateur hockey, so it cannot be confirmed or refuted. What is not in doubt is that frustration penalties happen, buy you nothing, and mostly happen to players who have just been beaten and want to even the ledger.

Three practical habits:

1. **Keep your feet moving.** When your feet stop, your stick starts reaching — and a stick used as a substitute for skating is what hooking and tripping calls are made of. Treat discipline as partly a conditioning and effort problem in disguise.
2. **Give up the last word.** After a whistle, be the one who skates away, and don't assume the officials saw who started it. USA Hockey's casebook takes exactly this case — a push after the whistle and a push back: *"In this instance, both players should be assessed minor penalties for roughing"* (Standard of Play, Situation 25). So answering costs you a minor whatever happens. The book also directs an *extra* minor against whoever started it (Rule 640, Situation 2), but says officials *often* skip it and give one each — a practice it condemns as one that *"discredits the officials"*. Condemned or not, it happens, and a minor apiece cancels the advantage you had just earned.
3. **Have a reset.** Something small and physical — a breath at the faceoff dot, a tap of the pads, a look at the clock. See [Mental Game](../off-the-ice/mental_game.md).

## Common Mistakes

- **Sitting on a lead from the second period.** Play your normal game until late. Retreating is a late-game tool; used early, it hands over the game and keeps you in your own end for forty minutes.
- **Reading raw shot totals without the score.** Being outshot while winning is the normal result of leading; being outshot in a tie game is the problem.
- **Changing while the other team has the puck in your zone.** Change on the way out: with the puck heading north, after a dump-in, or after a clear. Otherwise you are 4-on-5 for two seconds in the most dangerous area of the rink. Coaches rank it among the biggest sources of goals against in amateur hockey — an observation, not a measured ranking.
- **Long shifts because you feel fine.** Come off when your acceleration drops. You don't feel your decisions getting worse until you have made the bad one.
- **Letting your goalie freeze a dump-in when your line is stuck out.** Get to it first and move it. The whistle gives you an own-end draw with the same tired five — ⚠️ **and under the NHL, the IIHF and the PWHL, a freeze on a puck shot in from the attackers' own side of centre bars your change just as icing does, with no pulled-goalie exception** — though an injured player can still be replaced. See [Time-outs, icing, and what the evidence says](#time-outs-icing-and-what-the-evidence-says).
- **Icing the puck to get a whistle.** Chip it past the forechecker instead: you lose it far from your net and still get your change. Ice it and you get an own-end draw with five tired players, ⚠️ **and under the NHL, the IIHF and the PWHL no change and no time-out** — a hurt teammate may come off; a tired one may not. Not every book prices it that way; see [The icing trap](#the-icing-trap).
- **Waiting until 1:00 to pull the goalie.** Go earlier — down two with four or five minutes left is normal, not desperate. Beaudoin and Swartz report that every earlier analysis they review, back to the first in 1976, finds a one-minute pull too late.
- **Not knowing the delayed-penalty freebie.** When the arm goes up against them, get the goalie off at once and send the sixth skater as the goalie arrives. They cannot score unless you put it in your own net. Not every league allows it, so agree it beforehand.
- **Shooting from anywhere in 3-on-3 overtime.** Without a genuine look, regroup and come again. A miss is a 3-on-2 the other way.
- **Forcing a zone entry in the last minute of a period.** Get it deep and finish the period there. A turnover at their blue line with 20 seconds left is a 2-on-1 you won't recover from, with no time to answer.
- **Holding a loose puck in your own crease with your net empty.** Knock it straight down and keep your hand off it — a hand over the puck counts as holding it in several books, and catching it and dropping it outside the crease has still been ruled a goal under the IIHF. With a goalie in net, holding it is a penalty shot; ⚠️ **with the net empty it is an awarded goal** — on the bare fact in most books, under USA Hockey where it prevented an obvious and imminent goal, and CARHA writes it both ways, so treat any cover as the goal. See [Catching a loose puck in your own crease](#catching-a-loose-puck-in-your-own-crease).
- **Looking for a hit before you know you may throw one.** Find out first; where checking is barred, take the ice, not the body. It is barred at the younger ages (12 & under in USA Hockey, U13 and below in Hockey Canada), and in women's and girls' hockey under USA Hockey and Hockey Canada and in Britain's Women's NIHL. It is also barred in CARHA adult hockey and in non-check adult leagues — ⚠️ **and under USA Hockey for the whole game whenever a checking team plays a non-checking one.** Elsewhere in women's hockey under the IIHF, a body check needs a clear intention to play the puck, and stepping or gliding into her, or using the boards to pin or push her, is at least a minor. [Whether you may body check at all](#whether-you-may-body-check-at-all) has your book's list.
- **Finishing your check.** Let a player who has passed or shot go; if you could still pull out of the hit, pull out, and if they have turned away, leave them. ⚠️ **No book caps a late hit at a minor:** every one can reach a major. USA Hockey makes a reckless one a mandatory major that throws you out of the game; plan on a match and a likely multi-game suspension. A hit from behind throws you out of the game in almost every book, and under USA Hockey it must when it is forceful along the boards, head first or reckless. Full ladder in [What a late hit costs in each book](#what-a-late-hit-costs-in-each-book).
- **Retaliating.** Skate away and give up the last word. Otherwise their penalty becomes nothing, or their power play becomes your penalty kill.
- **Firing the puck away after the whistle.** Skate away and leave it. ⚠️ **Under USA Hockey and Hockey Canada it can get you thrown out of the game** — and under USA Hockey, doing it in protest of a call is exactly the trigger. See [When it absolutely isn't](#when-it-absolutely-isnt).
- **Playing your normal game down three with ten minutes left.** Go hard now, while it can still work. Moderately aggressive late is worse than very aggressive early.
- **Treating the time-out as a momentum spell.** Use it for rest and instruction, and don't finish with it unused. The evidence doesn't support momentum, and about 70% of available time-outs went unused across 2014-15 to 2019-20.
- **Assuming last change means anything in your league.** Plenty of rec leagues run no formal change procedure — this document has no count of how many, so ask yours. On the road, your matchup tool is the change on the fly.

## Key Takeaways

1. **Protecting a lead, play actively.** Manage the puck, take away the middle, shorten shifts and win your own-end draws. Passive is not the same as safe. Expect to be outshot: leading teams shoot less, which is why analysts score-adjust shot counts.
2. **Trailing, add risk in order** — neutral-zone speed and support, then a harder forecheck, a pinch, a defenceman into the rush, then the goalie — scaled to the clock and the deficit, not your emotions. Down three with ten minutes left, go hard now. Somebody is always the last player back.
3. **Pull the goalie sooner than feels comfortable** — down two with four or five minutes left is normal. With six on, get set up, keep a body in front who stays off the goalie, move it first-touch, and never pinch both point men.
4. **On a delayed penalty the extra attacker is free.** They cannot score unless you put it in your own net, so the goalie goes as the arm rises and the sixth skater jumps as the goalie arrives. Make no blind pass across your crease, and agree it beforehand — not every league allows it.
5. **Last change works only at stoppages**, so on the road, match on the fly. ⚠️ **With your line stuck out, beat your goalie to a dump-in and move it:** under the NHL, the IIHF and the PWHL your goalie's freeze of a puck shot in from the attackers' half bars your change, except to replace an injured player.
6. **A bad change costs three things** — a man short, a hole where the departing player's job was, and a teammate joining blind. Go with the puck heading north, after a dump-in or after a clear. Never when they have it in your zone, never as the last player back, and not in a period's last minute.
7. **Pinned with a tired line, chip it out — never ice it for a rest.** You lose the puck far from your net and still change. Ice it and you get an own-end draw with the same five, and under the NHL, the IIHF and the PWHL no change (except for an injury) and no time-out.
8. **3-on-3 overtime is a possession game.** Regroup rather than force a shot, and change only with the puck. In a shootout, decide your move before you start; in goal, stay square and let the shooter decide. Check what your competition plays — under the British In-House Rules many games simply end level.
9. **Defending an empty net, don't chase or fire it the length of the ice.** Hold a tight shape in the slot and lift sticks low on the shaft as the puck arrives. **Six against six is equal strength, so you can be iced** — under the NHL, the IIHF and the PWHL that freezes your bench and costs your time-out.
10. **With your goalie in, a penalty can beat a goal — if it stays a minor. With the goalie pulled, take none.** Fouling a breakaway from behind gives up a penalty shot; close the gap earlier. ⚠️ **In several books a reckless trip or hook throws you out of the game with nobody hurt, and USA Hockey makes that mandatory.**
11. **Knock a loose puck in your own crease straight down and keep your hand off it** — holding it is what buys the penalty. ⚠️ **With your own net empty, holding it is an awarded goal, not a penalty shot.** USA Hockey asks whether it prevented an obvious and imminent goal; treat any cover as the goal anyway.
12. **After a whistle, skate away and leave the puck where it is.** Firing it out is not always two minutes: under USA Hockey and Hockey Canada it can get you thrown out of the game, and under USA Hockey doing it *"in protest of an official's decision"* is what takes it there.
13. **After a goal against, play one simple shift, keep your feet moving and give up the last word.** Win a puck, get it deep, take your man, change. Don't chase the goal back or go looking for a hit. When your feet stop your stick reaches, so have a reset: a breath at the dot, a look at the clock.
14. **Hit only if checking is allowed in your game, and let a passer or shooter go: pull out if you can and leave anyone turned away.** ⚠️ **A late hit can cost a major or more, and a hit from behind ends your game in almost every book — under USA Hockey it must when forceful along the boards, head first or reckless.**
15. **Find out what your team plays rather than assuming this version.** Your forecheck with a lead, your 6-on-5 shape, who covers behind a pinch and where your fifth skater stands against a pulled goalie are your team's calls.
