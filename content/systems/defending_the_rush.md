## Key focus

**Stay goal side of every attacker, all the time — on the line between them and your own net.** Anything that puts an attacker between you and your goal has already gone wrong.

**Start skating backwards early and keep the gap closing all the way in.** You cannot close a gap from a standstill, and a big gap hands the attacker speed, time to look and a clean shooting lane.

**Watch the chest and hips, not the puck.** The puck, stick, head and eyes can all fake you; the torso has to go where the body goes.

**Take the middle and steer the carrier outside.** Sit slightly inside them with your stick flat in the passing lane, so the only route they see is toward the wall. Steer with your route, not your body, and pin the puck, not the player.

**Backcheck to the inside of your man, and make sure somebody owns the middle.** Skate to their inside hip, not their back, and pick up the attacker driving the middle without the puck — the one most often missed.

**Two attackers and one of you: take away the pass, give up the shot, and say it out loud.** Your goalie can stop a shot, not a pass across the crease. Some teams slide across the lane instead; settle which with your coach and goalie first.

**Know your job on every rush before it arrives, and call it.** On a 2-on-2 do not both go to the puck, on a 3-on-2 retreat level with your partner, on a 3-on-1 delay, and somebody takes the trailer. Short, early, loud.

**Step up or back off — never half.** Step up only when you are confident you will win the puck; otherwise back off with a tight gap. How aggressive to be is your coach's call, so ask.

**Chasing a lone attacker from behind, your stick goes on the puck and nothing else.** It is your goalie's save and they usually make it; a hook, trip or hold from behind gives away a penalty shot.

**Best of all, do not create the rush.** By coaching consensus rather than count, most odd-man rushes are your own team's making — a missed pinch or step-up, a forward leaving early, a turnover in the middle, a bad change, or nobody left above the puck.

## Overview

A **rush** is an attack that arrives before the defence is set — the goalie is still moving and nobody has picked up an opponent. Coaches and analysts widely treat rush chances as the most dangerous in hockey, and published tracking backs that. Your one job is to stay between the puck and your own net while the play comes at you.

**Goal side first, then the gap.** Be on the line between every attacker and your net, then close the distance. Start skating backwards while they are still collecting the puck, and keep the gap shrinking — roughly two to three stick lengths in open ice and about one by your blue line, as a guide, not a law. Watch their chest: the puck, stick and head can all lie.

**Take the middle and give them the wall.** Sit a little inside the carrier with your stick flat in the passing lane, so the only route they see is toward the boards, where shots rarely beat a goalie. Do it with your route. Many youth, women's and adult leagues bar body checking — Britain's WNIHL is played "non-checking" — and even where riding them is allowed, driving a carrier into the boards is boarding whether or not they saw you coming. Pin the puck, not the player.

**Backcheck inside, and own the middle.** A forward coming back skates to the inside hip of their man, between them and the net. The attacker most often forgotten is the one driving the middle without the puck; next is the trailer arriving late into the high slot. Who owns each depends on your team's system — there are four named ones, and two ask the first forward back to do opposite things — so find out which yours runs.

**On the numbers, know your job before the rush arrives.** Two attackers and one of you: the common instruction is to take away the pass and give up the shot, said out loud so the goalie owns the shooter and you own the pass. The alternative is a timed slide to force the shot, so settle which with your coach and goalie before the game. On a 2-on-2 do not both go to the puck; on a 3-on-2 retreat level with your partner with the middle sealed; on a 3-on-1 delay until help arrives. At the blue line, step up or back off but never half — when in doubt, back off with a tight gap.

**A breakaway is your goalie's save, so play the puck and nothing else.** A hook, trip, hold or slash from behind that denies the chance is a penalty shot rather than two minutes in every book here, and an awarded goal if your net is empty; under USA Hockey the team you fouled may take a minor instead.

**Most odd-man rushes start with your own team's mistake.** Five causes account for nearly all of them: a defenceman stepping up and missing the puck, a forward leaving early, a turnover in the middle, a line change with the puck loose, or nobody left above the puck in the offensive zone. That is coaching emphasis, not a counted ranking — no public data classifies odd-man rushes by cause.

## Why Rush Defence Matters More Than Anything You Do in the Zone

**In short:** A rush catches your goalie moving and nobody marked, so your best defence is never letting one turn into a chance.

```facts
Key: On a rush your goalie is moving and cannot be set — and being set is what makes routine saves routine
Key: Nobody has a mark, assignments are formed on the fly, and the attackers pick the play rather than you
Priority: Preventing a rush chance is worth far more than defending one well
Action: Kill rushes by holding a tight gap and forcing dump-ins rather than by making recoveries afterwards
Key: A forced dump-in still has to be retrieved at your own boards, usually under forecheck pressure — decide your posture on the way in. See Body Contact and Battles
```

Set defensive-zone play gets most of the practice time. Rush defence deserves more, and here is why.

When a shot comes from a **set** defensive zone — your team in position, the puck cycling around the outside — the goalie is square to the puck, stationary, and already tracking. Five defenders are between the shooter and the net. The shooter is usually taking the shot the defence has decided to allow.

On a rush, none of that is true:

- **Your goalie is moving.** They are pushing across, backing in, resetting depth. A goalie who is mid-movement when the shot is released cannot be set. Being set — feet planted, square, weight balanced — is what makes routine saves routine.
- **The defence is not organised.** Nobody has a mark. Assignments are being formed on the fly at around 30 kilometres an hour — an illustrative figure, not a measured one.
- **The attackers pick the play.** In a set zone you dictate what shot they get. On a rush, they do.
- **Everything happens facing the wrong way.** You are skating backwards and they are skating forwards. That asymmetry is why rush defence has its own technique.

The practical conclusion: **preventing a rush chance is worth far more than defending one well.** A defenceman who kills rushes with a tight gap and forces dump-ins does more for the goals-against column than one who makes spectacular recoveries. That dumped puck still has to be retrieved at your own boards, usually under forecheck pressure — decide how you will meet the wall before that race starts: see [Body Contact and Battles, section 6](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md#6-receiving-a-hit-safely).

> **A note on numbers.** Rush and odd-man chances are widely treated by coaches and analysts as the highest-danger chances in hockey, and there is published tracking behind that. Sportlogiq data covering every NHL game from 2017-18 to 2020-21, presented at the Ottawa Hockey Analytics Conference and reported by [Alison Lukan for NHL.com](https://www.nhl.com/kraken/news/seattle-kraken-analytics-with-alison-chances-off-the-rush-328173944), puts a *"normal shot"* at an **8.15% likelihood of becoming a goal** — and says that *"the minute an opponent gets an extra skater on an attack, that likelihood almost triples."*
>
> The per-situation numbers sit in a Sportlogiq graphic rather than in the article's text, so the multiplier is quoted here in the words the source used and not as a precise figure.
>
> **The other half of the picture is how rare they are.** [Tulsky and colleagues](http://hockeyanalytics.com/Research_files/Using%20Zone%20Entry%20Data%20To%20Separate%20Offensive,%20Neutral,%20And%20Defensive%20Zone%20Performance.pdf) tracked which zone entries were odd-man rushes and found their overall impact *"negligible"* — they are *"relatively infrequent in today's NHL (representing less than 3% of all 5-on-5 zone entries)"* and the difference against an ordinary carry-in is *"significant but not overwhelming (0.78 shots versus 0.57)"*. 
>
> Note that the two figures measure different things: 8.15% is per shot, 0.78 is shots per zone entry. Put together they say what coaches say — **a shot from an odd-man situation is worth roughly three ordinary ones** — while adding the part coaches skip, which is that you will face only a handful of them a game. Both halves matter here. The danger is why the habits below are worth drilling; the rarity is why *preventing* an odd-man rush beats defending one.

## The Organising Idea: Stay on the Defensive Side of the Puck

```facts
Key: Be between the puck and your net — every attacker, all the time
Position: On the line between your opponent and your own goal, which is what "defensive side" or "goal side" means
Risk: An attacker between you and your net has already gone wrong; everything after that is damage control
Key: "Don't get beat wide" and "don't get beat to the middle" are the same instruction
Read: When a rush goes wrong and you are not sure why, somebody lost the defensive side of somebody
```

**Be between the puck and your net. Always. Every attacker, all the time.**

"Defensive side" — sometimes called **goal side** — means you are on the line between your opponent and your own goal. Anything that puts an attacker between you and your net has already gone wrong, and everything after that point is damage control.

This one idea generates most of the specific advice below:

- Gap control is just staying on the defensive side while *close*.
- Backchecking on the inside of your man is just re-establishing the defensive side.
- Not chasing the puck carrier — under the lane-and-man systems taught here — is just refusing to give up the defensive side on someone else.
- "Don't get beat wide" and "don't get beat to the middle" are both the same instruction.

When a rush goes wrong and you're not sure why, this is nearly always the answer: somebody lost the defensive side of somebody.

## Gap Control — The Full Method

**Your gap is the distance between you and the puck carrier.** It is the single most important variable in rush defence, and it is almost entirely under your control.

This section expands the summary in [Defender](../positions/defender.md) — it does not replace it, and nothing here contradicts it.

### Why a big gap hands the attacker three things at once

```facts
Key: A large gap looks safe and is not — it hands the attacker three things at once
Risk: Space to accelerate into, so they arrive faster than you while you are moving backwards
Risk: Time to lift their head, find the trailer and the far winger, and pick the best of three options
Risk: A clean shooting lane from distance — a shot through your shins is not a real chance
Action: Close the gap and you remove speed, vision and shooting simultaneously
Key: Gap control is treated as the highest-leverage habit in defensive hockey — coaching emphasis, not a counted ranking
```

A large gap looks safe. It is not: it is the root of most rush goals — coaching emphasis, not a counted share. A gap gives the attacker three things, all of them bad for you:

1. **Speed.** They can accelerate into the space between you. By the time they reach you they are moving faster than you, and you are moving backwards.
2. **Time to look.** With four metres of clear ice they can lift their head, find the trailer, find the far winger, and pick the best of three options. With one stick length they can only handle the puck. Both distances there are illustrative rather than measured thresholds.
3. **A shooting lane.** A shot from distance with a clean lane is a real chance. A shot from distance through your shins is not.

Close the gap and you take away all three at once. This is why gap control is treated as the highest-leverage habit in defensive hockey — coaching emphasis, not a counted ranking — and it is one adjustment that removes speed, vision and shooting simultaneously.

### Start your feet early — you cannot close a gap from a standstill

```facts
Never: Stand still and wait for the attacker — the gap opens at the moment you most need it closed
Action: Begin skating backwards while the puck is still being retrieved or regrouped in their end
Read: Read the pace of the attack, not the position of the puck — a slow regroup lets you skate up and squeeze
Action: Reset your gap on every regroup; each new attack is a new gap problem
Technique: C-cuts and backward crossovers, low stance, chest up, weight over the balls of the feet
Key: At full backwards speed you can hold any gap you like; at zero you can hold none at all
```

This is the most common defenceman error at every level, and the hardest to unlearn — coaching emphasis, not a counted ranking.

Standing still and waiting for the attacker feels like patience. It is the opposite. When they arrive you are at zero and they are at full speed, so the first thing you must do is accelerate backwards — and while you are accelerating, they are gaining. The gap opens *at the moment you most need it closed*.

Instead:

- **Begin skating backwards while the puck is still being retrieved or regrouped in their end.** Match their speed before they arrive rather than after.
- **Read the pace of the attack, not the position of the puck.** A slow regroup lets you skate up and squeeze. A quick outlet means you need to be moving already.
- **Reset your gap every time they regroup.** Neutral-zone attacks often turn back, swing, and come again. Each new attack is a new gap problem — skate up into them again rather than sitting where you finished the last one.
- **If you are already at full backwards speed when they arrive, you can hold any gap you like.** If you are at zero, you cannot hold any gap at all.

**Backward skating is the physical foundation.** C-cuts and backward crossovers to generate speed, low stance with chest up, weight over the balls of the feet. See [Skating](../technique/skating.md); [Defender](../positions/defender.md) also covers the essentials. You cannot play a gap you are not fast enough to hold.

### Target distances

**In short:** Keep the gap shrinking the whole way in; the stick-length distances are a guide, not a law.

```facts
Position: Roughly two to three stick lengths through the neutral zone in open ice, around a stick and a half as the carrier crosses the centre red line, and roughly a stick length at your own blue line
Key: The gap should be closing the whole way in; a gap that grows as they approach is a goal waiting to happen
Convention: Distances are a guide, not a law — they vary with your backward speed and the attacker's skill, and the published coaching pages disagree: one prints two stick lengths at the centre red line, while another prints a stick and a half for the neutral zone that line sits in
Key: The blue line is an ally — squeezing tight at the line forces the carrier to slow for their trailers or enter alone
```

Distances are a guide, not a law, and they vary with your backward speed and the attacker's skill. **Aim at the shape — closing, never growing — rather than at three measured numbers.** A gap that grows as they approach is a goal waiting to happen; one that shrinks forces them to decide earlier than they wanted to.

- **Through the neutral zone, in open ice:** roughly **two to three stick lengths**. Close enough to be a problem, far enough to react to a change of direction.
- **As they cross the centre red line:** tighten to around **a stick and a half**. This is the moment the attack commits, and the moment your gap should be actively shrinking rather than holding.
- **At your blue line:** roughly **a stick length**. Close enough to reach the puck with a poke check; far enough that one deke doesn't put them past you.

**How tightly you play that ladder is a coaching choice, not a law of hockey.** A team playing an aggressive 1-2-2 neutral-zone press wants you challenging at the line; a team playing a passive 1-3-1 trap wants you holding and steering the carrier wide. **Find out which one your team plays before you commit to a number** — §Standing Up at the Blue Line vs Backing Off sets out the same choice from the other end.

**The blue line is an ally here.** Under NHL rules an attacker is offside only when **both skates** completely cross the blue line before the puck completely crosses it — so a defender squeezing tight at the line can force the carrier to either slow down for their own trailers or enter alone. See [Rules Primer](../foundation/rules_primer.md) and [Zone Entries](zone_entries.md).

**Where the numbers come from.** They are coaching convention, and the published pages disagree. The only page that anchors a distance to the red **line** prints **two** stick lengths there; a user-submitted drill on the same site prints **a stick and a half** for the neutral **zone**. At your **blue line** one page prints one stick length and another two. None is a governing body and none shows its working, and **no page found prescribes a stick and a half at the red line itself** — the figure stays because it expresses a gap that is shrinking as the attack commits.

**The word *tight* varies too:** [Neutral Zone Systems](neutral_zone_systems.md) uses it for one to one and a half stick lengths, while one page calls two to three *tight*. [Puck Support and Spacing](../hockey-iq/puck_support_and_spacing.md) converts stick lengths to feet.

**One coaching book sets the gap against the forwards rather than the lines.** *The Hockey Coaching Bible* (Human Kinetics — the excerpt credits the volume's author, Joseph Bertagna, but its figure numbers place it in the defencemen's chapter, credited to **Jack Parker**) says *"[t]he blue line does not determine where gap control begins"* and that the gap *"should be established by how high the opposing forwards are"*, and prints no distance. **It also wants a defenceman to *"maintain the good gap from blue line to blue line"*, where the ladder above closes the whole way in** — so ask which your coach wants.

### Read the chest and hips, not the puck, the hands or the head

```facts
Read: The chest and hips cannot lie — the torso has to point where the body mass is actually going
Key: The puck, blade, hands, shoulders, head and eyes are all deception tools, and all of them can lie
Action: Fix your eyes on the numbers on the front of their jersey and use peripheral vision for the puck
Read: A dangle that doesn't move the hips is not a dangle — if the chest stays square they have gone nowhere
Never: Watch the puck — your feet follow your eyes, and your eyes have just been sent the wrong way
```

A skilled attacker will fake with everything they can move. The puck, the stick blade, the hands, the shoulders, the head, the eyes — all of these are **deception tools**, and all of them can lie.

Their **chest and hips cannot lie.** Their torso has to point where their body mass is actually going. Watch the middle of them, and use your peripheral vision for the puck.

Practically:

- **Fix your eyes on the numbers on the front of their jersey.** You will still see the puck; you just will not be led by it.
- **A dangle that doesn't move the hips is not a dangle.** If the puck goes wide but the chest stays square, they have not gone anywhere.
- **Watching the puck is how you get beaten clean** — your feet follow your eyes, and your eyes have just been sent the wrong way.

### Stick on the ice, out front, in the passing lane

```facts
Key: Your stick is a passive weapon — used well it removes options without committing your body
Position: Blade flat on the ice, out in front, in the lane between the carrier and their most dangerous support option
Technique: One hand on the stick for reach, two hands for control — but never with the blade in the air
Action: Poke check only when you can reach the puck, not as a hope
Never: Telegraph the poke check. Hide it until you use it instead, or they pull the puck back and go around your reach
Risk: A missed poke check becomes a lever the attacker skates around, and often a hooking or tripping penalty
```

Your stick is a passive weapon. Used well it removes options without committing your body.

- **Blade flat on the ice**, out in front of you, in the lane between the carrier and their most dangerous support option.
- **One hand on the stick** for reach when you need it, two hands when you need control — but never with the blade in the air. A stick off the ice defends nothing.
- **Poke check only when you can reach the puck**, not as a hope. A missed poke check turns your stick into a lever the attacker skates around, and often into a hooking or tripping penalty.
- **Hide the poke check until you use it.** Telegraphing it lets the carrier pull the puck back and go around your reach.

### Angle to take the middle and force them outside

**In short:** Sit slightly inside the carrier so their only route is the wall; the route is yours everywhere, the contact is not.

```facts
Key: The middle of the ice is the dangerous ice — shots from the slot beat goalies, shots from the wall mostly do not
Position: Boardside shoulder roughly on the middle of their chest, your body between them and the middle of the ice
Never: Line up chest-to-chest, or put your inside shoulder on their chest — that slides you wide and opens the middle
Action: Force them outside, then close the outside — in a checking league ride them further wide, but seeing you coming does not make a carrier fair game: boarding is not only about a defenceless opponent
Rule: The ride is a checking-league instruction — USA Hockey 604(a) bars body checking at 12U and below, in all Girls'/Women's and all non-check Adult classifications, and Hockey Canada 7.3(a) at U13 and below, female hockey and any Member-approved division; angling with your route stays legal there
Rule: USA Hockey's Competitive Contact list is a floor, not a ceiling — 604(a) lets a local governing body bar body checking in any classification, and 604(b) bars it any time a Competitive Contact team plays a Body Checking team, so check the game and not the age group
Rule: No checking division in CARHA adult leagues: 49(a) prices intentionally bodying, pushing, shoving or standing in front of someone to make contact, or not averting it, at a minor or an ejecting major (30(a)), mandatory on injury; into the boards a double minor (49(b)), two of three that eject (32(a))
Rule: In women's hockey take the ice first and hold your ground — Britain's WNIHL plays "non-checking"; IIHF 101.1 bars using the boards to push, pin or eliminate an opponent competing for the puck, and makes any step or glide into her at least a minor for an illegal hit
Key: The boards are your second defender — no ice one side, a shrinking angle, nowhere to cut back — but they take the carrier's ice, not the carrier, and on a wide sheet gap and stick beat steering
Rule: Steering a carrier to the wall with your route is legal, and three of the six books say so — Hockey Canada's Interpretation 1 to 7.3(a), only while neither bumps, pushes or shoves; USA Hockey's Glossary "Angling"; and PWHL 52.1. The NHL and IIHF print neither word, so there it rests on silence
Rule: Where you may not check, take the route, never the contact at its end — except in the PWHL: Rule 52.1 permits angling "by using their body" yet prices at least a minor for opposite-directional force, leaving your skating lane, an extended arm, shoulder or hip, or any step or glide into an opponent
Rule: Angling in the PWHL has an ejection over the minor — 52.3 lets the referee reach a major for body checking, 52.5 then makes the game misconduct automatic, and 52.4 lets them reach a match — mandatory under PWHL 21.1 for a deliberate attempt to injure or a deliberate injury
Rule: Riding them into the wall is a different act, and defenceless is not the whole test — USA Hockey 603 asks no such question, and NHL, IIHF and PWHL 41.1 and Hockey Canada 7.2 name the icing or offside race as boarding, where nobody is defenceless
Mindset: Good rush defence is choosing which thing to concede, not taking everything away
```

**The middle of the ice is the dangerous ice.** Shots from the slot beat goalies; shots from below the faceoff dots on the wall mostly do not.

So do not line up chest-to-chest with the carrier. Position yourself slightly to the **inside** — your **boardside shoulder** (the shoulder nearer the wall) roughly on the middle of their chest — so that your body sits between them and the middle of the ice, and the only route they can see is toward the boards. Getting this backwards and putting your *inside* shoulder on their chest slides your torso to the boards side and opens the middle, which is exactly the lane you are trying to take away.

![](diagram:rush-gap-and-angle)

- **The boards are your second defender.** A carrier angled onto the wall has no ice on one side, a shrinking angle to the net, and nowhere to cut back — **but the boards take away the carrier's ice, not the carrier.** Steer them there with your route. ⚠️ **Read that as permission to take a route, never to make contact at the end of it unless your game allows checking.**
- **Force them to the outside, then close the outside.** Steer them wide with your route — legal everywhere. **In a checking league**, once they are committed outside, ride them further from the net — ⚠️ **and a carrier who can see you coming is protected by the boarding rule exactly as one who cannot.** Where body checking is barred — younger age groups, women's hockey, non-check adult leagues — the route still works and the contact at the end of it does not. **Check the game, not the age group.**
- **Know how much the wall is worth on your sheet.** On wide ice a carrier forced outside still has room, so gap and stick do more of the work than steering. Widths vary more than people expect — the IIHF allows 26 m to 30 m, USA Hockey and Hockey Canada recommend 85 ft to 100 ft, the NHL fixes 85 ft — and [Rink Map](../foundation/rink_map.md) advises a British reader to assume a *smaller* rink until it is measured. Go and look at yours.
- **You are giving them something on purpose.** Good rush defence is not taking everything away; it is choosing which thing to concede.

**What it costs.** Steering a carrier to the wall with your route is legal, and three of the six books say so. Hockey Canada's Interpretation 1 to Rule 7.3(a) lets a player *"steer or direct an opposing player into the boards, without actually touching or body-checking them"*, so long as neither uses the body to *"intentionally body-check, bump, push, or shove an opponent"* — and a bump is a great deal less than riding someone along the wall. USA Hockey's Glossary calls **angling** *"[a] legal defensive skill"*, and the Note to **604(c)** puts it inside legal competitive contact. The NHL and IIHF print neither *angling* nor *steer*, so there the permission rests on the absence of a ban — an inference, not a rule you can quote.

**Where checking is barred.** ⚠️ **The ride is a checking-league instruction; the route is not.** **USA Hockey 604(a)** bars body checking *"in the 12 & under youth age classifications and below, all Girls'/Women's age classifications and all non-check Adult classifications"* — a minor under **604(c)**, a major plus a game misconduct under **604(d)** where it *"recklessly endangers an opponent"*, and plan on a match there, and a likely multi-game suspension (602(a)). **Hockey Canada 7.3(a)** gives a minor for intentional body contact *"in divisions of U13 and below, female hockey, and any other divisions approved by a Member of Hockey Canada"*. That list is a floor: under **604(a)** *"[a] local governing body may prohibit body checking in any classification"*, and **604(b)** bars it whenever a Competitive Contact team plays a Body Checking team.

**The angle itself stays legal.** USA Hockey's Note to **604(c)** makes closing the gap on an established angle legal competitive contact, and its Casebook allows contact at the end of the angle only where you do not use *"any overt hip, shoulder or forearm action"* and *"simply maintains their skating lane"* (Standard of Play, Situation 15). Hockey Canada's body-checking definition catches a player who *"leaves their skating lane to make contact"* or *"extends an arm, shoulder, or hip after angling an opponent."* **The route is yours everywhere; the contact that moves them off it is not.**

**The PWHL grants the contact as well as the route, but narrowly.** Rule 52.1 lets players *"angle their opponent by using their body"*, then prices *"[a]ny move by a player to step or glide into an opposing player"* at *"at least a Minor Penalty (2’)"* — which is exactly finishing an angle with your body — and the same where the carrier is trying to avoid contact and you move into her. A body-checking major there carries an automatic game misconduct (**52.5**), and **52.4** reaches a match penalty, mandatory under **21.1** for a deliberate attempt to injure. ⚠️ **Finish the angle with your body in the PWHL and you are risking your game, not two minutes.**

**CARHA**, with no checking division, leaves you only the route. **49(a)** prices intentional body contact at a minor or a major — the major **mandatory** where injury results, ending your game under **30(a)**. Contact into the boards is a **49(b)** double minor, two of the three penalties that eject you (**32(a)**).

**In women's hockey** — every level of it in Britain, where the WNIHL is played *"non-checking"* — **IIHF Rule 101.1 governs**. It bars using the boards *"to make contact with an opponent to eliminate her from the play, push her into the boards, or pin her along the boards"*, and any step or glide into her is at least a minor for an *"illegal hit"*.

**And where the ride is allowed, boarding still limits it** in all six books. ⚠️ **Defenceless is not the whole test:** USA Hockey 603 does not ask it, and the NHL, IIHF, PWHL and Hockey Canada name the race for an iced puck as boarding, where nobody is defenceless. ### Pivot late

```facts
Action: Delay the moment you turn and skate forwards with them as long as you possibly can
Risk: Once you pivot to forwards you can no longer react, and a change of direction beats you
Technique: Hips lead the pivot, not shoulders — turning your upper body first opens the gate early
Convention: Pivot to the boards side rather than the middle — a default, not a rule; if beaten, be beaten toward the wall
Read: Some coaches want a middle pivot when turning away would take your eyes off the puck, or when you have inside help
```

At some point you must turn and skate forwards with them. Delay that moment as long as you possibly can.

- Once you pivot to forwards, **you can no longer react** — you are committed to their current direction, and a change of direction beats you.
- **Hips lead the pivot, not shoulders.** Turning your upper body first opens the gate before your feet are ready.
- **Pivot to the boards side rather than the middle — as a default, not a rule.** If you are beaten, be beaten toward the wall. But some coaches want a middle pivot on specific reads, such as when turning away would take your eyes off the puck, or when you have inside help and the greater danger is the attacker cutting behind you. Find out which your coach wants, and be competent both ways: the correct direction is set by where the attacker is, not by which way you can pivot. See [Skating → Which way to pivot](../technique/skating.md#which-way-to-pivot--a-coaching-default-not-a-law).

## Backchecking — Lanes and Assignments

**Backchecking** is racing back toward your own end when your team loses the puck. It is the difference between an odd-man rush and an even one, and it is the most commonly under-performed job in hockey — coaching emphasis, not a counted one. (Do not confuse it with **forechecking**, which is pressure in *their* end.)

### The three lanes

**In short:** In the standard assignment the centre takes the middle and the wingers the outside; under any variant, somebody owns the middle.

```facts
Position: Three vertical lanes — two outside along the boards, one middle between the faceoff dots
Convention: The standard assignment is the centre in the middle lane and the wingers in the outside lanes
Key: Of the three lanes the middle is the most dangerous to leave unattended — the middle-lane driver is the attacker skating hard through it without the puck
Key: That the middle-lane driver is the assignment most often missed is coaching emphasis, not a counted ranking
Read: They are missed because they do not have the puck, and eyes go to the puck
Options: Other teams give the middle to the first man back, or pick up man-on-man from the puck outward — find out which yours uses
Key: Under every variant, somebody owns the middle and nobody assumes it is somebody else
```

Think of the ice as three vertical lanes running the length of the rink: the two outside lanes along the boards, and the middle lane between the faceoff dots.

The standard assignment, and the one assumed throughout this document:

- **The centre takes the middle lane** — and, crucially, **the middle-lane driver**: the attacker skating hard through the middle without the puck.
- **The two wingers take the outside lanes**, each picking up the attacker on their side.

![](diagram:rush-backcheck-lanes)

**Of the three lanes the middle is the most dangerous to leave unattended, and the middle-lane driver is the assignment most often missed — that ranking is coaching emphasis, not a counted one.** They arrive into the slot unmarked, at speed, with a clean look. They are missed because they do not have the puck, and eyes go to the puck. If you are the centre coming back, that player is yours before anything else. See [Centre](../positions/center.md) and [Winger](../positions/winger.md).

> **This is a coaching choice, not a law.** Assigning lanes by position, as above, is the version assumed here. Others assign by **first man back takes the middle**, regardless of who it is — often when the centre is the one caught deep, and often in rec hockey where lines are less structured. A third variant has the backcheckers pick up **man-on-man from the puck outward**, taking whoever is nearest. **No count of which of the three is commonest has been found**, so treat any ranking of them, this document's ordering included, as an impression rather than a measurement. Find out which one your team uses. See [Neutral Zone Systems](neutral_zone_systems.md).
>
> Under every variant, two things hold: somebody owns the middle, and nobody assumes it is somebody else.
>
> **And there is a layer above this one.** The three assignments here all come from the same family — forwards taking lanes while the defence takes the puck. Two of the four named team systems set out later in this section do not work that way at all: under the **wide-lane lock** a defenceman slides to mid-ice and owns the middle, and under **hound the puck** the first forward is on the carrier and both defencemen hold inside. So "which lane is mine?" is the right question only once you know which system you are in. Read the two together.

### Come back on the defensive side of your man

**In short:** Skate a route to your man's inside hip, goal side of them, and take the ice rather than their body.

```facts
Never: Chase from behind their shoulder — that is jogging, and you cannot defend anything from behind
Action: Take a route that puts you inside them, between them and your net, even if it is slightly longer
Position: Arrive at their inside hip, not their back pocket, so your stick reaches the passing lane
Action: Take the ice, not the body — get ahead of your man and hold your lane; veering into a man off the puck can be called interference anywhere, and where checking is barred an illegal check too
Rule: Never finish the chase into their back, puck or not — plan on it ending your game: no minor in the NHL, PWHL or IIHF (43.2), a game misconduct even at the floor in Hockey Canada 7.5(a) and CARHA 53(a); under USA Hockey, if reckless or meant to injure, a match and likely multi-game ban (602(a))
Rule: A hit from behind into the boards or goal frame on a player unable to protect themselves is a mandatory match — Hockey Canada 7.5(c) (on the head 7.6(c)/7.7(c)) and CARHA 53(b) (goal net); a hit meant to injure is a match in every book here but the IIHF
Rule: Your blade goes in the passing lane, not on your man — NHL and IIHF 56.1 and PWHL 57.1 grant body position to lengthen their path to the puck on three conditions: stick not utilized, free hand not used, and no otherwise illegal check delivered off that position
Rule: Hockey Canada 7.4(v) makes a body check to a blind side charging in every division — Interpretation 2: an open-ice check from outside a 90° view of a vulnerable opponent, "whether or not the player has the puck". 7.4(b) a major and game misconduct, mandatory on injury; 7.4(c) a match penalty
Rule: USA Hockey 625 makes impeding a puckless player — picks, lane or speed changes to play the body — an interference minor; the hit can be roughing (640) or a check from behind (608), a mandatory ejection if forceful at the boards; if reckless or meant to injure, a match, likely multi-game ban (602(a))
Options: If you are genuinely beaten and can only chase, chase the stick — blade under theirs or on the puck, never reaching around their body, which is a hook or a trip
Risk: Reaching around their body from behind is a hook or a trip, and near your own net that gets expensive
```

Chasing an attacker from behind their shoulder is not backchecking — it is jogging. You cannot defend anything from behind.

- **Take a route that puts you inside them**, between them and your net, even if that route is slightly longer.
- **Arrive at their inside hip**, not their back pocket, and **take the ice, not the body** — get to the spot ahead of them and hold the lane you picked rather than veering into them. From there you are goal side and your stick reaches the passing lane — ⚠️ **and the lane is what the stick is for.** Laid across a man, puck or no puck, it is a restraining foul.
- **And never let the route end in their back — take the ice, take the puck, or angle them off it.** ⚠️ **Checking from behind is its own rule in all six books, and none waits for the opponent to have the puck** — and a backchecker arrives from behind by definition. **Swinging wide to hit them from the side is no way round it either.**
- **If you are genuinely beaten and can only chase**, chase the *stick*, not the body: get your blade under theirs or on the puck and never around their body, because reaching around them from behind is a hook or a trip, and near your own net that gets expensive (see the breakaway section below).

**What it costs.** The NHL, IIHF and PWHL grant body position on a man without the puck **on three conditions**: your stick *"is not utilized"*, your free hand *"is not used"*, and you do not use it *"to deliver an otherwise illegal check."* **The third is the one that reaches a backchecker: body position is not a licence for the contact that follows.** A stick used to impede without body position is hooking, not interference (NHL and IIHF 56.1). ⚠️ **The PWHL narrows it further:** Rule 52.1 calls it an *"illegal hit"* *"when a player leaves their skating lane to make contact"*, and prices *"[a]ny move by a player to step or glide into an opposing player"* at *"at least a Minor Penalty (2’)"* — and a hit on a trailer carries no intention of playing the puck.

**Under USA Hockey, interference prices only the route, at two minutes; the hit is priced higher.** **625(a)(4)** is a minor for a defender who *"changes their skating lane or foot speed in an effort to play the body of an opponent who is no longer in control of the puck"*, and its Note reaches a man who never had it. The hit itself is roughing. **640(d)** is a minor for a body check *"with no effort to gain possession of the puck"* with the stick blade above the knees, and **640(b)** (outside Adult Male) a minor for *"an avoidable body check to an opponent who is no longer in control of the puck"*. **640(g)** is a mandatory major plus game misconduct where it *"recklessly endangers"* — plan on a match and a likely multi-game suspension (602(a)), though 640(h) says only that a match *"may also be assessed"*. Where checking is barred, **604(c) to (e)** run from a minor to a match and a likely multi-game suspension. ⚠️ **So under USA Hockey a hit on a puckless man is never only a two-minute risk.**

**Hockey Canada and CARHA write no stick proviso, and are not narrower for it.** **Hockey Canada 8.3** makes a late hit its own interference limb — one outside *"a maximum distance of an arm and stick length"* of the carrier *"at the instant the puck has been released"* — and 8.3(a)'s interference reaches a trailer who never had it. **CARHA 66(a)(1)** prices impeding a man not in possession at a minor *"or, at the discretion of the Referee, a Major penalty and a Game Misconduct penalty"* — **the backcheck that can end your game** in the adult rec book.

**And CARHA is not a checking book:** you may *"stand their ground"* or *"shadow"* (66, Note 2), but **49(a)** prices intentional body contact at a minor or a major — the major ending your game (**30(a)**) and mandatory on injury — and into the boards a **49(b)** double minor, two of the three penalties that eject you (**32(a)**). ⚠️ **No version of the contact is free in CARHA.**

**From behind, it can end your game whether they have the puck or not.** Hockey Canada 7.5(a) reaches a hit from behind *"anywhere on the ice"*, and no book prices one that stays off the head cheaply: the NHL, PWHL and IIHF write no minor (**43.2**), and Hockey Canada and CARHA pair theirs with a game misconduct. ⚠️ **Into the boards on a player who cannot protect themselves it is a mandatory match** under Hockey Canada (**7.5(c)**) and CARHA (**53(b)**). A hit meant to injure is a match in every book but the IIHF, and under USA Hockey so is a reckless one — either way, plan there on a likely multi-game suspension (**602(a)**). The full ladder is in [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md#checking-from-behind-and-what-the-onus-does-and-does-not-mean).

**From the side.** ⚠️ **Hockey Canada 7.4 makes a blind-side body check charging in every division** — an open-ice check *"delivered from an angle of approach outside a 90° peripheral point of view of a vulnerable opponent, whether or not the player has the puck"*. It is at least a minor, a major and game misconduct on violence and mandatory on injury (7.4(b)), and a match if the opponent is left *"unable to defend themselves"* (7.4(c)). The IIHF's legal body check reaches only an opponent *"who is in possession of the puck"*, from the front or side, and a hit outside their peripheral vision *"may be penalized accordingly by the Referee"*.

### Skate first, then look

```facts
Action: Turn and sprint first, and look on the third or fourth stride
Never: Turn your head to watch what happens on a turnover — it costs you two strides you never get back
Key: The picture will still be there when you look up, and you will be five metres further into the play — an illustrative figure, not a measured one
Mindset: Full-speed backchecking is a conditioning issue as much as a hockey one
```

The instinct on a turnover is to turn your head and watch what happens. That costs you two strides you never get back.

**Turn and sprint first. Look on the third or fourth stride.** The picture will still be there when you look up, and you will be five metres further into the play — an illustrative figure rather than a measured one. Full-speed backchecking is a conditioning issue as much as a hockey one — see [Conditioning and Recovery](../off-the-ice/conditioning_and_recovery.md).

### Do not decide mid-rush to chase the puck carrier when your D has them

```facts
Never: Decide mid-rush to sprint in and help a defenceman who is already engaged with the carrier and playing a good gap
Risk: Two defenders on one attacker, and your actual assignment left unmarked
Action: Skate past the puck battle to the unmarked man — your job is the player nobody has
Mindset: Trusting your defenceman to win their own 1-on-1 is what turns a 3-on-2 into three 1-on-1s
Options: Some teams run the opposite by design — the first forward back pressures the carrier and the defenceman holds inside instead
Never: Turn that pressure into a hit from behind — where pressuring the carrier is your system it is your feet and your stick, and nothing else
Key: Under either system somebody has the carrier and somebody has the unmarked man, and it is agreed before the rush rather than during it
```

If a defenceman is already engaged with the carrier and playing a good gap, sprinting in to help them is worse than useless — it leaves your actual assignment unmarked and puts two defenders on one attacker.

**Your job as a backchecker is the player nobody has.** Skate past the puck battle to the unmarked man. Trusting your defenceman to win their own 1-on-1 is a skill, and it is what turns a 3-on-2 into three 1-on-1s.

> **This is a coaching choice, not a law — and the choice is about *who decides*.** The instruction above belongs to the family of systems where the backcheckers take lanes and men, and the defence takes the puck. **Other teams coach the reverse on purpose:** the first forward back **pressures the puck carrier** all the way in, and the defenceman adjusts to hold inside position and support rather than engaging. That is a legitimate system with its own logic — relentless pressure on the puck, and a defenceman who is never beaten wide because they never have to commit. **What it never means is finishing into the back of a player skating at their own end boards — see the subsection below.**
>
> What is wrong under *both* systems is **improvising**. The failure this section is really about is a forward deciding in the middle of a rush to abandon their man and double the puck, because that is the version nobody else on the ice is expecting. **Find out which your team plays, and if your team has no answer, that is the thing to raise** — teams that leave it to players to read in the moment get two players on the carrier, or none.

### The four named backchecking systems

**In short:** Your team's backchecking system decides your job on every rush, so ask which one it runs and where the hand-off happens.

```facts
Key: Backchecking is a team system with named variants, not only effort — which one you play changes your job on every rush
Options: Defence early strike — the defencemen tighten up early instead of sinking, and the wide one strikes across to take away the time the opposition expected on the way out
Options: Wide-lane lock — the first forward back takes an outside lane, owns everything between the dots and the boards on that side, and stays deeper than their man
Options: Midlane backcheck — the forwards all come back through the middle so the defencemen can play the outside, and the first one back covers inside for them
Options: Hound the puck — the first forward back chases the carrier and keeps pressuring after a pass, while the defencemen hold inside and protect the space between the dots
Convention: Midlane and hound the puck are opposites — one covers your defence, the other pressures the puck — and which one you run follows your coach's appetite rather than the situation in front of you
Action: Ask which one your team plays, and ask what happens when the forward cannot catch the carrier — that hand-off is where the confusion lives
```

Everything above is about *your* job on a backcheck. This is the layer above it: the shape your whole team is running, which decides what your job actually is. **Coaching material names four, and they are genuinely different — not degrees of effort.** Whether these four are all of them, or which is commonest, is not something any published count establishes.

**Defence early strike.** This one starts before the rush does, in your own end. Rather than sinking deep in defensive-zone coverage, the two defencemen **tighten up early** and may step up to close the ice around the opposition forwards — the ice, not the man. Bumping a forward there can be called interference wherever you play, and where checking is barred an illegal check as well. When the other team breaks out, the wide defenceman **strikes across** the ice at the puck carrier, and as they go, the strong-side partner shifts inside to cover. The point is surprise: it takes away the time and space the opposition expected to have as they left their zone.

**Wide-lane lock.** The first forward back immediately takes an **outside lane** and stays there. They may skate forwards or backwards, but must always be able to see both the puck and any opponent in that lane, and they own **anybody between the dots and the boards on their side**. One defenceman slides to mid-ice and takes that lane; the other takes the strong side where the carrier is. The ice ends up divided into three, each with an owner, and **three players are standing up the blue line** while the remaining two forwards trail the play and pressure it. If this is your system, your job as the locking forward is to deny the pass to your side and stay *deeper* than your man.

**Midlane backcheck.** The opposite instinct: the high forward gets to **mid-ice** and comes back hard through the centre seam, and so does everybody else. All three forwards return through the middle, which frees the defencemen to play the outside. The first forward back **stays between the puck and the net** — so if a defenceman is beaten inside, there is already somebody there to cover it. It also squeezes the carrier: with no space inside, the only lane left to them is wide. Come all the way to the low slot before moving out to support; the second and third forwards back look for late attackers and stop at the top of the circles.

**Hound the puck.** Explicitly the opposite of the midlane backcheck. The first forward back **chases the puck carrier as hard as they can**, and keeps pressuring the puck even after a pass. The defencemen hold **inside position**, protecting the space between the dots. Coaches who like it tend to want pressure everywhere on the ice.

**What each one costs you.** The lock and the midlane backcheck both put bodies between the puck and your net, and both are forgiving of a mistake by somebody else — the price is that you concede the puck carrier time. Hounding the puck denies that time and keeps the opposition under pressure the length of the neutral zone, and the inside defencemen kill the mid-ice pass. But it has a failure the others do not: **when the forward cannot catch the carrier by the blue line, or cannot get to the pass in time, the forward and the defencemen can end up unsure who has whom.**

That failure is the reason to ask the follow-up question. Teams that run it well carry a hand-off rule so nobody is guessing — one such rule is that **the forward hounds the puck as far as the red line, and if they are not level with the carrier by then, the defence takes over and the forward peels off to a lane or the wide attacker.** If your team pressures the puck and has never said where the hand-off happens, that is worth raising at practice rather than discovering at speed.

#### What hounding never means

```facts
Never: Finish into the back of a carrier heading for their own end boards. Take the puck, take the ice, or angle them off it instead, whichever book you are under
Rule: A forceful hit into a player's back at the wall that stays off the head ends your game in five of six books, and under the IIHF plan on it — NHL, PWHL and IIHF write no minor (43.2); meant to injure, a match in all but the IIHF; in USA Hockey reckless too, either way a likely multi-game ban (602(a))
Rule: From behind into the boards or goal frame on a player unable to protect themselves is a mandatory match — Hockey Canada 7.5(c), CARHA 53(b) (goal net) — and a match bars you from "any further games" until your case is dealt with (Hockey Canada 4.10(a); CARHA 34(a) in CARHA-affiliated adult leagues)
Rule: USA Hockey 604(a) bans body checking at 12 & under and below, in all Girls'/Women's and non-check Adult classifications; Hockey Canada 7.3(a) bans INTENTIONAL checks, bumps, shoves and pushes at U13 and below, in female hockey and in any Member-approved division — incidental contact is not a penalty
Rule: Hockey Canada does not cap its body-checking ban at a minor where Rule 7.3 applies — U13 and below, female hockey, and any Member-approved division. Rule 7.3(b) lets the referee assess a major plus a game misconduct on the degree of violence, and makes both mandatory if the check injures
Rule: Hockey Canada’s body-checking ladder does not stop at the major — where Rule 7.3 applies (U13 and below, female hockey, any Member-approved division), 7.3(e) makes the Game Misconduct automatic on any body-checking major, and 7.3(c) a Match for a deliberate attempt to injure or a deliberate injury
Rule: CARHA adult leagues, no checking: 49(a) prices intentional bodying, pushing, shoving at a minor or major, mandatory on injury; that major ejects (30(a)); Casebook a match if warranted; into the boards a double minor, two of three that eject (49(b), 32(a))
Rule: In the WNIHL, play it as no body checking — its rules of competition say "non-checking"; elsewhere IIHF 101.1 allows "bodychecking" only with clear intent to play the puck; hold ground you reached first, never use the boards to push or pin her, and any "step or glide into" her is at least a minor
Rule: In Britain the answer is in the competition regulations, not the IIHF book — IHUK’s 2026-27 Rules of Competition set U10 and U12 non-checking, U14, U16, U19 and adult NIHL "Full ice, checking", and WNIHL non-checking; the U10 book says "Bodychecking is NOT allowed in U10 matches"
Never: Turn hounding into contact where you may not body check at all — it is your feet, your stick and your angle, and nothing else
```

**What hounding never means.** You are arriving at speed behind a player skating toward their own end boards — or across the front of their own net, which the checking-from-behind rules name in the same words — the geometry those rules exist for. ⚠️ **Take the puck, take the ice, or angle them off it — never finish into their back.** For a forceful hit into a player's back at the wall that stays off their head, five of six books end your game, and under the IIHF, plan on it. ⚠️ **Into the boards on a player who cannot protect themselves it is a mandatory match** under Hockey Canada (7.5(c)) and CARHA (53(b)), and a match keeps you out of later games until your case is dealt with (Hockey Canada 4.10(a), CARHA 34(a), CARHA-affiliated adult leagues only). A hit meant to injure is a match in every book but the IIHF, and under USA Hockey so is a reckless one — either way, plan there on a likely multi-game ban (602(a)), and any game misconduct also costs your team's next game, and a second major in a game at least two (404(b), 403(b)). Every tier is in [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md#checking-from-behind-and-what-the-onus-does-and-does-not-mean).

**And check whether you may body check at all — under the book you actually play.** ⚠️ **In Britain the IIHF book does not answer this — IHUK’s Rules of Competition do.** For 2026-27: **U10 and U12 are non-checking; U14, U16 and U19 are *"Full ice, checking, stop clock"***, the U10 book adding *"Bodychecking is NOT allowed in U10 matches."* The men’s **NIHL** is *"Full ice, checking, stop clock"*; the women’s **WNIHL** is *"Full ice, non-checking, stop clock"* at both its levels — a label the regulations do not define, so read it as at least as tight as IIHF 101.1. ⚠️ **These cover IHUK’s junior leagues, the NIHL and the WNIHL; the Elite League publishes its own rules.**

**Hockey Canada 7.3** bars checking at U13 and below, in female hockey and in any Member-approved division: a minor, rising to a major plus game misconduct on violence (7.3(b)) — ⚠️ **mandatory if the check injures** — with the game misconduct automatic on any such major (**7.3(e)**) and a match for a deliberate attempt to injure (**7.3(c)**). **USA Hockey 604(a)** bars it in *"the 12 & under youth age classifications and below, all Girls'/Women's age classifications and all non-check Adult classifications"*. There it is a minor under **604(c)**, and a mandatory major and game misconduct under **604(d)** where it recklessly endangers. For that same check **604(e)** gives a match — the one to plan on, because **602(a)** says it *"shall be assessed"*. A multi-game suspension is likely too. There hounding is your stick, your feet and your angle; chasing hard is about **skating**, not contact.

**In CARHA** ⚠️ **there is no checking division at all**: **49(a)** gives *"[a] Minor or Major penalty, at the discretion of the Referee"* for intentional body contact, the major mandatory on injury, and its Casebook adds a match *"if warranted"* (Rule 49, Situation 1). A check into the boards is a **49(b)** double minor — two of the three penalties that eject you (**32(a)**) — with a major on injury and a match if serious. Either major ends your game (**30(a)**), and a major's game misconduct costs at least your next game, possibly more, unless it was for an accidental high stick (**32(d)**, CARHA-affiliated adult leagues only).

**In women's hockey — every level of it in Britain — the rule text is IIHF Rule 101.1, and the WNIHL's *"non-checking"* label sits on top, so do not plan a hit there at all.** Elsewhere 101.1 allows body checking only *"when there is a clear intention of playing the puck"*, lets players *"'hold their ground' any time that they have established their position on the ice"*, and prices *"[a]ny move by a Player to step or glide into an opposing Player"* at *"at least a minor penalty (2') for an 'illegal hit'."* So hounding is legal skating; a step or glide into the carrier is not, and never use the boards to push, pin or lean her out of the play.

## The Numerical Situations

The number always reads **attackers on defenders** — a 2-on-1 is two attackers against one defender.

### 1-on-1

**In short:** Steer the carrier to a bad angle and let them run out of ice; position first, contact only where your game allows checking.

```facts
Goal: A shot from a place your goalie is comfortable with — not a hit and not a turnover
Action: Steer them to a bad angle with your route, and keep steering them wide
Position: Stick in the lane, on the ice, out front — passing options are arriving behind the play
Never: Lunge — it is a single bet on their next move, and if you are wrong they have the whole net
Action: In a checking league ride them wide along the wall until their options collapse — but a carrier who can see you coming is no less protected, because defenceless is not the whole boarding test
Rule: Boarding is not only about a defenceless opponent — NHL, IIHF and PWHL 41.1 and Hockey Canada 7.2 write it that way, but USA Hockey 603 asks no such question, and those four name the obvious icing or offside race in terms, USA Hockey the icing half at 624(d)
Rule: No book caps boarding at a minor; it can eject you — Hockey Canada 7.2(b) major and game misconduct, a must if hurt; USA Hockey 603(b) both if reckless; reckless or meant to injure, a match, likely multi-game ban (602(a)); IIHF 41.4 can reach both, nobody hurt; CARHA's floor: a 49(b) double minor
Rule: IIHF women's Rule 101.1 prohibits using the boards to eliminate an opponent from the play, push her into the boards, or pin her along the boards — pin the puck, not the player
Rule: An illegal hit in women's hockey is a minor, or a major plus an automatic game misconduct (IIHF Rule 101.1) — and in British women's hockey any major ejects you at every age, adult included: In-House Rule 101 applies "in all cases" and states no age scope of its own
Never: Ride an opponent into the boards in any non-check adult or youth league, or finish a steer on a carrier's numbers anywhere, which is checking from behind. Pin the puck instead, not the player
Priority: Position first, contact second — a body check is a bonus, not a plan
```

You against the carrier. This is gap control applied, and it is the situation everything else is built from.

- **Steer them to a bad angle.** Your target is not a hit and not a turnover — it is a shot from a place your goalie is comfortable with. Keep steering them wide with your route.
- **Stick in the lane, on the ice, out front.** Even in a 1-on-1 there are passing options arriving behind the play.
- **Do not lunge.** A lunge is a single bet on their next move. If you are wrong, you are out of the play entirely and they have the whole net. Contain, keep your feet, and let them run out of ice.
- **Use the boards as a second defender.** **In a checking league**, ride them wide along the wall and their options collapse — no cutback, no shooting angle, no pass across — **but a carrier seeing you coming does not lift the boarding rule on that ride.** **In women's hockey and any league without body checking, the wall still does the work, but your route closes it, not your body:** get to the ice first, and never use the boards to push, pin or lean on the carrier. ⚠️ **Never finish a steer on their numbers** — steering turns a carrier round, and a hit on their back is a check from behind. **Pin the puck, not the player.**
- **A body check is a bonus, not a plan.** Missing a hit on a rush is far more costly than not attempting one. Position first, contact second. See [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md).

**What it costs.** ⚠️ **Sending an opponent into the boards is boarding in every book.** NHL, IIHF and PWHL **41.1**, and Hockey Canada **7.2(a)**, penalise a player *"who checks or pushes a defenseless opponent in such a manner that causes the opponent to hit or impact the boards violently or dangerously"*. **But *defenceless* is not the whole test.** **USA Hockey 603** never asks it — boarding is *"the action where a player pushes, **trips** or body checks an opponent causing them to go dangerously into the boards"*, including a check *"delivered for the purpose of punishment or intimidation"*, which reaches an opponent who watched you all the way in. And the NHL, IIHF, PWHL (41.1) and Hockey Canada (7.2) name the race for an iced or offside puck as boarding, where nobody is defenceless; USA Hockey reaches the icing case through **624(d)**.

**In a checking league, ride them wide, then play the puck — the ride is yours, the finish into the wall is not.** **The onus is on you as the rider.** No book caps boarding at a minor and it can end your game: ⚠️ **Hockey Canada makes the major and game misconduct mandatory if anyone is hurt** (7.2(b)), USA Hockey makes them mandatory for a reckless board, with a match and a likely multi-game suspension to plan on (603(b), 602(a)), and the IIHF reaches them with nobody hurt (41.4). A board meant to injure is a match in every book but the IIHF (NHL and PWHL 21.1, Hockey Canada 7.2(c), USA Hockey 602(a), CARHA 48(a)). In a CARHA-affiliated adult league the floor is a double minor, two of the three penalties that eject you (49(b), 32(a)). The full ladder is in [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md#boarding-and-the-player-who-cannot-see-you-coming).

**A check finished on a player's numbers is a check from behind**, and at the wall that can end your game in every book — see [Checking from behind](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md#checking-from-behind-and-what-the-onus-does-and-does-not-mean).

**In women's hockey — every level in Britain — the ride does not apply.** IIHF 101.1 bars using the boards *"to make contact with an opponent to eliminate her from the play, push her into the boards, or pin her along the boards"* — a minor, or *"a major penalty (5') and automatic game misconduct penalty"*, ⚠️ **so the major is itself an ejection at every level, adult included.** Under the British In-House Rules, **Rule 101** ejects any player assessed a major *"in all cases"* with no age scope, and Rule 100.1's Adult definition is not an exemption — [UK and England Rules](../foundation/uk_rules.md) sets out that reading and what has not been established about how it is called. **Pin the puck, not the player**, in any non-check league.

### 1-on-2

```facts
Never: Both defenders converge on the carrier — that opens the ice behind you for a late second attacker
Action: One defender takes the puck, normally the one on the carrier's side, and plays it as a 1-on-1 with a very tight gap
Position: The other stays back and inside, covering the trailer who has not arrived yet
Key: The engaged defender can afford to be aggressive with the gap — a miss leaves your partner in a 1-on-1 rather than nothing
Rule: Cover behind you is no defence to charging — USA Hockey Casebook 607, Situation 3 calls accelerating through a check charging even without the two fast strides, and Hockey Canada 7.4(ii) needs only two
Action: Call it — "I've got them" from the engaging defender, so the other holds
```

One attacker, two defenders. The luxury case — and one that teams waste constantly.

The failure mode is both defenders converging on the carrier. That opens the ice behind you for a late-arriving second attacker and turns a comfortable situation into a scramble.

- **One defender takes the puck** — normally the one on the carrier's side — and plays it exactly as a 1-on-1, with a very tight gap because there is backup.
- **The other stays back and inside**, watching for the second wave. You are not a spectator; you are covering the trailer who has not arrived yet.
- **The engaged defender can afford to be aggressive.** This is the one rush situation where stepping up hard is genuinely low risk *tactically*, because a miss leaves your partner in a 1-on-1 rather than nothing. ⚠️ **What is low risk is the gap, not the contact.** Having cover behind you is no defence to charging — USA Hockey calls a check you accelerate through charging even without the two fast strides, and under Hockey Canada two strides are already the offence. See [Standing up (stepping up)](#standing-up-stepping-up).
- **Call it.** "I've got them" from the engaging defender, so the other holds.

### 2-on-1 — the headline situation

**In short:** This guide's default is to take away the pass and leave the shot to your goalie; agree it with them first.

```facts
Convention: Take away the pass and give up the shot — your goalie can save the shot and cannot save the pass; the realistic alternative is a timed slide to take the pass away outright and force the shot, so find out which your team plays
Read: A shooter walking into the slot unpressured with no realistic pass available is no longer a 2-on-1, so close on the puck
Action: Say the contract out loud — you own the pass, the goalie owns the shooter, and neither of you covers both
Position: Sit in the passing lane, roughly on the line between the two attackers, biased slightly toward the middle
Technique: Stick flat on the ice on the passing side, blade extended into the lane, arm long — your stick takes the pass, not your body
Action: Take away the far attacker's stick near the net, where the pass becomes a tap-in — lift or press it low on the shaft, away from the hands, as the pass arrives, then let go
Never: Slide before the pass is released while you are playing the lane — you remove yourself from the play and open both options at once
Never: Try to block the shot — you abandon the pass, and a half-block deflects a puck your goalie was tracking
```

Two attackers, one defender — the situation every defenceman is judged on. **There is more than one legitimate way to play it.** The one taught here is below; the alternative is at the end of this section, and which you play is your team's decision, not yours mid-rush.

> ### **TAKE AWAY THE PASS. GIVE UP THE SHOT** — one of two legitimate systems, not a law; the other is a timed slide across the lane, so find out which your team plays

**Why.** Your goalie can save the shot and cannot save the pass.

- On the **shot**, the goalie can see the puck the whole way, is square to the shooter, and is set. That is a save they are trained to make and expect to make — even a good shot from the wing.
- On the **cross-ice pass**, the goalie must push all the way across the crease while the puck travels faster than they can move. They arrive late, off balance, and often still sliding when the shot comes. A backdoor tap-in into an open half of the net is essentially unsaveable.

So the arithmetic is not "which is the better chance for them" — it is "which chance can my goalie actually stop." One of them, and only one.

![](diagram:rush-2-on-1)

**The contract between goalie and defenceman — say it out loud.** This is a two-person agreement, and it only works if both people know it:

- **The defenceman owns the pass.** You take the lane and never leave it.
- **The goalie owns the shooter.** They play the puck carrier as if the second attacker does not exist — square to the shot, correct depth, set and stopped before release.
- Neither of you covers both. If the goalie also cheats toward the pass, the shooter gets an open net; if you also drift toward the shooter, the pass is open. **Both of you hedging is how a 2-on-1 becomes a goal.**

Talk about this with your goalie before the game, not during it. Most goalies want it exactly as described, but a few — especially at rec level, and especially against a weak shooter — will ask you to pressure the puck instead. **That split is an impression rather than a measurement**, which is why asking is the instruction here rather than playing the majority. See [Goaltender](../positions/goaltender.md).

**Your positioning, concretely:**

1. **Sit in the passing lane**, roughly on the line between the two attackers, biased slightly toward the middle of the ice.
2. **Stick flat on the ice on the passing side**, blade extended into the lane, arm long. Your stick, not your body, is what takes the pass away — that way your feet stay free.
3. **Match the retreat speed of the attack** and stay on the defensive side of both of them. Do not backpedal so fast that you end up behind your own goal line, and do not stall so the shooter walks in on top of your goalie.
4. **Take away the far attacker's stick as you get close to the net.** Late in the rush the danger becomes a tap-in, so as the pass arrives, lift or press their stick low on the shaft, well below their bottom hand, then let go. ⚠️ **Play it low and let go — never hold, hook or chop their stick, puck or no puck.** Holding a stick is a penalty in every book here, knocking it out of their hands is interference, and a forceful chop is slashing in the NHL, IIHF, PWHL and USA Hockey books. Stick-on-stick contact is excused from hooking in the NHL, IIHF and PWHL, but the line is the hands. The IIHF calls a stick against or near the hands hooking. Hockey Canada allows a lift to play the puck but calls it hooking once it catches the hands and impedes, and USA Hockey calls a press high on the stick, or one that simply impedes, slashing, holding or hooking.
5. **Do not slide. Not until the pass is released.** A slide is a one-time commitment: if the pass has not gone, you have taken yourself out and both options are open. If the pass *is* released and you can genuinely intercept it, sliding into the lane is correct. This belongs to the lane-holding method; the section below covers the system in which an early slide is the plan.
6. **Do not try to block the shot.** Blocking the shot means abandoning the pass, and worse, a half-block deflects a puck your goalie was tracking. See the shot-blocking section in [Defender](../positions/defender.md).

**The one honest caveat:** if the shooter is walking into the slot completely unpressured with no realistic pass available — for example the second attacker has been picked up by a backchecker — the situation is no longer a 2-on-1 and you should close on the puck. Read what is actually in front of you rather than the label.

#### The realistic alternative — the timed slide

```facts
Convention: Some teams play the opposite commitment — a timed slide across the passing lane to remove the pass outright and force the shot
Key: What does not change is the contract — the goalie still owns the shooter and you still own the pass; what changes is how you take the pass away
Risk: Going early or sliding past the lane leaves you on the ice and out of the play, and you cannot contest the rebound from there
Never: Go down with your head in the shot lane — this system exists to force a shot, so a shot is what you are going down in front of
Action: Ask the coach who asks you to slide exactly how they want you to go down — no method for going to the ice on a pass lane is set out here, and that part is what hurts you
Rule: A body placed across an attacker's path that causes them to trip and fall is a minor under NHL Rule 57.2; under the IIHF book the referee may assess a major (57.3), or a major plus a game misconduct (57.4), for reckless endangerment
Rule: In British women's hockey the IIHF's 57.3 major ejects you as well, at every age and adult included — In-House Rule 101 gives any major an additional game misconduct "in all cases" and states no age scope of its own
Risk: A late or wild slide is the one that trips somebody — but the timing is not the whole of the exposure
Rule: In USA Hockey, leaving your feet and sliding someone down is tripping "regardless of who gains possession of the puck" — except knees dropped to block a shot (Casebook 639, Sit. 4); a reckless one, a mandatory major and game misconduct (639(b)) — plan on a match and a likely multi-game ban (602(a))
Rule: A slide catching an opponent low reads as clipping, not tripping — NHL, IIHF, PWHL 44.1, "throwing the body across or below the knees of an opponent from any direction", though none names it; Hockey Canada 8.7 draws it at the hips; NHL and PWHL 44.3 mandate a major on injury, 44.5 a game misconduct
Rule: IIHF clipping needs no injury — 44.3 reaches a major plus an automatic game misconduct, at the referee's discretion, where the clip "recklessly endangers" an opponent and a minor would not sanction it sufficiently; on the NHL side 21.1 makes a match mandatory for a clip meant to injure
Rule: Never finish a low hit into the back — it is checking from behind, not clipping: IIHF 43.1 "in any manner", no minor (43.2); Hockey Canada 7.5(a), CARHA 53(a) game misconduct at the floor; USA Hockey: reckless or meant to injure, a match, likely multi-game ban (602(a)). NHL, PWHL: vaguer, no laxer
Action: Ask which of the two your team plays, and settle it with your goalie before the game rather than during it
```

Some coaches teach the opposite commitment: instead of holding the lane and living with the shot, the defender **times a slide across the passing lane** to remove the pass outright and force the carrier to shoot. **This is a different system, not a mistake.** Its trade is explicit — the pass is gone, but you are on the ice and out of the play if you go early or slide past the lane, and you cannot contest the rebound afterwards.

Note what does *not* change between the two: **the goalie still owns the shooter, and you still own the pass.** What changes is how you take the pass away and what it costs you if you are wrong.

**One thing holds whichever system endorses it: your head stays up and out of the shot lane.** **Do not carry the going-to-the-ice method from [Defender](../positions/defender.md) into this** — that is for dropping in front of a *shot*, and a slide across a *passing* lane is a different action on a different axis. **This guide does not tell you how to go down.** The coaching source this alternative comes from describes a flat slide with the feet toward your own net and names its failure modes — too early, past the lane, unable to contest the rebound — but that describes the play, not a technique for surviving it. ⚠️ **How you go down is the part that hurts you**, so ask the coach who asks you to do it.

**So ask, and ask early.** Which of the two your team plays is a coaching decision, and the worst outcome is not picking the less fashionable one — it is you and your goalie having different answers. Both of you hedging in different directions is how a 2-on-1 becomes a goal, and that is true whichever system you are in.

**What it costs.** **A body on the ice across an attacker's path is a trip if they go over you.** **NHL 57.2** makes it a minor — *"A minor penalty shall be imposed on any player who shall place his stick or any portion of his body in such a manner that it shall cause his opponent to trip and fall"*. **IIHF 57.3** allows a major where a player *"recklessly endangers the fouled Player"*, and **57.4** a major plus a game misconduct. ⚠️ **And timing is not the whole exposure.** USA Hockey's Casebook (639, Situation 4) makes a slide that puts *an opponent* down tripping *"regardless of who gains possession of the puck"*, its only exception a player who drops to their knees to block a shot.

**A slide that catches them low is clipping under the NHL, IIHF and PWHL** — 44.1, *"the act of throwing the body across or below the knees of an opponent from any direction"*. *(That those words reach a slide across a passing lane is this guide's reading; none of these three books names the play.)* **NHL and PWHL 44.3** make the major mandatory on injury and 44.5 the game misconduct with it; 44.4 allows a match, which **21.1** makes mandatory for a deliberate attempt to injure, in the PWHL too. **IIHF 44.3 needs no injury:** a major plus an **automatic** game misconduct, at the referee's discretion, where the clip *"recklessly endangers"* the opponent and a minor would not sanction it enough.

**USA Hockey** ⚠️ **makes the major mandatory with nobody hurt and no purpose shown.** **639(b)**: *"A major penalty plus a game misconduct penalty **shall be assessed** to any player who recklessly endangers an opponent as a result of tripping, clipping or leg checking"* — so whichever name the referee uses, that tier applies, and **639(c)** adds that a match *"may also be assessed"*. Plan on the match and a likely multi-game suspension, because **602(a)** says a match *"shall be assessed"* for reckless endangerment.

**Hockey Canada 8.7** draws clipping at the **hips** — *"where a player uses their body to make contact below an opponent's hips"* — the widest line here, with a major on the degree of violence, mandatory where it injures (8.7(b)). The **PWHL** prints the NHL's clipping rule word for word (44.1 to 44.5). **CARHA** ⚠️ **writes no clipping rule, but the act is not free:** **86(a)** reaches a player who *"falls or slides along the ice directly in the path of the puck, causing the puck carrier to trip"*, and **56(a)** a foul *"in any manner with their elbow or knee"*. Each is a minor or, at the referee's discretion, a major and game misconduct, with **56(b)** a major where it injures, which ends your game under **30(a)** (CARHA-affiliated adult leagues only). **A legal, well-timed slide is routine; a late or wild one is not a free mistake, and a low one is a different offence with a higher ceiling.**

**Never finish a low hit into someone's back.** ⚠️ **A hit from behind is priced as checking from behind, not clipping, and that rule starts higher** — under the IIHF (43.1 reaches one *"in any manner"*, with no minor) plan on a major and game misconduct, while Hockey Canada (7.5(a), *"anywhere on the ice"*) and CARHA (53(a)) add a game misconduct even at the floor. One meant to injure is a match in every book but the IIHF, and under USA Hockey so is a reckless one — either way, plan there on a likely multi-game suspension (602(a)). That Hockey Canada's wording reaches a low hit is this guide's reading, not a rule naming clipping. The NHL and PWHL write no such catch-all, and their silence is not permission. The full ladder is in [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md#checking-from-behind-and-what-the-onus-does-and-does-not-mean).

### 2-on-2

```facts
Convention: Play it man-on-man — each defender takes an attacker and holds a tight gap on them
Options: Zone is the realistic alternative — strong-side D takes whoever carries into their half, weak-side D holds the middle
Read: Zone survives a crossing rush better, man-on-man is tighter on a straight one — find out which your team wants
Never: Both drift toward the puck — a simple pass then leaves their partner the net
Action: Follow your man through a crossing or call a clean "switch" — never do half of each
Key: The weak-side defender is not free; being ready to help is not the same as leaving your man
```

Two attackers, two defenders. Even numbers, and it should be routine — but it is the situation most often botched, because "even" makes people relax. That ranking is coaching emphasis, not a counted one.

![](diagram:rush-2-on-2)

- **Play it man-on-man.** Each defender takes an attacker and holds a tight gap on them. This is the version assumed here, and the one the alternative below is described against. The realistic alternative is **zone**: the strong-side D takes whoever carries the puck into their half of the ice and the weak-side D holds the middle, so the two of you trade marks rather than following them across. Zone survives a crossing rush better; man-on-man is tighter on a straight one. Find out which your team wants, because mixing them on the same rush leaves an attacker free.
- **Both defenders drifting to the puck is how an even rush becomes a goal.** The puck is magnetic: two of you converge on the carrier, they make a simple pass, and their partner has the net. If you are the defender on the non-puck attacker, your job is to *stay with them* and let your partner do theirs.
- **Communicate the switch if they cross.** Attackers criss-cross specifically to make two defenders trade assignments badly. Either follow your man through the crossing or call a clean switch — "switch!" — but do not do half of each.
- **The weak-side defender is not free.** You are marking a person. Being ready to help is not the same as leaving your man.

### 3-on-2

**In short:** Agree in advance which system you play, then retreat level with your partner with the middle sealed between you.

```facts
Convention: The default assumed here is "two wide, middle to the backchecker" — a defenceman on each outside attacker; how many teams run it has never been counted
Never: Chase the puck carrier under the two-wide default — a wide carrier belongs to the D in that lane, a middle carrier to the backchecker
Options: Under the strong-side system the strong-side D takes the carrier instead, so ask which of the two you play
Options: The alternative is strong-side D on the puck as a 1-on-1, weak-side D splitting the middle and the far attacker
Read: If your backchecker is genuinely not coming, squeeze the middle and concede the far wide man — but look before you assume it
Action: Retreat together at the same speed, holding a flat line with the middle sealed between you
Never: Back all the way to the goal line — collapsing into your crease concedes the whole slot
Key: Ask your coach which version you play; two defencemen playing different ones on the same rush is worse than either
```

Three attackers, two defenders. Chaotic-looking, and the way you take the chaos out is to have agreed in advance which of two systems you are playing.

**The default assumed here — "two wide, middle to the backchecker."** This is what the rest of this section assumes unless you are told otherwise, and it is the baseline the alternative below is described against. **Nobody has published a count of what teams run on a rush**, as far as this document has been able to establish, so treat any "most teams run X" claim — its own included — as an impression rather than a measurement.

![](diagram:rush-3-on-2-default)

![](diagram:rush-3-on-2-alternative)

- **Each defenceman takes an outside attacker.** One D on each wide lane.
- **Nobody chases the puck carrier — under this default.** If the carrier is in the middle, they belong to the backchecker; if the carrier is wide, they belong to the defenceman in that lane and the other D holds the far attacker. Under the strong-side alternative set out below, the strong-side defenceman does take the carrier wherever they are, which is exactly why you need to know which one you are playing.
- **The middle belongs to the backchecking centre**, arriving from behind. This is exactly the middle-lane assignment above. If your backchecker is genuinely not coming, the defenceman on the puck side has to squeeze the middle and concede the far wide man — but do not assume that until you have looked.

> **The realistic alternative — "strong side takes the puck, weak side splits."** Some teams do not wait on the backchecker at all. The **strong-side defenceman takes the puck carrier** wherever they are, playing it as a 1-on-1; the **weak-side defenceman drops off and splits the middle and the far attacker**, sitting in the passing lane between them exactly as you would on a 2-on-1. Under this version the backchecker's job becomes the *third* attacker rather than the middle specifically.
>
> The trade: the default keeps three defenders on three attackers if the backcheck arrives, but concedes the middle entirely if it does not. The alternative never concedes the middle, but the weak-side D is covering two people and the far attacker gets more room. **Ask your coach which one you are playing** — they fail in different directions, and two defencemen playing different versions of it on the same rush is worse than either.
- **Retreat together.** Both defencemen back up at the same speed, holding a flat line across the ice with the middle sealed between you. A staggered retreat opens a diagonal seam straight to the slot.
- **Do not back all the way to the goal line.** Retreating together does not mean collapsing into your own crease — that concedes the entire slot. Retreat under control and force the shot from outside.

### 3-on-1

```facts
Position: Central, on the defensive side of the puck, stick in the most dangerous lane
Goal: Delay — the objective changes from "stop this" to "make it take longer"
Action: Retreat slowly, stay in the lanes, and force them to make an extra pass
Never: Commit to one attacker — attacking the carrier turns it into an unopposed 2-on-0
Priority: Give your goalie the shot from distance and take away the cross-ice options, as on a 2-on-1
Action: Call for help loudly — your backcheckers may not know how bad it is
```

Three attackers, one defender. This is a bad situation and pretending otherwise helps nobody.

- **Take the middle.** Position yourself centrally, defensive side of the puck, stick in the most dangerous lane. You cannot cover three; you can make the two middle options harder.
- **Delay.** Your objective changes from "stop this" to "make it take longer." Every extra second is another backchecker arriving. Retreat slowly, stay in the lanes, force them to make an extra pass.
- **Never commit to one attacker.** Attacking the carrier on a 3-on-1 turns it into an unopposed 2-on-0.
- **Give your goalie the shot from distance** and take away the cross-ice options, on the same logic as the 2-on-1.
- **Call for help loudly.** Your backcheckers may not know how bad it is.

### Breakaways

**In short:** Play the puck with your stick, or skate to catch them and angle them wide with your route; it is your goalie's save.

```facts
Priority: This is your goalie's save and they usually make it — do the one useful legal thing, then get out of the way
Action: Stick on the puck only — a poke at the puck itself, or a stick lift on their blade as they release
Options: Skating to catch them is legal, and angling them wide with your route turns it into a bad-angle chance — the route is yours everywhere, the contact that moves them off it is not
Rule: Angling ends where contact begins — Hockey Canada's 7.3 preamble makes extending "an arm, shoulder, or hip after angling an opponent" body checking; USA Hockey 604 Note 1 makes intentional contact with "overt hip, shoulder, forearm or torso action" an illegal body check
Rule: Hockey Canada's Rule 7.3 preamble carries a second limb that needs no deliberate hit at all — body checking results "when a player leaves their skating lane to make contact", so a route change into an opponent is enough on its own, at U13 and below, female hockey and any Member-approved division
Rule: Catching a breakaway carrier puts you behind them, so play the puck, never their back — the NHL, PWHL and IIHF write no minor for a hit from behind (43.2), and Hockey Canada 7.5(a) and CARHA 53(a) add a game misconduct even at the floor
Rule: A hook, trip, hold or slash from behind that denies the chance is a penalty shot, not a two-minute minor, in every book (NHL 57.3, IIHF 24.8, PWHL 58.3, Hockey Canada 4.11(a)(i), CARHA 86(c)); USA Hockey 616(a) lets the team you fouled take a minor instead (406(a))
Never: Stand up and look at the official — play does not stop until the attacking team loses possession (NHL Rule 57.3)
```

A **breakaway** is an attacker alone with the puck against the goalie, no defender between them and the net.

**The honest answer first: this is your goalie's save, and they usually make it.** *(No breakaway save percentage is quoted here: the NHL does not publish one, and penalty-shot and shootout rates measure a different situation. Take the direction — the goalie is favoured — not a number.)* Your job is to do the one useful legal thing and then get out of the way.

**What you can legally do from behind:**

- **Play the puck with your stick.** Get your blade onto the puck itself — a poke at the puck from behind, or a stick lift on their blade as they release. This is legal and it is the only reliable option you have.
- **Skate to catch them and re-establish position.** Legal, and worth doing if you have the speed.
- **Angle them wide** if you can get alongside — a bad-angle chance is a win. ⚠️ **You angle with your route, and the angle ends where contact begins.** Hockey Canada's Rule 7.3 preamble makes it a body check when a player *"extends an arm, shoulder, or hip **after angling** an opponent"*, and USA Hockey's Note to **604(c)** permits the angle but adds *"However, deliberate physical contact with an opponent, with no effort to legally play the puck, shall be penalized."* Its Casebook clears contact at the end of an angle only without *"any overt hip, shoulder or forearm action"* (Standard of Play, Situation 15). ⚠️ **And on a breakaway you arrive from behind**, where no book has a cheap tier for a hit that stays off the head — a hit into their back can end your game. The full ladder is in [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md#checking-from-behind-and-what-the-onus-does-and-does-not-mean).

**What you must not do:** a hook, a trip, a hold, a slash or any other foul from behind that denies the chance is a **penalty shot** — not a two-minute minor. NHL Rule 57.3 (Tripping) states it directly:

> "When a player, in the neutral or attacking zone, in control of the puck (or who could have obtained possession and control of the puck) and having no other opponent to pass than the goalkeeper, is tripped or otherwise fouled from behind, thus preventing a reasonable scoring opportunity, a penalty shot shall be awarded to the non-offending team."

The four conditions the officials are checking:

1. The infraction happened in the **neutral or attacking zone** (from the puck carrier's own blue line onward).
2. The foul was committed **from behind**.
3. There was **no opponent between the carrier and the goalie**.
4. A **reasonable scoring opportunity** was denied — and note that getting a shot away does not automatically rule out a penalty shot.

**And do not stand up and look at the official.** It is a delayed call: the referee does not stop play until the attacking team has lost possession, so the attacker may still score and you are still defending until the whistle.

### The carve-outs, and how far they reach in each book

**In short:** Stick on the puck only: touching it first is narrow relief that differs by book, so never trip or slide counting on it.

```facts
Action: Stick on the puck only — if your blade is not touching the puck or their blade, you are probably committing a penalty, and USA Hockey's relief needs a referee who judges you were clearly at the puck
Rule: Contacting the puck first and tripping the opponent in so doing is a tripping minor, not the penalty shot — NHL 57.3 and IIHF 24.8, same words. USA Hockey 616(a) leaves an optional minor instead, and that its 639(a) relief reaches 616 at all is this guide's reading of two rules, not a ruling
Rule: No puck-first relief saves a slide — USA Hockey's Casebook calls tripping where a player leaves their feet, slides into an opponent and causes them to fall, "regardless of who gains possession of the puck", excepting knees dropped to block a shot (639, Sit. 4 — its ruling says opponent, not carrier)
Rule: Outside NHL 57, PWHL 58 a reckless trip has no minor cap — USA Hockey 639(b) mandates major and game misconduct, plan on a match and a likely multi-game ban (602(a)); IIHF 57.3, 57.4 reach a major or major and game misconduct; 24.8's minor or 57.3 for a puck-first one: unsaid; HC 8.6(b) on injury
Rule: IIHF 57.3's tripping major is a bare major, with no game misconduct attached — but in British women's hockey at every age, adult included, any major carries an automatic game misconduct and ejection (In-House Rule 101 applies "in all cases" and states no age scope)
Rule: Tripping ceilings differ: NHL, PWHL 21.1, CARHA 48(a) and USA Hockey 602(a) mandate a match for deliberate injury or an attempt; a reckless USA Hockey trip: major and game misconduct (639(b)) — plan on a match and a likely multi-game ban; IIHF 57.4 stops at a discretionary major and game misconduct
Rule: Hockey Canada on a trip meant to injure: 8.6(c) bars a match and 7.1(c) reaches only what is "not otherwise covered within the playing rules", yet Appendix D’s Note 1 requires one. Unsettled for tripping, but 7.11(c) has the same bar and its Interpretation 2 allows 7.1(c): plan on the match
Rule: Under the IIHF book "from behind" is not required of a goalie — 24.8 criterion (II) excepts a foul from the front by the goalie on a breakaway, so that foul still concedes a penalty shot; NHL 57.3 criterion (ii) has no such exception
Rule: With your goalie pulled, a foul denying the chance is an awarded goal under the NHL, the IIHF and the PWHL — none of their awarded-goal clauses carries a "from behind" test (NHL 25.1 and 57.4; IIHF 25.3; PWHL 58.4, the NHL's wording whole)
Rule: USA Hockey, Hockey Canada and CARHA keep the "from behind" test with the net empty — 616(b) awards it only via the "fouled from behind provisions", Hockey Canada 4.12(a) routes through 4.11(a)(i) and CARHA 36(a)(3) writes it in; 616 catches a diagonal foul too
Rule: Hockey Canada writes the puck-first carve-out into the slide sentence of 8.6 — but 8.6 expressly does not reach 8.7 Clipping (contact below the hips) or 8.8 Slew-Footing (sweeping the feet), and neither carries any carve-out: both reach a match penalty (8.7(c), 8.8(c))
Rule: PWHL 58.3 is the NHL's breakaway rule whole, and 58 has no major or match penalty — but PWHL 21.1 and CARHA 48(a) mandate a match penalty for a deliberate attempt to injure or deliberate injury “in any manner”; CARHA 86(c) has the shot, 86(a) a discretionary major plus game misconduct over the minor
Rule: Slew-footing — sweeping an opponent's feet with a leg or foot, or pushing their upper body backward while your leg takes their skates — is a mandatory match penalty under NHL 52.2 and PWHL 53.2, no lower tier, no discretion — a fall the NHL, IIHF and PWHL call "violently to the ice" (52.1, 53.1)
Rule: Slew-footing ejects you at the floor in three of the five books besides the NHL — PWHL 53.2 a mandatory match, IIHF 52.2 a major plus automatic game misconduct, USA Hockey 639 a minimum major plus game misconduct. Hockey Canada 8.8 ejects only by a match penalty; CARHA 86(a) can eject without one
```

If your blade is not touching the puck or their blade, you are probably committing a penalty — and your goalie usually stops a breakaway, so take those odds over a penalty shot every single time.

**What it costs — the carve-outs, and where each of them stops:**

- **Under the NHL, IIHF and PWHL books, touch the puck first and you are safe from the penalty shot.** NHL 57.3: *"If, in the opinion of the Referee, a player makes contact with the puck first and subsequently trips the opponent in so doing, no penalty shot will be awarded, but a minor penalty for tripping shall be assessed."* That is why the instruction is "stick on the puck only". Rule 57 has no major or match tier, so under the NHL book a puck-first trip is two minutes — **but Rule 21.1 still reaches a match penalty** for a player who *"deliberately attempts to injure or who deliberately injures an opponent in any manner"*.
- **The IIHF grants the same carve-out in the same words (24.8) but does not cap the tier:** 57.3 lets the referee assess a major, and 57.4 a major plus a game misconduct for reckless endangerment. **Whether 24.8's "shall" or 57.3's discretion governs a reckless puck-first trip, the book does not say** — so do not treat the minor as a ceiling. That game misconduct is the referee's judgement, not automatic: 57.3 and 57.4 both read *"The Referee, at their discretion, may assess"* in the 2025/26 and 2026/27 editions (the 2026/27 book merges its major-penalty summary tables into one Table 5, so older table numbers no longer match). ⚠️ **In British women's hockey that discretion is gone at every age:** In-House **Rule 101** ejects any player assessed a major *"in all cases"* — see [UK and England Rules](../foundation/uk_rules.md).
- **Where the top tier comes from differs by book.** **NHL 21.1** and **PWHL 21.1**, word for word: a match for a player who *"deliberately attempts to injure or who deliberately injures an opponent in any manner"*. **USA Hockey 602(a)**: a match *"shall be assessed"* to a player who *"recklessly endangers or attempts to injure"* — and for a reckless trip **639(b)** makes a major plus game misconduct mandatory, so ⚠️ **plan on the match and a likely multi-game suspension**, though **639(c)**, inside the tripping rule, says only that a match *"may also be assessed"*. **CARHA 48(a)** writes the same road for a deliberate injury or an attempt *"in any manner"*, and its tripping rule does not bar it (CARHA-affiliated adult leagues only).
- **Hockey Canada is the awkward one.** Its deliberate-injury match, **7.1(c)**, reaches only conduct *"in any manner **not otherwise covered within the playing rules**"*, and tripping is covered by 8.6, whose (c) says *"No Match penalty may be assessed for tripping."* ⚠️ **Yet Note 1 to its own Appendix D says the opposite** — *"A Match penalty shall be assessed for any deliberate attempt to injure and for any infraction that results in a deliberate injury (Rule 7.1)."* The book never settles this for tripping, but it does for a rule with the same bar: **7.11(c)** says no match for an instigator or aggressor, and its **Interpretation 2** still lets the referee give 7.1(c)'s match for a deliberate attempt to injure. So plan on the match.
- **The IIHF book has no match tier at all** — the penalty types in its Rules 16 to 27 include none, and its Rule 21, *Dangerous Actions*, reaches only *"an action that is **not covered by the playing rules**"*. So under the IIHF the on-ice ceiling for a trip is a major plus a game misconduct. Beyond it sits Rule 28 Supplementary Discipline, which 57.7 invokes and which can act *"whether or not such offense has been penalized by the Referee(s)"* — in an Elite League game through the EIHL's Department of Player Safety and Hockey Operations — so a British reader's worst case is a suspension after the game.
- **USA Hockey writes its relief in two volumes, and neither saves a slide.** The Note to **639(a)**: *"no penalty shall be assessed **under this rule** if, in the opinion of the Referee, the player was clearly hook-checking or poke-checking the puck for the purpose of gaining possession."* **That is relief for a *stick* check**, not for a body thrown across the ice. And it is scoped to Rule 639, not to **616**, which awards the penalty shot and writes no puck-first relief of its own. 616's **Note 1** defines *"fouled from behind"* as an infraction *"that would normally be deemed a penalty"* — so where 639's Note leaves no penalty, arguably 616 has nothing to act on. **That is this guide's reading of the two rules together, not a USA Hockey ruling.** No situation in either volume joins them, and the phrase reads equally well as naming the *kind* of action, in which case 639's relief takes nothing from 616. **Do not defend as though the penalty shot has been ruled out.** On either reading the referee must judge you *"clearly"* at the puck, and an action graded as a hook or a hold never enters 639 at all.
- **USA Hockey's Casebook rules the slide out, whoever ends up with the puck** (Rule 639, Situation 4). ⚠️ **So never slide on the strength of a puck-first relief there:** *"When a player leaves their feet and slides into an opponent, thereby causing them to fall, a penalty for tripping must be called regardless of who gains possession of the puck."* The ruling says *opponent*, not carrier. Its one exception is not a puck-first one: *"The only exception is when a player drops to their knees to block a shot and their momentum carries them into the player shooting the puck, causing them to fall."*
- **Hockey Canada writes the carve-out too, and one limb is more generous than the NHL's.** Rule 8.6 counts a deliberate slide as tripping, except where the player *"make[s] contact with the puck prior to contacting their opponent"*. Interpretation 1, an opinion call, excuses a player who *"gains possession of the puck and, with the same action, trips the puck carrier"*. **Interpretation 2 has no opinion clause:** *"A penalty will NOT be assessed if, while falling or sliding along the ice, a player contacts the puck prior to contacting the body of their opponent and within that same motion causes the opponent to fall."* It is headed *"Rule 8.6 (a)"*, the minor, and whether *"while falling or sliding"* reaches a player who chooses to go down the text does not say. ⚠️ **8.6(b) is a mandatory major plus game misconduct wherever a trip injures.**
- **Hockey Canada's carve-out stops at 8.6, and that is the part that can end your season.** It *"does not apply to actions defined under Rule 8.7 – Clipping or Rule 8.8 – Slew-Footing"*. Clipping is contact *"below an opponent's hips"* — which a slide that catches a carrier low is, so plan on 8.7 for a low slide into the legs, puck first or not — and slew-footing is using *"a leg or a foot to knock, drag, or sweep an opposing player's feet from under them"*. ⚠️ **Neither carries a puck-first carve-out, and both reach a match penalty** (8.7(c), 8.8(c)). Interpretation 1 to 8.8(a) asks for *"serious consideration for a Match penalty ... when an offending player is 'in motion' (skating) while committing this foul"* — a backchecker's exact situation. Taken at speed a slew-foot can put a player on the back of their head; that is this guide's reading of why the tiers run high, not a rulebook sentence, though Interpretation 2 to 8.7 says such fouls *"may cause players to be thrown through the air and the subsequent impact with the ice can be very dangerous."*
- **Slew-footing is priced harder outside Hockey Canada.** NHL 52.2 and PWHL 53.2: *"Any player who is guilty of slew-footing shall be assessed a match penalty"* — mandatory, no lower tier. IIHF 52.2: the referee *"**shall**, at their discretion, assess a major penalty and an automatic game misconduct penalty"*, with no lesser tier. USA Hockey 639 sets a *minimum* of a major plus game misconduct. CARHA 86(b) is a discretionary match for knocking skates out *"with a kicking or leg dragging motion from behind"*, and 86(a) prices an ordinary trip at a minor *"or, at the discretion of the Referee, a Major penalty and a Game Misconduct penalty"* — so CARHA can end your game without a match (CARHA-affiliated adult leagues only).
- **Hockey Canada's 8.8 has no major and no game misconduct** — *"No Major penalty may be assessed for slew-footing"* (8.8(b)) — so it runs from a **double minor** straight to a **match**, mandatory *"to any player who injures an opponent by a slew-footing infraction that would otherwise call for a double Minor penalty"* (8.8(c)). So 8.6(c)'s *"No Match penalty may be assessed for tripping"* holds only if the referee calls it tripping. **Meant to injure, plan on the match in both books, hurt or not:** CARHA 48(a) makes one mandatory *"in any manner"*, and Hockey Canada's Appendix D Note 1 says a match *"shall be assessed for any deliberate attempt to injure"* — that the Note overrides 8.8(c)'s discretion is this guide's reading.
- **The PWHL and CARHA write the rule too.** **PWHL 58.3** is the NHL's breakaway rule whole, carve-out included, and Rule 58 has no major or match tier — but **PWHL 21.1** keeps the NHL's deliberate-injury match, and its **58.4** awarded goal drops the *"from behind"* test as the NHL's does. **CARHA 86(c)** has the penalty shot on a narrower test — the carrier *"on a breakaway"* and *"prevented from having a clear shot on goal"*. ⚠️ **Its carve-out reaches a slide, on a narrow test.** The Note to **86(b)**: *"If a player in the process of falling or sliding along the ice, hits or knocks the puck from their opponent's stick prior to making actual body contact, the tripping shall be ignored."* The puck has to come off their stick, not merely be touched — and where the Note does not reach, **86(a)** puts an ejection above a CARHA trip (CARHA-affiliated adult leagues only).
- **Play does not stop when the foul happens.** NHL 57.3: *"the Referee shall not stop play until the attacking team has lost possession of the puck to the defending team."* Keep defending until the whistle — the attacker may still score. If the fouled player recovers and gets a reasonable chance anyway, there is no penalty shot; a minor is called instead if no goal results.

- **If your goalie has been pulled** for an extra attacker and you foul an attacker on the empty net, **a goal is awarded** — there is no shot to take. **Note what the NHL's awarded-goal clauses do *not* say.** Rule 57.4 reads *"is tripped or **otherwise fouled** with no opposition between him and the opposing goal"*, and Rule 25.1 *"prevented from scoring as a result of an infraction"*. Neither carries the **from behind** requirement that the penalty-shot test in 57.3 does — so with the net empty the awarded goal is *easier* to trigger than the penalty shot, not harder, and stepping across from the front does it. Cite 25.1 and 57.4 as a pair; they are not alternatives. ([Risk Management](../hockey-iq/risk_management.md) sets this out at length.)

> **Rule-set note — the criteria are not the same everywhere.**
>
> **IIHF Rule 24.8** mirrors NHL 57.3 almost word for word on the *penalty shot*, including the four criteria and the puck-first carve-out, with one addition: criterion (II) reads *"The infraction must have been committed from behind (**except a foul committed from the front by the goalkeeper on a 'breakaway' situation**)"*. So under IIHF rules a goalie who fouls a breakaway attacker from the front concedes a penalty shot; the NHL text has no such exception in 57.3. The IIHF's tripping tiers do not mirror the NHL's, so do not carry the NHL's two-minute cap across.
>
> **USA Hockey Rule 616(a) is materially different**, and it changes how you defend: *"A penalty shot/optional minor penalty shall be awarded to the non-offending team any time a player is fouled from behind, **or diagonally from behind** by an opponent, and is denied a reasonable scoring opportunity **during a breakaway**."* "From behind" is defined by the fouled player's **peripheral vision** — Note 1 covers *"any infraction committed to an opponent either directly or diagonally from behind (beyond the fouled player's peripheral vision), **that would normally be deemed a penalty**"* — so ⚠️ **a diagonal approach does not protect you** the way it might in the NHL. It is a **penalty shot *or* an optional minor**, at the fouled team's election (406(a)), and it needs a **breakaway** as Note 2 defines it rather than the NHL's "neutral or attacking zone" test.
>
> **On the empty net the six books split three–three.** USA Hockey **616(b)** awards the goal only where the chance was *"prevented by the **fouled from behind** provisions"*, and Hockey Canada (4.12(a) via 4.11(a)(i)) and CARHA (36(a)(3), CARHA-affiliated adult leagues only) keep the from-behind test too. ⚠️ **The NHL, the IIHF (25.3) and the PWHL (58.4) drop it — so under those three, a foul from the front with your net empty is an awarded goal.**

### Covering the puck in your own crease

**In short:** Knock a puck in the air straight down and play it with your stick; smothering it is your goalie's job.

```facts
Rule: Five books print the permission to catch a puck in the air and drop it at once — NHL and IIHF 67.2, PWHL 69.2, Hockey Canada 10.2(a)(iii) and CARHA 61(a), the last two only "without gaining or attempting to gain" an advantage; USA Hockey's is in its Casebook (618 Situation 1, 614 Situation 3)
Never: Hold a caught puck, carry it, turn with it or throw it — that is what costs you, a minor in most books and in your own crease the penalty shot — not the goalie, whose rule is a different one
Rule: What buys the penalty is the conduct after the catch — holding it, taking a stride, turning to avoid a check, or throwing it — and not the height (NHL and IIHF 67.2(I), PWHL 69.2(i); Hockey Canada Interpretation 4 to 10.2(a)(iii))
Rule: Under USA Hockey a puck caught outside the crease and not dropped at once is a stoppage and a last-play faceoff, not a penalty (618(a); Casebook 618 Situation 1); held in the crease it is the penalty shot or optional minor (614(b), 618(a)); the goalie is exempt, by name
Rule: Outside the crease, never cover a loose puck on the ice with your hand — USA Hockey makes that a minor (614(a) and its Note; Casebook 614 Situation 1), as it does picking it up (618(a)); only a puck caught out of the air gets the stoppage there
Rule: Covering a puck that is itself in your goal crease is a penalty shot for every defender but the goalie — every book prints it (NHL 67.4 and 63.6, IIHF 63.6, PWHL 65.6, USA Hockey 614(b), CARHA 58(c), Hockey Canada 4.11(a)(v))
Rule: The converse — a minor where the puck is outside the paint and you are inside it — is rule text only in NHL and IIHF 63.6, PWHL 65.6; USA Hockey rules it in its Casebook (614 Situation 1); Hockey Canada 10.2(a)(v)-(vi) also go by where you stand: price the shot, or the goal with your net empty
Rule: With your own net empty, covering a puck in your crease is an awarded goal — NHL and IIHF 67.5, PWHL 69.5 and Hockey Canada 10.2(a)(vi) on the substitution alone; USA Hockey 614(b) and 618(a) only where it prevented an obvious and imminent goal; CARHA 58(c) Note 1 flat, 36(b) conditioned
Rule: CARHA prices a crease cover with your net empty twice — 58(c) Note 1 awards the goal whenever the goalie is off the ice, 36(b) only after a shot and an obvious and imminent goal prevented; play to 58(c): any cover there is the goal (CARHA adult leagues only)
Rule: Catching the puck is free; holding it is not. Height only sets the price of holding it — the crease runs four feet up to the top of the goal frame (NHL 1.7, 2.1), so above the crossbar is a minor and knee high in the paint the penalty shot (IIHF Situations 63.27 v 63.15, 67.4)
Never: Reach higher for a puck to keep it above the crossbar — height selects which penalty you pay, never whether you pay one, and the free play is still to knock it down
Rule: Throwing, shooting or kicking a stick or any object at the puck or carrier in your defending zone concedes a penalty shot in every book here — delayed, awarded only if no goal is scored (NHL and IIHF 53.7, Hockey Canada 10.5(a)(ii), USA Hockey 637(a) shot or optional minor, PWHL 54.7, CARHA 83(a))
```

**If a puck is in the air at your own crease, knock it straight down and play it with your stick — never close your hand on it, skate with it or fall on it.** Smothering it is your goalie's job. The catch is free; what you do after it is not, and with the puck in your own crease the price is a penalty shot.

**The permission first, because it is what makes the act free.** Five of the six books print it: you may catch a puck out of the air if you put it straight down. NHL and IIHF **Rule 67.2**: *"A player shall be permitted to catch the puck out of the air but must immediately place it or knock it down to the ice."* The **PWHL** prints it at **69.2**; Hockey Canada's **10.2(a)(iii)** and **CARHA 61(a)** allow it *"without gaining or attempting to gain"* an advantage — ⚠️ **and CARHA's Casebook shows how little room that leaves: one stride with the puck in the glove is a minor, *"as the player had gained an advantage on the opponent."***

**USA Hockey's playing rules print no permission for a skater at all; its Casebook supplies it** — the catch and drop *"must be virtually one continuous motion"* (618, Situation 1), and a puck *"caught and dropped to the ice immediately"* in the crease draws no penalty (614, Situation 3). Outside the crease, a caught puck not dropped at once is only a stoppage and a last-play faceoff (618(a)). ⚠️ **But cover a loose puck *on the ice* with your hand and it is a minor** (the Note to 614(a); Casebook 614, Situation 1), as is picking it up (618(a)). ⚠️ **In the crease, a hand that closes and does not open is holding the puck — the penalty shot or optional minor** (618(a), 614(b)). **Knock it down and no book penalises you.**

**What costs you is the conduct after the catch, not how high the puck was.** NHL and IIHF 67.2(I) and PWHL 69.2(i) reach the player who *"catches it and skates with it, either to avoid a check or to gain a territorial advantage"*. Hockey Canada's **Interpretation 4 to 10.2(a)(iii)** names three acts, none of them a height: throwing the puck, trying *"to maneuver around another player while holding the puck"*, and *"tak[ing] a stride while holding the puck in their hand."*

**Then the price.** **Falling on, holding, picking up or gathering into your body or hands a puck that is in your own goal crease** is a penalty shot — NHL **Rule 67.4** opens *"If a **defending player, except a goalkeeper**…"*, so your goalie may smother it and you may not. ⚠️ **It is the puck that has to be in the crease, not you.** NHL and IIHF **63.6**: *"The significant factor when determining whether or not a penalty shot is warranted is the location of the puck at the time it was held, grabbed or gathered into the body"*. So a puck outside the paint smothered while you stand in it is a minor, and a puck in the paint smothered from outside is still the shot. The PWHL's 65.6 is the same sentence; CARHA **58(c)** and USA Hockey **614(b)** also go by the puck (*"the decision as to whether the puck is in the crease is made at the moment the infraction occurs"*). ⚠️ **Only the NHL, IIHF and PWHL write the converse minor — puck outside, you inside — as rule text.**

**USA Hockey rules that converse in its Casebook; Hockey Canada does not rule it at all.** USA Hockey's **Rule 614, Situation 1** — a defender covers a puck **outside** the crease and their momentum carries them into it — answers *"No. The Referee must assess a minor penalty"*, because *"[t]he location of the puck at the instant it is covered or held is the determining factor as to whether or not a penalty shot is to be awarded."* Hockey Canada's **10.2(a)(v)** escalates its hand and body limbs to the shot *"when the infraction occurs inside the offending team's goal crease"* — words that could mean you or the puck. Its penalty-shot list, **4.11(a)(v)**, says *"while the puck is in the goal crease"*, which makes the puck reading plausible but does not cancel 10.2(a)(v); and **Interpretation 5** (a puck scooped out of the paint and then covered is a minor) has the player outside too, so it cannot settle the case of you in the paint and the puck outside. ⚠️ **Under Hockey Canada, price it as the penalty shot — and with your net empty as the goal, since 10.2(a)(vi) uses the same words.**

**And the crease is a volume, not a footprint — but height decides which penalty you pay, never whether you pay one.** All six books run it up to the top of the goal frame (NHL and PWHL 1.7, IIHF 1.7, Hockey Canada 1.5(c), CARHA 5(c), the Note to USA Hockey 618(a)) — four feet, the height NHL 2.1 gives the posts. The IIHF Situation Handbook rules a puck caught **above the crossbar** while you stand in the paint *"was not considered to be in the crease. Therefore, only a minor penalty is warranted"* (63.27), and one taken **knee high** in the crease the shot (63.15, 67.4). ⚠️ **In each the defender turned and released the puck — that conduct is what produced a penalty at all; the height only chose which one.** ⚠️ **So never reach higher for the puck.** The Handbook sits below the Rulebook — *"in case of any contradictions, the valid IIHF Rulebook is always the basis"* — so read those as worked examples, not rule text. **Nor does moving the puck cure it:** in Situation 67.6 a defender with their net empty takes it *"out of mid-air (around the height of the waist)"*, drops it **outside** the crease, and the ruling is *"an Awarded Goal to Team B."*

**USA Hockey alone lets the other side elect an optional minor in lieu of the shot** (Rule 406(a)) — **their election, not yours**, so it is never a discount. ⚠️ **With your goalie pulled for an extra attacker, the same act is an awarded goal**, and the books split on whether that is all it takes. NHL and IIHF 67.5, PWHL 69.5 and Hockey Canada 10.2(a)(vi) award it on the substitution alone. ⚠️ **USA Hockey adds a condition** — only where the act *"has prevented an obvious and imminent goal"* (614(b); 618(a)). ⚠️ **CARHA writes it twice and the two disagree:** 58(c)'s Note is flat — *"If the goalkeeper has been removed from the ice, then a goal is awarded"* — while 36(b) wants a shot already taken and an obvious and imminent goal. **Play to 58(c): with your net empty, any cover in your own crease is the goal** (CARHA-affiliated adult leagues only).

**Throwing your stick or any object** at the puck or the carrier in your defensive zone is a penalty shot in all six books — NHL and IIHF 53.7, PWHL 54.7, CARHA 83(a) (which names your own goalie and adds *"deliberately"*), Hockey Canada 10.5(a)(ii) (which adds kicking it), and USA Hockey 637(a) as a shot or optional minor. **It is a delayed call, and the shot is awarded only if no goal is scored.** Under Hockey Canada it is assessed *"regardless of whether the thrown stick or object actually makes contact with an opposing player or the puck"* (10.5 Note 2), so missing buys you nothing.

If the goal goes in, it counts and the minor is wiped out — **unless your team is already short on a minor, in which case you serve it and the goal ends the one already running** (NHL, IIHF and PWHL 15.2 and 15.4, USA Hockey 409(b), CARHA 39(b); Hockey Canada Interpretation 3 to 4.15(b)). Under CARHA the wiped-out minor still counts toward the three that eject you (39(b) Note 1; 32(a)). ⚠️ **With your goalie pulled, a thrown stick can cost a goal rather than a shot in all six books** — NHL and IIHF 53.8, PWHL 54.8, Hockey Canada 4.12(a), CARHA 36(a)(2) on a breakaway, and USA Hockey 637(b) only where it *"prevents an obvious and imminent goal."*

### The late man / trailer

```facts
Key: The player who arrives second is the one who gets forgotten — how often that is the reason a rush ends in a goal is coaching consensus, not a tracked share
Position: The trailer follows a few metres behind and arrives into the high slot with a clean, unopposed look
Convention: In this guide's standard system — low zone collapse behind a 2-1-2 forecheck — the trailer belongs to the backchecking forwards, not the defencemen
Options: Some teams give the trailer to the weak-side defenceman and drop the third forward to the net front — ask which yours runs
Action: If you are the last backchecker and everyone else is marked, the trailer is yours — do not skate into the pile
Action: Defencemen, call them out — "trailer!" or "high!" is a genuinely game-saving shout
```

The player who arrives second is the one who gets forgotten. How often that is the reason a rush ends in a goal is coaching consensus rather than a tracked share — [Neutral Zone Systems](neutral_zone_systems.md) sets out what is and is not counted about rush goals.

The **trailer** (or **late man**) is the attacker following the rush a few metres behind — often a defenceman who has activated, often a forward who was slow out of the zone. They arrive into the high slot just as the first wave has pulled everybody low, and they arrive with a clean, unopposed look.

![](diagram:rush-trailer)

- **In the standard system — low zone collapse behind a 2-1-2 forecheck — they belong to the backchecking forwards**, not to the defencemen. Your defencemen are occupied with the first wave, and if they step up to the trailer the first wave gets the net. **This is an assignment, not a law:** some teams have the weak-side defenceman take the trailer and the third forward drop to the net front instead, which is tighter on the trailer and thinner in front. Ask which your team runs, because a trailer covered by nobody and a trailer covered by two are both goals.
- **This is the specific reason "skate first, then look" matters.** A backchecker who is five metres behind cannot catch the trailer. One who sprinted from the turnover can.
- **If you are the last backchecker and everyone else is marked, the trailer is yours by default.** Do not skate into the pile.
- **Defencemen: call them out.** You will see the trailer before the backcheckers do — "trailer!" or "high!" is a genuinely game-saving shout.
- Note the symmetry: the trailer is the same play your own team should be running in attack, described from the other side in [Defender](../positions/defender.md) under activating, and in [Zone Entries](zone_entries.md).

### The drop pass and the stretch pass

**The drop pass.** The carrier crosses your blue line, leaves the puck behind for a teammate trailing them, and keeps driving the net — often straight through your defenceman, as a screen or a pick. **The common approach: the defenceman on the carrier stays with the body driving the net, and the dropped puck belongs to the backchecking forward who has the trailer.** Follow the puck back toward your blue line and you leave the net-front body free and hand the shooter a screen. Keep your stick in the lane to the slot and shout "drop!" the moment you see it. Some teams have the defenceman nearest the dropped puck step to it while the partner picks up the driver, with the switch called out loud — ask which yours plays, because both defencemen going to the puck is the version that loses.

**The stretch pass.** An attacker leaves early and a long pass from deep in their end finds them level with or behind your defence before you have turned. **The fix is depth before the pass, not speed after it.** While they have the puck in their own end, look over your shoulder for the forward leaving early, and the defenceman on that side starts giving ground soon enough to keep them in front — nobody gets behind you. Your partner holds the middle rather than drifting toward the puck. Some teams would rather hold a tighter, higher gap and accept that the occasional long pass gets through, trusting the backcheck and the goalie; how high your defence stands is your coach's call, so ask.

## Standing Up at the Blue Line vs Backing Off

Two legitimate ways to defend the line, and this is a genuine trade-off rather than a right answer. It is also frequently a **coaching choice** — many teams specify which one they want, and it usually follows from their neutral-zone system. See [Neutral Zone Systems](neutral_zone_systems.md).

### Standing up (stepping up)

**In short:** Stand up at the line to deny the entry when the read is right, and take the ice, not the man.

```facts
Position: Hold your ground at or just inside the blue line and challenge the carrier as they try to enter
Goal: Deny the zone entirely — the attack has to dump the puck in or turn back, and neither produces a chance
Rule: Both skates must cross the line after the puck under NHL, IIHF and PWHL rules (PWHL 85.1 prints the NHL’s plane test); USA Hockey 630(a) needs skate contact with the neutral zone or the blue line, Hockey Canada 6.11 only skates touching the ice, and CARHA 72(c) never defines "own side" either way
Risk: If you miss there is nothing behind you — your partner is left in a 2-on-1 or worse
Countered by: A skilled carrier who chips the puck past you and skates onto it
Read: Right for slow attacks, a head-down carrier, a carrier already near the boards, clear support behind you, and a clogged neutral zone
Never: Read a head-down carrier as a licence to arrive harder — it tells you they cannot change direction, and nothing more
Rule: USA Hockey's Casebook rules the head-down case charging in terms (607, Situation 2), and a check accelerated through without the two fast strides (Situation 3); Hockey Canada 7.4(ii) makes two strides the offence where USA Hockey's 607 Note needs more than two
Rule: Strides are not the only gate under CARHA — its Rule 52(a) Note makes more than two a charge, and Rule 49(a) prices intentional body contact anywhere on the ice with no stride count, at a minor or a major, mandatory on injury, and either major ejects you under 30(a) (CARHA adult leagues only)
Rule: No book caps charging at a minor — USA Hockey 607(a): minor and misconduct, or major and game misconduct, a must if reckless (607(b)) — plan on a match and a likely multi-game ban (602(a)); Hockey Canada 7.4(b): the pair on violence, a must if hurt; 7.4(c) a match if defenceless or meant to injure
Rule: An ejection can cost the next game too — under USA Hockey 404(b) any game misconduct also suspends you for the team's next game, at least two after a second major in a game (403(b)); CARHA 32(d) adds one game, possibly more, after a major's game misconduct, unless for an accidental high stick
Rule: NHL and PWHL 42.1 price charging at a minor or major, a game misconduct added for a head or face injury (42.5, and in the EIHL its Casebook's Rule 42) and a mandatory match if meant to injure (21.1); CARHA 52(a) a minor or an ejecting major (30(a)), mandatory on injury (CARHA adult leagues only)
Rule: A charge meant to injure is a match in every book but the IIHF (NHL and PWHL 21.1, Hockey Canada 7.4(c), CARHA 48(a), USA Hockey 602(a)) — and 602(a) also reaches a player who "recklessly endangers", so plan on a match and a likely multi-game ban for a reckless charge there, whatever 607(e)'s "may"
Rule: Charging binds every division, checking league or not — USA Hockey 607 and Hockey Canada 7.4 carry no division clause — and first check whether you may body check at all: USA Hockey 604(a), Hockey Canada 7.3(a) and, in Britain, IHUK's Rules of Competition bar it in many classifications
```

You hold your ground at or just inside the blue line and challenge the carrier as they try to enter, rather than retreating with them.

**What it gets you:**
- **Denies the zone entirely.** The attack has to dump the puck in or turn back, and neither produces a chance.
- **Uses the offside rule as an extra defender.** Because under the NHL, IIHF and PWHL books both skates must cross after the puck — **PWHL 85.1** carries the NHL’s vertical-plane test, so an airborne trail skate is onside there too — a defender squeezing the line can force the carrier to slow up or enter alone. ⚠️ **Two books are stricter than that, and one settles nothing:** **USA Hockey 630(a)** needs skate *contact* with the neutral zone or the blue line and **Hockey Canada 6.11** counts only a skate touching the ice, so a lifted trail skate is offside in both. **CARHA 72(c)** borrows the words *"own side"* without ever defining them, which is not a permission either way. A delayed offside also lets your team reset while the attackers tag up.
- **Keeps play out of your end**, which keeps your forwards fresh and available for the counter.

**What it costs you:**
- **If you miss, there is nothing behind you.** A step-up that fails in the neutral zone leaves your partner in a 2-on-1, or worse. This is the specific risk flagged in [Defender](../positions/defender.md).
- Standing up rewards a skilled carrier who chips the puck past you and skates onto it.

**When it's right:** slow attacks, an attacker with their head down, a carrier already near the boards, when you clearly have support behind you, and when your forwards have the neutral zone clogged.

**The head-down read is a read about their vision, not a licence to punish them.** It tells you the carrier will not change direction, so your body gets there first. ⚠️ **It does not tell you to arrive harder** — a step-up finished as a hit is the geometry the charging rules are written for. **Take the ice, not the man**, and before you plan any contact, check whether you may body check at all in this game.

**What it costs.** **USA Hockey's Casebook rules on this exact picture** (Rule 607, Situation 2): a player who sees *"an unsuspecting opponent skating up the ice with their head down"* and *"skates across the ice at full speed to deliver a hard body check"* is charging. The reason given is that the player was *"punishing a vulnerable or defenseless opponent and not simply making a good defensive hockey play"*. ⚠️ **And it needs no run-up:** **Situation 3** answers *"Yes"* for a player who *"delivers a check without taking the two fast strides, but instead accelerates through the check"*.

**The stride counts differ by book.** **Hockey Canada 7.4(ii)** makes *"two or more"* strides charging, where the Notes to **USA Hockey 607** and **CARHA 52** need *"more than two"* — ⚠️ **so two strides are your ceiling under one book and already the offence under the other.** The IIHF (42.1, *"multiple strides"*) and the NHL (42.1, *"distance traveled"*) write no number.

**No book caps charging at a minor.** NHL and PWHL 42.1 allow a minor or a major, with a match at 42.4 that **21.1** makes mandatory for a deliberate attempt to injure, and **42.5** a game misconduct on a major that injures the face or head — which the **EIHL Casebook**'s Rule 42 adds to the IIHF book for an Elite League game. **IIHF 42.4** reaches a major and game misconduct for reckless endangerment. **USA Hockey 607(a) never gives the minor alone** — a minor plus misconduct or a major plus game misconduct — with **607(b)** a mandatory major and game misconduct when reckless, and a match to plan on, with a likely multi-game suspension (607(e); 602(a)). **Hockey Canada 7.4(b)** makes the major and game misconduct mandatory on injury, **7.4(e)** the game misconduct automatic on any charging major, and **7.4(c)** a match on a player *"unable to defend themselves"*.

**CARHA prices it twice.** **52(a)** is a minor or a major — *"If injury results, a Major penalty and a Game Misconduct penalty shall be assessed"* — ⚠️ **52(b) makes that pair mandatory for charging a goalie in the crease, or for any charge that injures an opponent**, and **48(a)** makes a charge meant to injure a match. With no checking division, **49(a)** also prices any intentional body contact at a minor or a major — mandatory on injury, with a match *"if warranted"* (Casebook 49, Situation 1) — and any of these majors ends your game (**30(a)**, CARHA-affiliated adult leagues only).

**Charging binds every division.** ⚠️ **USA Hockey 607 and Hockey Canada 7.4 carry no division clause**, so in a no-checking league a step-up finished as a hit breaks two rules; which one the referee calls, neither book says. **Before any of that, check whether you may body check at all** — **USA Hockey 604(a)** bars it at 12U and below, in all Girls'/Women's and all non-check Adult classifications, and **Hockey Canada 7.3(a)** at U13 and below, in female hockey and in any Member-approved division. The full treatment is in [1-on-1](#1-on-1) and [What hounding never means](#what-hounding-never-means).

### Backing off (retreating)

```facts
Position: Give ground, holding your gap, and defend them as they enter
Goal: Nothing gets behind you — the worst case is a controlled entry, not a breakaway
Convention: Treated as the default for most defencemen most of the time because it is reliable and repeatable — an impression rather than a measurement
Risk: Concedes the blue line, so play happens in your end and the shift gets longer
Never: Back off with a growing gap — a soft retreat gives the attacker speed and the zone, and is worse than either option
Read: Right against attackers with speed, on odd-man situations, when you are the last defender, when unsure, and any time you are tired
```

You give ground, holding your gap, and defend as they enter.

**What it gets you:**
- **Nothing gets behind you.** The worst case is a controlled entry, not a breakaway.
- Backing off is reliable and repeatable, which is why it is treated as the default for most defencemen most of the time — an impression rather than a measurement, so do not read it as a count.

**What it costs you:**
- **Concedes the blue line**, so play happens in your end and the shift gets longer.
- **A soft retreat is worse than either option.** Backing off with a growing gap gives the attacker speed *and* the zone.

**When it's right:** attackers with speed, odd-man situations, when you are the last defender, when you are unsure, and any time you are tired.

### How to choose

```facts
Never: Step up unless you are confident you can win the puck — confidence, not hope
Convention: How aggressive "confident" should be is a coaching choice — an aggressive 1-2-2 press wants you challenging the line, a 1-3-1 trap wants you holding
Never: Half-commit — drifting forward, changing your mind, and arriving late with a huge gap
Priority: When in doubt, back off with a tight gap — it has no catastrophic failure mode
Key: Keep "step up" and "pinch" separate — stepping up is challenging in the neutral zone or at your own line, pinching is stepping down from the offensive blue line
```

- **Do not step up unless you are confident you can win the puck.** Confidence, not hope — a missed step-up is an odd-man rush behind you. **How aggressive "confident" should be is a coaching choice:** a team playing an aggressive 1-2-2 neutral-zone press wants you challenging at the line and will accept the occasional miss; a team playing a passive 1-3-1 trap wants you holding and steering the carrier wide. Find out which risk your coach wants to carry.
- **Step up or back off — do not do neither.** The killer is the half-commit: drifting forward, then changing your mind, and arriving late with a huge gap. Pick and go.
- **The default when in doubt is back off with a tight gap.** It has no catastrophic failure mode.
- **Keep "step up" and "pinch" separate.** Stepping up is challenging forward in the neutral zone or at your own line with nothing behind you. Pinching is stepping down from the *offensive* blue line with your forwards behind you. The pinch is the safer play; see [Defender](../positions/defender.md) and [Risk Management](../hockey-iq/risk_management.md).

## Odd-Man Rushes: How They Are Created

**In short:** By coaching consensus most odd-man rushes are your own making: missed pinches, early forwards, middle turnovers, bad changes, nobody above the puck.

```facts
Key: Almost every odd-man rush against you was manufactured by your own team, and five causes account for nearly all of them — coaching emphasis, not a counted ranking
Convention: No public data classifies odd-man rushes by the play that created them, so treat the five as the list worth drilling rather than as a measured census
Read: Pinch on a read — when you will clearly win the puck, when a forward can cover for you, and when score and time say the risk is worth it
Never: Turn up ice hunting a breakaway pass before your team actually has possession — the test is whether you can still get back if you are wrong
Never: Stickhandle through the middle under pressure, or force cross-ice passes in the neutral zone
Action: Change lines when the puck is in the offensive zone or moving away from your end, not during a scramble
Never: Go below the puck in the offensive zone with nobody left above it — the subtlest of the five, and usually both defencemen going below at once
Action: Before you go below the puck know who is above it — a person, not a position; on a defence pair normally one of the two, but a five-player rotation uses whichever player is highest
```

The best rush defence is not creating the rush. Almost every odd-man rush against you was made by your own team, and five causes account for nearly all of them — **coaching emphasis, not a counted ranking**, because no public data classifies odd-man rushes by the play that created them. [Neutral Zone Systems](neutral_zone_systems.md) sets out what is and is not counted about rush goals.

**1. A pinch that misses.** A defenceman steps down at the offensive blue line to keep the puck in and does not get it. Now one D is behind the play. The fix is not "never pinch" — it is pinch on a read: pinch when you will clearly win the puck, when a forward can cover for you, and when the score and time say the risk is worth it.

**2. Forwards cheating for offence.** A winger turning up ice before the team actually has possession, hunting a breakaway pass. When the puck does not come out, they are useless in both directions. Note the distinction made in [Winger](../positions/winger.md): *anticipating* possession while staying defensively accountable is a coached skill; abandoning coverage on a hope is not. The test is whether you can still get back if you are wrong.

**3. Neutral-zone turnovers.** The most direct cause of all, because a turnover in the middle of the ice hands the puck to a team already facing the right way with your forwards behind the puck. This is why every position document says the same thing: do not stickhandle through the middle under pressure, and do not force cross-ice passes in the neutral zone. See [Time and Space](../hockey-iq/time_and_space.md) and [Puck Support and Spacing](../hockey-iq/puck_support_and_spacing.md).

**4. A bad line change.** Changing while the puck is loose puts five players in transition and two of them on the ice cold. Change when the puck is in the offensive zone or moving away from your end. A five-player change off a clean dump-in deep in their end is normal; the same change with the puck loose at your own blue line is how a 3-on-2 is born. See [Game Management](game_management.md).

**5. Both defencemen below the puck in the offensive zone.** The subtlest of the five, because nothing looks wrong until the puck turns over and nobody is above it. **The failure is nobody above the puck; both defencemen going below is the usual way it happens.** A **five-player rotation**, or a standing green light for both defencemen, covers the line with whichever player is highest, and under a **single-high** structure the safety is the weak-side **winger**. Unless your team runs one of those, the rule from [Defender](../positions/defender.md) holds — one of the pair stays above the puck — so know *who* is above it before you go below it. See [Offensive Zone Play](offensive_zone_play.md).

## Communication During a Rush

```facts
Action: Short, early, loud — a call as it happens is information, a call after it happens is commentary
Action: Call the solution, not the problem — "back door" is useful, "watch out" is noise
Options: "Odd man!", "I've got the pass", "Take the shooter", "Back door!", "Pick them up", "Trailer" or "high", "Switch", "I'm back"
Key: "Back door!" — an attacker arriving unmarked at the far post — is the single most urgent call in defensive hockey; that ranking is coaching emphasis, not a counted one
Action: Name the number and the location rather than shouting vaguely: "pick up 12, high slot"
Mindset: Everyone talks, and the goalie has the best view of the rush and should be the loudest voice on the ice
```

A rush is loud, fast and facing away from most of your team. Talk is the only way anyone knows what is happening. Short, early, loud. Full detail in [On-Ice Communication](../foundation/on_ice_communication.md).

**The core vocabulary:**

- **"Odd man!"** — from whoever sees it first, usually a forward turning back. It tells the defencemen to stop thinking about stepping up and start thinking about lanes.
- **"I've got the pass"** — the defenceman on a 2-on-1 telling the goalie the contract is on. It lets the goalie commit fully to the shooter.
- **"Take the shooter"** — the same message aimed at the goalie, or from a defenceman to a partner assigning the puck carrier.
- **"Back door!"** — an attacker is arriving unmarked at the far post. The single most urgent call in defensive hockey — that ranking is coaching emphasis, not a counted one.
- **"Pick them up"** — a specific attacker is unmarked and someone needs to take them now. Better still, name the jersey number or the colour and the location: "pick up 12, high slot."
- **"Trailer"** or **"high"** — the late man is coming into the slot.
- **"Switch"** — two attackers crossed and you and your partner are trading assignments.
- **"I'm back"** or **"support"** — a backchecker announcing they have arrived, which lets a defenceman stop covering two people.

**Three rules about all of it:**

1. **Call early.** A call as it happens is information. A call after it happens is commentary.
2. **Call the solution, not the problem.** "Back door" is useful. "Watch out" is noise.
3. **Everyone talks.** The goalie has the best view of the whole rush and should be the loudest voice on the ice during one.

## From Rush Defence into Zone Coverage

```facts
Read: The attack losing forward momentum — a dump-in retrieved, a shot frozen or rebounded to the wall, a pass back to their point
Priority: Locate the net front before anything else, because during a rush nobody was assigned to it
Action: Switch from lanes to assignments in about one second — from "who is in my lane" to "who is my man"
Convention: In this guide's standard low zone collapse, a backchecking winger releases their man and gets back to the point; under man-on-man they stay with them — and those two are not the only coverages, so find out which yours plays
Action: Talk through the handover — every temporary assignment the rush created needs explicitly cancelling or confirming
Key: A rush is not over when the shot is saved; it is over when everybody has a mark
```

Stopping the rush is only half of it. The seconds immediately after are when teams that defended the rush well still concede — everyone relaxes, nobody picks up a mark, and the attackers who were rushing are now cycling against nobody.

**The transition, in order:**

1. **Recognise the moment it becomes a set situation.** The trigger is the attack losing its forward momentum: a dump-in retrieved in the corner, a shot stopped and frozen or rebounded to the wall, a pass back to their point, or the puck simply cycling below the goal line.
2. **Locate the net front before anything else.** The first thing to check after a rush dies is whether anybody is standing unmarked at your goalmouth, because during a rush nobody was assigned to them. This is the weak-side defenceman's job in the default system.
3. **Switch from lanes to assignments.** Rush defence is lane-based and situational. Defensive-zone coverage is structural. Everybody needs to move from "who is in my lane" to "who is my man / what is my area" in about one second.
4. **Backcheckers take up their zone roles, not the nearest puck.** In the standard **low zone collapse** with wingers high on the points, a backchecking winger who followed a man into the corner has to release them and get back to the point. Under **man-on-man** they stay with them. **Those two are not the only coverages, so find out which your team plays** — see [Defensive Zone Coverage](defensive_zone_coverage.md), which sets out the others.
5. **Talk through the handover.** "I've got the corner," "you take the point," "net front is mine." The rush created temporary assignments and every one of them needs to be explicitly cancelled or confirmed.
6. **Then look to exit.** Once the coverage is set, the job becomes the breakout — see [Breakouts](breakouts.md).

The habit to build: **a rush is not over when the shot is saved.** It is over when everybody has a mark.

## Common Mistakes

- **Standing still at the blue line** waiting for the attacker. Start skating backwards while the puck is still being retrieved in their end, and reset your gap on every regroup — you cannot close a gap from a standstill. That this is the commonest defenceman error is coaching emphasis, not a counted ranking.
- **Watching the puck instead of the chest.** Fix your eyes on the chest and hips. Your feet follow your eyes, and the puck is what they use to lie to you.
- **Lunging at a 1-on-1 carrier.** Contain instead: keep your feet, keep your stick in the lane, and let them run out of ice. A lunge is one bet on their next move, and if you are wrong they have the whole net.
- **Reading a head-down carrier as a licence to arrive harder.** Take the ice, not the man: a head down tells you they will not change direction, so your body gets there first — nothing more. ⚠️ **USA Hockey rules that exact hit charging, even without the two fast strides**; under Hockey Canada two strides are already the offence, and no book caps charging at a minor. Thresholds are in [Standing up (stepping up)](#standing-up-stepping-up).
- **Angling a breakaway carrier wide and then finishing with your body.** Angle with your route and take your stick to the puck. Hockey Canada and USA Hockey count an arm, hip or shoulder at the end of an angle as a body check, barred in their non-checking games. And you are arriving from **behind**, where no book writes a cheap tier for a hit that stays off the head: a hit into their back can end your game. Tiers are in [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md#checking-from-behind-and-what-the-onus-does-and-does-not-mean).
- **Riding a carrier into the boards in a league that does not permit it.** Steer them there with your route and pin the puck, not the player. ⚠️ **Pinning the player is a penalty wherever checking is barred** — in IIHF women's hockey up to a major plus an automatic game misconduct, and in a CARHA-affiliated adult league at least a double minor. **Check the game, not the age group:** a checking team playing a non-checking one is in a no-checking game for the night.
- **Riding a carrier into the boards in a league that *does* permit it, because they can see you coming.** Steer them to the wall with your route, then play the puck. ⚠️ **Driving a player dangerously into the boards can be boarding whether or not they saw you coming**, and no book caps boarding at a minor; several reach a match penalty. Rule text is in [1-on-1](#1-on-1).
- **Both defenders converging on a lone carrier on a 1-on-2.** One of you takes the puck — normally the one on the carrier's side — and calls "I've got them"; the other stays back and inside for the late attacker. Converging opens the ice behind you for exactly that player.
- **Sliding on a 2-on-1 before the pass is released, when your team plays the passing lane.** Hold the lane until the pass is gone — slide early and you open both options. Some teams coach a timed slide as the method, so find out which yours plays; where it is, keep your head up and out of the shot lane, and **ask the coach how to go down — it is not settled teaching here.** ⚠️ **A slide across an attacker's path is still a trip, and one that catches them low is clipping** — under USA Hockey a reckless one is a mandatory major that throws you out of the game, and plan on a match penalty and a likely multi-game suspension too. Tiers are in [The realistic alternative — the timed slide](#the-realistic-alternative--the-timed-slide).
- **Trying to block the shot on a 2-on-1.** Keep your stick in the passing lane and leave the shot to your goalie. Go for the block and you abandon the pass, and a half-block deflects a puck your goalie was tracking cleanly.
- **The goalie and the defenceman both hedging on a 2-on-1.** Settle it before the game, then say it on the rush — "I've got the pass" — so each of you commits to one option.
- **Defending the rush in silence.** Call it early and call the solution — "back door!", "switch", "trailer!", "I'm back". A call after the play is commentary, not information.
- **Both defenders drifting toward the puck on a 2-on-2**, leaving the far attacker with an open net. If your man is not the carrier, stay with them and let your partner play the puck, following them through a crossing or calling a clean "switch" — or, if your team plays it zone, hold your half and let the crossing trade your marks.
- **Collapsing into your own crease on a 3-on-2.** Retreat level with your partner at the same speed, keep the middle sealed between you, and force the shot from outside. Backing all the way to the goal line concedes the whole slot.
- **Chasing the puck carrier mid-rush as a backchecker** when your defenceman already has them, leaving your own man free. Some teams coach that pressure by design; the mistake is improvising it when nobody else expects it.
- **Not knowing which backchecking system your team runs.** The four named systems are different jobs, two of them ask the opposite thing of the first forward back, and under two the middle belongs to a defenceman. Ask which one you play, and where the hand-off happens.
- **Hounding a carrier into their own end boards and finishing into their back.** Take the puck, take the ice, or angle them off it — chasing hard is a skating instruction, not a licence to hit. ⚠️ **There is no cheap tier for this hit: a forceful hit into a player's back at the wall that stays off the head ends your game in five of the six books here, and under the IIHF plan on it; meant to injure, it is a match penalty in every book but the IIHF, and under USA Hockey a reckless one is too — with a likely multi-game suspension there either way.** Into the boards on a player who cannot protect themselves it is a mandatory match penalty under Hockey Canada and CARHA. Every tier is in [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md#checking-from-behind-and-what-the-onus-does-and-does-not-mean).
- **Losing the middle-lane driver.** They do not have the puck, so nobody looks at them, and they score. Whoever owns the middle in your system — the centre, in the version assumed here — takes that player before anything else.
- **Leaving the trailer to nobody.** The late attacker arrives in the high slot just as everybody has gone low. If you are the last backchecker and everyone else is marked, the trailer is yours. Defencemen see them first, so shout "trailer!".
- **Looking before skating on a backcheck**, costing two strides you never recover. Turn and sprint first, and look on the third or fourth stride.
- **Backchecking behind your man's shoulder** instead of to their inside hip. You cannot defend from behind. ⚠️ **And never finish the chase into their back or blind side** — take the ice, take the puck, or angle them off it. All six books make checking from behind its own rule, and none prices it cheaply for a hit that stays off the head; under Hockey Canada an open-ice blind-side hit is charging too. Tiers are in [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md#checking-from-behind-and-what-the-onus-does-and-does-not-mean).
- **The half-commit at the blue line** — drifting up, changing your mind, and arriving late with a huge gap. Worse than either full option. Pick one and go; when in doubt, back off with a tight gap.
- **Backing off with a growing gap.** Keep the gap closing as you retreat — a soft retreat hands the attacker speed *and* the zone, which is worse than either standing up or backing off properly.
- **Reaching around an attacker from behind on a breakaway.** Your stick goes on the puck and nothing else. Reaching around gives away a penalty shot, and with your goalie pulled an awarded goal. A *reckless* trip is not capped at a minor under the IIHF, and under USA Hockey it is a mandatory major that throws you out of the game — plan on a match penalty and a likely multi-game suspension too. A trip meant to injure is a mandatory match penalty in several books; Hockey Canada's book contradicts itself on that, so plan on one. Under Hockey Canada a slide that takes them below the hips is clipping, where the referee can throw you out with nobody hurt.
- **Reading "puck first" as a licence.** Get your blade to the puck and nothing else, and defend as though the penalty shot is still on. ⚠️ **The puck-first carve-out saves you the penalty shot under the NHL, IIHF and PWHL books and nothing more.** Under USA Hockey it never saves a slide — its Casebook calls sliding an opponent down tripping *"regardless of who gains possession of the puck"* — and a reckless trip there is a mandatory major that throws you out of the game, with a match penalty and a likely multi-game suspension to plan on; Hockey Canada's stops at the hips, below which it is clipping. **Whether USA Hockey's relief reaches its penalty shot at all is this guide's reading of two rules, not a USA Hockey ruling**, so do not play the gap. Detail is in [The carve-outs, and how far they reach in each book](#the-carve-outs-and-how-far-they-reach-in-each-book).
- **Relaxing the instant the rush is stopped**, before anybody has picked up the net front. Find the net front first, then switch from lanes to marks and talk through who has whom.
- **Pivoting early** and giving up your ability to react to a cutback. Delay the turn as long as you can, and when it comes let your hips lead it.
- **Leaving nobody above the puck in the offensive zone**, usually by both defencemen going below at once. Nothing looks wrong until the puck turns over. **Both going is not always the error** — a **five-player rotation** covers the line with whichever player is highest, and a **single-high** structure makes the safety the weak-side winger — so know **who** is above the puck before you go below it.

## Check yourself

*Answer each question before you go on — a real attempt, not a guess. Then go
and check, because the checking is the part that does the work. Producing an
answer and getting it wrong is worth more than recognising a right one, but only
when the right answer follows: the section named with each question, and the Key
Takeaways after them, are where you get it. Where the answer lives in another
document, that is named too.*

1. You are at your own blue line and you stand still to wait for the rush to
   arrive. Name the three things the gap hands the attacker, and say why
   accelerating backwards once they get there does not fix it. *(§Why a big gap
   hands the attacker three things at once, §Start your feet early)*

## Key Takeaways

1. **Stay on the defensive side of every attacker, all the time.** Anything that puts an attacker between you and your own net has already gone wrong.
2. **Start your feet early and keep the gap shrinking all the way in:** roughly two to three stick lengths in open ice, a stick and a half at the red line, one at your blue line. **Aim at the shape — closing, never growing — rather than the numbers**, which move with your speed and the attacker's skill.
3. **Read the chest and hips.** The puck, stick, hands and head can all lie; the torso cannot.
4. **Take the middle and give a carrier the wall — with your route, not your body.** Sit slightly inside them, stick flat in the passing lane, and pin the puck, not the player. ⚠️ **Check whether this game allows checking at all;** where it does, driving the carrier into the boards is boarding whether or not they saw you coming.
5. **2-on-1: take away the pass, give up the shot, tell your goalie the shooter is theirs.** Stick flat in the lane; no slide until the pass is gone. If your team slides instead, keep your head out of the shot lane and ask your coach how to go down. ⚠️ **A slide that takes them down is still a trip.**
6. **Know your job before each rush arrives.** On a 2-on-2 never both go to the puck — stay with your man, call "switch", or hold your half if you play zone. On a 3-on-2 retreat level with your partner, middle sealed. On a 3-on-1 take the middle and delay. **If everyone else is marked, the trailer is yours.**
7. **Chasing a lone attacker from behind, your stick goes on the puck and nothing else, and you stay on your feet.** ⚠️ **A foul from behind that denies the chance gives away a penalty shot, or an awarded goal with your goalie pulled.** Puck first does not excuse a low slide or a slew-foot. Keep defending until the whistle.
8. **On a backcheck, somebody owns the middle and nobody assumes it is somebody else.** The attacker driving the middle without the puck is the one most often missed; who owns that attacker is your team's choice. Skate to your man's inside hip, and do not chase a carrier your defenceman already has unless your team coaches it.
9. **From behind, pressure with feet and stick — never a player's back or blind side.** ⚠️ **A forceful hit into a player's back at the wall that stays off the head ejects you in five of the six books, puck or not; under the IIHF there is no minor, so plan on it.** Into the boards on a player who cannot protect themselves, it is a mandatory match under Hockey Canada and CARHA.
10. **At the blue line, step up or back off — never half.** Step up only when sure you will win the puck; otherwise back off with a tight gap. ⚠️ **A head-down carrier is not a licence to arrive harder** — USA Hockey calls that hit charging even without the two fast strides. **Take the ice, not the man.**
11. **The best rush defence is not creating the rush.** Pinches with no read, forwards leaving early, neutral-zone turnovers, badly timed changes, and nobody left above the puck — coaching emphasis, not a counted ranking. Know **who** is above the puck before you go below it; a five-player rotation or single-high structure changes who that is.
12. **The rush is not over when the shot is saved — it is over when everybody has a mark.** Find the net front first, because nobody was assigned to it during the rush, then pick up your mark under your team's zone coverage.
