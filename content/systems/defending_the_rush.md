## Overview

A **rush** is an attack that arrives before the defence is set, and it produces the most dangerous chances in hockey: the goalie is still moving and nobody has picked up an opponent yet.

**Stay between the puck and your own net — every attacker, all the time.**

**You cannot close the distance to a puck carrier from a standstill**, so start skating backwards while they are still collecting the puck at their end. Space gives them speed, time to lift their head and choose, and a clean lane to shoot down. Two to three stick lengths in open ice, about one by your own blue line, shrinking the whole way in — a guide rather than a law. And watch their chest: the puck, stick, hands and head can all lie.

**Two attackers and one of you: take away the pass, give up the shot** — your goalie can save a shot and cannot save a pass across the front of the net. Say it out loud, so you own the pass and the goalie owns the shooter, and do not commit to the lane until the pass is released. **That is not the whole of hockey** — it is a coaching choice with more than one legitimate answer, and the named alternative is a timed slide across the lane to force the shot — so find out which one yours plays. Most goalies want the version above; a few will ask you to pressure the puck, so ask yours.

**Backchecking is a team system with a name, not just effort.** Coaching material names four — defence early strike, wide-lane lock, midlane backcheck and hound the puck — and two of them are opposites on the question that matters most: whether the first forward back covers inside for the defence or chases the puck carrier. Ask which one you play, and ask what happens when the forward cannot catch the carrier.

**Steering a carrier toward the side wall is standard; riding them into it is not permitted everywhere.** In women's hockey, including every level of it in Britain, IIHF Rule 101.1 forbids using the boards to make contact with, push or pin an opponent competing for the puck — as does any league without body checking. Pin the puck, not the player.

**Chasing a lone attacker from behind, your stick goes on the puck and nothing else.** A hook, trip, hold or slash from behind that denies the chance is a penalty shot, not a two-minute penalty, in the NHL and IIHF books — and an awarded goal if your own net is empty. **Under USA Hockey, which most North American rec and youth players play, it is not automatic:** Rule 616(a) awards *"a penalty shot/optional minor penalty"*, so the referee may give a minor instead — and it counts a foul from **diagonally** behind, beyond your peripheral vision, as from behind.

**Best of all, do not create the rush.** Most attacks where they outnumber you were manufactured by your own team, and five causes account for nearly all of them — coaching emphasis, not a counted ranking, because no public data classifies odd-man rushes by the play that created them. They are: a defenceman stepping forward and missing the puck, a forward leaving early, a turnover in the middle, a line change with the puck loose, or both defencemen caught below the puck in the offensive zone.

## Why Rush Defence Matters More Than Anything You Do in the Zone

```facts
Key: On a rush your goalie is moving and cannot be set — and being set is what makes routine saves routine
Key: Nobody has a mark, assignments are formed on the fly, and the attackers pick the play rather than you
Priority: Preventing a rush chance is worth far more than defending one well
Action: Kill rushes by holding a tight gap and forcing dump-ins rather than by making recoveries afterwards
```

Set defensive-zone play gets most of the practice time. Rush defence deserves more, and here is why.

When a shot comes from a **set** defensive zone — your team in position, the puck cycling around the outside — the goalie is square to the puck, stationary, and already tracking. Five defenders are between the shooter and the net. The shooter is usually taking the shot the defence has decided to allow.

On a rush, none of that is true:

- **Your goalie is moving.** They are pushing across, backing in, resetting depth. A goalie who is mid-movement when the shot is released cannot be set. Being set — feet planted, square, weight balanced — is what makes routine saves routine.
- **The defence is not organised.** Nobody has a mark. Assignments are being formed on the fly at 30 kilometres an hour.
- **The attackers pick the play.** In a set zone you dictate what shot they get. On a rush, they do.
- **Everything happens facing the wrong way.** You are skating backwards and they are skating forwards. That asymmetry is why rush defence has its own technique.

The practical conclusion: **preventing a rush chance is worth far more than defending one well.** A defenceman who kills rushes by holding a tight gap and forcing dump-ins does more for the goals-against column than one who makes spectacular recoveries after they have already happened.

> **A note on numbers.** Rush and odd-man chances are widely treated by coaches and analysts as the highest-danger chances in hockey, and there is published tracking behind that. Sportlogiq data covering every NHL game from 2017-18 to 2020-21, presented at the Ottawa Hockey Analytics Conference and reported by [Alison Lukan for NHL.com](https://www.nhl.com/kraken/news/seattle-kraken-analytics-with-alison-chances-off-the-rush-328173944), puts a *"normal shot"* at an **8.15% likelihood of becoming a goal** — and says that *"the minute an opponent gets an extra skater on an attack, that likelihood almost triples."* The per-situation numbers sit in a Sportlogiq graphic rather than in the article's text, so the multiplier is quoted here in the words the source used and not as a precise figure.
>
> **The other half of the picture is how rare they are.** [Tulsky and colleagues](http://hockeyanalytics.com/Research_files/Using%20Zone%20Entry%20Data%20To%20Separate%20Offensive,%20Neutral,%20And%20Defensive%20Zone%20Performance.pdf) tracked which zone entries were odd-man rushes and found their overall impact *"negligible"* — they are *"relatively infrequent in today's NHL (representing less than 3% of all 5-on-5 zone entries)"* and the difference against an ordinary carry-in is *"significant but not overwhelming (0.78 shots versus 0.57)"*. Note that the two figures measure different things: 8.15% is per shot, 0.78 is shots per zone entry. Put together they say what coaches say — **a shot from an odd-man situation is worth roughly three ordinary ones** — while adding the part coaches skip, which is that you will face only a handful of them a game. Both halves matter here. The danger is why the habits below are worth drilling; the rarity is why *preventing* an odd-man rush beats defending one.

## The Organising Idea: Stay on the Defensive Side of the Puck

```facts
Key: Be between the puck and your net — every attacker, all the time
Position: On the line between your opponent and your own goal, which is what "defensive side" or "goal side" means
Risk: An attacker between you and your net has already gone wrong; everything after that is damage control
Key: "Don't get beat wide" and "don't get beat to the middle" are the same instruction
Read: When a rush goes wrong and you are not sure why, somebody lost the defensive side of somebody
```

Before any of the technique, one principle sits above everything else in this document.

**Be between the puck and your net. Always. Every attacker, all the time.**

"Defensive side" — sometimes called **goal side** — means you are on the line between your opponent and your own goal. Anything that puts an attacker between you and your net has already gone wrong, and everything after that point is damage control.

This one idea generates most of the specific advice below:

- Gap control is just staying on the defensive side while *close*.
- Backchecking on the inside of your man is just re-establishing the defensive side.
- Not chasing the puck carrier — under the lane-and-man systems taught here — is just refusing to give up the defensive side on someone else.
- "Don't get beat wide" and "don't get beat to the middle" are both the same instruction.

When a rush goes wrong and you're not sure why, this is nearly always the answer: somebody lost the defensive side of somebody.

## Gap Control — The Full Method

**Your gap is the distance between you and the puck carrier.** It is the single most important variable in rush defence, and it is almost entirely under your control.

This section expands the summary in [Defender](../positions/defender.md) — it does not replace it, and nothing here contradicts it.

### Why a big gap hands the attacker three things at once

```facts
Key: A large gap looks safe and is not — it hands the attacker three things at once
Risk: Space to accelerate into, so they arrive faster than you while you are moving backwards
Risk: Time to lift their head, find the trailer and the far winger, and pick the best of three options
Risk: A clean shooting lane from distance — a shot through your shins is not a real chance
Action: Close the gap and you remove speed, vision and shooting simultaneously
Key: Gap control is the highest-leverage habit in defensive hockey
```

A large gap looks safe. It is not: it is the root of most rush goals — coaching emphasis, not a counted share. A gap gives the attacker three things, all of them bad for you:

1. **Speed.** They can accelerate into the space between you. By the time they reach you they are moving faster than you, and you are moving backwards.
2. **Time to look.** With four metres of clear ice they can lift their head, find the trailer, find the far winger, and pick the best of three options. With one stick length they can only handle the puck.
3. **A shooting lane.** A shot from distance with a clean lane is a real chance. A shot from distance through your shins is not.

Close the gap and you take away all three at once. This is why gap control is the highest-leverage habit in defensive hockey — it is one adjustment that removes speed, vision and shooting simultaneously.

### Start your feet early — you cannot close a gap from a standstill

```facts
Never: Stand still and wait for the attacker — the gap opens at the moment you most need it closed
Action: Begin skating backwards while the puck is still being retrieved or regrouped in their end
Read: Read the pace of the attack, not the position of the puck — a slow regroup lets you skate up and squeeze
Action: Reset your gap on every regroup; each new attack is a new gap problem
Technique: C-cuts and backward crossovers, low stance, chest up, weight over the balls of the feet
Key: At full backwards speed you can hold any gap you like; at zero you can hold none at all
```

This is the most common defenceman error at every level, and the hardest to unlearn — coaching emphasis, not a counted ranking.

Standing still and waiting for the attacker feels like patience. It is the opposite. When they arrive you are at zero and they are at full speed, so the first thing you must do is accelerate backwards — and while you are accelerating, they are gaining. The gap opens *at the moment you most need it closed*.

Instead:

- **Begin skating backwards while the puck is still being retrieved or regrouped in their end.** Match their speed before they arrive rather than after.
- **Read the pace of the attack, not the position of the puck.** A slow regroup lets you skate up and squeeze. A quick outlet means you need to be moving already.
- **Reset your gap every time they regroup.** Neutral-zone attacks often turn back, swing, and come again. Each new attack is a new gap problem — skate up into them again rather than sitting where you finished the last one.
- **If you are already at full backwards speed when they arrive, you can hold any gap you like.** If you are at zero, you cannot hold any gap at all.

**Backward skating is the physical foundation.** C-cuts and backward crossovers to generate speed, low stance with chest up, weight over the balls of the feet. See [Skating](../technique/skating.md); [Defender](../positions/defender.md) also covers the essentials. You cannot play a gap you are not fast enough to hold.

### Target distances

```facts
Position: Roughly two to three stick lengths through the neutral zone in open ice, around a stick and a half as the carrier crosses the centre red line, and roughly a stick length at your own blue line
Key: The gap should be closing the whole way in; a gap that grows as they approach is a goal waiting to happen
Convention: Distances are a guide, not a law — they vary with your backward speed and the attacker's skill
Key: The blue line is an ally — squeezing tight at the line forces the carrier to slow for their trailers or enter alone
```

Distances are a guide, not a law, and they vary with your own backward speed and the attacker's skill.

- **Through the neutral zone, in open ice:** roughly **two to three stick lengths**. Close enough to be a problem, far enough to react to a change of direction.
- **As they cross the centre red line:** tighten to around **a stick and a half**. This is the moment the attack commits, and the moment your gap should be actively shrinking rather than holding.
- **At your blue line:** roughly **a stick length**. Close enough to reach the puck with a poke check; far enough that one deke doesn't put them past you.

Notice the direction of travel: the gap should be **closing** the whole way in. A gap that grows as they approach is a goal waiting to happen; a gap that shrinks forces them to make their decision earlier than they wanted to.

**The blue line is an ally here.** Under NHL rules an attacker is offside only when **both skates** completely cross the blue line before the puck completely crosses it — so a defender squeezing tight at the line can force the carrier to either slow down for their own trailers or enter alone. See [Rules Primer](../foundation/rules_primer.md) and [Zone Entries](zone_entries.md).

### Read the chest and hips, not the puck, the hands or the head

```facts
Read: The chest and hips cannot lie — the torso has to point where the body mass is actually going
Key: The puck, blade, hands, shoulders, head and eyes are all deception tools, and all of them can lie
Action: Fix your eyes on the numbers on the front of their jersey and use peripheral vision for the puck
Read: A dangle that doesn't move the hips is not a dangle — if the chest stays square they have gone nowhere
Never: Watch the puck — your feet follow your eyes, and your eyes have just been sent the wrong way
```

A skilled attacker will fake with everything they can move. The puck, the stick blade, the hands, the shoulders, the head, the eyes — all of these are **deception tools**, and all of them can lie.

Their **chest and hips cannot lie.** Their torso has to point where their body mass is actually going. Watch the middle of them, and use your peripheral vision for the puck.

Practically:

- **Fix your eyes on the numbers on the front of their jersey.** You will still see the puck; you just will not be led by it.
- **A dangle that doesn't move the hips is not a dangle.** If the puck goes wide but the chest stays square, they have not gone anywhere.
- **Watching the puck is how you get beaten clean** — your feet follow your eyes, and your eyes have just been sent the wrong way.

### Stick on the ice, out front, in the passing lane

```facts
Key: Your stick is a passive weapon — used well it removes options without committing your body
Position: Blade flat on the ice, out in front, in the lane between the carrier and their most dangerous support option
Technique: One hand on the stick for reach, two hands for control — but never with the blade in the air
Action: Poke check only when you can reach the puck, not as a hope
Never: Telegraph the poke check. Hide it until you use it instead, or they pull the puck back and go around your reach
Risk: A missed poke check becomes a lever the attacker skates around, and often a hooking or tripping penalty
```

Your stick is a passive weapon. Used well it removes options without committing your body.

- **Blade flat on the ice**, out in front of you, in the lane between the carrier and their most dangerous support option.
- **One hand on the stick** for reach when you need it, two hands when you need control — but never with the blade in the air. A stick off the ice defends nothing.
- **Poke check only when you can reach the puck**, not as a hope. A missed poke check turns your stick into a lever the attacker skates around, and often into a hooking or tripping penalty.
- **Hide the poke check until you use it.** Telegraphing it lets the carrier pull the puck back and go around your reach.

### Angle to take the middle and force them outside

```facts
Key: The middle of the ice is the dangerous ice — shots from the slot beat goalies, shots from the wall mostly do not
Position: Boardside shoulder roughly on the middle of their chest, your body between them and the middle of the ice
Never: Line up chest-to-chest, or put your inside shoulder on their chest — that slides you wide and opens the middle
Action: Force them outside, then close the outside — ride them further wide and away from the net
Key: The boards are your second defender — no ice on one side, a shrinking angle, and nowhere to cut back
Mindset: Good rush defence is choosing which thing to concede, not taking everything away
```

**The middle of the ice is the dangerous ice.** Shots from the slot beat goalies; shots from below the faceoff dots on the wall mostly do not.

So do not line up chest-to-chest with the carrier. Position yourself slightly to the **inside** — your **boardside shoulder** (the shoulder nearer the wall) roughly on the middle of their chest — so that your body sits between them and the middle of the ice, and the only route they can see is toward the boards. Getting this backwards and putting your *inside* shoulder on their chest slides your torso to the boards side and opens the middle, which is exactly the lane you are trying to take away.

![](diagram:rush-gap-and-angle)

- **The boards are your second defender.** A carrier pushed to the wall has no ice on one side, a shrinking angle to the net, and nowhere to cut back.
- **Force them to the outside, then close the outside.** Steering wide and then giving them the wall lane is only half the job — once they are committed outside, ride them further outside and away from the net.
- **You are giving them something on purpose.** Good rush defence is not taking everything away; it is choosing which thing to concede.

### Pivot late

```facts
Action: Delay the moment you turn and skate forwards with them as long as you possibly can
Risk: Once you pivot to forwards you can no longer react, and a change of direction beats you
Technique: Hips lead the pivot, not shoulders — turning your upper body first opens the gate early
Convention: Pivot to the boards side rather than the middle — a default, not a rule; if beaten, be beaten toward the wall
Read: Some coaches want a middle pivot when turning away would take your eyes off the puck, or when you have inside help
```

At some point you must turn and skate forwards with them. Delay that moment as long as you possibly can.

- Once you pivot to forwards, **you can no longer react** — you are committed to their current direction, and a change of direction beats you.
- **Hips lead the pivot, not shoulders.** Turning your upper body first opens the gate before your feet are ready.
- **Pivot to the boards side rather than the middle — as a default, not a rule.** If you are beaten, be beaten toward the wall. But some coaches want a middle pivot on specific reads, such as when turning away would take your eyes off the puck, or when you have inside help and the greater danger is the attacker cutting behind you. Find out which your coach wants, and be competent both ways: the correct direction is set by where the attacker is, not by which way you can pivot. See [Skating → Which way to pivot](../technique/skating.md#which-way-to-pivot--a-coaching-default-not-a-law).

## Backchecking — Lanes and Assignments

**Backchecking** is racing back toward your own end when your team loses the puck. It is the difference between an odd-man rush and an even one, and it is the most commonly under-performed job in hockey — coaching emphasis, not a counted one. (Do not confuse it with **forechecking**, which is pressure in *their* end.)

### The three lanes

```facts
Position: Three vertical lanes — two outside along the boards, one middle between the faceoff dots
Convention: The standard assignment is the centre in the middle lane and the wingers in the outside lanes
Key: Of the three lanes the middle is the most dangerous to leave unattended — the middle-lane driver is the attacker skating hard through it without the puck
Key: That the middle-lane driver is the assignment most often missed is coaching emphasis, not a counted ranking
Read: They are missed because they do not have the puck, and eyes go to the puck
Options: Other teams give the middle to the first man back, or pick up man-on-man from the puck outward — find out which yours uses
Key: Under every variant, somebody owns the middle and nobody assumes it is somebody else
```

Think of the ice as three vertical lanes running the length of the rink: the two outside lanes along the boards, and the middle lane between the faceoff dots.

The standard assignment, and the one most teams use:

- **The centre takes the middle lane** — and, crucially, **the middle-lane driver**: the attacker skating hard through the middle without the puck.
- **The two wingers take the outside lanes**, each picking up the attacker on their side.

**Of the three lanes the middle is the most dangerous to leave unattended, and the middle-lane driver is the assignment most often missed — that ranking is coaching emphasis, not a counted one.** They arrive into the slot unmarked, at speed, with a clean look. They are missed because they do not have the puck, and eyes go to the puck. If you are the centre coming back, that player is yours before anything else. See [Center](../positions/center.md) and [Winger](../positions/winger.md).

![](diagram:rush-backcheck-lanes)

> **This is a coaching choice, not a law.** Most teams assign lanes by position as above. Others assign by **first man back takes the middle**, regardless of who it is — common when the centre is the one caught deep, and common in rec hockey where lines are less structured. A third variant has the backcheckers pick up **man-on-man from the puck outward**, taking whoever is nearest. Find out which one your team uses. See [Neutral Zone Systems](neutral_zone_systems.md).
>
> Under every variant, two things hold: somebody owns the middle, and nobody assumes it is somebody else.
>
> **And there is a layer above this one.** The three assignments here all come from the same family — forwards taking lanes while the defence takes the puck. Two of the four named team systems set out later in this section do not work that way at all: under the **wide-lane lock** a defenceman slides to mid-ice and owns the middle, and under **hound the puck** the first forward is on the carrier and both defencemen hold inside. So "which lane is mine?" is the right question only once you know which system you are in. Read the two together.

### Come back on the defensive side of your man

```facts
Never: Chase from behind their shoulder — that is jogging, and you cannot defend anything from behind
Action: Take a route that puts you inside them, between them and your net, even if it is slightly longer
Position: Arrive at their inside hip, not their back pocket, so your stick reaches the passing lane
Options: If you are genuinely beaten and can only chase, chase the stick — blade under theirs or on the puck
Risk: Reaching around their body from behind is a hook or a trip, and near your own net that gets expensive
```

Chasing an attacker from behind their shoulder is not backchecking — it is jogging. You cannot defend anything from behind.

- **Take a route that puts you inside them**, between them and your net, even if that route is slightly longer.
- **Arrive at their inside hip**, not their back pocket. From there your stick reaches the passing lane and your body is already goal side.
- **If you are genuinely beaten and can only chase**, chase the *stick*, not the body: get your blade under theirs or on the puck. Reaching around their body from behind is a hook or a trip, and near your own net that gets expensive (see the breakaway section below).

### Skate first, then look

```facts
Action: Turn and sprint first, and look on the third or fourth stride
Never: Turn your head to watch what happens on a turnover — it costs you two strides you never get back
Key: The picture will still be there when you look up, and you will be five metres further into the play
Mindset: Full-speed backchecking is a conditioning issue as much as a hockey one
```

The instinct on a turnover is to turn your head and watch what happens. That costs you two strides you never get back.

**Turn and sprint first. Look on the third or fourth stride.** The picture will still be there when you look up, and you will be five metres further into the play. Full-speed backchecking is a conditioning issue as much as a hockey one — see [Conditioning and Recovery](../off-the-ice/conditioning_and_recovery.md).

### Do not decide mid-rush to chase the puck carrier when your D has them

```facts
Never: Decide mid-rush to sprint in and help a defenceman who is already engaged with the carrier and playing a good gap
Risk: Two defenders on one attacker, and your actual assignment left unmarked
Action: Skate past the puck battle to the unmarked man — your job is the player nobody has
Mindset: Trusting your defenceman to win their own 1-on-1 is what turns a 3-on-2 into three 1-on-1s
Options: Some teams run the opposite by design — the first forward back pressures the carrier and the defenceman holds inside instead
Never: Turn that pressure into a hit from behind — where pressuring the carrier is your system it is your feet and your stick, and nothing else
Key: Under either system somebody has the carrier and somebody has the unmarked man, and it is agreed before the rush rather than during it
```

If a defenceman is already engaged with the carrier and playing a good gap, sprinting in to help them is worse than useless — it leaves your actual assignment unmarked and puts two defenders on one attacker.

**Your job as a backchecker is the player nobody has.** Skate past the puck battle to the unmarked man. Trusting your defenceman to win their own 1-on-1 is a skill, and it is what turns a 3-on-2 into three 1-on-1s.

> **This is a coaching choice, not a law — and the choice is about *who decides*.** The instruction above belongs to the family of systems where the backcheckers take lanes and men, and the defence takes the puck. **Other teams coach the reverse on purpose:** the first forward back **pressures the puck carrier** all the way in, and the defenceman adjusts to hold inside position and support rather than engaging. That is a legitimate system with its own logic — relentless pressure on the puck, and a defenceman who is never beaten wide because they never have to commit. **What it never means is finishing into the back of a player skating at their own end boards — see the subsection below.**
>
> What is wrong under *both* systems is **improvising**. The failure this section is really about is a forward deciding in the middle of a rush to abandon their man and double the puck, because that is the version nobody else on the ice is expecting. **Find out which your team plays, and if your team has no answer, that is the thing to raise** — teams that leave it to players to read in the moment get two players on the carrier, or none.

### The four named backchecking systems

```facts
Key: Backchecking is a team system with named variants, not only effort — which one you play changes your job on every rush
Options: Defence early strike — the defencemen tighten up early instead of sinking, and the wide one strikes across to take away the time the opposition expected on the way out
Options: Wide-lane lock — the first forward back takes an outside lane, owns everything between the dots and the boards on that side, and stays deeper than their man
Options: Midlane backcheck — the forwards all come back through the middle so the defencemen can play the outside, and the first one back covers inside for them
Options: Hound the puck — the first forward back chases the carrier and keeps pressuring after a pass, while the defencemen hold inside and protect the space between the dots
Convention: Midlane and hound the puck are opposites — one covers your defence, the other pressures the puck — and which one you run follows your coach's appetite rather than the situation in front of you
Action: Ask which one your team plays, and ask what happens when the forward cannot catch the carrier — that hand-off is where the confusion lives
```

Everything above is about *your* job on a backcheck. This is the layer above it: the shape your whole team is running, which decides what your job actually is. **Coaching material names four, and they are genuinely different — not degrees of effort.** Whether these four are all of them, or which is commonest, is not something any published count establishes.

**Defence early strike.** This one starts before the rush does, in your own end. Rather than sinking deep in defensive-zone coverage, the two defencemen **tighten up early** and may step up on the opposition forwards. When the other team breaks out, the wide defenceman **strikes across** the ice at the puck carrier, and as they go, the strong-side partner shifts inside to cover. The point is surprise: it takes away the time and space the opposition expected to have as they left their zone.

**Wide-lane lock.** The first forward back immediately takes an **outside lane** and stays there. They may skate forwards or backwards, but must always be able to see both the puck and any opponent in that lane, and they own **anybody between the dots and the boards on their side**. One defenceman slides to mid-ice and takes that lane; the other takes the strong side where the carrier is. The ice ends up divided into three, each with an owner, and **three players are standing up the blue line** while the remaining two forwards trail the play and pressure it. If this is your system, your job as the locking forward is to deny the pass to your side and stay *deeper* than your man.

**Midlane backcheck.** The opposite instinct: the high forward gets to **mid-ice** and comes back hard through the centre seam, and so does everybody else. All three forwards return through the middle, which frees the defencemen to play the outside. The first forward back **stays between the puck and the net** — so if a defenceman is beaten inside, there is already somebody there to cover it. It also squeezes the carrier: with no space inside, they are pushed wide. Come all the way to the low slot before moving out to support; the second and third forwards back look for late attackers and stop at the top of the circles.

**Hound the puck.** Explicitly the opposite of the midlane backcheck. The first forward back **chases the puck carrier as hard as they can**, and keeps pressuring the puck even after a pass. The defencemen hold **inside position**, protecting the space between the dots. Coaches who like it tend to want pressure everywhere on the ice.

**What each one costs you.** The lock and the midlane backcheck both put bodies between the puck and your net, and both are forgiving of a mistake by somebody else — the price is that you concede the puck carrier time. Hounding the puck denies that time and keeps the opposition under pressure the length of the neutral zone, and the inside defencemen kill the mid-ice pass — but it has a specific failure that the others do not: **when the forward cannot catch the carrier by the blue line, or cannot get to the pass in time, the forward and the defencemen can end up unsure who has whom.**

That failure is the reason to ask the follow-up question. Teams that run it well carry a hand-off rule so nobody is guessing — one such rule is that **the forward hounds the puck as far as the red line, and if they are not level with the carrier by then, the defence takes over and the forward peels off to a lane or the wide attacker.** If your team pressures the puck and has never said where the hand-off happens, that is worth raising at practice rather than discovering at speed.

#### What hounding never means

```facts
Never: Finish into the back of a carrier heading for their own end boards. Take the puck, take the ice, or angle them off it instead, whichever book you are under
Rule: There is no minor-penalty option for checking from behind — NHL Rule 43.2, "There is no provision for a minor penalty for checking from behind", and IIHF Rule 43.2, "serious and dangerous, therefore there is no option to award a minor penalty"
Rule: The major and the automatic game misconduct that follow it are NHL Rules 43.3 and 43.5, and IIHF Rule 43.3
Rule: USA Hockey 604(a) bans body checking at 12 & under and below, in all Girls'/Women's and non-check Adult classifications; Hockey Canada 7.3(a) bans INTENTIONAL checks, bumps, shoves and pushes at U13 and below, in female hockey and in any Member-approved division — incidental contact is not a penalty
Rule: Hockey Canada does not cap its body-checking ban at a minor where Rule 7.3 applies — U13 and below, female hockey, and any Member-approved division. Rule 7.3(b) lets the referee assess a major plus a game misconduct on the degree of violence, and makes both mandatory if the check injures
Rule: The Hockey Canada body-checking ladder does not stop at the major — where Rule 7.3 applies (U13 and below, female hockey, and any division a Member of Hockey Canada approves), 7.3(e) makes the Game Misconduct automatic on any body-checking major
Rule: Where Hockey Canada bars body-checking — U13 and below, female hockey, and any Member-approved division — 7.3(c) assesses a Match penalty to a player who "deliberately attempts to or deliberately injures an opponent by body-checking"
Rule: In women's hockey — every level of it in Britain — IIHF Rule 101.1 allows bodychecking "when there is a clear intention of playing the puck", lets a player hold ground, and makes "any move by a Player to step or glide into an opposing Player" at least a minor
Never: Turn hounding into contact where you may not body-check at all — it is your feet, your stick and your angle, and nothing else
```

⚠️ **What hounding never means.** You are arriving at speed behind a player skating toward their own end boards, and that is the exact geometry the checking-from-behind rules exist for. **Take the puck, take the ice, or angle him off it — never finish into their back.** There is no cheap version of getting this wrong: the NHL's Rule 43.2 states that *"There is no provision for a minor penalty for checking from behind"*, and the IIHF's Rule 43.2 that the infraction is *"serious and dangerous, therefore there is no option to award a minor penalty"*. It is a major, with a game misconduct attached. The obligations of the arriving player are owned by [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md), and they apply to a backchecker exactly as they apply to a forechecker — [Forechecking Systems](forechecking_systems.md) makes the same point about arriving on a defenceman facing his own wall.

**And check whether you may body-check at all — under the book you actually play.** **Hockey Canada Rule 7.3** bars it at U13 and below, throughout female hockey, and in any division a Member has approved — 7.3(b) escalating to a major plus game misconduct on the degree of violence, and **mandatorily** if the check injures. **And the ladder has two rungs above that one.** **7.3(e)** removes the referee's discretion over the ejection: *"A Game Misconduct penalty must be assessed any time a Major penalty is assessed for body-checking, as detailed under Rule 7.3 (b)."* **7.3(c)** is a match penalty — *"A Match penalty will be assessed to any player who deliberately attempts to or deliberately injures an opponent by body-checking."* So in a division where checking is barred, the same act runs from two minutes to a match penalty on how violent it was and whether it hurt somebody, and the middle rung takes you out of the game whether or not the referee wanted it to. Under USA Hockey's Rule 604(a), body checking is prohibited in *"the 12 & under youth age classifications and below, all Girls'/Women's age classifications and all non-check Adult classifications"* — which covers a large share of the people reading this. Where that applies, hounding the puck is entirely a matter of your stick, your feet and your angle, and the instruction to chase hard is an instruction about **skating**, not about contact. **In women's hockey — which is every level of it in Britain — the governing text is IIHF Rule 101.1, not the USA Hockey one.** Read it whole, because both halves matter. It permits contact: *"In Women's Hockey 'bodychecking' is allowed when there is a clear intention of playing the puck or attempting to 'gain possession' of the puck…"* — the sentence goes on to except the situation the rest of the rule describes, which is the step-in below. It lets you hold your ground — *"No Player is required to move out of the way of an oncoming Player to avoid a collision."* And it closes the gap a hounding forward is most likely to fall into: *"Any move by a Player to step or glide into an opposing Player will be assessed at least a minor penalty (2') for an 'illegal hit'."* So hounding is legal skating, and contact made going for the puck is legal too; stepping into the carrier for its own sake is not.

## The Numerical Situations

The number always reads **attackers on defenders** — a 2-on-1 is two attackers against one defender.

### 1-on-1

```facts
Goal: A shot from a place your goalie is comfortable with — not a hit and not a turnover
Action: Steer them to a bad angle, force them wide, and keep forcing
Position: Stick in the lane, on the ice, out front — passing options are arriving behind the play
Never: Lunge — it is a single bet on their next move, and if you are wrong they have the whole net
Action: Ride them into the boards, your second defender, until their options collapse to zero
Rule: IIHF women's Rule 101.1 prohibits using the boards to eliminate an opponent from the play, push her into the boards, or pin her along the boards — pin the puck, not the player
Rule: An illegal hit in women's hockey is a minor, or a major plus an automatic game misconduct (IIHF Rule 101.1) — and in British women's hockey any major ejects you at every age, adult included: In-House Rule 101 applies "in all cases" and states no age scope of its own
Never: Ride an opponent into the boards in any non-check adult or youth league — the instruction above is for checking leagues only
Priority: Position first, contact second — a body check is a bonus, not a plan
```

You against the carrier. This is gap control applied, and it is the situation everything else is built from.

- **Steer them to a bad angle.** Your target is not a hit and not a turnover — it is a shot from a place your goalie is comfortable with. Force them wide and keep forcing.
- **Stick in the lane, on the ice, out front.** Even in a 1-on-1 there are passing options arriving behind the play.
- **Do not lunge.** A lunge is a single bet on their next move. If you are wrong, you are out of the play entirely and they have the whole net. Contain, keep your feet, and let them run out of ice.
- **Use the boards as a second defender.** Ride them into the wall and their options collapse to zero — no cutback, no shooting angle, no pass across. **In women's hockey this instruction does not apply, and that includes every level in Britain.** IIHF Rule 101.1: two or more players competing for possession are *"not allowed to use the boards to make contact with an opponent to eliminate her from the play, push her into the boards, or pin her along the boards"* — a minor, or *"a major penalty (5') and automatic game misconduct penalty"* — **so under the IIHF book the major is the ejection in itself, at every level of women's hockey, adult included.** **And the British amendment reaches every age too, rather than juniors only.** In-House **Rule 101** ejects any player assessed a major *"in all cases"* and states no age scope of its own; the age-scoped provision is the bullet beside it, which applies IIHF Rule 201.1 to *"all non-adult age categories of Women's Hockey"* and governs only itself. **Do not read the Adult definition in Rule 100.1 as an exemption from the ejection** — it says who is in which category, not who the ejection reaches. [UK and England Rules](../foundation/uk_rules.md) sets out that reading in full, and states plainly what has not been established about how it is called on the ice. **Pin the puck, not the player** ([Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md)). The same applies in any non-check adult or youth league.
- **A body check is a bonus, not a plan.** Missing a hit on a rush is far more costly than not attempting one. Position first, contact second. See [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md).

### 1-on-2

```facts
Never: Both defenders converge on the carrier — that opens the ice behind you for a late second attacker
Action: One defender takes the puck, normally the one on the carrier's side, and plays it as a 1-on-1 with a very tight gap
Position: The other stays back and inside, covering the trailer who has not arrived yet
Key: The engaged defender can afford to be aggressive — a miss leaves your partner in a 1-on-1 rather than nothing
Action: Call it — "I've got him" from the engaging defender, so the other holds
```

One attacker, two defenders. The luxury case — and one that teams waste constantly.

The failure mode is both defenders converging on the carrier. That opens the ice behind you for a late-arriving second attacker and turns a comfortable situation into a scramble.

- **One defender takes the puck** — normally the one on the carrier's side — and plays it exactly as a 1-on-1, with a very tight gap because there is backup.
- **The other stays back and inside**, watching for the second wave. You are not a spectator; you are covering the trailer who has not arrived yet.
- **The engaged defender can afford to be aggressive.** This is the one rush situation where stepping up hard is genuinely low risk, because a miss leaves your partner in a 1-on-1 rather than nothing.
- **Call it.** "I've got him" from the engaging defender, so the other holds.

### 2-on-1 — the headline situation

```facts
Convention: Take away the pass and give up the shot — your goalie can save the shot and cannot save the pass; the realistic alternative is a timed slide to take the pass away outright and force the shot, so find out which your team plays
Read: A shooter walking into the slot unpressured with no realistic pass available is no longer a 2-on-1, so close on the puck
Action: Say the contract out loud — you own the pass, the goalie owns the shooter, and neither of you covers both
Position: Sit in the passing lane, roughly on the line between the two attackers, biased slightly toward the middle
Technique: Stick flat on the ice on the passing side, blade extended into the lane, arm long — your stick takes the pass, not your body
Action: Take away the far attacker's stick as you get close to the net, where the danger converts into a tap-in
Never: Slide before the pass is released while you are playing the lane — you remove yourself from the play and open both options at once
Never: Try to block the shot — you abandon the pass, and a half-block deflects a puck your goalie was tracking
```

Two attackers, one defender. This is the situation every defenceman is judged on. **There is more than one legitimate way to play it**, and the one taught here is the one below; the alternative is set out at the end of this section, because which of the two you play is a decision for your team rather than for you in the middle of a rush.

> ### **TAKE AWAY THE PASS. GIVE UP THE SHOT.**

**Why.** Your goalie can save the shot and cannot save the pass.

- On the **shot**, the goalie can see the puck the whole way, is square to the shooter, and is set. That is a save they are trained to make and expect to make — even a good shot from the wing.
- On the **cross-ice pass**, the goalie must push all the way across the crease while the puck travels faster than they can move. They arrive late, off balance, and often still sliding when the shot comes. A backdoor tap-in into an open half of the net is essentially unsaveable.

So the arithmetic is not "which is the better chance for them" — it is "which chance can my goalie actually stop." One of them, and only one.

**The goalie/defenceman contract — say it out loud.** This is a two-person agreement, and it only works if both people know it:

- **The defenceman owns the pass.** You take the lane and never leave it.
- **The goalie owns the shooter.** They play the puck carrier as if the second attacker does not exist — square to the shot, correct depth, set and stopped before release.
- Neither of you covers both. If the goalie also cheats toward the pass, the shooter gets an open net; if you also drift toward the shooter, the pass is open. **Both of you hedging is how a 2-on-1 becomes a goal.**

Talk about this with your goalie before the game, not during it. Most goalies want it exactly as described, but a few — especially at rec level, and especially against a weak shooter — will ask you to pressure the puck instead. See [Goaltender](../positions/goaltender.md).

![](diagram:rush-2-on-1)

**Your positioning, concretely:**

1. **Sit in the passing lane**, roughly on the line between the two attackers, biased slightly toward the middle of the ice.
2. **Stick flat on the ice on the passing side**, blade extended into the lane, arm long. Your stick, not your body, is what takes the pass away — that way your feet stay free.
3. **Match the retreat speed of the attack** and stay on the defensive side of both of them. Do not backpedal so fast that you end up behind your own goal line, and do not stall so the shooter walks in on top of your goalie.
4. **Take away the far attacker's stick as you get close to the net.** Late in the rush, the danger converts from a pass into a tap-in — get your blade to their stick.
5. **Do not slide. Not until the pass is released.** A slide is a one-time, irreversible commitment: if the pass has not gone, you have removed yourself from the play and both options are now open. If the pass *is* released and you can genuinely intercept it, sliding into the lane is correct. **This instruction belongs to the method above** — playing the lane and living with the shot. It is not a universal prohibition on sliding, and the section below explains the system in which an early slide is the plan rather than the error.
6. **Do not try to block the shot.** Blocking the shot means abandoning the pass, and worse, a half-block deflects a puck your goalie was tracking. See the shot-blocking section in [Defender](../positions/defender.md).

**The one honest caveat:** if the shooter is walking into the slot completely unpressured with no realistic pass available — for example the second attacker has been picked up by a backchecker — the situation is no longer a 2-on-1 and you should close on the puck. Read what is actually in front of you rather than the label.

#### The realistic alternative — the timed slide

```facts
Convention: Some teams play the opposite commitment — a timed slide across the passing lane to remove the pass outright and force the shot
Key: What does not change is the contract — the goalie still owns the shooter and you still own the pass; what changes is how you take the pass away
Risk: Going early or sliding past the lane leaves you on the ice and out of the play, and you cannot contest the rebound from there
Never: Go down with your head in the shot lane — this system exists to force a shot, so a shot is what you are going down in front of
Action: Ask the coach who asks you to slide exactly how they want you to go down — no method for going to the ice on a pass lane is set out here, and that part is what hurts you
Rule: A body placed across an attacker's path that causes them to trip and fall is a minor under NHL Rule 57.2; under the IIHF book the referee may assess a major (57.3), or a major plus a game misconduct (57.4), for reckless endangerment
Risk: A late or wild slide is the one that trips somebody — but the timing is not the whole of the exposure
Rule: Under USA Hockey, leaving your feet and sliding an opponent down is tripping "regardless of who gains possession of the puck" — sole exception, knees dropped to block a shot, not puck-first (Casebook 639, Sit. 4); one that recklessly endangers is a mandatory major plus game misconduct (639(b))
Rule: A slide catching an opponent low reads as clipping, not tripping — NHL and IIHF 44.1, "throwing the body across or below the knees of an opponent from any direction", though neither names it; Hockey Canada 8.7 draws it at the hips; NHL 44.3 mandates the major on injury, 44.5 the game misconduct
Rule: IIHF clipping needs no injury — 44.3 reaches a major plus an automatic game misconduct, at the referee's discretion, where the clip "recklessly endangers" an opponent and a minor would not sanction it sufficiently; on the NHL side 44.4 adds a match penalty at the referee's discretion
Action: Ask which of the two your team plays, and settle it with your goalie before the game rather than during it
```

Some coaches teach the opposite commitment: instead of holding the lane and living with the shot, the defender **times a slide across the passing lane** to remove the pass outright and force the carrier to shoot. **This is a different system, not a mistake.** Its trade is explicit — the pass is gone, but you are on the ice and out of the play if you go early or slide past the lane, and you cannot contest the rebound afterwards.

Note what does *not* change between the two: **the goalie still owns the shooter, and you still own the pass.** What changes is how you take the pass away and what it costs you if you are wrong.

**One thing holds whichever system endorses it: your head stays up and out of the shot lane.** Beyond that, **do not carry the going-to-the-ice method from [Defender](../positions/defender.md) into this.** That method is written for dropping in front of a *shot*, and a slide across a *passing* lane is a different action on a different axis. **What this guide will not do is tell you how to go down.** The coaching source this alternative comes from describes the slide as a flat one with the feet toward your own net, and names its three failure modes — going too early, sliding past the lane, and being unable to contest the rebound afterwards. That is a description of the play, not a technique for surviving it, and no method for going to the ice on a pass lane is set out here. **How you go down is the part that hurts you**, so ask the coach who is asking you to do it rather than working it out at speed. And a body on the ice across an attacker's path is a trip if they go over you. That is a minor under **NHL Rule 57.2** — *"A minor penalty shall be imposed on any player who shall place his stick or any portion of his body in such a manner that it shall cause his opponent to trip and fall"* — and the book British readers play under goes further: **IIHF Rule 57.3** allows a major at the referee's discretion where a player *"recklessly endangers the fouled Player"*, and **57.4** a major plus a game misconduct. ⚠️ **And do not read that exposure as purely a matter of timing.** **USA Hockey's Casebook rules on exactly this play**, at Rule 639, Situation 4: *"When a player leaves their feet and slides into an opponent, thereby causing them to fall, a penalty for tripping must be called regardless of who gains possession of the puck."* Note that it is written for **an opponent** and not for the puck carrier, and that its only stated exception is not a puck-first one — *"a player [who] drops to their knees to block a shot and their momentum carries them into the player shooting the puck."* ⚠️ **And under the NHL and IIHF books a slide that catches them low is not tripping at all.** NHL and IIHF Rule 44.1 define clipping as *"the act of throwing the body across or below the knees of an opponent from any direction"*. *(That those words reach a slide across a passing lane, and not only a check, is this guide's reading of them — neither book names the play.)* And the tiers above the minor are not the tripping rule's. **NHL 44.3 makes the major mandatory** where the clipping check injures, 44.5 makes a game misconduct mandatory with it, and 44.4 puts a match penalty in the referee's discretion for attempting to or deliberately injuring. **The IIHF book needs no injury at all, and its major is not mandatory:** 44.3 lets the referee assess a major plus an **automatic** game misconduct where the clip *"recklessly endangers"* the opponent and *"the Player would not be sufficiently sanctioned by imposing a minor penalty"*. ⚠️ **So the trigger is not the same in the two books — injury under the NHL, reckless endangerment under the IIHF — and the ceiling is not reached the same way either.** ⚠️ **USA Hockey is the one book of the four that makes that major mandatory with nobody hurt, and its purpose requirement does not soften it.** Its **639 Note 2** does need the purpose before the act is labelled *clipping* — *"deliberately leaving the feet or lowering the body for the purpose of making contact with the opponent at or below the knees"* — **but under that book the label does not gate the tier.** **639(b) hangs off tripping as well as clipping**: *"A major penalty plus a game misconduct penalty **shall be assessed** to any player who recklessly endangers an opponent as a result of tripping, clipping or leg checking."* And its own Casebook already calls a slide that puts an opponent down tripping, whoever ends up with the puck. **So whichever of the two names the referee reaches for, 639(b) is there, and no purpose has to be shown for it.** **639(c)** goes further still — *"A match penalty for reckless endangerment may also be assessed under this rule"* — and **Casebook 639, Situation 5** puts the three together: *"Leg checking, clipping and slew footing are the three common tripping actions calling for a more severe penalty to be assessed."* **So under USA Hockey a reckless low slide is a major and an ejection with nobody hurt and no purpose shown.** And Hockey Canada reaches it through **8.7**, which draws its line at the **hips** rather than the knees — *"where a player uses their body to make contact below an opponent's hips"* — and so is the widest of the four on what counts as low; its own major, though, is discretionary *"based on the degree of violence of impact (including the impact with the ice or boards)"* and mandatory only where the clip injures (8.7(b)). **A legal, well-timed slide is routine; a late or wild one is not a free mistake, and a low one is a different offence with a higher ceiling.**

**So ask, and ask early.** Which of the two your team plays is a coaching decision, and the worst outcome is not picking the less fashionable one — it is you and your goalie having different answers. Both of you hedging in different directions is how a 2-on-1 becomes a goal, and that is true whichever system you are in.

### 2-on-2

```facts
Convention: Play it man-on-man — each defender takes an attacker and holds a tight gap on them
Options: Zone is the realistic alternative — strong-side D takes whoever carries into their half, weak-side D holds the middle
Read: Zone survives a crossing rush better, man-on-man is tighter on a straight one — find out which your team wants
Never: Both drift toward the puck — a simple pass then leaves their partner the net
Action: Follow your man through a crossing or call a clean "switch" — never do half of each
Key: The weak-side defender is not free; being ready to help is not the same as leaving your man
```

Two attackers, two defenders. Even numbers, and it should be routine — but it is the situation most often botched, because "even" makes people relax. That ranking is coaching emphasis, not a counted one.

- **Play it man-on-man.** Each defender takes an attacker and holds a tight gap on them. This is the default in almost every system. The realistic alternative is **zone**: the strong-side D takes whoever carries the puck into their half of the ice and the weak-side D holds the middle, so the two of you trade marks rather than following them across. Zone survives a crossing rush better; man-on-man is tighter on a straight one. Find out which your team wants, because mixing them on the same rush leaves an attacker free.
- **The single biggest danger is both defenders drifting to the puck.** It is magnetic and it is fatal: two of you converge on the carrier, they make a simple pass, and their partner has the net. If you are the defender on the non-puck attacker, your job is to *stay with them* and let your partner do theirs.
- **Communicate the switch if they cross.** Attackers criss-cross specifically to make two defenders trade assignments badly. Either follow your man through the crossing or call a clean switch — "switch!" — but do not do half of each.
- **The weak-side defender is not free.** You are marking a person. Being ready to help is not the same as leaving your man.

### 3-on-2

```facts
Convention: The common default is "two wide, middle to the backchecker" — a defenceman on each outside attacker
Never: Chase the puck carrier under the two-wide default — a wide carrier belongs to the D in that lane, a middle carrier to the backchecker
Options: Under the strong-side system the strong-side D takes the carrier instead, so ask which of the two you play
Options: The alternative is strong-side D on the puck as a 1-on-1, weak-side D splitting the middle and the far attacker
Read: If your backchecker is genuinely not coming, squeeze the middle and concede the far wide man — but look before you assume it
Action: Retreat together at the same speed, holding a flat line with the middle sealed between you
Never: Back all the way to the goal line — collapsing into your crease concedes the whole slot
Key: Ask your coach which version you play; two defencemen playing different ones on the same rush is worse than either
```

Three attackers, two defenders. Chaotic-looking, and the way you take the chaos out is to have agreed in advance which of two systems you are playing.

**The common default — "two wide, middle to the backchecker."** This is what most teams run, and what the rest of this section assumes unless you are told otherwise.

- **Each defenceman takes an outside attacker.** One D on each wide lane.
- **Nobody chases the puck carrier — under this default.** If the carrier is in the middle, they belong to the backchecker; if the carrier is wide, they belong to the defenceman in that lane and the other D holds the far attacker. Under the strong-side alternative set out below, the strong-side defenceman does take the carrier wherever they are, which is exactly why you need to know which one you are playing.
- **The middle belongs to the backchecking centre**, arriving from behind. This is exactly the middle-lane assignment above. If your backchecker is genuinely not coming, the defenceman on the puck side has to squeeze the middle and concede the far wide man — but do not assume that until you have looked.

> **The realistic alternative — "strong side takes the puck, weak side splits."** Some teams do not wait on the backchecker at all. The **strong-side defenceman takes the puck carrier** wherever they are, playing it as a 1-on-1; the **weak-side defenceman drops off and splits the middle and the far attacker**, sitting in the passing lane between them exactly as you would on a 2-on-1. Under this version the backchecker's job becomes the *third* attacker rather than the middle specifically.
>
> The trade: the default keeps three defenders on three attackers if the backcheck arrives, but concedes the middle entirely if it does not. The alternative never concedes the middle, but the weak-side D is covering two people and the far attacker gets more room. **Ask your coach which one you are playing** — they fail in different directions, and two defencemen playing different versions of it on the same rush is worse than either.
- **Retreat together.** Both defencemen back up at the same speed, holding a flat line across the ice with the middle sealed between you. A staggered retreat opens a diagonal seam straight to the slot.
- **Do not back all the way to the goal line.** Retreating together does not mean collapsing into your own crease — that concedes the entire slot. Retreat under control and force the shot from outside.

### 3-on-1

```facts
Position: Central, on the defensive side of the puck, stick in the most dangerous lane
Goal: Delay — the objective changes from "stop this" to "make it take longer"
Action: Retreat slowly, stay in the lanes, and force them to make an extra pass
Never: Commit to one attacker — attacking the carrier turns it into an unopposed 2-on-0
Priority: Give your goalie the shot from distance and take away the cross-ice options, as on a 2-on-1
Action: Call for help loudly — your backcheckers may not know how bad it is
```

Three attackers, one defender. This is a bad situation and pretending otherwise helps nobody.

- **Take the middle.** Position yourself centrally, defensive side of the puck, stick in the most dangerous lane. You cannot cover three; you can make the two middle options harder.
- **Delay.** Your objective changes from "stop this" to "make it take longer." Every extra second is another backchecker arriving. Retreat slowly, stay in the lanes, force them to make an extra pass.
- **Never commit to one attacker.** Attacking the carrier on a 3-on-1 turns it into an unopposed 2-on-0.
- **Give your goalie the shot from distance** and take away the cross-ice options, on the same logic as the 2-on-1.
- **Call for help loudly.** Your backcheckers may not know how bad it is.

### Breakaways

```facts
Priority: This is your goalie's save and they usually make it — do the one useful legal thing, then get out of the way
Action: Stick on the puck only — a poke at the puck itself, or a stick lift on their blade as they release
Options: Skating to catch them and re-establish position is legal, and angling them wide turns it into a bad-angle chance
Rule: A hook, trip, hold or slash from behind that denies the chance is a penalty shot, not a two-minute minor (NHL Rule 57.3)
Never: Stand up and look at the official — play does not stop until the attacking team loses possession (NHL Rule 57.3)
```

A **breakaway** is an attacker alone with the puck against the goalie, no defender between them and the net.

**The honest answer first: this is your goalie's save, and they usually make it.** Breakaways are stopped far more often than they are scored on. *(No breakaway save percentage is quoted here on purpose: the NHL does not publish one, and the penalty-shot and shootout conversion rates people reach for instead measure a different situation. Take the direction — the goalie is favoured — and not a number.)* Your job is to do the one useful legal thing and then get out of the way.

**What you can legally do from behind:**

- **Play the puck with your stick.** Get your blade onto the puck itself — a poke at the puck from behind, or a stick lift on their blade as they release. This is legal and it is the only reliable option you have.
- **Skate to catch them and re-establish position.** Legal, and worth doing if you have the speed.
- **Angle them wide** if you can get alongside — turning a breakaway into a bad-angle chance is a win.

**What you must not do:** a hook, a trip, a hold, a slash or any other foul from behind that denies the chance is a **penalty shot** — not a two-minute minor. NHL Rule 57.3 (Tripping) states it directly:

> "When a player, in the neutral or attacking zone, in control of the puck (or who could have obtained possession and control of the puck) and having no other opponent to pass than the goalkeeper, is tripped or otherwise fouled from behind, thus preventing a reasonable scoring opportunity, a penalty shot shall be awarded to the non-offending team."

The four conditions the officials are checking:

1. The infraction happened in the **neutral or attacking zone** (from the puck carrier's own blue line onward).
2. It was committed **from behind**.
3. There was **no opponent between the carrier and the goalkeeper**.
4. A **reasonable scoring opportunity** was denied — and note that getting a shot away does not automatically rule out a penalty shot.

### The carve-outs, and how far they reach in each book

```facts
Action: Stick on the puck only — if your blade is not touching the puck or their blade, you are probably committing a penalty, and USA Hockey's relief needs a referee who judges you were clearly at the puck
Rule: Contacting the puck first and tripping the opponent in so doing is a tripping minor, not the penalty shot — NHL 57.3 and IIHF 24.8, same words. USA Hockey 616(a) leaves an optional minor instead, and that its 639(a) relief reaches 616 at all is this guide's reading of two rules, not a ruling
Rule: No puck-first relief saves a slide — USA Hockey's Casebook calls tripping where a player leaves their feet, slides into an opponent and causes them to fall, "regardless of who gains possession of the puck", excepting knees dropped to block a shot (639, Sit. 4 — its ruling says opponent, not carrier)
Rule: Outside NHL Rule 57 a reckless trip is not capped at a minor — IIHF 57.3/57.4 reach a major and a major plus game misconduct, and whether 24.8's minor or 57.3's discretion governs a reckless puck-first trip the book does not say; USA Hockey 639(b)/(c), HC 8.6(b) on injury
Rule: The ceiling above the minor differs by book — NHL 21.1 and USA Hockey 602(a)/639(c) reach a match penalty, and the IIHF writes no match penalty anywhere; Hockey Canada's 7.1(c) is residual and 8.6(c) bars a match for tripping, but its Appendix D chart, Note 1, says the opposite
Rule: Under the IIHF book "from behind" is not required of a goalkeeper — 24.8 criterion (II) excepts a foul from the front by the goalkeeper on a breakaway, so that foul still concedes a penalty shot; NHL 57.3 criterion (ii) has no such exception
Rule: With your goalie pulled, a foul denying the chance is an awarded goal under the NHL and IIHF — neither awarded-goal clause carries a "from behind" test (NHL 25.1 and 57.4; IIHF 25.3)
Rule: USA Hockey and Hockey Canada keep the "from behind" test with the net empty — USA Hockey 616(b) awards it only via the "fouled from behind provisions", and Hockey Canada 4.12(a) routes through 4.11(a)(i); 616's test catches a foul delivered diagonally from behind too
Rule: Hockey Canada writes the puck-first carve-out into the slide sentence of 8.6 — but 8.6 expressly does not reach 8.7 Clipping (contact below the hips) or 8.8 Slew-Footing (sweeping the feet), and neither carries any carve-out: both reach a match penalty (8.7(c), 8.8(c))
Rule: Slew-footing — sweeping an opponent's feet away with a leg or foot, or pushing their upper body backward while your leg takes their skates — is a mandatory match penalty under NHL 52.2, with no lower tier and no discretion — a fall the NHL and IIHF books call "violently to the ice" (52.1)
Rule: Two of the other three eject you at their floor — IIHF 52.2 a discretionary major plus an automatic game misconduct, USA Hockey 639 a minimum of a major plus a game misconduct — and Hockey Canada is the outlier: 8.8(a)'s double minor does not eject, and only 8.8(c)'s match does
```

**The carve-outs that change what you actually do — and where each of them stops:**

- **Under the NHL and IIHF books, touch the puck first and you are safe from the penalty shot.** Rule 57.3: *"If, in the opinion of the Referee, a player makes contact with the puck first and subsequently trips the opponent in so doing, no penalty shot will be awarded, but a minor penalty for tripping shall be assessed."* This is the whole reason "stick on the puck only" is the instruction. **Under the NHL book** getting your blade to the puck first converts the worst outcome in *Rule 57* into a two-minute minor — and *Rule 57* is the whole of it, because that rule has no major and no match tier. **What it does not convert is Rule 21.1**, which still reaches a match penalty for a player who *"deliberately attempts to injure or who deliberately injures an opponent in any manner"*. Getting a blade to the puck does not license the rest of it — and if you get the puck cleanly, into nothing at all. ⚠️ **The IIHF book grants the same carve-out in the same words** — 24.8: *"no 'Penalty Shot' will be awarded, but a Minor Penalty for 'tripping' shall be assessed"* — **but it does not cap the tier the way the NHL's Rule 57 does.** IIHF 57.3 lets the referee assess a major and 57.4 a major plus a game misconduct for reckless endangerment, tiers the NHL's tripping rule has no equivalent of. **Whether 24.8's mandatory "shall" or 57.3's discretion governs a reckless puck-first trip, the book does not say** — so play the puck, and do not treat the minor as a ceiling you can rely on. **And 57.4 sits on the IIHF's Appendix IV Table 7 in the 2025/26 book**, so where a referee does reach that tier, a first major there lets them end your game — that table is headed *"Summary of major penalties that result in a game misconduct based on the Referee's judgement"*, which makes it discretionary rather than automatic; Table 6, *"Summary of major penalties that result in an automatic game misconduct"*, is the automatic one. ⚠️ **Look those numbers up in the current IIHF book and you will land on the wrong tables.** The 2026/27 edition merges Tables 5, 6 and 7 into a single **Table 5** with two columns, *"MAJOR ONLY"* and *"MAJOR + GMP"* (GMP being the game misconduct penalty), shifting every later table down by two, and tripping — listed there as 57.3, 57.4 — carries a tick in **both** columns. **That merged table is headed only *"Summary of major penalties"*, so the words "automatic" and "Referee's judgement" are no longer printed with it.** What has not changed is the rule text, and the rule text is what decides: IIHF 57.3 and 57.4 both read *"The Referee, at their discretion, may assess"* in the 2025/26 and 2026/27 editions alike. **And none of these tripping rules is the whole of your exposure — though where the top tier comes from differs so much between the books that it is worth setting out.** **NHL 21.1** is the cleanest: *"A match penalty shall be imposed on any player who deliberately attempts to injure or who deliberately injures an opponent in any manner"* — and "in any manner" means it survives the tripping rule's silence. **USA Hockey** reaches it twice over: **602(a)** for a player who *"recklessly endangers or attempts to injure"*, and **639(c)** — *inside* the tripping rule — for reckless endangerment. Note that USA Hockey never writes the words *deliberately injures* anywhere in its book; its category is attempt and reckless endangerment. **Hockey Canada is the awkward one.** Its match penalty for deliberate injury, **7.1(c)**, is expressly residual — it reaches conduct *"in any manner **not otherwise covered within the playing rules**"* — and tripping *is* covered, by 8.6, whose (c) says *"No Match penalty may be assessed for tripping."* So under Hockey Canada the ceiling on a deliberate injurious trip turns entirely on what the referee calls it. **And the IIHF book has no general deliberate-injury rule at all** — searching it for that concept returns only the abuse-of-officials rule. Its Rule 21 is headed *Dangerous Actions* and reaches *"an action that is **not covered by the playing rules**"*, so it does not reach a trip either. What the IIHF has instead is 57.3 and 57.4 above, and no match penalty anywhere in the book.
- ⚠️ **USA Hockey writes its relief in two volumes, and neither of them saves a slide.** Its playing rule is the Note to **639(a)**: *"no penalty shall be assessed **under this rule** if, in the opinion of the Referee, the player was clearly hook-checking or poke-checking the puck for the purpose of gaining possession."* Two things follow. **The relief is for a *stick* check** — a hook check or a poke check, by name — so it does not describe a player who has thrown their body across the ice. **And being scoped *"under this rule"*, it is written into Rule 639 and not into Rule 616, the rule that awards the penalty shot — which writes no puck-first relief of its own, in the playing rules or in the four Rule 616 situations of the Casebook. Whether it needs one is the question.** Rule 616 has no independent definition of a foul; it borrows one. Its **Note 1** defines *"fouled from behind"* as *"any infraction committed to an opponent either directly or diagonally from behind (beyond the fouled player's peripheral vision), **that would normally be deemed a penalty**, including cross-checking, high sticking, holding, hooking, slashing and tripping."* So where the Note to 639(a) leaves *"no penalty"* assessed, there is arguably no infraction that would normally be deemed a penalty, and **616 has no predicate to act on**. ⚠️ **That last step is this guide's reading of the two rules together, not a USA Hockey ruling.** No situation in either volume joins 639(a)'s Note to Rule 616 — not the four Casebook situations under 616, not the four under 639 — and Note 1's *"that would normally be deemed a penalty"* reads equally well as classing the *kind* of action (tripping normally is a penalty) rather than the instance in front of the referee, in which case 639's carve-out takes nothing away from 616. **Neither reading is settled in the books, so do not defend as though the penalty shot has been ruled out.** The Casebook fences the same ground from 616's own side, setting out five criteria that must all hold — the fourth being that *"the fouled player is fouled from behind"* — and adding that *"All of these criteria need to be met in order to award a penalty shot. If one or more are not met, then the appropriate penalty shall be assessed in the normal manner"* (Rule 616, Situation 1). ⚠️ **And on either reading there is a second thing to earn.** The referee has to judge that you were *"clearly"* hook- or poke-checking at the puck, and Note 1's list of infractions is expressly open (*"including"*), so an action graded as a hook or a hold rather than a trip never enters Rule 639 at all and the Note never bites. ⚠️ **And its separate Casebook rules the slide out in terms, whoever ends up with the puck** — at Rule 639, Situation 4: *"When a player leaves their feet and slides into an opponent, thereby causing them to fall, a penalty for tripping must be called regardless of who gains possession of the puck."* **Read the ruling and not the question.** The situation put to the Casebook is a slide into the puck *carrier*, but the answer is written for **an opponent** — so it is not only the carrier you may not slide into, and reaching the puck first buys you nothing. The Casebook allows exactly one exception, and it is not a puck-first one: *"The only exception is when a player drops to their knees to block a shot and their momentum carries them into the player shooting the puck, causing them to fall."*
- **Hockey Canada writes the carve-out too, and one limb of it is more generous than the NHL's.** Rule 8.6 counts a deliberate slide as tripping — and then carves it out where the player *"make[s] contact with the puck prior to contacting their opponent"*. Two Interpretations go further. Interpretation 1: no penalty where a player *"gains possession of the puck and, with the same action, trips the puck carrier"* — that one turns on the referee's opinion. **Interpretation 2 does not:** *"A penalty will NOT be assessed if, while falling or sliding along the ice, a player contacts the puck prior to contacting the body of their opponent and within that same motion causes the opponent to fall."* No opinion clause at all. ⚠️ **But read where Rule 8.6 stops, because that is the part that can end your season.** Its own definition says *"This rule does not apply to actions defined under Rule 8.7 – Clipping or Rule 8.8 – Slew-Footing"* — and 8.7 reads: *"Clipping, also known as a 'low hit', is where a player uses their body to make contact below an opponent's hips."* A slide that catches a carrier below the hips is that. **Neither 8.7 nor 8.8 carries any puck-first carve-out, and both reach a match penalty** (8.7(c), 8.8(c)). **Slew-footing is sweeping an opponent's feet out from under them** — Rule 8.8 defines it as using *"a leg or a foot to knock, drag, or sweep an opposing player's feet from under them"*, or pushing the upper body back with an arm or elbow while doing it. **Taken at speed it puts a player on the back of their head with nothing to break the fall — that is this guide's reading of why the tiers run where they do, not a sentence any rulebook writes.** Rule 8.8(c) itself turns on the *"degree of violence of impact (including the impact with the ice, goal, or boards)"*, and Interpretation 1 to 8.8(a) tells referees to give *"serious consideration for a Match penalty ... when an offending player is 'in motion' (skating) while committing this foul"* — one of three listed considerations, alongside the degree of violence and the player's intent. (The parallel interpretation for *clipping*, Interpretation 2 to 8.7, is blunter about why: those fouls *"may cause players to be thrown through the air and the subsequent impact with the ice can be very dangerous."*) — which is exactly the situation a backchecker is in. ⚠️ **And do not read Hockey Canada's ladder as the sport's. The NHL is the harshest of the four and has no ladder at all: Rule 52.2 reads *"Any player who is guilty of slew-footing shall be assessed a match penalty"* — mandatory, no lower tier, no discretion.** The IIHF (52.2) gives a discretionary major plus automatic game misconduct, and USA Hockey (639) excludes the minor outright and sets a *minimum* of a major plus game misconduct. **Hockey Canada's is the outlier, and it is the one that starts lowest**: Rule 8.8 has *no major tier at all* — **8.8(b)** reads *"No Major penalty may be assessed for slew-footing"* and **8.8(e)** *"No Game Misconduct penalty may be assessed for slew-footing"* — so it runs from a **double minor** (8.8(a)) straight to a **match penalty**, which 8.8(c) makes mandatory *"to any player who injures an opponent by a slew-footing infraction that would otherwise call for a double Minor penalty."* So 8.6(c)'s ceiling — *"No Match penalty may be assessed for tripping"* — holds only if the referee calls it tripping. Inside 8.6 itself, (b) is a **mandatory** major plus game misconduct wherever the trip injures; and Interpretation 2 is headed *"Rule 8.6 (a)"*, the minor, and its words are *"while falling or sliding along the ice"* — whether that reaches a player who chooses to go down is not something the text says.
- **Play does not stop when the foul happens.** Rule 57.3: *"the Referee shall not stop play until the attacking team has lost possession of the puck to the defending team."* So do not stand up and look at the official. It is a delayed call: the attacker may still score, and your team is still defending until the whistle. Rule 57.3 also notes that if the fouled player recovers and gets a reasonable chance anyway, no penalty shot is awarded — the appropriate minor is called instead if no goal results.

One more thing worth knowing:

- **If your goalie has been pulled** for an extra attacker and you foul an attacker on the empty net, **a goal is awarded** — there is no shot to take. **Note what the awarded-goal clauses do *not* say.** Rule 57.4 reads *"is tripped or **otherwise fouled** with no opposition between him and the opposing goal"*, and Rule 25.1 *"prevented from scoring as a result of an infraction"*. Neither carries the **from behind** requirement that the penalty-shot test in 57.3 does — so with the net empty the awarded goal is *easier* to trigger than the penalty shot, not harder, and stepping across from the front does it. Cite 25.1 and 57.4 as a pair; they are not alternatives. ([Risk Management](../hockey-iq/risk_management.md) sets this out at length.)

> **Rule-set note — the criteria are not the same everywhere.**
>
> **IIHF Rule 24.8** mirrors NHL 57.3 almost word for word on the *penalty shot*, including the four criteria and the puck-first carve-out — but the IIHF's tripping tiers do not mirror the NHL's, so do not carry the two-minute cap across with it — with one addition: criterion (II) reads *"The infraction must have been committed from behind (**except a foul committed from the front by the goalkeeper on a 'breakaway' situation**)."* So under IIHF rules a goalie who fouls a breakaway attacker from the front concedes a penalty shot; the NHL text has no such exception in 57.3.
>
> **USA Hockey Rule 616(a) is materially different**, and the difference changes how you defend: *"A penalty shot/optional minor penalty shall be awarded to the non-offending team any time a player is fouled from behind, **or diagonally from behind** by an opponent, and is denied a reasonable scoring opportunity **during a breakaway**."* Two things follow. First, "from behind" is defined by the fouled player's **peripheral vision** — Rule 616 Note 1 covers *"any infraction committed to an opponent either directly or diagonally from behind (beyond the fouled player's peripheral vision), **that would normally be deemed a penalty**"* — so coming in at a diagonal does not protect you the way it might in the NHL. **And read the second half of that definition, because it is the half that does the work**: 616 defines its foul by borrowing from the rest of the book, so where another rule assesses no penalty, 616 has nothing to award a shot for. Second, it is a **penalty shot *or* an optional minor at the referee's discretion**, not an automatic penalty shot, and it requires a **breakaway** as Note 2 defines it (in control of the puck, beyond your own defending blue line, no opponent between you and the goalkeeper) rather than the NHL's broader "neutral or attacking zone" test. ⚠️ **And USA Hockey 616(b) does *not* match the NHL on the empty net.** It awards the goal only where the chance was *"prevented by the **fouled from behind** provisions"* — the test the NHL drops when the net is empty is the one USA Hockey keeps. **Hockey Canada keeps it too**, routing 4.12(a)'s awarded goal through 4.11(a)(i), whose criteria include *"tripped or fouled from behind."* **The IIHF drops it as the NHL does** (25.3: *"is tripped or otherwise fouled with no opposition between the Player and the opposing goal"*). So the four books split two–two, and stepping across from the front awards a goal only under the NHL and the IIHF.

**The practical instruction: stick on the puck only.** If your blade is not touching the puck or their blade, you are probably committing a penalty. Take the save-percentage odds over a penalty shot every single time.

### Covering the puck in your own crease

```facts
Rule: Three books print the permission to catch a puck out of the air and put it straight down — NHL and IIHF 67.2, Hockey Canada 10.2(a)(iii), which adds "without gaining or attempting to gain an advantage"; USA Hockey's playing rules print none, its express words being Casebook Situation 1 to Rule 618
Never: Hold a caught puck, carry it, turn with it or throw it — that is the act every book penalises, and in your own crease it is the penalty shot
Rule: What buys the penalty is the conduct after the catch — holding it, taking a stride, turning to avoid a check, or throwing it — and not the height (NHL and IIHF 67.2(I); Hockey Canada Interpretation 4 to 10.2(a)(iii))
Rule: Under USA Hockey a hand closed OUTSIDE the crease and not opened immediately is a stoppage and a last-play face-off, not a penalty (618(a)) — but a hand closed on a puck that is in the crease is the penalty shot or optional minor (614(b), 618(a))
Rule: USA Hockey's minor under 618(a) is a different act — picking the puck up off the ice with the hand while play is in progress
Rule: Covering a puck that is itself in your goal crease is a penalty shot, goalkeeper excepted — and all four books make the puck's location the test (NHL 67.4 and 63.6, IIHF 63.6, USA Hockey 614(b) and 618(a), Hockey Canada 10.2(a)(iv)-(v))
Rule: The converse — a minor where the puck is outside the paint and you are inside it — is written only by the NHL and the IIHF (63.6); neither Hockey Canada nor USA Hockey prints that sentence, so do not assume it
Rule: With your own net empty that becomes an awarded goal — NHL and IIHF 67.5 and Hockey Canada 10.2(a)(vi) on the bare fact of the substitution; USA Hockey alone conditions it, awarding it only where the act "has prevented an obvious and imminent goal" (614(b), 618(a))
Rule: The catch is free; the hold is not. Height only sets the price of holding it — the crease runs four feet up to the top of the goal frame (NHL 1.7, 2.1), so above the crossbar is a minor and knee high in the paint the penalty shot (IIHF Situations 63.27 v 63.15/67.4)
Never: Reach higher for a puck to keep it above the crossbar — height selects which penalty you pay, never whether you pay one, and the free play is still to knock it down
```

**The permission first, because it is what makes the act free.** Three of the four books print it in their playing rules, and you may catch a puck out of the air provided you put it straight back down. NHL and IIHF **Rule 67.2**, in identical words but for the IIHF's capitalised *"Player"*: *"A player shall be permitted to catch the puck out of the air but must immediately place it or knock it down to the ice."* Hockey Canada's **10.2(a)(iii)** excludes the honest catch from the minor in terms — it *"will not include a player who closes their hand on the puck to catch or stop the puck and immediately drops it to the ice without gaining or attempting to gain an advantage."* ⚠️ **USA Hockey's playing rules print no permission for a skater to catch it at all.** What that book prints is the consequence, and the consequence is what leaves the act free: **outside the crease, 618(a)** does not reach a penalty, because a hand that does not open immediately means only *"Play shall be stopped and a last play face-off shall occur"*, its minor being reserved for a player who *"picks the puck up from the ice with their hand(s) while play is in progress."* **The express words do exist one volume over**, in the separate *Rules and Casebook*: Situation 1 to Rule 618 answers that *"[t]he motion of catching, then dropping the puck, must be virtually one continuous motion"*, and that *"[p]rovided the player catches the puck and drops it to their stick in one continuous motion, play should be allowed to continue."* **So under USA Hockey the permission is a casebook interpretation rather than rule text — the same permission in practice, and not the same kind of evidence.** ⚠️ **In the crease that same rule is the penalty shot or optional minor, because a hand that closes and does not open is holding the puck** — 618(a)'s own third paragraph reaches a defending player other than the goalkeeper who *"picks the puck up off the ice with their hand **or holds the puck** while play is in progress **and the puck is in the goal crease**"*, and **614(b)** says it again: *"or uses the hands to hold the puck while in the goal crease."* **Knock it down and no book penalises you.**

**What costs you is the conduct after the catch, not how high the puck was.** NHL and IIHF 67.2(I) reach the player who *"catches it and skates with it, either to avoid a check or to gain a territorial advantage"*, and Hockey Canada's **Interpretation 4 to 10.2(a)(iii)** names three acts, none of them a height: throwing the puck, trying *"to maneuver around another player while holding the puck"*, and *"tak[ing] a stride while holding the puck in their hand."*

**Then the price.** **Falling on, holding, picking up or gathering into your body or hands a puck that is itself in your own goal crease** is a penalty shot under NHL **Rule 67.4**, which opens *"If a **defending player, except a goalkeeper**…"* — your goalie may smother it, you may not — the trigger is not only "deliberately covering it with your hand." ⚠️ **Read that first clause carefully, because the qualifier decides the call: it is the puck that has to be in the crease, not you.** NHL and IIHF **Rule 63.6** both say so — *"The significant factor when determining whether or not a penalty shot is warranted is the location of the puck at the time it was held, grabbed or gathered into the body"* — so under the NHL and the IIHF a puck lying outside the paint that you smother while standing in it is only a minor, while a puck in the paint that you smother from outside it is still the shot. **All four books make the puck's location the test for the shot** (NHL and IIHF 63.6, USA Hockey 614(b) and 618(a), Hockey Canada 10.2(a)(iv) and (v)). ⚠️ **The converse — the minor where the puck is outside and you are inside — is written only by the NHL and the IIHF.**

**Hockey Canada and USA Hockey each split the offence across two rules, and neither prints the contrast case — so do not carry the outside-puck minor into either book.** Hockey Canada's hand limb is **10.2(a)(iii)** and its body limb **10.2(a)(iv)**, *"Deliberately falls on or gathers the puck into their body while standing or laying on the ice"*; **10.2(a)(v)** escalates **both** to the shot *"when the infraction occurs inside the offending team's goal crease (see Rule 4.11 (a)(v) – Penalty Shots and Rule 1.5 (c) – Goal Crease)"*. Read alone those locating words could mean you or the puck — but 4.11(a)(v) reaches the **hand** only, while **Rule 1.5(c)** defines the crease as a *space* that *"will extend vertically to the level of the top of the goal frame"*, which is what a puck-located test needs. And **Interpretation 5 rules on the body limb**, not the hand: its ruling is *"Assess a Minor penalty under **Rule 10.2 (a)(iv)**"*, and its reason is in puck terms — *"The puck was not actually covered in the crease; therefore, no penalty shot may be awarded."* **The limit worth knowing is that in its facts the player and the puck are both outside the crease at the moment of the cover**, so it does not isolate the variable; its stated reason does. **USA Hockey is no more explicit than Hockey Canada.** Its 614(b) — *"or uses the hands to hold the puck while in the goal crease"* — is ambiguous read alone in exactly the same way, and is rescued only by the sentence after it; its plainest puck wording sits in a different rule, **618(a)**: *"picks the puck up off the ice with their hand **or holds the puck** while play is in progress **and the puck is in the goal crease**."*

⚠️ **And the crease is a volume, not a footprint — but height decides which penalty you pay, never whether you pay one.** All four books run it upward — NHL 1.7 *"extending vertically four feet (4') to the level of the top of the goal frame"*, four feet being the height NHL 2.1 gives the posts; IIHF 1.7 *"extends vertically until the top of the crossbar"*; Hockey Canada 1.5(c); the Note to USA Hockey 618(a) — and the IIHF Situation Handbook rules both ways on the height: a puck caught **above the crossbar** while you stand in the paint *"was not considered to be in the crease. Therefore, only a minor penalty is warranted"* (Situation 63.27), while a puck taken **knee high** in the crease is the shot (Situations 63.15 and 67.4). ⚠️ **Note what actually decided both.** In 63.27 the defender *"turns to avoid opposing players, and then tosses it towards the boards"*; in 63.15 and 67.4 he *"turns to avoid the attackers, drops the puck to the stick and shoots it out."* He turned and released in each — **that conduct is what produced a penalty at all; the height only selected which one.** So neither Situation is a reason to reach higher for the puck, and the crossbar is only four feet up. Its Foreword places the Handbook below the Rulebook — *"in case of any contradictions, the valid IIHF Rulebook is always the basis"* — so read those as worked applications rather than rule text. **Nor does moving the puck cure it:** in Situation 67.6 a defender with his own net empty takes it *"out of mid-air (around the height of the waist)"* and drops it **outside** the crease, and the ruling is *"an Awarded Goal to Team B."*

**USA Hockey alone lets the other side elect an optional minor in lieu of the shot** (Rule 406(a)) — **their election, not yours**, so it is never a discount on the offence; and 406(a) adds that *"If the penalty shot is selected, the offending team shall not be reduced in on-ice strength."* ⚠️ **With your own goalie pulled for an extra attacker, the same act is an awarded goal** under **Rule 67.5**, not a penalty shot — **and three of the four books attach no condition to it beyond the substitution.** The IIHF's 67.5 is the NHL's clause in the IIHF's wording, and Hockey Canada's **10.2(a)(vi)** awards the goal *"when the infraction occurs inside the offending team's goal crease and the goaltender has been legally substituted for an extra skater."* ⚠️ **USA Hockey alone conditions it**, awarding it only where the act *"has prevented an obvious and imminent goal"* (614(b); 618(a) says the same of the hand). **Throwing your stick or any object** at the puck or the carrier in your defensive zone also produces a penalty shot — **NHL Rule 53.7**, which reaches *"any member of the defending team, including the Coach or any non-playing person"*. All of these come up in desperation moments at the end of a rush.

### The late man / trailer

```facts
Key: The player who arrives second is the one who gets forgotten — how often that is the reason a rush ends in a goal is coaching consensus, not a tracked share
Position: The trailer follows a few metres behind and arrives into the high slot with a clean, unopposed look
Convention: In the house default — low zone collapse behind a 2-1-2 forecheck — the trailer belongs to the backchecking forwards, not the defencemen
Options: Some teams give the trailer to the weak-side defenceman and drop the third forward to the net front — ask which yours runs
Action: If you are the last backchecker and everyone else is marked, the trailer is yours — do not skate into the pile
Action: Defencemen, call them out — "trailer!" or "high!" is a genuinely game-saving shout
```

The player who arrives second is the one who gets forgotten. How often that is the reason a rush ends in a goal is coaching consensus rather than a tracked share — [Neutral Zone Systems](neutral_zone_systems.md) sets out what is and is not counted about rush goals.

The **trailer** (or **late man**) is the attacker following the rush a few metres behind — often a defenceman who has activated, often a forward who was slow out of the zone. They arrive into the high slot just as the first wave has pulled everybody low, and they arrive with a clean, unopposed look.

![](diagram:rush-trailer)

- **In the house-default structure — low zone collapse behind a 2-1-2 forecheck — they belong to the backchecking forwards**, not to the defencemen. Your defencemen are occupied with the first wave, and if they step up to the trailer the first wave gets the net. **This is an assignment, not a law:** some teams have the weak-side defenceman take the trailer and the third forward drop to the net front instead, which is tighter on the trailer and thinner in front. Ask which your team runs, because a trailer covered by nobody and a trailer covered by two are both goals.
- **This is the specific reason "skate first, then look" matters.** A backchecker who is five metres behind cannot catch the trailer. One who sprinted from the turnover can.
- **If you are the last backchecker and everyone else is marked, the trailer is yours by default.** Do not skate into the pile.
- **Defencemen: call them out.** You will see the trailer before the backcheckers do — "trailer!" or "high!" is a genuinely game-saving shout.
- Note the symmetry: this is the same play your own team should be running in attack, described from the other side in [Defender](../positions/defender.md) under activating, and in [Zone Entries](zone_entries.md).

## Standing Up at the Blue Line vs Backing Off

Two legitimate ways to defend the line, and this is a genuine trade-off rather than a right answer. It is also frequently a **coaching choice** — many teams specify which one they want, and it usually follows from their neutral-zone system. See [Neutral Zone Systems](neutral_zone_systems.md).

### Standing up (stepping up)

```facts
Position: Hold your ground at or just inside the blue line and challenge the carrier as they try to enter
Goal: Deny the zone entirely — the attack has to dump the puck in or turn back, and neither produces a chance
Rule: Both skates must cross the line after the puck under NHL and IIHF rules; USA Hockey 630(a) requires skate contact with the neutral zone or the blue line and Hockey Canada 6.11 counts only skates touching the ice, which is stricter still — two books of the four
Risk: If you miss there is nothing behind you — your partner is left in a 2-on-1 or worse
Countered by: A skilled carrier who chips the puck past you and skates onto it
Read: Right for slow attacks, a head-down carrier, a carrier already near the boards, clear support behind you, and a clogged neutral zone
```

You hold your ground at or just inside the blue line and challenge the carrier as they try to enter, rather than retreating with them.

**What it gets you:**
- **Denies the zone entirely.** The attack has to dump the puck in or turn back, and neither produces a chance.
- **Uses the offside rule as an extra defender.** Because under NHL and IIHF rules both skates must cross after the puck (and under USA Hockey 630(a) the attacker needs skate *contact* with the neutral zone or the blue line, and under Hockey Canada 6.11 only a skate touching the ice counts at all — two books of the four, both stricter), a defender squeezing the line can force the carrier to slow up or enter alone. A delayed offside also lets your team reset while the attackers tag up.
- **Keeps play out of your end**, which keeps your forwards fresh and available for the counter.

**What it costs you:**
- **If you miss, there is nothing behind you.** A step-up that fails in the neutral zone leaves your partner in a 2-on-1, or worse. This is the specific risk flagged in [Defender](../positions/defender.md).
- It rewards a skilled carrier who chips the puck past you and skates onto it.

**When it's right:** slow attacks, an attacker with their head down, a carrier already near the boards, when you clearly have support behind you, and when your forwards have the neutral zone clogged.

### Backing off (retreating)

```facts
Position: Give ground, holding your gap, and defend them as they enter
Goal: Nothing gets behind you — the worst case is a controlled entry, not a breakaway
Convention: The default for most defencemen most of the time, because it is reliable and repeatable
Risk: Concedes the blue line, so play happens in your end and the shift gets longer
Never: Back off with a growing gap — a soft retreat gives the attacker speed and the zone, and is worse than either option
Read: Right against attackers with speed, on odd-man situations, when you are the last defender, when unsure, and any time you are tired
```

You give ground, holding your gap, and defend as they enter.

**What it gets you:**
- **Nothing gets behind you.** The worst case is a controlled entry, not a breakaway.
- Reliable and repeatable, which is why it is the default for most defencemen most of the time.

**What it costs you:**
- **Concedes the blue line**, so play happens in your end and the shift gets longer.
- **A soft retreat is worse than either option.** Backing off with a growing gap gives the attacker speed *and* the zone.

**When it's right:** attackers with speed, odd-man situations, when you are the last defender, when you are unsure, and any time you are tired.

### How to choose

```facts
Never: Step up unless you are confident you can win the puck — confidence, not hope
Convention: How aggressive "confident" should be is a coaching choice — an aggressive 1-2-2 press wants you challenging the line, a 1-3-1 trap wants you holding
Never: Half-commit — drifting forward, changing your mind, and arriving late with a huge gap
Priority: When in doubt, back off with a tight gap — it has no catastrophic failure mode
Key: Keep "step up" and "pinch" separate — stepping up is challenging in the neutral zone or at your own line, pinching is stepping down from the offensive blue line
```

- **Do not step up unless you are confident you can win the puck.** Confidence, not hope — a missed step-up is an odd-man rush behind you. **How aggressive "confident" should be is a coaching choice:** a team playing an aggressive 1-2-2 neutral-zone press wants you challenging at the line and will accept the occasional miss; a team playing a passive 1-3-1 trap wants you holding and steering the carrier wide. Find out which risk your coach wants to carry.
- **Step up or back off — do not do neither.** The killer is the half-commit: drifting forward, then changing your mind, and arriving late with a huge gap. Pick and go.
- **The default when in doubt is back off with a tight gap.** It has no catastrophic failure mode.
- **Keep "step up" and "pinch" separate.** Stepping up is challenging forward in the neutral zone or at your own line with nothing behind you. Pinching is stepping down from the *offensive* blue line with your forwards behind you. The pinch is the safer play; see [Defender](../positions/defender.md) and [Risk Management](../hockey-iq/risk_management.md).

## Odd-Man Rushes: How They Are Created

```facts
Key: Almost every odd-man rush against you was manufactured by your own team, and five causes account for nearly all of them — coaching emphasis, not a counted ranking
Convention: No public data classifies odd-man rushes by the play that created them, so treat the five as the list worth drilling rather than as a measured census
Read: Pinch on a read — when you will clearly win the puck, when a forward can cover for you, and when score and time say the risk is worth it
Never: Turn up ice hunting a breakaway pass before your team actually has possession — the test is whether you can still get back if you are wrong
Never: Stickhandle through the middle under pressure, or force cross-ice passes in the neutral zone
Action: Change lines when the puck is in the offensive zone or moving away from your end, not during a scramble
Never: Go below the puck in the offensive zone with nobody left above it — the subtlest of the five, and usually both defencemen going below at once
Action: Before you go below the puck know who is above it — a person, not a position; on a defence pair normally one of the two, but a five-man rotation uses whichever player is highest
```

The best rush defence is not creating the rush. Almost every odd-man rush against you was manufactured by your own team, and five causes account for nearly all of them — **coaching emphasis, not a counted ranking.** No public data classifies odd-man rushes by the play that created them, so take the five as the list worth drilling rather than as a measured census; [Neutral Zone Systems](neutral_zone_systems.md) sets out what is and is not counted about rush goals.

**1. A pinch that misses.** A defenceman steps down at the offensive blue line to keep the puck in and does not get it. Now one D is behind the play. The fix is not "never pinch" — it is pinch on a read: pinch when you will clearly win the puck, when a forward can cover for you, and when the score and time say the risk is worth it.

**2. Forwards cheating for offence.** A winger turning up ice before the team actually has possession, hunting a breakaway pass. When the puck does not come out, they are useless in both directions. Note the distinction made in [Winger](../positions/winger.md): *anticipating* possession while staying defensively accountable is a coached skill; abandoning coverage on a hope is not. The test is whether you can still get back if you are wrong.

**3. Neutral-zone turnovers.** The most direct cause of all, because a turnover in the middle of the ice hands the puck to a team already facing the right way with your forwards behind the puck. This is why every position document says the same thing: do not stickhandle through the middle under pressure, and do not force cross-ice passes in the neutral zone. See [Time and Space](../hockey-iq/time_and_space.md) and [Puck Support and Spacing](../hockey-iq/puck_support_and_spacing.md).

**4. A bad line change.** Changing while the puck is loose, or while your team does not have clear possession, means five players in transition and two of them stepping onto the ice cold. Change when the puck is in the offensive zone or moving away from your end, and not during a scramble. Full five-man changes on the fly are routine at every level and are not the problem in themselves — the problem is *when* you go. A five-man change off a clean dump-in deep in their end is normal; the same change with the puck loose at your own blue line is how a 3-on-2 gets born. See [Game Management](game_management.md).

**5. Both defencemen below the puck in the offensive zone.** The subtlest of the five, because nothing has visibly gone wrong until the puck turns over and there is nobody above it. **The failure is nobody above the puck; both defencemen going below is the usual way it happens, not the definition of it.** Teams running a **five-man rotation**, or giving both defencemen a standing green light, cover the line with whichever player is highest on purpose, and under a **single-high** offensive-zone structure the safety is the weak-side **winger**. The rule of thumb from [Defender](../positions/defender.md) is that **somebody is above the puck** — on a defence pair normally one of the two — and it exists precisely to prevent this. Hear it as a person rather than a position: unless one of those alternatives is what your team runs, assume the default and know *who* is above the puck before you go below it. See [Offensive Zone Play](offensive_zone_play.md).

## Communication During a Rush

```facts
Action: Short, early, loud — a call as it happens is information, a call after it happens is commentary
Action: Call the solution, not the problem — "back door" is useful, "watch out" is noise
Options: "Odd man!", "I've got the pass", "Take the shooter", "Back door!", "Pick him up", "Trailer" or "high", "Switch", "I'm back"
Key: "Back door!" — an attacker arriving unmarked at the far post — is the single most urgent call in defensive hockey; that ranking is coaching emphasis, not a counted one
Action: Name the number and the location rather than shouting vaguely: "pick up 12, high slot"
Mindset: Everyone talks, and the goalie has the best view of the rush and should be the loudest voice on the ice
```

A rush is loud, fast and facing away from most of your team. Talk is the only way anyone knows what is happening. Short, early, loud. Full detail in [On-Ice Communication](../foundation/on_ice_communication.md).

**The core vocabulary:**

- **"Odd man!"** — from whoever sees it first, usually a forward turning back. It tells the defencemen to stop thinking about stepping up and start thinking about lanes.
- **"I've got the pass"** — the defenceman on a 2-on-1 telling the goalie the contract is on. It lets the goalie commit fully to the shooter.
- **"Take the shooter"** — the same message aimed at the goalie, or from a defenceman to a partner assigning the puck carrier.
- **"Back door!"** — an attacker is arriving unmarked at the far post. The single most urgent call in defensive hockey — that ranking is coaching emphasis, not a counted one.
- **"Pick him up"** — a specific attacker is unmarked and someone needs to take them now. Better still, name the jersey number or the colour and the location: "pick up 12, high slot."
- **"Trailer"** or **"high"** — the late man is coming into the slot.
- **"Switch"** — two attackers crossed and you and your partner are trading assignments.
- **"I'm back"** or **"support"** — a backchecker announcing they have arrived, which lets a defenceman stop covering two people.

**Three rules about all of it:**

1. **Call early.** A call as it happens is information. A call after it happens is commentary.
2. **Call the solution, not the problem.** "Back door" is useful. "Watch out" is noise.
3. **Everyone talks.** The goalie has the best view of the whole rush and should be the loudest voice on the ice during one.

## From Rush Defence into Zone Coverage

```facts
Read: The attack losing forward momentum — a dump-in retrieved, a shot frozen or rebounded to the wall, a pass back to their point
Priority: Locate the net front before anything else, because during a rush nobody was assigned to it
Action: Switch from lanes to assignments in about one second — from "who is in my lane" to "who is my man"
Convention: In the house default of low zone collapse, a backchecking winger releases their man and gets back to the point; under man-on-man they stay with them
Action: Talk through the handover — every temporary assignment the rush created needs explicitly cancelling or confirming
Key: A rush is not over when the shot is saved; it is over when everybody has a mark
```

Stopping the rush is only half of it. The seconds immediately after are when teams that defended the rush well still concede — everyone relaxes, nobody picks up a mark, and the attackers who were rushing are now cycling against nobody.

**The transition, in order:**

1. **Recognise the moment it becomes a set situation.** The trigger is the attack losing its forward momentum: a dump-in retrieved in the corner, a shot stopped and frozen or rebounded to the wall, a pass back to their point, or the puck simply cycling below the goal line.
2. **Locate the net front before anything else.** The first thing to check after a rush dies is whether anybody is standing unmarked at your goalmouth, because during a rush nobody was assigned to them. This is the weak-side defenceman's job in the default system.
3. **Switch from lanes to assignments.** Rush defence is lane-based and situational. Defensive-zone coverage is structural. Everybody needs to move from "who is in my lane" to "who is my man / what is my area" in about one second.
4. **Backcheckers take up their zone roles, not the nearest puck.** In the house default of **low zone collapse** with wingers high on the points, a backchecking winger who followed a man into the corner has to release them and get back to the point. Under **man-on-man** they stay with them. **Find out which one your team plays** — see [Defensive Zone Coverage](defensive_zone_coverage.md).
5. **Talk through the handover.** "I've got the corner," "you take the point," "net front is mine." The rush created temporary assignments and every one of them needs to be explicitly cancelled or confirmed.
6. **Then look to exit.** Once the coverage is set, the job becomes the breakout — see [Breakouts](breakouts.md).

The habit to build: **a rush is not over when the shot is saved.** It is over when everybody has a mark.

## Common Mistakes

- **Standing still at the blue line** waiting for the attacker, then trying to accelerate backwards as they arrive. You cannot close a gap from a standstill: the gap opens at the moment you most need it closed. That this is the most common defenceman error is coaching emphasis, not a counted ranking.
- **Watching the puck instead of the chest.** Your feet follow your eyes, and the puck is the thing they are using to lie to you.
- **Riding a carrier into the boards in a league that does not permit it.** Steering them there with your route and pinning them there with your body are two different acts, and the second is a minor, or a major plus an automatic game misconduct, in women's hockey — every level of it in Britain — and in any league without body checking (IIHF Rule 101.1).
- **Sliding on a 2-on-1 before the pass is released, when your team plays the passing lane.** You take yourself out of the play and open both options at once. The mistake is doing it *inside a system that did not ask for it* — some teams coach a timed slide as the method itself, so find out which yours plays rather than deciding mid-rush. Where the slide is the method, your head still stays up and out of the shot lane — and how you go down is not settled teaching here, so ask the coach who is asking you to do it. Where the slide is the method, a late or wild one across an attacker's path is still a trip — and the timing is not the whole of it: under USA Hockey, leaving your feet and sliding into an opponent and putting them down is tripping whoever ends up with the puck (Casebook 639, Situation 4), and a slide that catches them low reads as clipping rather than tripping, a different offence with a higher ceiling — at or below the knees under NHL and IIHF 44.1, though neither of those two names the play; below the **hips** under Hockey Canada 8.7, whose tripping rule 8.6 does name a deliberate slide and then expressly excludes clipping from itself; and under USA Hockey's 639 Note 2 where that was the purpose of going down. ⚠️ **But under that book the label does not gate the tier, and nobody has to be hurt.** **639(b)** makes a major plus a game misconduct mandatory — *"shall be assessed"* — for a player who *"recklessly endangers an opponent as a result of tripping, clipping or leg checking"*, so it reaches a reckless slide under either name, with no purpose shown.
- **Trying to block the shot on a 2-on-1.** You abandon the pass, and a half-block deflects a puck your goalie was tracking cleanly.
- **The goalie and the defenceman both hedging on a 2-on-1** because they never agreed who has what. Neither option ends up covered.
- **Both defenders drifting toward the puck on a 2-on-2**, leaving the far attacker with an open net.
- **Deciding mid-rush to chase the puck carrier as a backchecker** when your defenceman already has them, leaving your actual assignment free. Some teams do coach exactly that pressure by design, with the defence adjusting to hold inside — the mistake is improvising it when nobody else on the ice is expecting it.
- **Not knowing which backchecking system your team runs.** Defence early strike, wide-lane lock, midlane backcheck and hounding the puck are genuinely different jobs, and two of them — the midlane backcheck and hounding the puck — ask the opposite thing of the first forward back. Under two of them the middle belongs to a defenceman, not to you. Playing one while your defencemen play another is how the middle opens.
- **Hounding a carrier into their own end boards and finishing into their back.** Chasing hard is a skating instruction: take the puck, take the ice, or angle them off it. Checking from behind carries no minor option and is a major with an automatic game misconduct (NHL Rules 43.2–43.5, IIHF Rules 43.2–43.3) — and under USA Hockey Rule 604(a) a great many readers may not body-check at all.
- **Losing the middle-lane driver.** They do not have the puck, so nobody looks at them, and they score.
- **Looking before skating on a backcheck**, costing two strides you never recover.
- **Backchecking behind your man's shoulder** instead of taking a route to their inside hip. You cannot defend anything from behind.
- **The half-commit at the blue line** — drifting up, changing your mind, and arriving late with a huge gap. Worse than either full option.
- **Reaching around an attacker from behind on a breakaway.** Under NHL Rule 57.3 that is a penalty shot, and if your goalie is pulled it is an awarded goal — and under the IIHF and USA Hockey books a *reckless* trip is not capped at a minor either. Hockey Canada is the exception: absent an injury 8.6 does cap it, because 8.6(b) needs one and (c) and (d) bar the match and the misconduct.
- **Reading "puck first" as a licence.** It saves you the penalty shot under the NHL and IIHF books and nothing more. **USA Hockey's is written somewhere else in the book, and it never saves a slide** — the Note to 639(a) excuses a clear poke- or hook-check, which is a *stick* check. **Whether that relief reaches the 616 penalty shot is this guide's reading of two rules together, not a USA Hockey ruling** — Rule 616 has no definition of a foul of its own, its Note 1 borrowing any infraction *"that would normally be deemed a penalty"*, so where 639's Note leaves none there is arguably nothing for 616 to act on; but no situation in either USA Hockey volume joins the two, and Note 1's phrase reads equally well as classing the kind of action rather than the instance. **Do not defend as though the penalty shot is off the table.** ⚠️ **And on either reading you still have to earn the referee's judgement that you were *"clearly"* checking at the puck**, and an action graded as a hook or a hold rather than a trip never enters Rule 639 at all. And its Casebook rules that leaving your feet and sliding into **an opponent**, causing them to fall, is tripping *"regardless of who gains possession of the puck"* (Rule 639, Situation 4), the one exception being knees dropped to block a shot. Note **an opponent**, not the carrier: sliding into somebody who never had the puck is inside that ruling too. Hockey Canada writes a wide one, and 8.6 extends it to a deliberate slide expressly. What it does **not** cover is a slide that catches them **below the hips** — that is clipping under 8.7, which carries no carve-out at all and reaches a match penalty at the top. And a deliberate attempt to injure is not capped at a minor in three of the four books, though each gets there by a different road. **NHL Rule 21.1** imposes a match penalty on any player who *"deliberately attempts to injure or who deliberately injures an opponent in any manner"*, and **USA Hockey 602(a)** one on any player who *"recklessly endangers or attempts to injure any opposing player or team official"*. **The IIHF writes no match penalty anywhere in its book**, so its ceiling for a trip is 57.3's major and 57.4's major plus a game misconduct, both for reckless endangerment. ⚠️ **The fourth is Hockey Canada, where the book disagrees with itself and does not reconcile it.** Rule **8.6(c)** reads *"No Match penalty may be assessed for tripping"*, and 8.6(b) reaches a major and game misconduct only for a player *"who injures an opponent"* — so on Rule 8.6 alone a deliberate trip that hurts nobody caps at the 8.6(a) minor, which is what the bullet above says. But Note 1 to the book's own Appendix D penalty chart says *"A Match penalty shall be assessed for any deliberate attempt to injure and for any infraction that results in a deliberate injury (Rule 7.1)."* Nothing in the book joins the two. **And 7.1(c) does not close it**, because it is written for a deliberate injury caused *"in any manner not otherwise covered within the playing rules"* — and tripping is covered, by 8.6. **Which reading a referee takes is not something you get to find out in advance, so do not play the gap.**
- **Relaxing the instant the rush is stopped**, before anybody has picked up the net front.
- **Pivoting early** and giving up your ability to react to a cutback.
- **Leaving nobody above the puck in the offensive zone**, usually by both defencemen going below at once — one of the five ways a team manufactures an odd-man rush against itself, and coaching emphasis rather than a counted ranking. Nothing looks wrong until the puck turns over. **Both going is not always the error**: a **five-man rotation** covers the line with whichever player is highest, and a **single-high** structure makes the safety the weak-side winger. Unless one of those is what your team runs, assume the default and know **who** is above the puck before you go below it.

## Key Takeaways

1. **Stay on the defensive side of every attacker, all the time.** Anything that puts an attacker between you and your own net has already gone wrong.
2. **Start your feet early and keep the gap shrinking:** two to three stick lengths through the neutral zone in open ice, a stick and a half at the centre red line, one stick length at your own blue line, and never growing. **These are a guide, not a law** — they move with your backward speed and the attacker's skill.
3. **Read the chest and hips.** The puck, stick, hands, head and eyes can all lie; the torso cannot. Fix your eyes on the numbers on the front of their jersey.
4. **Riding a carrier into the wall is not permitted everywhere — but steering them there with your route is, and stays legal even where checking is banned.** Hockey Canada's interpretation of its own non-checking rule says so in terms: *"A player may steer or direct an opposing player into the boards, without actually touching or body-checking them"* — as long as neither player uses their body to bump, push or shove. ⚠️ **In women's hockey the line is lower still:** IIHF Rule 101.1 — the illegal-hit rule for women's hockey, and the one British women's hockey runs — makes *any* move to **step or glide into** an opponent at least a minor, which is a stricter test than Hockey Canada's bump-push-shove. Using the boards to make contact is a different act. In women's hockey under the IIHF book — adopted by **England Ice Hockey, the SIHA and the BUIHA** — two or more players competing for the puck may not use the boards to eliminate, push or pin an opponent: a minor, or a major plus an automatic game misconduct **anywhere the IIHF book runs**. **Either way a major ejects you** — the IIHF's own game misconduct does that for this foul. What the British In-House Rules add is breadth: *any* foul carrying a major in women's hockey brings a game misconduct and ejection, not just this one. **And note their Adult category starts at the 14th birthday, not 18.** **And the same holds in any league without body checking, which is where most adult readers play.** **Pin the puck, not the player.** Rule numbers in [1-on-1](#1-on-1) and [What hounding never means](#what-hounding-never-means).
5. **On a 2-on-1, take away the pass and give up the shot — and say the contract out loud.** You own the pass, the goalie owns the shooter, and you do not slide until the pass is released. **That is a coaching choice with more than one legitimate answer, not the whole of hockey.** The named alternative is a timed slide across the lane to force the shot — head up and out of the shot lane, and ask your coach how they want you to go down, **because that is the part that hurts you** — and because a body on the ice across an attacker's path is still a trip, **which under the IIHF book is not capped at two minutes if it is reckless**. **Most goalies want the version above, but some will ask you to pressure the puck instead, so ask yours before the game.** And if the shooter is walking in unpressured with no realistic pass available, it is no longer a 2-on-1 — close on the puck. Full treatment in [2-on-1 — the headline situation](#2-on-1--the-headline-situation).
6. **Chasing a lone attacker from behind, your stick goes on the puck and nothing else.** A foul from behind that denies the chance is a **penalty shot**, not two minutes. Playing the puck first is what turns it into a minor — **but the books write that carve-out differently, and USA Hockey writes it somewhere else entirely.** Rule 616, its breakaway rule, writes no puck-first relief of its own, in the playing rules or in the four Rule 616 situations of its separate Casebook. The relief is the Note to Rule 639(a): *"no penalty shall be assessed under this rule"* where the referee judges the player was *"clearly hook-checking or poke-checking the puck for the purpose of gaining possession."* ⚠️ **Whether it reaches the penalty shot is this guide's reading of the two rules together, not a USA Hockey ruling.** Rule 616 has no definition of a foul of its own — its Note 1 borrows any infraction *"that would normally be deemed a penalty"* — so where 639 leaves no penalty there is arguably nothing for 616 to act on, and the Casebook's five criteria must all be met or *"the appropriate penalty shall be assessed in the normal manner"* (Rule 616, Situation 1). **But no situation in either volume joins the two rules, and Note 1's phrase reads equally well as classing the kind of action rather than the instance, so do not defend as though the penalty shot is off the table.** ⚠️ **And on either reading you still have to earn the referee's judgement that you were *"clearly"* checking at the puck**, and an action graded as a hook or a hold rather than a trip never enters Rule 639 at all. **And it does not save a slide** — the Casebook rules that leaving your feet and sliding into **an opponent**, causing them to fall, is tripping *"regardless of who gains possession of the puck"* (Rule 639, Situation 4), excepting only knees dropped to block a shot. **The ceiling above the minor differs too, and no book's tripping rule is the whole of your exposure:** only the NHL writes a clean one — **21.1**, a match penalty for deliberately injuring an opponent *"in any manner"*. USA Hockey reaches you through attempt and reckless endangerment instead; Hockey Canada's match penalty is residual and may not reach a trip at all, so the ceiling there turns on what the referee calls it; and the IIHF writes no general deliberate-injury rule and no match penalty anywhere, reaching its top tier through reckless endangerment instead. **And the carve-out stops where the real danger starts.** It does not reach a slide landing low, nor slew-footing — which **the NHL makes a mandatory match penalty with no lower tier at all (52.2)**, USA Hockey a minimum major plus game misconduct, and Hockey Canada, the one that *starts* lowest, runs from a double minor to a match. Note *starts*: its ceiling is a match penalty, above the IIHF's, which writes none. **And Hockey Canada's own Interpretation 1 to 8.8(a) tells referees to weigh a match seriously when the offender was *skating*** — which is what you are doing. That it lands them on the back of the head is this guide's reading; what the NHL and IIHF books write (52.1) is that they fall *violently to the ice*. **This is one to read rather than remember**: the four books diverge on the carve-out, the tiers, and whether an empty net removes the "from behind" test at all. They are set out in [Breakaways](#breakaways).
7. **Of the three backcheck lanes the middle is the most dangerous to leave unattended, and the middle-lane driver is the assignment most often missed** — that ranking is coaching emphasis, not a counted one. Under the lane-and-man systems taught here nobody chases a puck carrier your defenceman already has — though other teams coach the first forward back to pressure the carrier by design, so ask which yours runs before improvising it. **What pressuring never means:** you arrive behind a player skating at their own end boards, so take the puck, take the ice, or angle them off it — **never finish into their back, which carries no minor option in either the NHL or the IIHF book: a major with an automatic game misconduct.** And check whether you may body-check at all before you plan to: **USA Hockey 604(a) bars it at 12 & under and below, in all Girls'/Women's and all non-check adult classifications, and Hockey Canada 7.3(a) at U13 and below, throughout female hockey, and in any division a Member has approved.** More in [What hounding never means](#what-hounding-never-means). **Who owns the middle is a coaching choice, not a law** — the centre in the most common scheme, the first man back in others, man-on-man from the puck outward, or a defenceman rather than a forward under two of the four named systems — but under every version somebody owns it and nobody assumes it is somebody else. Find out which yours runs: [The three lanes](#the-three-lanes) and [The four named backchecking systems](#the-four-named-backchecking-systems).
8. **Step up or back off — never half.** Step up only when you are confident you can win the puck, not hopeful; otherwise back off with a tight gap. **How aggressive "confident" should be is a coaching choice:** an aggressive neutral-zone press wants you challenging at the line, a passive trap wants you holding and steering the carrier wide. Ask which risk your coach wants to carry.
9. **The best rush defence is not creating the rush.** Five causes account for nearly all of them — **coaching emphasis, not a counted ranking, because no public data classifies odd-man rushes by the play that created them.** Pinches with no read, forwards leaving early, neutral-zone turnovers, badly timed line changes, and nobody left above the puck in the offensive zone — usually both defencemen going below at once, though **both going is not always the error**, because a **five-man rotation** covers the line with whichever player is highest and a **single-high** structure makes the safety the weak-side winger. The habit that prevents it is knowing **who** is above the puck before you go below it, as a person rather than a position.
10. **The rush is not over when the shot is saved — it is over when everybody has a mark.** Locate the net front first, because nobody was assigned to it during the rush. Which mark is yours depends on your team's defensive-zone coverage, so know whether you play low zone collapse or man-on-man before the puck gets there.
