## Key focus

**Get below the puck in your own end.** Your defenceman needs an outlet underneath them — inside the dots and below the hash marks, not on the boards. That is the swing breakout; under a centre fly the weak-side winger drops in instead, so ask which your team plays.

**Never leave the slot empty in your own end.** With the puck at the net, hold the high slot and mark whoever is there. With it in the corner, help win it and call the weak-side winger in behind you — that is a zone collapse; under man-on-man you follow your check.

**Own the middle lane in both directions.** Going forward it is where you see everyone from. Coming back, the forward arriving late behind the carrier usually hurts you more than the carrier does — take that forward, on their inside, between them and your net.

**In their end, play the slot and finish at the net.** Shoot from between the circles, and pass across the middle to make the goalie move. Keep moving until your defenceman winds up, then plant in front of the goalie's eyes and go for the rebound — around the goalie, never into them.

**Be first in on the forecheck, and steer rather than hit.** Arrive under control, not off a run-up — even two hard strides can be charging — and drive at the carrier on an angle that pushes them toward one boards, so your winger knows where to pin the puck. That is the 2-1-2; a 1-2-2 sends only one forward in, so ask.

**Under pressure, put the puck to the outside.** The middle of your own zone and the middle of the neutral zone are the worst places on the ice to try to beat somebody. Rim it, chip it off the boards, or give it to a winger on the wall and go and get it back.

**Take the draws that matter seriously:** your own end, special teams, the last minute and after an icing. Agree with your linemates beforehand where you are winning each. If you cannot win it clean, lift or press the other centre's stick with yours for an instant, then play the puck — stick on stick only, never a hand on their stick or your stick on their hands.

## Overview

```facts
Convention: Low support on the breakout belongs to the swing breakout, a coaching choice — too high and your defenceman has no outlet, too wide and one opponent covers you and your winger; under a centre fly you leave early up the middle instead
Key: Coming back, the forward arriving late and unmarked behind the puck carrier is usually more dangerous than the carrier — coaching emphasis rather than a counted ranking
Convention: Which backchecker owns the middle lane is a coaching choice — the centre under the positional assignment assumed here, the first man back on some teams, an assigned check under man-on-man; if nobody has called it, take it
Key: The slot pays for where it is rather than for how hard you shoot — on coaching-consensus ranges a slot shot is worth roughly three to seven times a perimeter one, from a source that names no dataset
Priority: Put it to the outside in the middle of your own zone and the neutral zone — both graded high for turnover cost: shot danger in one, odd-man rushes in the other. Coaching consensus, not counted
Key: Prepare the draws that matter — computed from NHL official statistics, team faceoff percentage explains about 6% of points-percentage variation over eleven seasons; in 2016-17 the link was negative
Convention: "The centre skates the furthest" is coaching craft — no study separates centres from wingers on distance, though the NHL publishes per-skater distance against a position code, so the comparison is unrun rather than impossible
Key: Measured, the workload belief reverses — Lignell 2018, 36 NHL players: defencemen skated 29% more in total than forwards and 47% longer on the ice; forwards 54% more high-intensity skating per minute
```

You are the link between the other four skaters: you play the middle of the ice in all three zones, and your job moves with the puck. A winger owns a lane and a defenceman owns an end; you own the space between them. That is why a centre's instructions vary more from team to team than anyone else's.

**Getting below the puck is the base of all your own-end work.** Too high, and your defenceman has no safe outlet and the shift starts in your own end. Too wide, out on the boards, and one opponent covers you and your winger at once. **That low support belongs to the swing breakout, which is a coaching choice.** Under a **centre fly** you leave early up the middle and the weak-side winger drops in to do the low job; under a **flat or spread breakout** both wingers hold higher and the exit uses longer passes. The commonest breakdown is a centre flying while the wingers hold high, which leaves nobody under the puck. [Breakouts](../systems/breakouts.md) is the authority.

**Protecting the slot gives the centre the most defensive work of the three forwards, and the job moves.** Your ice is the high slot when the puck is in front of your net and the strong-side corner when it is not; the read is which needs you more. The failure is leaving both: going to the corner without calling the winger into the slot trades a battle you might win for a chance nobody can stop. Hold the slot when your defenceman and the strong-side winger already have the corner. **Under the low zone collapse assumed throughout, a coaching choice, when you leave the slot for the corner the weak-side winger covers it** — under man-on-man the weak-side winger stays with their check, and you may be handed the net-front man yourself. Under the collapse the goalmouth is the weak-side defenceman's, not yours.

**Owning the middle is two jobs on one piece of ice.** With the puck it is a vantage point: from the centre lane you see both wingers and both defencemen, so carry through there to distribute. Coming back, it is the lane an attack scores from — the player who beats you is usually the forward arriving late behind the carrier, which is **coaching emphasis rather than a counted ranking**. Get between that player and your net; a trailing backchecker defends nothing. **Who takes that lane is a coaching choice too**: the centre here, the first man back on some teams, man-on-man on others, and no count of which is commonest has been found. If nobody has called it, take it.

**In the offensive zone the slot is your ice — use it.** Get open between the circles, shoot when you have space there, screen the goalie when a point shot is coming, and go to the net for the rebound under control, taking the ice in front of the goalie and never the goalie. On the coaching-consensus ranges in [Shooting](../technique/shooting.md) a slot shot is worth several times a perimeter one; the source names no dataset, so trust the size of the gap, not the decimals. The limits are the crease and the goalie: screen from outside the crease, with your skates off the red crease line and not merely out of the blue paint, and never so tight that the goalie cannot move. What crossing either line costs differs by rulebook.

**On the forecheck you are usually first in, and the job is the angle, not the hit.** Drive at the carrier on a line that pushes them toward one boards, so your strong-side winger knows where the puck is going. Steering is legal in every league; a push or a body check may not be in yours, and a run-up at a carrier, even two hard strides, can be charging. F1, F2 and F3 are taken in order of arrival, so if a winger gets there first you support. The 2-1-2 assumed here is a coaching choice — [Forechecking Systems](../systems/forechecking_systems.md) sets out the alternatives.

**Getting the puck to the outside survives every system.** The middle of your own zone is the most expensive place to lose it, and the neutral zone is where odd-man rushes are made — both **coaching consensus rather than a measured ranking**. Under pressure in either, move it to the boards or behind your own net first, and distribute from there.

**The draws are your defining skill.** An NHL game has **roughly 56 faceoffs**, **computed from NHL official statistics**, yet team faceoff percentage explains only about **6% of the variation** in points percentage across eleven seasons, and in 2016-17 the link went negative. So prepare for the ten or fifteen draws a game that carry real consequence — a coaching estimate, not a count. [Faceoffs](../systems/faceoffs.md) carries all three figures with their limits.

**One belief you will hear about the position is half wrong.** That the centre skates the furthest is coaching craft: **no published study separates centres from wingers on skating distance.** What has been measured points the other way — Lignell et al. (2018), 36 NHL players in one game, found that **defencemen covered 29% more skating in total than forwards and were on the ice 47% longer**, while forwards did 54% more high-intensity skating per minute. The surviving half is **territory, not mileage** — your job spans all three zones and the full width of the rink — and that is coaching craft too: no measurement of how much ice a position covers was found for this document.

**Every line, zone and area of the ice is defined in [Rink Map](../foundation/rink_map.md), and the vocabulary on top of it — high and low, strong and weak side, the slot, the half-wall, F1, F2 and F3 — in [Language and Glossary](../foundation/language_and_glossary.md).**

Three of those words do particular work here. **High** means toward the blue line you are facing and **low** toward the goal line — and you will be told to **swing low** more than anyone, which means below the hash marks, not merely below the puck. **Strong side** is the side the puck is on, and yours switches every time the puck does. And the **half-wall** — the boards roughly level with the faceoff dot — is your wingers' ice; drifting onto it is the commonest way a centre wrecks their line's spacing.

Each zone has the same four situations. *Without the Puck* means the puck is not on your stick **and** you are not pressuring whoever has it — a different job in each zone.

## Defensive Zone

The centre is normally given the most demanding defensive role among the forwards — coaching craft rather than a measured comparison, and it follows from the assignment below rather than from the position itself. You support the defenders and protect the most dangerous part of the ice — the slot, where most goals are scored from: close to half of NHL goals come from the inner slot and just over a quarter from the outer slot. [Shooting](../technique/shooting.md) has that figure, and the limit travels with it — it is Sportlogiq tracking of three seasons at five-on-five, and the tracking is not public.

---

### Defensive Zone: Carrying the Puck — Free Space

```facts
Position: Low in the defensive zone, below the hash marks, swinging toward the puck — the conventional swing breakout; under a centre fly you leave early up the middle instead
Action: Receive the puck from your defenceman and immediately look to exit the zone
Options: Carry up the middle if the lane is clearly open, or distribute to a winger on the boards
Goal: Be the reliable outlet that starts the breakout and gets the puck out of the zone
Key: On the conventional swing breakout your low positioning is what gives the defencemen a safe pass — if you don't swing low, they are stuck with the puck under pressure
```

**Where to be:** Low in the defensive zone, below the hash marks, swinging toward the puck. (Hash marks are the small alignment marks on the sides of the faceoff circles, roughly level with the faceoff dot. "Below" means in the area between them and the goal line.)

**What to do:** Receive the puck from your defenceman and immediately look to exit the zone. Carry up the middle if the lane is clearly open, or distribute to a winger on the boards. Your movement low in the zone is what makes the breakout (your team's controlled exit from the defensive zone) work.

**Goal:** Be the reliable outlet that starts the breakout and carries or passes the puck out of the zone.

**Key considerations:** Your low positioning is what gives the defencemen a safe pass. If you don't swing low, they are stuck with the puck under pressure. **That low support job belongs to the conventional swing breakout, which is a coaching choice** — under a **centre fly** you leave the zone early up the middle instead and the weak-side winger drops in to do it in your place. Find out which your team plays — [Breakouts](../systems/breakouts.md) is the authority.

### Defensive Zone: Carrying the Puck — Under Pressure

**In short:** Get out of the middle to the boards or behind the net, then rim or reverse it, choosing before the puck arrives.

```facts
Position: Low in the defensive zone near the boards or behind the net
Never: Carry through traffic in your own zone — the middle of your own end is the most expensive place to lose it, on coaching consensus reasoned from shot danger and recovery time, not measurement
Read: Rim it onward around the boards to beat pressure that is sealing you in; reverse it back against the flow, to a defenceman arriving behind you, to beat pressure that has over-committed
Priority: Getting the puck out of danger beats making a great play — get to a safe position first, then move it to a winger or rim it around the boards
Action: Make the rim-or-reverse read while the puck is still travelling to you — a look over your shoulder before it arrives, not after
Never: Turn your back to anyone coming at you on a rim or a reverse — USA Hockey warns them: "Even a light hit from behind could inflict a severe head or neck injury". Skates parallel, head up
Never: Duck to avoid a check — in a head-down collision with the boards or a goal post, your neck can break at walking speed; no speed is slow enough to make that safe. Head up, chin off your chest instead
Rule: Where body checking is barred it is barred both ways — a forechecker behind you on a rim or reverse may not check you either, and it is at least a minor on them (USA Hockey 604(c), Hockey Canada 7.3(a)). Incidental board contact is legal everywhere, so never turn your back to anyone coming at you
Rule: CARHA 79(a) makes a rim with a wind-up more than fifteen inches behind the puck a slap-shot minor, and a major if it injures, which ends your game and at least the next — in a CARHA league keep the backswing short
Rule: A minor is the floor and not the ceiling on a check thrown where checking is barred — Hockey Canada 7.3(b) allows a major and game misconduct on the degree of violence and makes both mandatory if the check injures you; USA Hockey 604(d) is mandatory for reckless endangerment
```

**Where to be:** Low in the defensive zone near the boards or behind the net.

**What to do:** Don't try to carry through traffic in your own zone. Get to a safe position and quickly move the puck to a winger or rim it around the boards (a hard shot along the boards that follows the curve of the rink around to a teammate further along — typically from one corner, around behind the net, to the far corner). The priority is getting the puck out of danger, not making a great play. In a CARHA league keep the backswing short: a wind-up more than fifteen inches behind the puck is a slap-shot minor, and a major that ends your game and at least the next if it injures an opponent.

A **rim** and a **reverse** are different plays: a rim sends the puck *onward* around the boards in the direction you're facing; a reverse sends it *back the way it came*, against the flow of the forecheck, usually to your defence partner or a defenceman arriving behind you. A reverse beats pressure that has over-committed; a rim beats pressure that is sealing you in.

**Both plays put a forechecker behind you, so take the contact properly. Never turn your back to anyone coming at you, and never duck.** Arrive with your skates already parallel to the boards, forearm and hip to the wall — never the point of your shoulder, and not the whole shoulder if you can help it — head up and chin off your chest. Facing the wall square is the last resort, for when you are already facing it and cannot turn parallel: get your stick and gloves up and spread on the glass to cushion the blow. Never turn to face the wall with the checker behind you.

They are two prohibitions against two different injuries. A turned back makes the contact a hit from behind, which you can neither see nor brace for, and USA Hockey's warning to the player delivering one is that *"Even a light hit from behind could inflict a severe head or neck injury."* Ducking tucks your chin to your chest and straightens your neck, and USA Hockey records that the injury from that head-down collision with the boards or a goal post can occur at walking speed. [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md) covers the full three-case rule and the technique.

**Make that read while the puck is still travelling to you, not after it's on your stick.** While your defenceman is retrieving it behind the net, take a look over your shoulder for the side the forecheck is sealing — that is the same look that lets you go straight into a rim or a reverse the instant you receive, instead of discovering the answer with a forechecker already on you. [Scanning and Anticipation](../hockey-iq/scanning_and_anticipation.md) covers the habit and the research behind it.

![](diagram:centre-out-of-the-middle)

**Goal:** Relieve pressure and safely move the puck toward an exit.

**Key considerations:** The middle of your own zone is the worst place to lose the puck — **coaching consensus reasoned from shot danger and recovery time, not a measured ranking**. [Risk Management](../hockey-iq/risk_management.md) grades every area of the ice by what a turnover there costs you.

**What it costs.** Whether that forechecker may body check you at all depends on your classification, and where checking is barred it is barred both ways: a check on you is a minor on the forechecker in a USA Hockey Competitive Contact category, and under Hockey Canada at U13 and below, in female hockey and in any division a Member has approved. ⚠️ **Neither minor is a ceiling** — Hockey Canada makes a major and game misconduct **mandatory** where a check that would otherwise be a minor injures you, and *May you check at all, and what a check from behind costs* below sets out the rest. ⚠️ **None of it takes anything off the posture.** Both books keep contact incidental to chasing the puck along the boards legal, so the wall still arrives at every level; what changes is only whether anybody may put you into it.

### Defensive Zone: Pressuring an Opponent with the Puck

**In short:** In a low zone collapse, help win the corner when the puck is there; cover the high slot when it is in front.

```facts
Position: Low in the zone, supporting the strong-side defender
Read: Puck in the corner or behind the net — join the strong-side defender; two of you on the puck usually wins it
Read: Puck at the net or in the slot — cover the high slot and mark any opposing forward set up there
Never: Take the goalmouth under a low zone collapse — it is the weak-side defender's, and yours is the layer one further out; under man-on-man or a hybrid it may be yours, so ask which your team plays
Goal: Help the defenders clear the zone — by winning the corner, or by denying the slot
Convention: Low zone collapse (zone) coverage is a coaching choice, not a rule — under man-on-man, or a hybrid of the two, the wingers follow their check into the corner, so find out which your team plays
Action: Under low zone collapse, when you leave the slot for the corner the weak-side winger covers it — call the rotation early so the slot is never empty; under man-on-man they stay with their check
Read: Strong-side winger already down with their man — the corner is covered, so hold the slot; weak-side winger already low with theirs — no collapse is coming, so weigh corner against slot and call it
```

**Where to be:** Low in the zone, supporting the strong-side defender. When the puck is in the corner or behind the net, go in and help win it. When the puck is at the net front or in the slot, cover the **high slot** — the layer between the faceoff dots and the top of the circles. **Under the low zone collapse assumed here** the goalmouth itself belongs to the weak-side defender, not to you; under **man-on-man** or a **hybrid** it belongs to whoever is matched to the net-front forward, and the centre is one of the low three who can be given that man.

**What to do:** Read where the puck is and provide the right support:
- **Puck in the corner or behind the net:** Join the strong-side defender. Two of you on the puck usually wins it. **Go in with your head up and your eyes off your own skates** — a corner is a 2-on-1 by default, and [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md#8-corner-battles) sets out the battle, the escape routes and the safety of it.
- **Puck at the net or in the slot:** Cover the high slot — the area between the faceoff dots and the top of the circles. Mark any opposing forward set up there. The weak-side defender stays glued to the player directly at the net (the goalmouth); your job is the player one layer further out.

The wingers stay high covering the points (the areas near the blue line where the opposing defencemen normally stand), and the weak-side defender holds the net front. **"Stay high" in your own end means level with the opposing point man, and it expires the moment your team wins the puck** — then the winger comes down the wall to the outlet spot. [Winger](winger.md) covers that instruction in full. You are the flexible low support that links the corner battle and the slot.

> **This describes a low zone collapse (zone) coverage system** — the one assumed throughout this document. It is a coaching choice, not a rule of hockey. Under **man-on-man**, every player tracks an assigned opponent and the wingers *do* follow their check into the corner; under a **hybrid**, it's man-on-man below the dots and zone above. Your job changes materially between them. Find out which one your team plays before your first shift — see [Defensive Zone Coverage](../systems/defensive_zone_coverage.md).

**Goal:** Help the defenders clear the zone — either by winning the puck in the corner, or by denying the slot to opposing forwards.

**Key considerations:** Your responsibility shifts with the puck. The wingers are not coming down to help in the corner unless they're following their own man — that role belongs to you. If you stay high in the slot when the puck is in the corner, the strong-side defender is alone against the puck carrier and is likely to lose the battle. When you leave the slot to help in the corner, the **weak-side winger** should collapse from the point to cover the high slot in your absence. Under **man-on-man** the weak-side winger stays with their check, so no rotation is coming. Anyone with a clear view can call the rotation (you, the weak-side defender, or the winger themselves) — speak up early so the slot is never empty.

![](diagram:centre-low-zone-collapse)

**Exception — strong-side winger has walked down with their man:** If the opposing strong-side defenceman has come down off the point and the strong-side winger has followed them, you have extra help low. Read the situation: if your defender and the winger have the puck battle covered, hold the slot rather than committing all the way to the corner.

**Exception — weak-side winger is already low:** If the weak-side opposing defenceman has come down off the point and the weak-side winger is following them, the high-slot collapse isn't available to you when you commit to the corner. Weigh whether the corner needs your help more than the slot needs coverage — and call clearly so the defenders know what to expect.

### Defensive Zone: Without the Puck — Getting Open for the Breakout

```facts
Position: Swinging low — inside the faceoff dots and below the hash marks, in the inside half of your strong-side faceoff circle
Never: Drift out onto the boards — that is the winger's ice, and one opponent can then cover you both
Action: Keep moving, stay open and communicate so the defencemen always have an option
Goal: Be the first and most reliable outlet for the defencemen when they gain possession
Convention: Low support belongs to the conventional swing breakout — a coaching choice; under a centre fly you leave early and the weak-side winger drops in instead
Action: Find out which breakout your team plays — a centre flying while the wingers hold high near the blue line leaves nobody underneath the puck
Key: If you are too high when your defenceman gets the puck, they have no safe outlet and come under pressure immediately
```

*This is the situation where your team is about to have the puck: your defencemen are winning it or have won it, and you are getting open to receive.*

**Where to be:** Swinging low in the defensive zone — **inside the faceoff dots and below the hash marks**, which in practice means the inside half of your strong-side faceoff circle rather than out on the boards. The boards are the strong-side winger's ice; if you drift out there, one opposing player can cover the two of you and the defenceman loses the middle-lane option entirely. Move toward where the puck is going.

> **Low support is a coaching choice, and the authority says so.** The five-role structure described here belongs to the **conventional swing breakout**, the version the coaching guides behind this document describe and the one assumed throughout — nobody has counted what teams actually run. You will meet two alternatives. Under a **centre fly** (also "centre high") you deliberately leave the zone early up the middle to stretch the ice, and the **weak-side winger drops in** to take the low support job instead. Under a **flat or spread breakout** both wingers hold higher near the blue line and the exit is made with longer passes. **Find out which one your team plays** — the failure that produces most weekend breakdowns is a centre flying in a system where the wingers are holding high, leaving nobody underneath the puck at all. See [Breakouts](../systems/breakouts.md).

![](diagram:centre-swing-low)

**What to do:** Keep moving. Your constant movement low in the zone creates passing options for the defencemen. Stay open and communicate. When your team wins the puck, be in position to receive the first pass and start the breakout.

**Goal:** Be the first and most reliable outlet for the defencemen when they gain possession.

**Key considerations:** If you are too high when your defenceman gets the puck, they have no safe outlet and will come under pressure immediately.

## Neutral Zone

The transition zone is where the centre connects defence and attack.

---

### Neutral Zone: Carrying the Puck — Free Space

```facts
Position: Through the middle of the ice, leading or driving the rush
Action: Carry with pace through the centre of the ice and distribute to the best option
Read: If a winger is open, pass and let them enter in stride; if you have space, carry through yourself
Goal: Lead or direct a controlled transition through the neutral zone and into the offensive zone
Key: The centre of the ice is the best place to decide from — you can see both wingers and both defencemen
Never: Carry for its own sake. If a teammate is in a better position, move the puck instead
Never: Carry through the middle with your head down — you cannot brace for a hit you cannot see
Rule: Head up under every book: IIHF Rule 48.1 does not call a square, no-upward check on a carrier skating head down at the hitter a head hit — it can still be charging, and is illegal wherever checking is barred — and NHL 48.1(ii) and PWHL 48.3(i) weigh that posture against you
```

**Where to be:** Through the middle of the ice, leading or driving the rush.

**What to do:** Carry with pace through the centre of the ice. Read the play — if a winger is open, pass and let them enter in stride. If you have space, carry through yourself. Distribute to the best option and keep the play moving at speed.

![](diagram:centre-nz-deciding-from-the-middle)

**Goal:** Lead or direct a controlled transition through the neutral zone and into the offensive zone.

**Key considerations:** The centre of the ice is the best place to make decisions from — you can see both wingers and both defencemen. Use that view, and don't carry for its own sake; if a teammate is in a better position, move the puck. ⚠️ **And carry through the middle with your head up.** You cannot brace for a hit you cannot see, so the view you are carrying the puck for is the same thing that keeps you safe in it.

**What it costs.** No book fully protects a head-down carrier. The IIHF's — the British reader's — is the only one read here that excuses, for the head-contact penalty alone, a square check on a carrier skating head down toward the hitter, where the hitter does not drive up into them; the same hit can still be charging, and where checking is barred it is still illegal. The NHL and the PWHL weigh that posture against you too. [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md#keep-your-head-up-always--you-cannot-brace-for-a-hit-you-cannot-see) sets out the rule.

### Neutral Zone: Carrying the Puck — Under Pressure

```facts
Position: Through the middle, or shifting to the boards if the middle is congested
Action: Move the puck to a winger or chip it off the boards
Never: Carry through heavy traffic in the middle of the neutral zone. Move the puck to a winger or chip it off the boards instead
Risk: Coaching material puts the making of odd-man rushes in the middle third — consensus rather than a tracked figure, because no public data classifies one by the play that created it
Priority: Quick decision-making is worth more than individual skill here — keep it simple and get the puck to the outside
Goal: Maintain possession through the neutral zone or safely advance the puck
```

**Where to be:** Through the middle, or shifting to the boards if the middle is congested.

**What to do:** If pressured in the middle of the neutral zone, move the puck to a winger or chip it off the boards. Don't try to carry through heavy traffic in the middle: coaching material puts the making of **odd-man rushes** — attacks where the attackers outnumber the defenders coming back, like a 2-on-1 or a 3-on-2, and a high-danger scoring chance — in the middle third of the ice. **That is coaching consensus rather than a tracked figure**: no public data classifies an odd-man rush by the play that created it. Quick decision-making is more valuable than individual skill here.

![](diagram:centre-nz-get-it-to-the-outside)

**Goal:** Maintain possession through the neutral zone or safely advance the puck.

**Key considerations:** Keep it simple under pressure and get the puck to the outside. [Neutral Zone Systems](../systems/neutral_zone_systems.md) and [Risk Management](../hockey-iq/risk_management.md) both carry the odd-man-rush point and its limit.

### Neutral Zone: Pressuring an Opponent with the Puck

```facts
Position: In the middle of the neutral zone — the closest forward initiates pressure, and that is usually you
Action: Drive at the puck carrier under control, stick on the puck and not the body, or cut off the centre lane to force them wide
Read: Step up to challenge when the opportunity is clear; otherwise deny the middle and force the play to the boards
Goal: Deny a clean entry, or force a turnover before they reach your blue line
Convention: How hard you press is your team's neutral-zone system, not a law — containment under a 1-2-2, 1-2-2 trap or 1-3-1; two forwards hunting the puck under an aggressive press
Action: Find out which neutral-zone system your team plays and what the call is for switching — most teams run more than one
Key: If you don't initiate pressure, the opponent gets a free run at your defencemen
```

**Where to be:** In the middle of the neutral zone. You are the primary forechecker here — the closest forward to the puck initiates pressure, and that is usually you.

**What to do:** Drive at the puck carrier to disrupt their advance, or cut off the centre lane to force them wide. The wingers support by blocking passing lanes on their sides, and the defencemen maintain their gap at the blue line. Step up to challenge when the opportunity is clear; otherwise, deny the middle and force the play to the boards. When you do step up, arrive under control and go for the puck with your stick, not the carrier's body: a run-up at them, even two hard strides, can be charging, and in a non-checking division a hit is a penalty.

> **How hard you press, and whether your wingers hold their lanes at all, is your team's neutral-zone system — a coaching choice, not a law of hockey.** What is described here is the **containment** job: under a **1-2-2**, a **1-2-2 trap** or a **1-3-1**, you are the lone forward taking the middle away and steering the carrier to a side, the wingers hold their lanes, and getting the turnover is a bonus rather than the objective. The realistic alternative is an **aggressive neutral-zone press** — the middle-third shape behind a 2-1-2 forecheck — where two forwards are gone hunting the puck, the wingers *do* leave their lanes to pressure, and the point is to take the puck back rather than wait for a mistake. Your job changes materially between them. **Find out which your team plays and what the call is for switching**, because most teams run more than one depending on the score and the shift. [Neutral Zone Systems](../systems/neutral_zone_systems.md) is the authority.

**Goal:** Deny the opposing team a clean entry through the neutral zone, or force a turnover before they reach your blue line.

**Key considerations:** You are the line's first wave of defence in transition. If you don't initiate pressure, the opponent gets a free run at your defencemen. Controlling the middle of the neutral zone limits what the opposing team can do — if the centre lane is blocked, they are forced wide, where the wingers can support.

### Neutral Zone: Without the Puck — Supporting the Rush Through the Middle

**In short:** Stay level with or just behind the puck through the middle, and set the line's pace by arriving at full speed, not coasting.

```facts
Position: Through the centre of the neutral zone, level with or slightly behind the puck
Action: Be a moving target — keep pace with the play and stay connected to both wingers
Key: You set the line's pace into the zone — time your stride so the puck crosses first and arrive at full speed
Never: Coast to stay onside — a line that enters slowly is the one a defence stands up at the blue line, and that costs more than the whistle would
Never: Drop your head through the middle to gather a pass or to count your linemates out — you cannot brace for a hit you cannot see
Rule: You are offside only when both skates have completely crossed the blue line before the puck has; a trailing skate on the line keeps you onside (NHL Rule 83.1)
Rule: A trail skate held in the air is onside under NHL, IIHF and PWHL rules but offside under USA Hockey Rule 630(a) and Hockey Canada Rule 6.11, which require skate contact; CARHA never defines its "own side", so keep the blade down
Rule: USA Hockey Rule 630(d) grants delayed offside and tag-up only at Youth 15-Only and above, Girls 16U and above, High School and Adult — below those the whistle is immediate
Rule: Tag-up is a five-player act — the offside is nullified only when every player of the offending team makes skate contact with the blue line at the same instant (NHL Rule 83.3(i)), so count your linemates out
```

**Where to be:** Moving through the centre of the neutral zone, level with or slightly behind the puck.

**What to do:** Be a moving target in the middle of the ice. Keep pace with the play so you are available to receive a pass and carry through. Stay connected to both wingers.

![](diagram:centre-nz-arrive-behind-the-puck)

**Goal:** Be the primary target for outlet passes and keep the rush moving at pace.

**Key considerations:** Stay onside without giving up speed. The centre is the player who most often coasts to stay onside, and that costs the line more than a whistle would. **Keep your trailing blade down on the line**, and where tag-up exists, count your linemates out before you turn and go back in.

**You set the line's pace into the zone.** You are attacking through the middle, the widest and most-watched lane, so it is tempting to slow down and be safe. Instead, time your stride so the puck crosses first and arrive at full speed. If you coast, both wingers arrive with you, and a line that enters slowly is exactly the one a defence stands up at the blue line. ⚠️ **Do all of it with your head up.** Arriving at speed through the middle, and counting your linemates out to tag up, both invite you to drop your eyes, and you cannot brace for a hit you cannot see. [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md#keep-your-head-up-always--you-cannot-brace-for-a-hit-you-cannot-see) sets out the rule and the books that weigh a head-down posture against you.

**When your defence regroups the puck behind you, curl back with them.** Swing low through the middle of the neutral zone so you meet the pass already skating forward, rather than standing at the red line waiting for it — a pass taken standing still gives the other team time to set up in front of you. Teams differ on whether the centre or a winger curls back for it, so ask which yours wants.

**What it costs.** You are offside only when **both** skates have completely crossed the blue line before the puck. A blade on the line is onside in all six books. A skate held in the air over the neutral zone is onside under the NHL, the IIHF and the PWHL, but offside under USA Hockey and Hockey Canada, which require skate *contact*. **CARHA never defines its "own side" of the line, so in a CARHA-affiliated league drag the blade.**

A delayed offside can be undone by tagging up — **in the books that have one.** **Under USA Hockey, delayed offside exists only at Youth 15-Only and above, Girls 16U and above, High School and Adult**; below those levels the whistle goes immediately and the entry has to be right first time. Where tag-up exists it is a five-player act: the offside is cancelled only when every player of the offending team has cleared the zone at the same instant. The full rule is in [Rules Primer](../foundation/rules_primer.md).

## Offensive Zone

The centre operates in the most dangerous part of the offensive zone — the slot — and directs the attack.

---

### Offensive Zone: Carrying the Puck — Free Space

**In short:** With the puck and space in the slot, shoot; the one pass worth making first is the one across the middle.

```facts
Position: In the slot — between the two faceoff circles, from the top of the circles down to the front of the net
Action: Look to shoot from the slot — on coaching-consensus conversion ranges a slot shot is worth roughly three to seven times a perimeter one
Key: The slot-versus-perimeter conversion ranges come from a source that names no dataset, so trust the size of the gap rather than the decimals
Action: Before you shoot, look for the pass that crosses in front of the net rather than one up your own side — a puck that crosses the middle makes the goalie push sideways and arrives before they are set
Options: If defenders close in, find a winger on the half-wall or in the circle, or a defenceman at the point
Goal: Create a shot on net, either directly or by finding a teammate in a better position
Key: With the puck in the slot and space in front of you, shoot — don't overthink it
Action: Keep moving and keep the puck moving
```

**Where to be:** In the slot — the high-danger area between the two faceoff circles, stretching from the top of the circles down to the front of the net.

**What to do:** Look to shoot from the slot. On the coaching-consensus conversion ranges in [Shooting](../technique/shooting.md) — roughly 10–15% from the slot against 2–4% from the perimeter, **on a source that names no dataset**, so trust the gap rather than the decimals — a slot shot is worth roughly three to seven times a perimeter one. If defenders close in, distribute to a winger on the half-wall (the boards area roughly level with the faceoff dot — between the corner and the point) or in the circle, or to a defenceman at the point for a shot. Keep moving and keep the puck moving.

**One pass does beat the shot, and it is the one across the middle.** A pass that crosses the **royal road** — the imaginary line running lengthwise down the centre of the ice, from the middle of one net to the middle of the other — immediately before a shot forces the goalie out of small shuffles into a hard lateral push, so the puck arrives before they are set. In Alan Wells' analysis of Ryan Stimson's Passing Project data it is the highest-value pass type measured, finishing at **15.50%, roughly double the next one** — volunteer-tracked, 51,308 shots at five-on-five, counting all attempts rather than shots on goal. As a centre you are at both ends of it more often than anyone: the far-side receiver when a winger has the puck on the wall, and the passer when you come out from behind the net, because a pass from below the goal line up to the slot crosses the road almost by definition. So the pass to refuse is the extra one along your own side of the ice — never the one that changes which side of the net the goalie is looking at. [Offensive Zone Play](../systems/offensive_zone_play.md) owns the figure and the habits that create one.

![](diagram:centre-oz-slot-options)

**Goal:** Create a shot on net, either directly or by finding a teammate in a better position.

**Key considerations:** The slot is where the most goals are scored — [Shooting](../technique/shooting.md) has the figures and their limits. If you have the puck there with space, shoot. Don't overthink it. If you're covered, quick passing creates the next opportunity.

### Offensive Zone: Carrying the Puck — Under Pressure

**In short:** Protect it on the wall or behind the net and cycle to open the slot, but make every cycle end in an attack.

```facts
Position: In the slot, behind the net, or along the boards near the circles
Technique: Use your body to protect the puck
Never: Turn your back to anyone coming at you on a cycle, or coming out from behind the net — USA Hockey warns them: "Even a light hit from behind could inflict a severe head or neck injury". Head up
Never: Duck to avoid a check — a head-down collision with the boards or a goal post can break your neck at walking speed, and behind the net the post is right there. Head up, chin off your chest instead
Options: A quick outlet to a winger on the half-wall, or to the defenceman at the point
Action: Use the back of the net as a pivot — come out the other side or find a late-arriving teammate
Goal: Maintain possession under pressure and keep the offensive play alive
Key: Behind the net you can see the whole ice and decide without risking a direct shot or a dangerous turnover
```

**Where to be:** In the slot, behind the net, or along the boards near the circles.

**What to do:** Use your body to protect the puck. Look for a quick outlet to a winger on the half-wall or the defenceman at the point. If you go behind the net, use it as a pivot point — come out the other side or find a late-arriving teammate.

**Cycling is how you hold the puck low in their end until the slot opens — a set-up, not a way to use up the shift.** A **cycle** is your line rotating around the low zone — corner, half-wall, behind the net — passing the puck short distances to each other while protecting it from the defence. The version you will use most is low-to-low: pass it backwards and down the boards to a teammate arriving behind you, then rotate up into the space they have just left. It works because the defenders are skating without the puck, cannot change lines, and shrink toward it as they tire — which opens the slot you want to arrive in. Four reads make it work:

- **Rim or reverse, and the checker tells you which.** If the checker is still in front of you, **rim** it — onward around the boards, past the player sealing you to the wall. If they have skated past you, **reverse** it — back against the flow, into the space they have just vacated. Picking the wrong one is a leading cycle turnover; **nobody has counted cycle turnovers by cause**, so take the ranking as coaching craft and the read itself as the point.
- **Bump it off the wall rather than onto a stick.** Fire the puck hard at a spot on the boards so it caroms to your teammate. A stick in the passing lane cannot block that, and a defender standing between you does not matter — but call it, because a bump nobody is expecting is a turnover.
- **Cut back when you run out of wall.** A defender who has committed their momentum toward the corner cannot change direction as fast as you can, so skating back up the boards you came down buys two or three seconds and usually opens the pass up to the point. Turn while they are still too far away to hit you; once they can reach you it is too late to turn, so go side-on to the wall, forearm and hip against it, and never turn to face the wall with them behind you.
- **Scissor to catch a switch.** You and a supporting teammate skate at each other and cross, the support player going on the outside; you drop it to them or keep it. Defenders in the middle of swapping assignments are briefly covering nobody.

**And it has to end in something.** A cycle that does not finish in a shot from in front rather than the perimeter, a pass across the middle, a low-to-high with a screen, or a drive to the net has accomplished nothing — three tidy laps and no shot is the defence's result, not yours. [Offensive Zone Play](../systems/offensive_zone_play.md) owns the cycle, including when to stop cycling and attack the net instead.

**The same posture applies here as in your own end: never turn your back to anyone coming at you, and never duck.** **Behind the net the second one matters twice over, because the goal post is right there** — USA Hockey names the post alongside the boards for the head-down collision that can injure your neck at walking speed. Skates parallel to the boards, forearm and hip to the wall — not the point of your shoulder, and not the whole shoulder if you can help it — head up and chin off your chest. [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md) covers the full three-case rule.

![](diagram:oz-behind-the-net)

**Goal:** Maintain possession under pressure and keep the offensive play alive.

**Key considerations:** Behind the net is a useful safe area. From there you can see the whole ice and make a decision without being at immediate risk of a direct shot or a dangerous turnover.

### Offensive Zone: Pressuring an Opponent with the Puck

**In short:** As the first forechecker, take an angle on the carrier, arrive under control, and steer them to one side; steer, don't push.

```facts
Key: F1/F2/F3 are roles, not people — if a winger beats you to the puck they are F1 and you take the support role, immediately
Action: Be first in as F1 — forecheck through the middle at the opposing defenceman, or wherever the puck has gone
Technique: Drive at the carrier and take an angle, steering them to one side so your F2 knows where to support — steering is legal in every league; a push or a check may not be, so check whether you may check
Goal: Force a turnover or a bad outlet pass, and keep the opposing defence under constant pressure
Convention: The 2-1-2 with F3 on the weak side is one system among several, not a law — the stacked variant shades F3 to the puck side and concedes the far-side breakout, so find out which your team runs
Rule: Under NHL and IIHF rules the opposing goalie may play the puck behind the goal line only inside the trapezoid — the rules write one exception, skate contact with the crease; the IIHF Situation Handbook adds two more, at Situations 27.6 and 27.9 (NHL 27.8; IIHF 27.7)
Rule: USA Hockey, Hockey Canada and CARHA print no trapezoid, and the PWHL drops it in 1.8 and 27.8 though its 65.2(viii) keeps the minor, so their goalie can retrieve your dump-in and the dump-and-chase is worth much less — check your book, and whether your rink has the lines painted
Rule: Even with no trapezoid the forecheck still pays under USA Hockey — 614(c) bars their goalie freezing the puck outside the "goalkeeper's privileged" area, which excludes the corners, and 614(c)(1) bars it behind the goal line with the body entirely clear of the crease, so the puck has to move
Rule: Hockey Canada is blunter still — Interpretation 3 to Rule 10.1(a), clause v: "When a goaltender leaves their crease, they will not be allowed to freeze the puck. If they do, they will be assessed a Minor penalty. No warning will be issued."
Rule: The restricted area is IIHF Rule 27.7; nothing in the Elite League's Casebook switches it off, but England Ice Hockey's Rules & Regulations 22.3 call it "not currently enforced", scope and standing unsettled — in any British competition, plan for their goalie to come out
Action: Aim dump-ins away from the trapezoid so a defenceman must retrieve the puck with you arriving — how much rec and youth hockey marks one is a question about leagues, and this document has no count
Action: If their goalie comes out for your dump-in, pull up and take the lane they want to pass into — never finish on a goalie
```

**Where to be:** Forechecking through the middle — directly at the opposing defenceman or wherever the puck has gone in the opposition's defensive zone.

**What to do:** Be first on the forecheck (F1). Drive straight at the puck carrier, cutting off their options and forcing a quick decision. Take a deliberate angle — **steer** them toward one side (typically the strong-side boards) so your F2 winger knows where to support. Take the angle with your feet and arrive under control, because a run-up at a carrier, even two hard strides, can be charging. ⚠️ **Steer, don't push** — steering is legal in every league; a push is a penalty in a non-checking division, and a violent one can end your game.

**Goal:** Force a turnover or a bad outlet pass. Keep the opposing defence under constant pressure.

**Key considerations:** Forechecks are named by counting players from the goal line outwards — always three groups, always adding to five — so the common **2-1-2** is two forwards in on the puck, one in the middle, two defencemen high at the line. It is one system among several. See [Forechecking Systems](../systems/forechecking_systems.md) for the alternatives (1-2-2, 1-3-1, 1-4) and when each is chosen.

In a 2-1-2:
- **F1 (usually you, as the centre):** First in. Pressure the puck, take a deliberate angle.
- **F2 (the strong-side winger):** Second support on the strong side, sealing the puck wherever you've pushed it — usually the boards (corner) or behind the net.
- **F3 (the weak-side winger):** Stays higher to cover the breakout pass and any opposing forward leaking up the middle. **Which side F3 sits on is a genuine coaching split, not a law of hockey.** The weak-side default described here is the common one; in the **strong-side (stacked) variant** F3 shades to the puck side instead, so all three forwards overload one corner and the far-side breakout is conceded deliberately. Find out which your team runs — [Forechecking Systems](../systems/forechecking_systems.md) sets out both.

The defencemen hold the blue line at the points. These are **roles, not people**, defined by order of arrival rather than by position — if a winger beats you to the puck they are F1 and you take the support role, immediately, and without swapping back. [Language and Glossary](../foundation/language_and_glossary.md#f1-f2-and-f3) sets that out in full, including the part that catches people out: the numbering belongs to whichever team is forechecking, so on a breakout F1 is the opponent bearing down on you. Communicate clearly so the line moves as a unit and no one is left guessing.

![](diagram:forecheck-212)

**The trapezoid works in your favour here.** Under NHL and IIHF rules the opposing goalie may only play the puck behind their goal line inside the marked trapezoid, so they cannot come out to the corner and calmly move a dumped puck — a defenceman has to, with you arriving on them. That is exactly why dump-and-chase works, so aim your dump-ins away from the trapezoid. The rule writes one exception, a goalie playing the puck *"while maintaining skate contact with"* the crease (NHL Rule 27.8; IIHF Rule 27.7). ⚠️ **The IIHF *Situation Handbook* adds two more, both narrow:** a puck stopped **on** the goal line is not in the restricted area (Situation 27.6), and a puck that merely hits a retreating goalie is not one they played (27.9). **Neither moves where you aim** — they only tell you not to expect a whistle every time their goalie touches a puck out there.

**Find out whether your league has a trapezoid at all.** USA Hockey, Hockey Canada and CARHA print none, and the PWHL switches it off (Rules 1.8 and 27.8) while its Rule 65.2(viii) still writes the minor, so that book contradicts itself. Against a goalie who can play the corner, dump-and-chase is worth much less. **How much rec, beer-league and youth hockey that covers is a question about leagues, and this document has no count for it**, so look behind the net and ask.

**It is still worth something, so forecheck their goalie as a puck-handler, not as a whistle.** Under USA Hockey a goalie may not fall on or gather the puck outside their privileged area — which excludes the corners — or behind the goal line with their body entirely outside the crease; under Hockey Canada a goalie who freezes the puck after leaving the crease takes a minor, with no warning. So their goalie may fetch your dump-in but may not sit on it. If the goalie comes out for it, pull up and take the lane they want to pass into, never the goalie. ⚠️ **But know where their cover is legal under USA Hockey**: in front of the goal line inside the privileged area, and on a loose rebound outside the crease while you are pressuring them. **The whistle is the officials' call, not yours** — play on through a cover you decided was illegal and you arrive into a goalie, which is priced below.

**🇬🇧 In any British competition, plan for their goalie to come out for your dump-in.** The IIHF book carries the rule at 27.7, and nothing in the Elite League's Casebook switches it off. But England Ice Hockey's *Rules & Regulations* 22.3 call the restricted area *"not currently enforced"*, its scope and standing unsettled, and the In-House Rules never mention it. The published documents do not settle it, so the attacking team's safe assumption is a goalie who can play the corner. Ask your league, and treat an unmarked sheet as a hint rather than a ruling: IIHF 27.7 turns on *"the position of the puck"*, not on the markings.

**What it costs.** In a non-checking classification the push is a penalty on its own — a minor under Hockey Canada and under USA Hockey — and ⚠️ **neither minor is a ceiling.** Hockey Canada allows a major and game misconduct for a violent push and requires one if it injures; USA Hockey requires one if it recklessly endangers; and both books reach a match penalty — under USA Hockey plan on one for a reckless push, because its Rule 602(a) says one *"shall be assessed"*.

Driving at a carrier from distance is a charge: Hockey Canada counts *"two or more strides immediately prior to making contact"*, USA Hockey *"more than two strides or … an excessive distance"*, and the NHL judges *"distance traveled"*. Whether you may body check at all is answered by your book and your classification rather than your coach — see [§May you check at all](#may-you-check-at-all-and-what-a-check-from-behind-costs) below.

**And never arrive into their goalie.** ⚠️ **Under USA Hockey a deliberate hit on one is charging, never two minutes alone**, and a goalie outside the privileged area is *"NOT 'fair game'"*; under Hockey Canada a charge on a goalie is a mandatory major and game misconduct. *Keep your body out of the crease* below sets out every book. Rule detail is in [Rules Primer](../foundation/rules_primer.md).

### Offensive Zone: Without the Puck — Getting Open in the Slot

**In short:** Get open in the slot: be still as a screen at the release, blade down for tips, and decide on rebounds before the shot.

```facts
Position: In the slot between the two faceoff circles, just outside the crease — close enough to tip shots and pounce on rebounds
Technique: Work just outside the blue paint, off the red crease line rather than on it. Screen hard, keep your stick free for tips and keep your body out of the crease
Rule: Stay off the red crease line, not just the blue paint — every book that settles it counts that line as crease — IIHF 1.7, USA Hockey's 625(b) Note, Hockey Canada's Glossary, CARHA's Creases. The NHL's and PWHL's texts do not say; Table 14 in both, and the IIHF's (16 in 2025/26), allow a goal on it
Action: Screen the goalie when your defenceman is about to shoot — plant, and be still at the moment of release, because shuffling gives the goalie flashes of the puck through the gaps
Never: Plant at the net front without knowing where the near post is — a defender walks you sideways by feel, not sight, with the post at one edge of that path and the goalie at the other. Head up
Never: Duck away from the shot — a flexed neck in a head-first collision with a post can break your neck at walking speed. Chin off your chest, and meet a post with an arm or a leg, never head first
Never: Hold your spot into the goalie — give ground against a goalie, even when it costs the screen
Goal: Be the primary scoring threat in the high-danger area and create chances for yourself and teammates
Key: Keep moving to get open, and slide with the point man as they walk the line — a stationary centre in the slot is easy to cover; the stillness belongs to the moment of release, not to the shift
```

**Where to be:** In the slot, between the two faceoff circles, just outside the crease (the painted area directly in front of the goalie). Stay close enough to tip shots and pounce on rebounds.

**What to do:** Get open in the slot. Look to position yourself for a shot, a tip-in, or a rebound. Screen the goalie when your defenceman is about to shoot, and be still at the moment of release. Between shots, keep moving — a stationary centre in the slot is easier to cover.

**The net front is three separate skills, not one place to stand.**

- **Screening.** Stand between the goalie's **eyes and the puck**, not between the goalie and the net — two different lines, and they move when the point man walks the line, so slide along with them. Chest up and square to the shooter, hips low and knees bent: the posture of a squat, not of a bow. Be still at the moment of release, because constant shuffling gives the goalie flashes of the puck through the gaps. **Track the puck, not the shot** — keep your eyes on the puck at the shooter's stick, ready to tip it. **Your eyes move; your chest does not**: turning your body to follow the flight un-screens the goalie and takes you out of the tip and the rebound. Stand inside the faceoff dots, six to eight feet out from the goal line; the crease arc reaches six, so live at the far end of that range. ⚠️ **Know where the near post is before you plant.**

  Expect contact there. ⚠️ **Take it head up, chin off your chest, and never duck.** You are standing still with your eyes off the man beside you, so a defender can walk you sideways before you see it coming, along a path with a steel post at one edge and the goalie at the other. USA Hockey's *Heads Up Hockey Program Guide* writes the goal post into the spinal-injury mechanism alongside the boards: *"almost all on-ice cervical spine injuries have been due to the head being slightly flexed (head down) while making head-on contact with the boards or goal post"*, and it *"can occur at walking speed"*. Meet a post with an arm or a leg, never your head first. **A box-out — legal or not in your book — walks you along the same path, so this is not a checking-league point.** **And the goalie is the one body at the net front you never take that contact against — give ground rather than hold your spot into them.**
- **Tips.** **Blade on the ice, always** — the overwhelming majority of tippable pucks come in low, and a stick held off the ice at the net front is a stick doing nothing. You are redirecting, not shooting: ten to thirty degrees is enough to beat a set goalie, and a big swing usually misses the puck entirely. Angle the blade and absorb slightly rather than following through — hard hands send it into the glass. And present a target: hold the blade out in the lane you want the puck in, and shout for it.
- **Rebounds.** Decide to go **before** the shot rather than reacting after it. The defending team recovers roughly 60% of rebounds — Greg Revak's figure, read off a chart by Alex Novet that states no league, season or sample size — so take the direction and not the decimal. **Time the arrival by which job you have.** If the net front is your assignment, be planted there before the release. If you are driving in from the wall or your own circle, arrive as the puck is released, not well before it — go early and the defender has time to establish position on you and turn you away from the puck. That split is coaching craft rather than a measured finding. When it comes out, shoot it straight back: do not settle it and do not look for a pass, because the window is a second or two while the goalie is still down. If you get tied up, go to the far post — nobody is covering it. Whichever way you arrive, arrive under control and around the goalie, never into them.

[Offensive Zone Play](../systems/offensive_zone_play.md) owns all three, including the tip-height rules, which differ by book, and the limits on that 60%.

**Goal:** Be the primary scoring threat in the high-danger area and create chances for yourself and teammates.

**Key considerations:** The slot is the hardest area of the ice to defend — **coaching craft rather than a measured comparison** — and the most effective place to score from, which is the half with figures behind it in [Shooting](../technique/shooting.md). Make the opposing centre and defencemen work hard to keep you out of it.

**When your defenceman pinches down the wall, somebody has to fill the point they left — and in many systems that is the highest forward, which is often you.** Read it as the puck goes low: if your defenceman steps in to keep the puck in the zone, slide up to the blue line on their side and play as a defenceman until they are back, rather than staying in the slot and leaving an odd-man rush going the other way. Some teams give that job to the weak-side winger or to whichever forward is F3, so ask your bench who covers the pinch.

#### Keep your body out of the crease — what it costs you differs by book

```facts
Rule: A screen outside the crease without contact usually keeps the goal — NHL Table 14 and IIHF Table 14 (16 in 2025/26), Situations 5C and 5E — but contact, or a position that stops the goalie moving freely in the crease, can void it (69.1, PWHL 71.1)
Rule: Contact with the goalie outside the crease costs the goal under Hockey Canada 8.5 Interpretation 1 and CARHA 66(b), and interfering with the goalie does under USA Hockey 617(c)(4). USA Hockey also counts your stick as you: Casebook 625 Sit. 10 voids it for a stick in the paint, goalie in the crease
Rule: Hockey Canada 8.5 is not flat — a teammate's goal from outside the crease stands if you stay passive in it, and is disallowed once you play the puck, interfere with the play or obstruct the goalie's view or movements; its two goalie-view references both reach only a player in the crease
Rule: Contact you initiate with a goalie in their crease kills the goal (NHL 69.3; IIHF 69.3 requires "a relevant contact"), and the NHL's own sentence signposts the exception — "(refer to Rule 69.7 for an exception)"
Rule: NHL and IIHF 69.7 and PWHL 71.7 allow a goal after "incidental contact" with the goalie on a rebound or a loose puck you both play, "whether inside or outside the crease". The EIHL Casebook disallows a goal for contact in the blue paint; Hockey Canada, USA Hockey and CARHA write no such carve-out
Never: Take your body inside the blue paint, or onto the red crease line — IIHF, USA Hockey, Hockey Canada and CARHA count that line as crease; the NHL does not say. Assume any contact there costs the goal
Never: Stay in the crease after being shoved in — get out, avoiding the goalie. NHL and IIHF 69.1 kill the goal for impairing position, no contact needed; USA Hockey 625(b) relieves position, not conduct
Rule: Being pushed, shoved or fouled into the goalie is not contact you initiated, "provided the attacking player has made a reasonable effort to avoid such contact" (NHL and IIHF Rule 69.1)
Rule: USA Hockey does not run 69.1's premise — 625(b) bars a goal with an attacking player in the crease whoever scored, unless the puck preceded them in, the goalie is out of it, or a defender's interference put them there; blocking a returning goalie is the goal off and a minor (Casebook Situation 9)
Rule: NHL and IIHF 69.4 protects a goalie who has played the puck outside the crease and is then "prevented from returning" by "the deliberate actions of an attacking player" — a separate foul from 69.1's crease-position rule, turning on intent rather than on contact
Rule: Fouled into the goalie, you can be penalised in every book — a mandatory minor under Hockey Canada 8.5(a) Interpretation 2 and CARHA 66(b) if you make no effort to avoid them, NHL, IIHF and PWHL tables without "a reasonable effort", USA Hockey's Casebook unless you "clearly made every attempt"
Rule: Hockey Canada's Interpretation 1 to 8.5(a) governs the goal rather than the penalty, and asks more of you — the goal stands only where the foul left you "unable to avoid contact with the goaltender", so it is no relief from that minor
Rule: Hockey Canada is not capped at a minor — 8.5(b) reaches a major plus game misconduct at the referee's discretion on the degree of violence of impact and mandatorily where the foul injures; 8.5(c) is a match penalty, and 8.5(d) leaves no rung between the minor and the ejection
Rule: Charging a goalie can eject in every book — mandatory in Hockey Canada 8.5(b), CARHA 52(b) in the crease or on injury, NHL, PWHL 42.5 and EIHL 42 if a major injures face or head, USA Hockey 607(b) if reckless, 607(c) has no bare minor in crease or privileged area; only the IIHF has no match penalty
```

You are in the goalie's kitchen more than anyone except a net-front winger, and playing too timidly here costs you goals. **Work just outside the blue paint, off the red crease line rather than on it. Screen hard, keep your stick free for tips, keep your body out of the crease, and never crowd the goalie so tightly they cannot move.** A screen from outside the crease is one of the most valuable things you do.

![](diagram:oz-net-front-screen)

**Stand clear of the red line, not merely the blue paint.** Four books count that line as crease — the IIHF (*"[t]he marked line belongs to the Goal Crease"*), USA Hockey, Hockey Canada and CARHA — and the NHL's own rule settles it neither way. ⚠️ **Under USA Hockey a skate on the red is a skate in the crease**, and that alone can cost your teammate the goal. [Rink Map](../foundation/rink_map.md) sets out what each book says.

**Keep your stick out of the paint as well.** ⚠️ **Under USA Hockey the stick you are keeping free for a tip can cost the goal with both skates outside the line**, whichever teammate shot it. Its Casebook says that *"if a goal is scored while an attacking player has their stick and/or skates in the opponent's goal crease while the goalkeeper is in the crease, the Referee shall have no alternative but to disallow the goal"*. [Language and Glossary](../foundation/language_and_glossary.md) sets this out under *the paint*.

**When their goalie comes out to play a puck, leave them their path home, and screen them once they are back.** ⚠️ **Never put your body between a scrambling goalie and their net.** Under the NHL and the IIHF, deliberately keeping a goalie who has played the puck outside the crease from getting back can be penalised with no contact at all; under USA Hockey, interference that stops a returning goalie making a play is the goal off **and** two minutes for you.

**Play the rebound, never the goalie.** Inside the blue paint, assume any contact costs the goal. The NHL, the IIHF and the PWHL carve out one case: *"incidental contact"* with the goalie *"In a rebound situation, or where a goalkeeper and attacking player(s) are simultaneously attempting to play a loose puck, whether inside or outside the crease"*. ⚠️ **Do not carry that carve-out anywhere else.** The Elite League's Casebook writes the test flat — *"Contact in the blue paint area calls for disallowing a goal"* — and Hockey Canada, USA Hockey and CARHA write no rebound exception at all. So go and get the rebound, and never lean on the goalie.

**Shoved in? Get out at once — around the goalie, not through them.** Under the NHL and the IIHF, contact after you are *"pushed, shoved, or fouled"* into the goalie is not contact you initiated, *"provided the attacking player has made a reasonable effort to avoid such contact."* That proviso is part of the relief, and the relief decides only who started the contact: staying in the crease can still cost the goal for impairing the goalie, and the IIHF lets the referee stop play for a neutral-zone draw if you do not leave immediately. **Every book penalises you for not trying to avoid the goalie, whatever put you there** — set out below.

**What it costs — the goal.** The NHL's and the IIHF's goalie-interference tables allow a goal through a screen set *"on the crease line or outside the goal crease"* (Situation 5E) or one outside it that *"remains in motion"* (5C); the rows that disallow put the attacker inside the crease. **But a screen outside the crease is not automatically safe:** contact, or a position that stops the goalie moving freely in the crease, can still cost the goal (69.1). Under Hockey Canada and CARHA contact with the goalie outside the crease costs the goal too, and under USA Hockey interfering with the goalie does. The table is the NHL's Table 14, and the IIHF's Table 14 (Table 16 in 2025/26), so name the edition.

**Under USA Hockey the crease rule is stricter and its relief broader.** The bar is flat — *"No goal may be scored with an attacking player in the goal crease unless the puck has preceded the player(s) into the goal crease or the goalkeeper is out of the goal crease area"* — and merely standing there can stop play for a neutral-zone draw. But where a defender physically puts you in the crease, *"any legal goal scored shall be allowed"*, with no effort proviso; that relieves your position, not what you then do to the goalie. **Hockey Canada** carves out the net-front case: if you are in the crease without interfering and a teammate outside it scores, *"the goal WILL BE ALLOWED provided that the player who was in the goal crease does not attempt to play the puck, interfere with the play or obstruct the Goaltender's view or movements."* So on that play the two books give opposite answers. None of it changes the instruction: keep your feet out of the blue.

**What it costs — the penalty.** ⚠️ **Every book read here penalises an attacker who does not try to avoid the goalie.** The NHL's, IIHF's and PWHL's tables penalise one who *"did not make a reasonable effort to avoid such contact"*. USA Hockey spares a player forced into the goalie only where they *"clearly made every attempt to avoid the contact"*, and prices one *"going hard to the goal"* who only then *"makes an honest attempt to avoid the goalkeeper"* at a minor plus a misconduct. Hockey Canada says *"If the player does not make an effort to avoid contact ... they must be penalized for interference with the goaltender"*. CARHA writes a minor, with the goal disallowed where an attacker *"has physically interfered with the goalkeeper"*. **The test asks for an effort, not a result.**

**Hockey Canada's relief answers a different question — the goal, not the penalty.** It lets the goal stand only where a defender's foul left you **unable** to avoid the goalie. ⚠️ **It does not relieve you of the minor**, and being fouled first does not either.

**And price contact by the contact, not by the scoreboard.** Going through a goalie is a foul whether or not the puck goes in, and whether or not either of you is in the crease. The NHL and the IIHF give a minor or major for *"intentional or deliberate"* contact *"whether or not the goalkeeper is inside or outside the goal crease, and whether or not a goal is scored"*. CARHA writes a minor that *"shall be imposed"*, with a major at the referee's discretion that ends your game and carries at least a one-game suspension. ⚠️ **Make that shove with your stick shaft on a goalie in the crease and CARHA gives the referee no choice**: a major and game misconduct, as for a charge there. Nor does the contact have to be deliberate: a goalie outside the crease is not *"fair game"*, and unnecessary contact with one is penalised in every book.

**Under USA Hockey** ⚠️ **a deliberate shove on a goalie is charging, not interference** — a minor plus a misconduct, or a major plus a game misconduct, and accidental contact that interferes with the goalie's movement is still a minor. **A reckless charge leaves the referee no choice**: the major and game misconduct are mandatory, and plan on a match penalty too, because USA Hockey's general Rule 602(a) says one *"shall be assessed"* to any player who *"recklessly endangers or attempts to injure"* an opponent.

**And Hockey Canada does not cap goalie interference at two minutes.** A major and game misconduct is at the referee's discretion on the violence of the impact, and **mandatory** for any player who *"charges the goaltender"* or who *"injures an opponent"* with an interference that would otherwise be a minor. Attempting to injure a goalie is a match penalty, and there is no ten-minute rung between two minutes and the ejection. ⚠️ **For a centre working the slot: arriving into the goalie is an ejection under this book, and a two-minute foul becomes one the moment it injures someone.**

**Do not file that ejection under "Canadian rules" —** ⚠️ **charging a goalie reaches an ejection tier in all six books.** CARHA makes a major and game misconduct mandatory for charging a goalie *"within the goal crease"*, or injuring an opponent with a charge, and adds at least a one-game suspension. The NHL and the PWHL allow a minor, a major, or a major and game misconduct for charging a goalie in the crease, and make the game misconduct mandatory once a charging major injures the face or head; the Elite League adds the same face-or-head limb to the IIHF book. The IIHF reaches a major and game misconduct for a charge that *"recklessly endangers"*, no injury required. USA Hockey writes no bare minor for body checking or charging a goalie in the crease or privileged area, and makes the major and game misconduct mandatory for a reckless charge, with a next-game suspension on top (404(b)).

**And every book but the IIHF's** ⚠️ **goes on to a match penalty for charging.** The NHL, the PWHL and CARHA reach it for a charge meant to injure or a deliberate injury; Hockey Canada for a charge on a player unable to defend themselves or meant to injure; and USA Hockey for a reckless one, where Rule 602(a) says plan on it. The IIHF book has no match penalty at all — the phrase appears in no edition checked here — and its severest tier for charging is a major and game misconduct, with supplementary discipline after.

**Hit a goalie from behind** ⚠️ **and the IIHF takes the two-minute option away**: there is *"no option to award a minor penalty"* for checking from behind, and the game misconduct on the major is *"automatic"*. What puts you under that rule is where your contact lands — on the back of a player unable to protect themselves — so a goalie facing you is not one you are behind; a charge that reaches the head goes to the head-contact rule instead: a minor, or a reckless major and automatic game misconduct. A hit from behind is different: under the IIHF, plan on the major and game misconduct, head or not — never on a minor. A major and game misconduct is still the most this book can do to you inside the game, and a second major of any kind in one game ejects you. *(That the rule reaches goalies is a reading of how the IIHF book is assembled — it defines "Player" to cover goalies and the rule names the goal frame, though its glossary defines a vulnerable position for "a skater" — rather than a sentence it prints.)*

The full rules are in [Rules Primer](../foundation/rules_primer.md) and [Offensive Zone Play](../systems/offensive_zone_play.md); the same situation from the goalie's side of it is in [Goaltender](goaltender.md).

## Other Situations

### Faceoffs — a defining responsibility of the centre

**In short:** Win the draws that matter — own end, special teams, the last minute, after an icing — and warn your wingers before each.

```facts
Priority: Ten or fifteen draws a game matter enormously — your own end, special teams, the last minute, and after an icing; the count is a coaching estimate, not a measurement
Read: Own end — win it back to your defenders, ideally the boards-side one for a quick rim out; their end — to a defender for a one-timer, or back to a winger in the circle
Action: If you can't win it clean, lift or press their stick for an instant, over or under the blade, then play the puck — stick on stick only; never a hand on it, never hook it or hold it down
Action: Warn your wingers that their encroachment takes you off the draw, not them — true in all six books, and avoidable; under USA Hockey, Hockey Canada and CARHA their stick counts too
Rule: The draw after an icing is the faceoff-violation exception in four of the six books — the first violation is a warning rather than an ejection (NHL and IIHF 2026/27 Rule 76.4, PWHL 78.4; Hockey Canada Rule 6.5(a), Junior divisions only; neither USA Hockey nor CARHA writes such a carve-out)
Rule: Across the Home Countries the IHUK In-House Rules 2026-27 settle faceoff violations without the edition question — "the linesperson shall immediately replace the centre for all violations", faceoffs following icings excepted (Section 10, Rule 76)
Rule: Pre-drop contact by a player facing off is a minor — discretionary under NHL and IIHF 76.7 and PWHL 78.7, mandatory under USA Hockey 613(e), Hockey Canada 6.2(c) and CARHA 57(b); NHL, PWHL and IIHF 2026/27 also replace the centre
Rule: A second violation on one draw is a bench minor under NHL and IIHF 76.7, PWHL 78.7, a minor on the offender under USA Hockey 613(d), and discretionary under Hockey Canada 6.2(e) and CARHA 57(d) — though Hockey Canada makes it automatic if the offender then contacts a linesperson clearing the dot
Never: Push your head over the dot — get low with knees and hips. Helmet to helmet before the drop is pre-drop contact in all six books; the 2026/27 IIHF handbook takes the centre responsible off the draw
Rule: The ninth spot is centre ice, where the visitor's blade goes down first; at the other eight, the defending centre (NHL and IIHF 76.4, PWHL 78.4). USA Hockey 613(a) reverses it to the attacker at all but centre ice, Hockey Canada 6.2(a) puts the visitor first at all nine, CARHA 57 writes no order
Rule: ⚠️ **Across the Home Countries there is no stick-down order** — IHUK scraps it ("Either centre can now put their stick down first for a face-off") and wants "the whole of their stick blades (not toe or heel)" in the white; CARHA 57(a) wants "the full blade of their sticks flat on the ice"
Rule: In the Elite League the stick-down order still binds — the In-House Rules never name the EIHL and the Casebook writes no Rule 76, so IIHF 76.4 governs: at the eight outer spots the defending centre puts their stick down first, the visiting centre at centre ice
```

You are the primary faceoff taker, and **most draws barely matter.** An NHL game has **roughly 56** — 56.6 per game in 2024-25 and 56.0 in 2025-26, **computed from the NHL's official statistics** — and team faceoff win percentage explains only about **6% of the variation** in a team's points percentage across eleven NHL seasons (r = +0.25); in 2016-17 the relationship was *negative*. **Ten to fifteen draws a game matter enormously** — a coaching estimate, not a counted figure: the draws in your own end, on special teams, in the last minute, and after an icing. Prepare for those. [Faceoffs](../systems/faceoffs.md) carries all these figures with their limits. Key principles by location:

- **Defensive zone:** Win the puck back to your defenders — ideally to the boards-side defender (positioned near the boards-side hash marks supporting your outer winger) for a quick rim out. If you can't win clean, tie up their stick with yours for an instant — stick on stick, never a hand, never hook it or hold it down — and play the puck so your wingers can pounce on it. A clean loss in your defensive zone puts a set opponent running a prepared play into the slot while your five are stationary and facing the wrong way. **And if you lose it, stay with their centre** — tie up their stick if you can, then go with them toward the slot rather than chasing the puck. That is the usual assignment off a defensive-zone draw, though some teams hand the centre a different man, so ask yours.
- **Offensive zone:** Win to a defender for a one-timer, or back to a winger in the circle for a quick chance. Read your linemates' positioning before the puck drops.
- **Neutral zone:** Win possession cleanly if you can; otherwise, a tie-up your wingers can chase down is fine too.

**General:** Study your opponent's tendencies (most centres favour one hand or one technique). Vary your own approach. **Technique, grip, the five-player alignments and set plays are all in [Faceoffs](../systems/faceoffs.md)**, which is the authority; the procedure and the violations as written are in [Rules Primer](../foundation/rules_primer.md).

![](diagram:faceoff-dzone-alignment)

**If you have just been moved here from the wing, this is the skill you have never taken seriously** — [Switching Positions](switching_positions.md) sets out what a new centre should work on first, and in what order.

**Staying in the dot — the part that is uniquely your problem.** Warn your wingers before every draw that matters, and tell them what counts. Be set when the linesperson is ready, and never time your arrival to beat the clock. Get your depth from your knees and hips rather than pushing your head over the dot, or two centres finish helmet to helmet — and move back when you are told to. ⚠️ **A violation usually costs *you* the draw, whoever made it.**

**Your wingers can get you thrown out and never know it.** If a linemate creeps inside the circle or over the hash marks before the drop, **you** are the one removed. The NHL, the IIHF and the PWHL let a non-taker's stick inside the hash marks *"provided there is no physical contact with his opponent or his opponent's stick"*. **USA Hockey, Hockey Canada and CARHA do not**: there every stick and skate must be behind the marks, so a winger's blade puts you out as surely as their skate. Hockey Canada and CARHA also count a skate on the circle line, so tell your wingers to stay off it. It is one of the most avoidable ways a centre loses a draw — coaching judgement, not a counted ranking.

**When you cannot win it clean, lift or press their stick with yours for an instant, over or under the blade, then play the puck — stick on stick only: never a hand on their stick, never your stick on their hands, never hook it or hold it down.** ⚠️ **And keep your hand off the puck until a third player has touched it** — at a live draw the two centres lose the permission everyone else has to catch it; the section below sets out why, and what a closed hand costs.

**When you get tossed:** a winger steps in. Agree in advance who takes it and what you are trying to do on that draw — usually a safe tie-up rather than anything clever. ⚠️ **Agree two names, because under Hockey Canada the player who caused the violation cannot be the replacement**, and under Hockey Canada and CARHA no change of players is allowed unless a penalty makes a team shorthanded. **So the replacement comes from the teammates already on the ice — not the goalie, and not the one who put you out.**

**Which centre places their stick first depends on the book.** Under the NHL, the IIHF and the PWHL **the defending centre puts their blade down first** at eight of the nine spots, and the **visiting** centre at centre ice. ⚠️ **USA Hockey reverses it**: *"The attacking player shall be the first to place their stick on the ice, except for a center ice face-off where the visiting team player shall be first to place their stick on the ice."* Hockey Canada puts the **visiting** player first at every spot, then lets the home player go first *"if it does not cause a problem"*. CARHA writes no order at all. Find out which book you are in before you build a habit on it.

**Under IHUK's In-House Rules none of those answers is yours.** *"Either centre can now put their stick down first for a face-off"*, and both centres must square up *"with the whole of their stick blades (not toe or heel) in the white portion of the face-off circle"*, where the IIHF asks only for the toe. ⚠️ **Put the whole blade down.** Those rules reach games *"in England, Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland"* and do not name the Elite League, where the IIHF order still binds. CARHA also wants *"the full blade of their sticks flat on the ice"*, so a CARHA centre is under the same instruction by a different route.

**What it costs.** A linemate's violation takes you off the draw in all six books — in the IIHF's from its 2026/27 book, since 2025/26 only warned. Your own failure to take position is a mandatory removal under the NHL, the IIHF 2026/27 book and the PWHL, any violation removes you under Hockey Canada, and only USA Hockey and CARHA leave it to the official's choice — so plan on losing the dot. ⚠️ **Timing your arrival to game the five seconds is a mandatory removal** under the NHL, the PWHL and the IIHF 2026/27 book, except on the draw after an icing. A premature move has the NHL draw *"conducted again"* rather than removing you, and USA Hockey and CARHA write no premature-movement violation at all — **do not plan on either.** Hockey Canada names early movement a violation and removes you for it (6.5(a), 6.2(d)), except on a Junior draw after an icing. 🇬🇧 Under the IHUK In-House Rules 2026-27 the linesperson *"shall immediately replace the centre for all violations"* except on the draw after an icing, so in their competitions you do lose the dot for a premature move; they do not name the Elite League.

**A second violation on the same draw** is a **bench** minor under the NHL, the IIHF and the PWHL, and IHUK does not amend that. ⚠️ **The other three books route it elsewhere, so do not tell a replacement taker it is automatically two minutes off your bench.** USA Hockey gives the minor to the player who commits the second violation; Hockey Canada and CARHA make it discretionary — but Hockey Canada's discretion runs out if the offender then contacts the linesperson before they have had reasonable time to clear the circle, when the minor *"will be assessed"*. So give the official room. *(Only IIHF Rules 76.3, 76.4, 76.6 and 76.7 have been read in the 2026/27 edition; [Faceoffs](../systems/faceoffs.md) carries the full comparison.)*

**Pre-drop contact with the other centre adds a minor.** It is the referee's choice under the NHL, the IIHF and the PWHL, where the linesperson also replaces you (the IIHF's 2025/26 edition warned instead); it is mandatory under USA Hockey, Hockey Canada and CARHA. ⚠️ **Do not rank those three against each other: under any of them, plan on the penalty.** Helmet-to-helmet contact from leaning in is a faceoff violation in the IIHF book, and its 2026/27 handbook has the linesperson *"immediately eject the center that is responsible for the helmet to helmet contact"*, or *"eject both centers"* where they cannot tell. A centre who will not move back from *"side by side, almost ear to ear"* is *"removed from the face-off"*.

**The exception is on the draw you care most about — after an icing.** NHL Rule 76.4: *"When an icing infraction occurs, the first face-off violation by either team will not result in the center being removed."* The linesperson warns the team instead, any later violation by that team is a bench minor, and the warning is per team — one free attempt each. The IIHF's 2026/27 book, the PWHL and the IHUK In-House Rules carry the same carve-out. **Hockey Canada gives it in Junior divisions only**, and there it is wider, reaching *"any face-off violation, including but not limited to late arrival, encroachment, or early movement"*, with a later violation by the same team a minor. **Outside Junior, Hockey Canada gives you nothing after an icing, and USA Hockey and CARHA give you nothing at all.** Read it as one attempt, not a free draw. Full detail in [Faceoffs](../systems/faceoffs.md).
### Handling the puck — catching it is free, holding it is not

**In short:** Knock a caught puck straight down and play it; at a draw, keep your hand off until a third player touches it.

```facts
Rule: Catching the puck out of the air is not penalised in any of the six books — NHL, IIHF and PWHL (67.2 and 69.2) ask only that you "immediately place it or knock it down to the ice"; Hockey Canada 10.2(a)(iii) and CARHA Rule 61(a) exempt an immediate drop; USA Hockey's permission is casebook only
Never: Hold a puck you have caught, carry it, or drop it somewhere better. Knock it straight down where you caught it instead
Rule: At a live draw, though, the two centres may not play the puck with their hand at all — the note to NHL and IIHF 67.2 bars it "until such time as a third player (from either team) has at least touched the puck", and the PWHL carries the same note at 69.2
Rule: Closing your hand on the puck — at a draw or anywhere else — is a minor under the NHL and IIHF (67.2), and a penalty shot rather than a minor where you do it in your own goal crease (the note to 67.2(ii), and 67.4)
Rule: Hockey Canada 10.2(a)(iii) makes a closed hand a minor only if it "gains an advantage", and 10.2(a)(v) escalates to a penalty shot "when the infraction occurs inside the offending team's goal crease" — words that locate the infraction, not the puck; 4.11(a)(v), which it cites, locates the puck
Rule: Smothering the puck under your body is a minor under NHL 63.2(v), USA Hockey 614(a), Hockey Canada 10.2(a)(iv) and CARHA Rule 58(a), and escalates inside your own crease to a penalty shot — or, under USA Hockey 614(b), a penalty shot or optional minor. Neither limb is a faceoff violation
Rule: USA Hockey 618(a) makes a caught puck not dropped outside the crease a stoppage, not a penalty — but covering a loose puck with your hand is a minor (614(a) Note), and in the crease it is a penalty shot or optional minor, the other team's election (406(a))
Rule: With your net empty a hand closed on a puck in the crease is an awarded goal too, not only a body smother — USA Hockey through Rule 618(a) and Rule 614(b), but only where it prevented an obvious and imminent goal; the NHL, IIHF and PWHL through the note to Rule 67.2 or 69.2 and then 67.5 or 69.5
Rule: A skater other than the goalie who covers a puck itself in the crease concedes a penalty shot — in five books the puck's location decides, not yours (NHL 67.4 and 63.6; IIHF 63.6; PWHL 65.6; CARHA Rule 58(c); USA Hockey 614(b))
Rule: With your own net empty, a skater covering a puck itself in the crease concedes an awarded goal, not a penalty shot (NHL and IIHF 67.5; PWHL 69.5; Hockey Canada 10.2(a)(vi); CARHA 58(c) Note 1, read flat; USA Hockey 614(b) only where it prevented a goal)
Rule: Hockey Canada gives a penalty shot for a puck in your crease (4.11(a)(v)), a goal with your net empty (10.2(a)(vi)); 10.2(a)(v) also names an infraction inside it, so treat a puck just outside covered while you stand in it as a possible shot or goal
```

Never hold it, carry it or drop it somewhere better; never cover a puck in your own crease with your hand or your body; and at a live draw keep your hand off it altogether until a third player has touched it.

**Catching it is not the offence.** NHL and IIHF **Rule 67.2**, and the PWHL's 69.2: *"A player shall be permitted to catch the puck out of the air but must immediately place it or knock it down to the ice."* Hockey Canada exempts a player who closes their hand on the puck *"to catch or stop the puck and immediately drops it to the ice without gaining or attempting to gain an advantage"*, and CARHA lets play continue in the same case. USA Hockey's permission is in its Casebook rather than its playing rules — the catch and drop must be *"one continuous motion"* — and a hand not opened at once outside the crease is a stoppage there, not a penalty. ⚠️ **But covering a loose puck on the ice with your hand is a minor under USA Hockey**, and inside the crease a closed hand is a **penalty shot or optional minor**.

**The word carrying all six is "immediately".** Knock it straight down where you caught it. Relocating the puck does not cure it. The IIHF's Situation Handbook works the case of a defender whose own net is empty, who grabs the puck *"out of mid-air (around the height of the waist)"* and drops it **outside** the crease: the answer is *"an Awarded Goal to Team B"* (Situation 67.6 — a worked application rather than rule text).

**And the crease is a volume, not a patch of ice.** Every book runs it from the ice up to the level of the top of the goal frame. ⚠️ **So a puck you catch out of the air above your own paint, below the bar, is a puck in the crease** — the height you took it at buys you nothing. Knock it straight down and play it with your skate or your blade.

![](diagram:the-puck-decides-not-you)

**And the permission is suspended for you, and only for you, while a draw is live.** The NHL, the IIHF and the PWHL all write it; in the NHL's words, *"The two players involved in the actual face-off (the centers) are not permitted to play the puck with their hand without incurring a penalty under this rule until such time as a third player (from either team) has at least touched the puck."* ⚠️ **Between the drop and someone else touching the puck, your hand is out of it.** No equivalent was found in USA Hockey, Hockey Canada or CARHA, which were searched in full — a search result rather than an established absence.

**What it costs.** Closing a hand over the puck to conceal it, or smothering it with your body, is a **minor penalty**. Inside your own crease it is a **penalty shot** — and **it is the puck that has to be in the crease, not you.** The NHL, the IIHF and the PWHL make *"the location of the puck at the time it was held, grabbed or gathered into the body"* the test, so a puck outside the crease gathered in by a player inside it is a minor, and CARHA awards the shot for a puck *"within their goal crease"*. Hockey Canada asks where the puck is too, and a player standing outside the crease who scoops the puck out and then covers it draws a minor, not a shot. ⚠️ **But no Hockey Canada text rules on a puck outside the paint gathered in by a player standing inside it, so treat that case as a possible penalty shot, and with your net empty a possible goal.** **USA Hockey does not make the shot automatic**: the other team chooses a penalty shot or a minor.

**With your own net empty it is worse still.** If your goalie has been pulled for an extra attacker, covering the puck in your crease is an awarded goal: outright under the NHL, the IIHF and the PWHL; under Hockey Canada where the puck is in the crease; and under USA Hockey where the act *"has prevented an obvious and imminent goal"*. ⚠️ **CARHA looks flat here and is not** — its Note awards the goal whenever the goalie has been removed, while the rule it points to carries USA Hockey's condition. **Play the flat Note: with your own net empty, never cover a puck in your crease with your hand or your body.**

**And the body is priced separately from the hand**: deliberately falling on or gathering the puck into your body is a minor under every book, CARHA's for any player *"except a goalkeeper"*.

### Power Play (your team has the man advantage)

```facts
Position: In the 1-3-1 you most often play the bumper — the middle of the three across the slot, between the point man and the net front
Action: Be a passing target for both half-walls, take quick shots from the slot, screen the goalie and tip point shots
Key: A box has nobody assigned to the middle, which is exactly why a kill switches to a diamond against you
Position: In an umbrella or an overload you will usually be low or on the strong-side wall — there is no bumper in a true umbrella
Action: Drop back to receive the breakout pass and carry through the neutral zone, then transition into the bumper or slot
Never: Skate straight into the opposing defence, or dump into traffic. Enter with control instead, carrying into space or passing in
Key: Passing in is an alternative to carrying in, not an upgrade on it — the tracked figures have the two about level on the power play and carrying ahead at even strength; Zone Entries covers them
```

**The formations themselves — 1-3-1, umbrella, overload, spread, 2-3 — are set out in [Special Teams](../systems/special_teams.md).** What matters here is where they put you.

In the **1-3-1**, widely described as the modern default, the centre most often plays the **bumper**: the middle of the three across the slot, sitting in the soft spot between the single point man and the net-front player. Your job is to be a passing target for both half-walls, take quick shots from the slot, screen the goalie, and tip point shots. The bumper is the hardest player for a penalty kill to cover, because a **box** has nobody assigned to the middle — which is exactly why a kill switches to a **diamond** against you.

![](diagram:pp-131)

In an **umbrella** or an **overload** you will usually be low or on the strong-side wall instead. There is no bumper in a true umbrella, so don't go looking for the spot you're used to.

**On zone entries:** You often drop back to receive the breakout pass and become the puck carrier through the neutral zone (using drop passes or swing-entries with the wingers). Once in the offensive zone, you transition into the bumper or slot position. Avoid skating straight into the opposing defence, and avoid dumping the puck into traffic. Both hand the puck over; what you want is a **controlled entry** — carried into space, or passed in across the line.

**Passing in is an alternative to carrying in, not an upgrade on it.** The tracked figures put pass-ins and carry-ins about level on the power play and carrying clearly ahead at even strength, so pass in because the passing lane is the one that is open, not because passing beats carrying. [Zone Entries](../systems/zone_entries.md) covers those figures, the league they were measured in and their limits.

### Penalty Kill (your team is shorthanded)

**In short:** Know which shape you are running before the drop, hold your place in it, and pressure the puck rather than chase it.

```facts
Position: In a box you are one of the two high players, each responsible for one point — there is no single "top" to drift into
Position: In a diamond you are usually the single apex, pressuring the point; in wedge+1 you are frequently the "+1"
Action: As the +1, pressure whoever has the puck; when the puck moves the nearest wedge player takes over as the new +1, so you never follow it round
Options: Your route out as the +1 is a coaching choice — straight out from the goal, sweeping across the top of the zone, or roaming to the wall and point; ask which one your team runs
Action: Take the high forward spot at the mid-to-high slot, block shooting lanes and clear it while you are still shorthanded; Special Teams covers the shape
Never: Get pulled out of position chasing the puck around the perimeter
Rule: A shorthanded team may send the puck down the ice without being called for icing, but the exemption is conditional — USA Hockey Rule 624(b)(1) grants it only from Youth 15-Only, Girls 16U, High School and Adult up
Rule: The major-penalty clawback in NHL 81.6 and PWHL 83.6 stops a kill icing only once a major expires with the penalty box empty, then only until the next stoppage — not during the major; Rule 20.3 wants the substitute seated at a stoppage before it ends, or immediately where minors are stacked on it
Rule: Shorthanded icing turns on the on-ice count, not on whether a penalty is running — expiry counts back only the player whose penalty ended, so a 5-on-3 kill is still below strength and still exempt, and a clear released before expiry draws no icing however late it lands (NHL and IIHF 81.6, PWHL 83.6)
Rule: Expiry is not the only thing that levels the sides — a penalty to them takes you to 4-on-4, which is not below their on-ice strength, so you can be iced while a major of yours is still running (NHL and IIHF 81.6, PWHL 83.6)
Key: Know which shape you are running before the puck drops — a box and a diamond ask very different things of you
```

A kill is four skaters, normally two forwards and two defencemen. **The shapes are described in [Special Teams](../systems/special_teams.md);** your job inside each one:

- **Box** — the standard. You are **one of the two high players**, alongside a winger, each responsible for one point. There is no single "top" of a box, so don't drift into the middle looking for one.
- **Diamond** — used against a 1-3-1. Here there *is* a single apex and it is usually you: pressure the point while the middle two take the half-walls and someone accounts for the bumper.
- **Wedge+1** — you are frequently the "+1", the single player working outside the triangle. That job is **pressure, not pursuit**. Get at whoever has the puck and make every possession uncomfortable. When the puck moves you stop, and the nearest wedge player steps out as the new +1 while you drop into the triangle. Following the puck around the perimeter is how this system is beaten, not how it is played. **The route you take to the puck is your coach's choice, not a law** — published descriptions differ, some sending the +1 straight out from the goal towards the carrier, some sweeping them laterally across the top of the zone above the circles, some letting them roam to the wall and the point. Ask which one you are running.

![](diagram:pk-diamond)

Don't get pulled out of position chasing the puck around the perimeter, block shooting lanes, and clear the puck whenever you get it. Know which shape you're running *before* the puck drops; a box and a diamond ask very different things of you.

**What it costs.** You may ice the puck while shorthanded, **but check your rule set before you build the kill on it.** ⚠️ **USA Hockey grants the exemption only at Youth 15-Only and above, Girls 16U and above, High School and Adult**; below those levels a shorthanded team is called for icing like anyone else.

**The exemption follows the count on the ice, not the penalty clock.** Every book read here gives it only to the side *below* its opponent's on-ice numbers, and the NHL, the IIHF and the PWHL settle it **at the instant a penalty expires** — the player whose penalty just ran out counts as back on the ice even while they wait at the box door. Killing a 5-on-3, the first expiry leaves you four against five and you may still ice it; the second stops you. ⚠️ **A penalty to *them* levels the sides too**: at 4-on-4 you can be iced while a major of yours is still running. **The relief is in the same sentence**: a puck *"released from the stick of the player shooting the puck down the ice prior to the penalty expiring"* draws no icing **however late it lands**, so do not stop making a legal play.

**Under the NHL and the PWHL,** ⚠️ **a major left to expire with nobody on the penalty box to come out withdraws the exemption** until the next stoppage. That bites at expiry, not during the penalty, because the bench may sit empty until a stoppage before the major ends — **unless minors are stacked on the major, when the substitute must be seated *"immediately"***. [Special Teams](../systems/special_teams.md) covers this rule, and [Faceoffs](../systems/faceoffs.md) sets the books side by side.

#### Blocking shots on the kill

```facts
Rule: Blocking a shot is protected — a player who drops to their knees is not penalised if the puck is shot under them or lodged in their equipment, though using the hands to make it unplayable is (NHL 63.2(v) NOTE; IIHF 63.2(V), PWHL 65.2(v), USA Hockey 614(a))
Technique: Take a lane before the shot, close and on your feet, head out of it, hands behind your body, stick flat beside you and never leading — and never in front of your own goalie's eyes
Never: Block the shot on a 2-on-1 — as the lone defender back your job is the pass and your goalie has the shooter; the full list of when not to block is in Defender and Body Contact and Battles
Convention: How much your team blocks at all is a coaching choice, not a law — some teams want aggressive blocking, others want the lane taken away while you stay on your feet so the goalie sees everything. Find out which yours plays, ask your coach which they want, and ask your goalie what they want
```

**Taking a shooting lane away means a puck can hit you, so take it properly.** Be in the lane *before* the shot rather than sliding into it, block from close and on your feet, square to the shooter, head out of the lane and hands behind your body with the backs of the gloves out. **Never lead with your stick** — keep it flat on the ice beside you or out of the lane, because a puck off an outstretched blade tends to go upward, into your face (coaching craft, not a measured finding). **If you go down**, go down toward the shooter rather than sideways, hands behind your body or tucked in behind your shin pads — and **never from in front of your own goalie's eyes**, where all you do is deflect a puck they never saw.

**How much your team blocks at all is a coaching choice, not a law** — some teams want you blocking aggressively, others want the lane taken away while you stay on your feet so the goalie sees everything. Find out which yours plays: ask your coach, and ask your goalie what they want. **And never block the shot on a 2-on-1** — as the lone defender back your job is the pass, and your goalie has the shooter. [Defender](defender.md) and [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md) own the technique and the full list of when *not* to block.

**What it costs.** Blocking a shot is protected: in the NHL, the IIHF, the PWHL and USA Hockey a player who drops to their knees is not penalised if the puck is shot under them or lodges in their equipment — but **using your hands to make the puck unplayable is a minor**. Hockey Canada and CARHA print no such note, but their smothering minors reach only a player who *"deliberately falls on or gathers the puck"*. So a blocked shot that dies under you is fine; reaching for it to hold it there is not.

### Icing

**In short:** Carry the puck past the red line before you dump it, and if your legs are gone, put it off the glass instead.

```facts
Risk: Your icing risk is the lazy dump from behind the red line, not the defensive-zone clear
Technique: Carry the extra stride so the puck is past the red line on your stick before you dump it — legal however far it goes, unless your league splits the ice at the blue lines
Options: Or beat the icing the other way — make the dump soft enough and short enough that a winger would clearly reach the puck first, which only works where your book runs a hybrid-icing race
Rule: The linesperson judges which player would first touch the puck, not who first reaches the dot (NHL and IIHF Rule 81.1; PWHL Rule 83.1)
Rule: The hybrid-icing race is the NHL's, IIHF's and PWHL's (81.1; PWHL 83.1) — CARHA 65 has none, and USA Hockey is automatic outside sled hockey, its Rule 624 Note completing the icing "the instant the puck has completely crossed the goal line"
Rule: Hockey Canada runs the hybrid-icing race only in U18AAA and Junior, and in Senior at the Member's option (6.7(e))
Rule: After your own icing your line cannot change and your team cannot take a time-out under NHL, IIHF and PWHL rules (PWHL 83.4 and 89.1) — USA Hockey and CARHA impose neither and Hockey Canada is a third answer; an injured player can still be replaced
Rule: Hockey Canada 6.7(d) applies the no-change rule only in U18AAA, Junior and, at the Member's option, Senior — in every other category under that book, minor and female hockey included, your line changes after your own icing
Rule: Where Hockey Canada's no-change bar does apply, its time-out rule goes the other way from the NHL's — 6.7(d)(iii) permits either team to take a time-out after an icing, barring only the icing team from substituting during it
Action: Legs gone? Put the puck off the glass and out, not down the ice — never over it: from your own zone that is a minor in the NHL, IIHF and PWHL even by accident, and on purpose in all six
Key: When they ice it under NHL, IIHF or PWHL rules you take an offensive-zone draw against five who cannot change — have a set play. USA Hockey and CARHA let them change, as does most of Hockey Canada
```

**The rule itself — hybrid icing, the full wave-off list, "gaining the line", and how USA Hockey and rec leagues differ — is in [Rules Primer](../foundation/rules_primer.md).** What is specific to you is *which* icings are yours and what they cost you.

**Your icing risk is the neutral-zone dump, not the defensive-zone clear.** Your defencemen make most of the clears; what a centre throws away is a lazy dump from behind the centre red line. Two cheap fixes. Carry the extra stride so the **puck is past the red line on your stick** before you let it go — that alone makes the dump legal, however far it goes. Or make the dump soft and short enough that a winger would clearly reach the puck first: the linesperson judges who would reach the puck first, not who reaches the dot first.

**The second fix needs a race to exist.** ⚠️ **Under three of the books most hockey is played under there is none.** USA Hockey's icing is automatic in every standing classification, Hockey Canada races only in U18AAA, Junior and Senior at the Member's option, and CARHA runs no race at all; under those, a soft dump that crosses the goal line is iced whoever would have reached it first. **A dump that stops short of the goal line works everywhere, and so does the first fix — unless you play in a CARHA league that divides the ice at the blue lines instead of the red**: **ask your league where its line is.** [Rules Primer](../foundation/rules_primer.md) sets out the book-by-book comparison.

![](diagram:icing-gaining-the-line)

**After your own icing, the penalty is aimed at you personally.** The draw comes back to your defensive zone, and **under NHL, IIHF and PWHL rules** your line **cannot change** (an injured player can still be replaced) and your team cannot take a timeout — so a lazy dump leaves a tired centre taking a defensive-zone draw they badly need to win. If your legs are gone, the puck goes off the glass and out, not down the ice. ⚠️ **Off the glass, never over it** — from your own zone, a puck that flies straight out over the glass is a delay-of-game minor in the NHL, the IIHF and the PWHL even when you did not mean it, and one put out on purpose is a minor in every book.

**What it costs, book by book.** The IIHF adds an exception for *"a Player with a broken skate"*, but ⚠️ **do not count on it without the official's say-so.** If the officials refuse the change, your players are sent back and your team is warned that any later violation in the game is a bench minor. It is one warning per team per game, so a bench already warned takes the penalty straight away. **USA Hockey imposes neither bar**, so a tired line gets off — ⚠️ **at the ordinary speed**: each bench gets five seconds, then no more substitutions until the faceoff, and undue delay draws a warning and then a delay-of-game minor. **Hockey Canada applies the no-change rule only in U18AAA, Junior and, at the Member's option, Senior**; everywhere else your line changes as after any whistle, and where it does apply either team may take its time-out but the icing team still cannot substitute during it. **CARHA writes no bar at all**, and allows each team one change at any stoppage. [Rules Primer](../foundation/rules_primer.md) carries the book-by-book comparison.

**When the other team ices it, that's one of your best draws of the night — wherever they cannot change.** Under the NHL, the IIHF and the PWHL, and under Hockey Canada in U18AAA, Junior and Senior-at-the-Member's-option, you are taking an offensive-zone draw against five players who are stuck out there and probably tired; under USA Hockey and CARHA, and in every other Hockey Canada category, they get a fresh line and the draw is an ordinary one. Know it's coming and have a set play ready. Where the book forgives the first faceoff violation on the draw after an icing — the NHL, the IIHF, the PWHL, Britain's In-House Rules and Hockey Canada's Junior divisions — expect their centre to use their free one, and use yours.

On the matchup: the bar falls only on the team that iced the puck. So the fresh-legs advantage is yours home or away when *they* ice it, and forfeited home or away when you do. See [Faceoffs](../systems/faceoffs.md).

### Backchecking — the middle lane is usually yours

**In short:** In this guide's default, come back inside the middle-lane driver and leave the puck carrier to your defence.

```facts
Convention: The centre takes the middle lane and the wingers the outside — the assignment assumed here, but a coaching choice, not a law, and no count of which is commonest was found
Read: Not every team gives the centre the middle. Some give it to the first man back, others pick up man-on-man from the puck outward. Find out which yours plays
Action: Take the middle-lane driver — the forward arriving late and unmarked into the slot is usually more dangerous than the puck carrier, which is coaching emphasis rather than a counted ranking
Position: Come back on the defensive side of your man, between them and your own net — stick in the passing lane, not on their body, and not chasing behind their shoulder
Rule: The middle-lane driver has no puck — NHL and IIHF Rule 56.1 and PWHL Rule 57.1 grant body position on three conditions: stick not utilized, free hand not used, no otherwise illegal check off it
Rule: USA Hockey 625(a)(4) makes changing lane or foot speed to play the body of a player without the puck an interference minor — that rule stops at two minutes, but the same hit can be called under others that reach an ejection or a match penalty
Rule: Under USA Hockey an ejection by game misconduct costs you the next game too — any game misconduct also suspends you for your team's next game (404(b)), and a second major in one game at least two (403(b))
Action: Skate first, then look, and don't decide mid-rush to chase the carrier — your defencemen have that player, and swinging wide abandons the ice they are protecting
Options: Some teams coach the first forward back to pressure the carrier, with the defence holding inside — where that is your system, pressuring is your feet and your stick, never a hit from behind
Priority: Last forward back with your defence outnumbered — take away the pass, don't go for the hit
Action: Once you're set, become the centre again — cover the high slot and support the corner
```

When your team loses the puck going the other way, the centre normally has the single most important backcheck assignment in hockey: **the middle lane**.

> **Which backchecker owns which lane is a coaching choice, not a law.** The positional version described here — centre in the middle, wingers outside — is the default assumed throughout this guide, and **no count of which of the three is commonest has been found**. Other teams use **first man back takes the middle**, whoever that is, which is common when the centre is the one caught deep and common in rec hockey. Others pick up **man-on-man from the puck outward**. Find out which your team plays. [Defending the Rush](../systems/defending_the_rush.md) sets them out, along with the four named team backchecking systems that decide which assignment applies; under every one of them, somebody owns the middle and nobody assumes it is somebody else.

- **Take the middle-lane driver.** The most dangerous attacker on a rush is usually not the puck carrier — it's the forward filling the middle of the ice behind them, arriving late and unmarked into the slot. **That the middle is the lane most dangerous to leave unattended, and the middle-lane driver the assignment most often missed, is coaching emphasis and not a counted ranking**; [Defending the Rush](../systems/defending_the_rush.md) sets out that ordering and states the limit with it. Under the positional assignment that player is yours, the wingers have the outside lanes, and nobody else is going to pick this up. Under the other two variants you may end up on an outside man instead — but then somebody has called that they have the middle, and if nobody has, take it.
- **Get to the inside.** Come back on the defensive side of your man — between them and your own net — not chasing behind their shoulder; if you're trailing them, you can't defend anything. **Take the ice, not the body:** your stick goes in the passing lane rather than on them. Stepping into them can be called interference wherever you play, and where checking is barred an illegal check as well. ⚠️ **Under Hockey Canada, skate your line inside them and never step across their path** — impeding a player without the puck is interference there, with no body-position grant.
- **Skate first, then look, and don't chase the carrier.** Your defencemen have the puck carrier; if you swing wide to help them, you have abandoned the exact area they are trying to protect. **Unless your team has told you otherwise:** some coaches want the first forward back — usually you — to pressure the carrier while the defence holds inside instead. That is a system, not an instinct, and [Defending the Rush](../systems/defending_the_rush.md) sets out both. What is always wrong is deciding it yourself in the middle of a rush — and pressuring is your feet and your stick, never a hit from behind. **Before you plan any of it around contact, read the subsection below** — your rule book and classification, not your coach, decide whether you may body check at all and what a check from behind costs, and the books do not agree.
- **Once you're set, become the centre again.** Transition into your normal defensive-zone role — cover the high slot, support the corner.

![](diagram:centre-backcheck-middle-lane)

If you are the last forward back and your defence is outnumbered, the priority is taking away the pass, not making a hit.

**What it costs.** Body position on a player without the puck is yours under the NHL, the IIHF and the PWHL only while your stick is not used to make yourself bigger, your free hand is not used, and you do not *"take advantage of his body position to deliver an otherwise illegal check"* — and that third condition bites hardest. Under USA Hockey, changing your lane or speed to play the body of a player without the puck is an interference minor, and that rule stops at two minutes. ⚠️ **But two minutes is not the ceiling on the hit.** A body check with no effort to play the puck and the stick blade above the knees is roughing, and a reckless one a major plus a game misconduct. A cross-check, a hit from behind and a check where checking is barred each go to their own rule. Plan on a match penalty if any of them recklessly endangers them or is meant to injure them, because USA Hockey's Rule 602(a) says one *"shall be assessed"*.

#### May you check at all, and what a check from behind costs

```facts
Rule: USA Hockey 604(a) bans body checking at 12 & under and below, in all Girls'/Women's age classifications and in all non-check Adult classifications
Rule: USA Hockey's age list is not exhaustive — 604(a) also lets a local governing body prohibit body checking in any classification, 604(b) bars it whenever a Competitive Contact team plays a Body Checking team, and its Blind Hockey rules bar it at every age. Ask your affiliate
Rule: Hockey Canada 7.3(a) bans INTENTIONAL body checks, bumps, shoves and pushes at U13 and below, in female hockey and in any division a Member has approved — incidental contact is not a penalty
Rule: A barred body check escalates — Hockey Canada 7.3(b) allows a major plus game misconduct on violence and mandates it on injury, as USA Hockey 604(d) does for a reckless one; plan on a match under 602(a) for reckless endangerment, and 7.3(c)'s match is mandatory for an attempt to injure
Rule: The IIHF writes no classification ban on body checking; its women's Rule 101.1 allows it only with "a clear intention of playing the puck or attempting to 'gain possession'" of it, never to pin or push into the boards. An illegal hit is a minor, or a major and automatic game misconduct
Rule: British checking ages are set by IHUK's Rules of Competition 2026-27, one document per competition — junior U10 and U12 non-checking, U14, U16 and U19 checking, NIHL Division 1 and 2 checking, every WNIHL women's division non-checking. None covers the Elite League. Get yours in writing
Rule: CARHA 49(a) prices any player who "intentionally bodies, pushes, shoves" an opponent or "does not avert body contact" at a minor or a major, mandatory if intentional contact injures — any such major ejects and bans you at least a game (30(a), 32(d)); an attempt to injure is a match penalty (48(a))
Rule: IIHF 101.1 excepts itself more than once — competing for possession you may not use the boards "to eliminate her from the play, push her into the boards, or pin her along the boards", you must skate around a stationary opponent, and any move to step or glide into an opponent is an illegal hit
Rule: The NHL, IIHF and PWHL write no minor for checking from behind (43.2) — only a major and game misconduct, mandatory under NHL 43.3 and 43.5; under the IIHF plan on its major and game misconduct (43.3), head or not; on the head, NHL and PWHL the harsher of 43 and 48
Rule: From behind: CARHA's minor adds a game misconduct (53(a); major on injury); USA Hockey's a misconduct (608(a)), at least a major and game misconduct if head first into boards or goal frame (608(b)) or forceful along the boards (Casebook); reckless, plan on the match (602(a))
Rule: Hockey Canada from behind, off the head: minor/major and game misconduct, major a must if hurt, a match if driven into boards or goal frame defenceless, or meant to injure (7.5). On the head, head contact alone (7.6; 7.7); head first into boards or goal frame, the harsher of the two
Rule: Hockey Canada from behind, on the head: head contact, up to a match, a must if defenceless or meant to injure; major and game misconduct a must on high cross-check, in minor and female hockey if hurt (7.6), in Junior/Senior if highly violent or head hurt off glass, ice or boards (7.7)
Rule: A hit from behind into boards or net on a player "unable to protect or defend themselves" is a mandatory match in Hockey Canada (7.5(c); 7.6(c) or 7.7(c) on the head) and CARHA 53(b); a reckless one with excessive force on a vulnerable or defenseless player is a match anywhere in USA Hockey 608(c)
Never: Finish a backcheck into the back of a player skating at their own end boards — take the puck, the ice, or angle them off it. Ask your book and classification before you plan any hit
```

**Take the puck, take the ice, or angle them off it — never finish into their back.** If you can see their numbers, pull up and play the puck. And before you plan a shift around any hit, find out whether you may body check at all: your rule book and your classification answer that, not your coach.

USA Hockey bars body checking at 12 & under and below, in all Girls'/Women's and all non-check adult classifications; Hockey Canada at U13 and below, throughout female hockey, and in any division a Member of Hockey Canada has approved. ⚠️ **A checking classification is not the same thing as a checking game.** Under USA Hockey a local governing body may bar checking in any classification, it is barred whenever a Competitive Contact team plays a Body Checking team, and it is barred in all Blind Hockey games, adult rosters included. Ask your affiliate, before the puck drops.

**Both books escalate a barred check.** Hockey Canada allows a major plus game misconduct on the degree of violence and makes it **mandatory** if the check injures; USA Hockey makes the same pair **mandatory** for a check that *"recklessly endangers an opponent"* in a Competitive Contact category; and each reaches a match penalty — Hockey Canada's mandatory for an attempt to injure. Under USA Hockey, plan on a match for a reckless check or one meant to injure, because its general **Rule 602(a)** says one *"shall be assessed"* to any player who *"recklessly endangers or attempts to injure"* an opponent.

**In a CARHA-affiliated adult league,** ⚠️ **plan no hit.** Its **Rule 49(a)** assesses a minor or a major to any player who *"intentionally bodies, pushes, shoves, stands in front of an opponent for the purpose of making contact, and/or does not avert body contact with an opponent"*. The major is mandatory when intentional contact injures, and it ends your game under **Rule 30(a)** with at least a one-game suspension (**32(d)**). A hit meant to hurt is a match penalty under **Rule 48(a)**.

**The IIHF book carries no classification ban**, so ⚠️ **in British men's, mixed and junior hockey neither rule book states a body-checking age.** IHUK publishes them in its **Rules of Competition 2026-27**: junior hockey **non-checking at U10 and U12** (*"Bodychecking is NOT allowed in U10 matches"*) and **checking at U14, U16 and U19**, **NIHL Division 1 and 2** checking, and **every WNIHL women's division non-checking, U16 and senior alike**. **None of those covers the Elite League**, whose Casebook does not mention body checking anywhere. **And they set the format without pricing a breach**, so treat the band as the instruction and the price as unsettled, and get your division's contact status in writing before your first shift. [UK and England Rules](../foundation/uk_rules.md) quotes all four.

**Where IIHF women's hockey allows checking, wanting the puck is not a licence.** Rule 101.1 allows body checking only *"when there is a clear intention of playing the puck or attempting to 'gain possession' of the puck"*, and even then you may not use the boards *"to eliminate her from the play, push her into the boards, or pin her along the boards"*. A stationary player *"is entitled to that area of the ice"*, and you must skate around her. And *"[a]ny move by a Player to step or glide into an opposing Player will be assessed at least a minor penalty (2') for an 'illegal hit'"*. ⚠️ **So a player who genuinely wants the puck and steps into a stationary opponent still takes the penalty**, and an illegal hit can be a major and automatic game misconduct rather than two minutes.

**The PWHL draws the line differently:** its Rule 52.1 writes no boards limb, but makes it an *"illegal hit"* to make contact *"with opposite-directional force"*, to leave *"their skating lane to make contact"*, or to extend *"an arm, shoulder, or hip after angling an opponent"*.

**And where you may check, a check from behind is priced like nothing else — though not the same way in every book.** ⚠️ **No book lets you count on two minutes and a return to the bench.** The NHL, the IIHF and the PWHL write no minor for it at all: what is left is a **major** — mandatory under the NHL; under the IIHF given for a check that *"recklessly endangers the fouled Player"*, so plan on it — with a game misconduct that the NHL says *"must be assessed"* and the IIHF calls *"automatic"*. If it reaches their head, the NHL and PWHL price it at the harsher of Rule 43 and Rule 48; under the IIHF plan on the same major and game misconduct, never a minor.

**USA Hockey, Hockey Canada and CARHA write a minor, and it never travels alone.** USA Hockey's is a minor plus a misconduct, or a major plus a game misconduct. The major and game misconduct are **mandatory** for a check from behind that recklessly endangers or sends a player head first into the boards or goal frame. And ⚠️ **a reckless check from behind with excessive force on an opponent in a vulnerable or defenceless position is a match penalty, with no boards condition.** **Under USA Hockey, plan on a match and a likely multi-game suspension for any reckless hit from behind**, because Rule 602(a) says one *"shall be assessed"*.

**USA Hockey's own Casebook goes further.** ⚠️ **It takes the minor off the hit you are most likely to throw.** It keeps the minor for a push on an opponent *"not near the boards"* and for *"minimal body contact"* producing a *"pinch"* against them. It directs that *"[t]he major plus a game misconduct penalty, or match penalty, must be called"* for a forceful check on a player standing along the boards, one *"thrust head first into the boards or goal frame"*, and a reckless check from behind *"regardless of whether or not board contact is made."* Its *"danger zone"* is *"approximately ten feet out from the boards"*.

**Hockey Canada** makes a check from behind a minor and a game misconduct, or a major and game misconduct on the violence of the impact — **mandatory** if it injures. It is a match penalty for a hit from behind into the boards or goal frame on a player *"unable to protect or defend themselves"*, or for deliberately injuring or attempting to injure by checking from behind, anywhere. **CARHA** makes it a minor or a major, plus a game misconduct, *"anywhere on the ice"*, the major mandatory on injury and bringing at least a one-game ban, and a mandatory match for propelling a player who cannot protect themselves into the boards or goal net. Under Hockey Canada that covers a hit that stays off their head; one that reaches it is head contact, set out below.

**Once a hit from behind reaches their head, Hockey Canada prices it as head contact**, because its head-contact rule *"supersedes any other rules, with the exception of Rule 7.10 – Fighting"*. But a head-first drive into the boards or goal frame is still a check from behind under Hockey Canada's rule, so if it reaches their head as well, the harsher price stands.

In minor and female hockey (Rule 7.6), head contact is a minor if accidental and a double minor if intentional, and a third such penalty in one game is a game ejection. The referee may make it a major and game misconduct on the violence of the hit alone, and **must** if the player is injured at all or if it was a cross-check above the shoulders. It is a **match penalty** if they were hit in a way that left them *"unable to protect or defend themselves"*, or if you tried to injure them. In Junior and Senior hockey (Rule 7.7), minimal violence may be called as roughing, charging or another appropriate penalty, and moderate violence is a minor plus a ten-minute misconduct. High violence is a major and game misconduct — as is a cross-check above the shoulders, or an illegal hit that puts their head into the glass, the ice or the boards and causes a head injury. A **match penalty** sits above that, for a head hit that leaves them unable to protect or defend themselves, or an attempt to injure.

### Line Changes

```facts
Action: When the line changes as a group you go off first — call it, and the wingers follow you
Convention: A line that changes as a group changes on the centre's legs, because the centre fades first — coaching craft rather than a measured finding
Never: Change into an offensive-zone faceoff you are needed to take
Rule: After your own icing a healthy skater cannot change under NHL and IIHF 81.4 and PWHL 83.4 — injury, a pulled goalie returning and a strength-changing penalty are the exceptions, plus an IIHF broken skate its own 82.1 omits; USA Hockey and CARHA write no icing bar
Rule: Hockey Canada restricts it only in U18AAA and Junior, and in Senior at the Member's option (6.7(d)), and there its third exception is wider than the NHL's — any penalty assessed to either team, whether or not it changes the on-ice strength
Rule: Try to change after the Referee signals the change closed — a late broken-skate swap included — and those players go back to the bench with a warning through your coach; a bench already warned takes a bench minor for delay of game (IIHF 82.2(V), which names no broken skate)
Key: Shift length, the safe and unsafe windows to change, and why a bad change concedes goals are all in Game Management
```

**Change on your legs, and go first.** The centre's route covers more of the ice than either winger's and the centre fades first — **coaching craft rather than a measured finding** — so a line that changes as a group should change on *your* legs: call it and go, and the wingers follow you off. Don't change into an offensive-zone faceoff you are needed to take, and after your own icing don't start drifting toward the bench where your line cannot change.

**What it costs.** Under the NHL, the IIHF and the PWHL a tired centre cannot change after their own team's icing. The exceptions are injury, a pulled goalie going back in, a penalty that changes the strength on the ice, and in the IIHF book only a player with a broken skate — and ⚠️ **a refused broken-skate change gets your team warned, or a bench minor for delay of game if it has already been warned.** **USA Hockey and CARHA set no such bar**, so there you change after your own icing as after any whistle. **Hockey Canada applies it only in U18AAA, Junior and, at the Member's option, Senior**, and there its penalty exception is **wider** than the NHL's: *"[w]hen a penalty or penalties have been assessed to either team, a change of players will be permitted"*, whether or not the strength on the ice changes. Find out which book and category you are in before you plan a shift around any of this. [Rules Primer](../foundation/rules_primer.md) carries the book-by-book comparison.

Shift length, the safe and unsafe windows to change, and why a bad change concedes goals are all in [Game Management](../systems/game_management.md).

## Common Mistakes

- **Staying high in the slot when the puck is in the corner — under a low zone collapse.** Go down and help your defenceman win it, and call for the weak-side winger to drop into the high slot behind you; stay high and your defenceman is left alone in a battle they will probably lose. **Under man-on-man — or a hybrid, which marks man-on-man low — the job changes**: you stay with your check, and holding the slot may be exactly right. Even in a collapse, hold the slot when your strong-side winger has already walked down with their man and the battle is covered.
- **Carrying the puck through the middle of your own zone.** Get to the boards or behind the net first, then distribute. Coaching consensus makes it the most expensive place on the ice to lose the puck — reasoned from shot danger, not counted: no study ranking turnovers by location was found for this document. [Risk Management](../hockey-iq/risk_management.md) grades every area of the ice by what a turnover there costs.
- **Turning your back to anyone coming at you on a rim or a reverse — and ducking when the check arrives.** Arrive with your skates parallel to the boards, head up and chin off your chest. Moving the puck from the wall or behind your own net puts the forechecker behind you by design, so this is every shift. A turned back makes the contact a hit from behind you can neither see nor brace for — USA Hockey warns that *"Even a light hit from behind could inflict a severe head or neck injury"* — and ducking straightens the neck, which USA Hockey records can injure you against the boards or a post at walking speed. ⚠️ **Take the posture even where body checking is barred** — contact incidental to chasing the puck along the boards stays legal, and the wall does not care which. [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md) covers both halves.
- **Coasting through the neutral zone to avoid going offside.** Arrive with speed and time your stride so the puck crosses first, with your trail skate **touching** the line. A blade in contact is onside in every book; a raised one is offside under USA Hockey and Hockey Canada, and CARHA's book does not say.
- **Skating through the middle with your head down.** **Head up, chin off your chest** — at full speed and while counting your linemates out to tag up: you cannot brace for a hit you cannot see. ⚠️ **And no book fully protects a head-down carrier** — the IIHF does not count a square check on one skating head down toward the checker as a head hit, and the NHL and the PWHL weigh that posture against you. [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md#keep-your-head-up-always--you-cannot-brace-for-a-hit-you-cannot-see) sets out all of them.
- **Puck-watching on the backcheck** and losing the middle-lane driver. Find the middle-lane driver first and stay with them; if you cannot tell who has called them, pick them up. That driver is yours under the positional assignment assumed here — a coaching choice, not a law — and somebody's under every alternative.
- **Planning the backcheck around a hit you may not be allowed to throw.** **Take the ice, not the body**: angle with your feet, get inside them with your stick in the passing lane, and reach the spot ahead of them rather than stepping into them. It works whatever your division's contact status, and against the middle-lane driver it is the only legal version. Step into them and it can be called interference wherever you play, and an illegal check as well if your league bars checking — two minutes at the least, and it can go higher in five of the six books. Under CARHA it can be a major and a game misconduct, with at least a one-game suspension. Even under USA Hockey, whose interference rule stops at two minutes, the same hit can be called under rules that reach an ejection, with a next-game suspension, or a match penalty. ⚠️ **Before you plan a shift around a hit on a puck carrier, get your division's contact status from your affiliate, in writing.**

  **On the forecheck,** ⚠️ **steer rather than push** — in a non-checking classification an intentional push is a penalty in its own right; a violent one can end your game, and one that injures (Hockey Canada, CARHA) or is reckless (USA Hockey) does.
- **Finishing a backcheck into a player’s back.** Take the puck, take the ice, or angle them off it — if you can see their numbers, pull up and play the puck. A forward skating at their own end boards has their back to you and is not braced for it.

  No book lets you count on two minutes for a hit from behind. The NHL, the IIHF and the PWHL write no minor for it at all. CARHA’s and Hockey Canada’s minor carries a **game** misconduct, and a major and game misconduct is mandatory if they are hurt — under CARHA with at least a one-game ban too. USA Hockey’s minor carries a misconduct, and a forceful check on a player along the boards, one that sends them head first into the boards or goal frame, or a reckless one anywhere is at least a major and game misconduct, which also suspends you for the next game. Under USA Hockey, plan on a match and a likely multi-game suspension for a reckless one. Put them into the boards when they cannot protect themselves and it is a mandatory **match** penalty under Hockey Canada and CARHA, and under USA Hockey 608(c) when it is reckless. Once it reaches their head, Hockey Canada prices it as head contact, up to a match — a match if they could not protect themselves — and if it also drove them head first into the boards or goal frame, the harsher price stands.
- **Getting thrown out of the faceoff dot** for something avoidable — including a winger's encroachment you failed to warn them about. Warn your wingers before every draw that matters, and ⚠️ **agree two back-up draw takers, not one** — under Hockey Canada the player who caused the violation may not take the draw, so if your one replacement is the winger who just encroached, your plan is gone at the moment you need it.
- **Leaning your head in to get low at the dot.** Get your depth from the knees and hips; a head pushed out over the dot ends up against the other centre's. Helmet-to-helmet contact before the drop is pre-drop contact in every book: it can put you — or both centres — out of the draw, and the minor on top is discretionary under the NHL, the IIHF and the PWHL and mandatory under USA Hockey, Hockey Canada and CARHA.
- **Taking long shifts.** Change when your legs go, not when the play happens to stop: call it yourself, go first, and let the wingers follow you off. A well-managed shift is nearer **30–45 seconds** — coaching craft, not a measured band. The measured range is **roughly 30 to 80 seconds**, from **Lignell et al. (2018), Brocherie et al. (2018) and Bracko et al. (1998)** as collected in Vigh-Larsen and Mohr's 2024 physiology review, which summarises those three rather than measuring the band itself. The top of that range is where the wheels come off, and the centre is generally reckoned to fade first of the three forwards — coaching craft, not a measured comparison. [Conditioning and Recovery](../off-the-ice/conditioning_and_recovery.md) covers both figures.
- **Treating the slot as a place to stand — or standing still at the wrong moment.** **Keep moving to get open, slide with the point man as they walk the line, and plant only at the release** — shuffling then gives the goalie flashes of the puck through the gaps. A stationary centre in the slot is easy to cover.
- **Planting at the net front without knowing where the post is.** Know where the post is before you plant, and keep your head up and chin off your chest the whole time. A screen has you standing still with your eyes off the man beside you, so you get walked sideways — with the post at one edge of that path and the goalie at the other. **The goalie is the one body there you never take that contact against — give ground rather than hold your spot into them.** Never duck: the flexed neck turns a collision with a post into a spinal injury, and USA Hockey names the goal post in that mechanism alongside the boards, recording that the injury *"can occur at walking speed"*. Meet a post with an arm or a leg, never your head first. **A box-out, legal or not in your book, puts you on the same path.** [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md#9-net-front-battles) owns it in full.
- **Screening from inside the blue paint, or with a skate on the red line at its edge.** Screen from outside the paint and off the red line, and keep the blade you are saving for a tip out of there too — four books count that line as crease, and the NHL's and the PWHL's rule text does not say. ⚠️ **The cost is not only your own shot.** Under USA Hockey, which counts your stick as you, a goal scored with an attacker's stick or skates in the crease while the goalie is in it is disallowed — whichever teammate shot it. ⚠️ **And when their goalie has come out to play the puck, leave them their path home** — under NHL and IIHF Rule 69.4 deliberately keeping them from getting back can be penalised as goalkeeper interference, with no contact at all.
- **Arriving into the goalie instead of stopping in front of them.** Take the ice in front of the net, never the goalie, and arrive under control. Off the forecheck or down from the slot, a screen and a charge are one stride apart. ⚠️ **Charging a goalie risks a major plus a game misconduct in every book.** It is mandatory under Hockey Canada for any charge on a goalie or any interference that injures one, and under CARHA where the goalie is in the crease or anyone is injured, with at least a one-game ban. USA Hockey makes it mandatory for a reckless charge, with a next-game suspension — plan on a match penalty too. The NHL, the PWHL and the Elite League make it mandatory once a charging major injures the face or head.

  **Arrive into a goalie from behind** ⚠️ **and it is checking from behind:** the NHL, the IIHF and the PWHL write no minor at all. Hockey Canada takes the harsher of its checking-from-behind and goalie-interference prices (7.5 and 8.5), and on the head the harsher of head contact and 8.5. Hockey Canada and CARHA make it a mandatory match penalty if it drives a goalie who cannot protect themselves into the net or boards. So does Hockey Canada's head-contact rule wherever a hit to the head leaves them unable to protect themselves or is meant to injure.
- **Blocking a shot from in front of your own goalie's eyes.** Block from close and on your feet, head out of the lane, hands behind your body and stick flat beside you rather than out in front, or get out of the lane and let them see it. In a wedge you stand at the mid-to-high slot, on the line between the point and your own net. **And never block the shot on a 2-on-1** — as the lone defender back your job is the pass, and your goalie has the shooter. [Defender](defender.md) and [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md) own the technique and the list of when *not* to block.
- **Grabbing a loose puck in front of your own net and holding it.** Knock it straight down where you caught it and play it with your stick or your skate. Catching it is not the mistake — no book penalises the catch, provided you put it down immediately. ⚠️ **Holding on, carrying it, or dropping it somewhere better is what costs you**, and inside your own crease that is a penalty shot rather than a minor (USA Hockey lets the other team take a minor instead). With your own net empty, never cover a puck in your crease: it is an awarded goal on the bare fact in most books, and under USA Hockey wherever you prevented *"an obvious and imminent goal"*; CARHA writes it both ways, so treat any cover as the goal.

## Key Takeaways

1. **Play the middle of the ice in all three zones, and let your job move with the puck.** You are the link between the other four skaters: a winger owns a lane and a defenceman owns the back of the play, and you own the space between them.
2. **When the puck goes into your own corner, help your defenceman win it and call the weak-side winger into the high slot behind you; when it is at the net, hold the high slot.** That is the low zone collapse, a coaching choice — under man-on-man you track one opponent instead, so ask which your team plays.
3. **Swing low so your defenceman has a safe outlet** — that is the conventional swing breakout. Under a centre fly you leave early and the weak-side winger drops in, so find out which your team plays.
4. **Own the middle of the neutral zone, with your head up.** Under the containment systems assumed here you are the lone forward taking the middle away and steering the carrier wide; under an aggressive press two forwards hunt the puck, so ask which your team plays. [You cannot brace for a hit you cannot see](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md#keep-your-head-up-always--you-cannot-brace-for-a-hit-you-cannot-see).
5. **Take the middle-lane driver on the backcheck, and get inside them** — between them and your net, stick in the passing lane, never on their body. Stepping into them can be interference anywhere, and an illegal check if your league bars checking. The middle is usually yours, a coaching choice; if you cannot tell who has called it, take it.
6. **Never finish a check on a turned back.** ⚠️ **If you can see their numbers, pull up and play the puck.** No book lets you count on two minutes. Driving a player who cannot protect themselves into the boards or net is a mandatory match penalty under Hockey Canada and CARHA, and under USA Hockey if reckless. Ask your division whether you may body check at all.
7. **Prepare the draws that matter — your own end, special teams, the last minute, after an icing.** Get low from your knees, not your head. ⚠️ **Warn your wingers that their encroachment puts you out, and agree *two* replacement takers**, because under Hockey Canada the one who caused the violation may not take the draw.
8. **Under pressure anywhere, get the puck to the outside** — not through the middle of your own zone or the neutral zone. Rim it, chip it off the boards, or give it to a winger on the wall and support them. Simple beats clever.
9. **Screen just outside the crease, skates off the red line, stick out of the paint, and go for the rebound — never into the goalie.** ⚠️ **Arrive under control**: charging a goalie risks ejection in every book — automatic under Hockey Canada, as is any interference that injures one; under CARHA in the crease or on injury; under USA Hockey if reckless.
10. **A puck in the air near your own net: knock it straight down and play it.** Holding it, carrying it or dropping it somewhere better costs a penalty shot inside your own crease — and above your paint, below the bar, counts as inside — and an awarded goal with your net empty.
11. **Cross the blue line at full speed with your trailing blade down on the line or the ice behind it.** Time your stride so the puck crosses first rather than coasting to stay onside. A blade in contact is onside in every book; a raised one is not under USA Hockey or Hockey Canada, so keep it down.
12. **Never block the shot on a 2-on-1.** As the lone defender back, your job is the pass — your goalie already has the shooter, on the penalty kill and off it. [Defender](defender.md) and [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md) carry the technique and the other times not to block.
13. **Before you shoot, look for the pass across the front of the net.** A pass across the **royal road** — the line down the middle of the ice from net to net — forces the goalie into a hard lateral push, so the puck arrives before they are set. Refuse the extra pass along your own side.
14. **On a 1-3-1 power play you are most often the bumper** — in the soft spot in the middle of the slot, the target for both half-walls, shooting quickly. In an umbrella or an overload you are low or on the strong-side wall, so ask which your team runs.
15. **On the penalty kill, know your shape before the puck drops, and never chase the puck around the perimeter.** Ice it only while you are a man short on the ice — and under USA Hockey only at Youth 15-Only and above, Girls 16U and above, High School and Adult; otherwise clear it to the neutral zone.
16. **Carry the extra stride before you dump it.** With the puck past the red line on your stick the dump is legal however far it goes — unless your league divides the ice at the blue lines, as some CARHA leagues do. When the other team ices it and cannot change, have a set play ready.
17. **Change on your legs, and go first.** When the line changes as a group, you call it and the wingers follow you off — the centre is generally reckoned to fade first, which is coaching craft rather than a measured finding. Don't change into an offensive-zone faceoff you are needed to take.
18. **On the forecheck you are usually first in: drive at the carrier on an angle and steer them to one side.** Steer with your feet and stick, never a push — in a non-checking division a push is a penalty. Arrive under control: a run-up, even two hard strides, can be charging. If a winger beats you there, support.

*Charging a goaltender, and body-checking eligibility (the §Keep your body out of the crease section, the §Backchecking → May you check at all, and what a check from behind costs subsection, Common Mistakes and Key Takeaways 6 and 9): read in the primary texts on 1 September 2026 and quoted from them. **NHL** Rule 42.1 — *"A minor, major or a major and a game misconduct shall be imposed on a player who charges a goalkeeper while the goalkeeper is within his goal crease."* That sentence runs across a page break in the published book, with a running header and a page number printed in the middle of it, so search for it in pieces. **IIHF** Rule 42.1 — *"A penalty shall be imposed on a Player who charges a goalkeeper while the goalkeeper is within their goal crease"* — with **42.2** (a minor *"based on the degree of force and violence of the check"*), **42.3** (a major for a charge that recklessly endangers) and **42.4** (_"a major penalty and a game misconduct penalty"_ for a Player who *"recklessly endangers their opponent by 'charging'"*, **no injury required**). **USA Hockey** Rule 607(a)–(e), which writes **no bare minor at any limb**: 607(b) is mandatory — *"A major penalty plus game misconduct penalty shall be assessed to any player who recklessly endangers an opponent as a result of charging"* (read 30 September 2026) — and 607(c) is *"[a] minor plus a misconduct or a major plus a game misconduct penalty … to a player who body checks or charges a goalkeeper while the goalkeeper is within the goal crease or privileged area"*, and Note 1 sends *"[a]ny deliberate body contact or check that is delivered to the goalkeeper"* to that rule. **Hockey Canada** Rule 8.5(b), the mandatory *"Major penalty and Game Misconduct penalty … to any player who charges the goaltender"*, its Note 1 referring the definition to Rule 7.4(b) Interpretation 2 — which in the 2026-28 book is the 7.4(a) blindside definition;
