## Key focus

**Get below the puck in your own end.** When your defenceman picks it up they need someone underneath them, and that is normally you — inside the faceoff dots and below the hash marks, not out on the boards. That is the conventional **swing breakout**; under a **centre fly** you leave early up the middle and the weak-side winger drops in instead. Ask which your team plays — and if nobody is under the puck, be the one who is.

**Protect the slot in your own end, and never leave it empty.** With the puck at the net or in the slot, cover the high slot — between the faceoff dots and the top of the circles — and mark whoever is there. When it goes to the corner, help your defenceman win it, and call early for the weak-side winger to drop in behind you. That rotation belongs to the low zone collapse, a coaching choice; under man-on-man you follow your check and nobody is coming, so ask which your team plays.

**Own the middle of the ice in both directions.** Going forward it is the best place to decide from, because you can see both wingers and both defencemen. Coming back it is the lane nobody else covers: the forward arriving late and unmarked behind the carrier usually hurts you more than the carrier does. Come back on the inside of your man, between them and your net.

**In their end, play the slot and finish at the net.** Shoot from between the circles rather than passing for something prettier; when you do pass, put it across the middle, which makes the goalie move. Keep moving to get open — but when your defenceman winds up, plant and be still in front of the goalie's eyes, then go to the net for the rebound. Arrive under control and go around the goaltender, never into them.

**Be first in on the forecheck, and steer rather than hit.** Under the 2-1-2 assumed here the first forward in is usually you: arrive under control, not off a long run, and drive at the carrier on an angle that pushes them toward one boards so your winger knows where to seal. If a winger gets there first, you support. A 1-2-2 sends only one forward in, so ask which your team plays.

**Under pressure, put the puck to the outside.** The middle of your own zone and the middle of the neutral zone are the worst places on the ice to try to beat somebody. Rim it, chip it off the boards, or give it to a winger on the wall and go and get it back.

**Take the draws that matter seriously.** Most draws barely matter; the ones in your own end, on special teams, in the last minute and after an icing do. Agree with your linemates beforehand where you are trying to win each. When you cannot win it clean, lift or press the other centre's stick — the stick, never their hands — so a winger gets the loose puck.

## Overview

```facts
Convention: Low support on the breakout belongs to the swing breakout, a coaching choice — too high and your defenceman has no outlet, too wide and one opponent covers you and your winger; under a centre fly you leave early up the middle instead
Key: Coming back, the forward arriving late and unmarked behind the puck carrier is usually more dangerous than the carrier — coaching emphasis rather than a counted ranking
Convention: Which backchecker owns the middle lane is a coaching choice — the centre under the positional assignment assumed here, the first man back on some teams, an assigned check under man-on-man; if nobody has called it, take it
Key: The slot pays for where it is rather than for how hard you shoot — on coaching-consensus ranges a slot shot is worth roughly three to seven times a perimeter one, from a source that names no dataset
Priority: Put it to the outside in the middle of your own zone and the neutral zone — both graded high for turnover cost: shot danger in one, odd-man rushes in the other. Coaching consensus, not counted
Key: Prepare the draws that matter — computed from NHL official statistics, team faceoff percentage explains about 6% of points-percentage variation over eleven seasons; in 2016-17 the link was negative
Convention: "The centre skates the furthest" is coaching craft — no study separates centres from wingers on distance, though the NHL publishes per-skater distance against a position code, so the comparison is unrun rather than impossible
Key: Measured, the workload belief reverses — Lignell 2018, 36 NHL players: defencemen skated 29% more in total than forwards and 47% longer on the ice; forwards 54% more high-intensity skating per minute
```

You are the link between the other four skaters: you play the middle of the ice in all three zones, and your job moves with the puck. A winger owns a lane and a defenceman owns an end; you own the space between them. That is why a centre's instructions vary more from team to team than anyone else's — so most of what follows names the common version, the realistic alternative, and the question to ask your bench.

**Getting below the puck is the base of all your own-end work.** Too high, and your defenceman has no safe outlet and the shift starts in your own end. Too wide, out on the boards, and one opponent covers you and your winger at once. **That low support belongs to the swing breakout, which is a coaching choice.** Under a **centre fly** you leave early up the middle and the weak-side winger drops in to do the low job; under a **flat or spread breakout** both wingers hold higher and the exit uses longer passes. The commonest breakdown is a centre flying while the wingers hold high, which leaves nobody under the puck. [Breakouts](../systems/breakouts.md) is the authority.

**Protecting the slot gives the centre the most defensive work of the three forwards, and the job moves.** Your ice is the high slot when the puck is in front of your net and the strong-side corner when it is not; the read is which needs you more. The failure is leaving both: going to the corner without calling the winger into the slot trades a battle you might win for a chance nobody can stop. Hold the slot when your defenceman and the strong-side winger already have the corner. **That rotation belongs to the low zone collapse assumed throughout, a coaching choice** — under man-on-man the weak-side winger stays with their check, and you may be handed the net-front man yourself. Under the collapse the goalmouth is the weak-side defenceman's, not yours.

**Owning the middle is two jobs on one piece of ice.** With the puck it is a vantage point: from the centre lane you see both wingers and both defencemen, so carry through there to distribute. Coming back, it is the lane an attack scores from — the player who beats you is usually the forward arriving late behind the carrier, which is **coaching emphasis rather than a counted ranking**. Get between that player and your net; a trailing backchecker defends nothing. **Who takes that lane is a coaching choice too**: the centre here, the first man back on some teams, man-on-man on others, and no count of which is commonest has been found. If nobody has called it, take it.

**In the offensive zone the slot is your ice — use it.** Get open between the circles, shoot when you have space there, screen the goaltender when a point shot is coming, and go to the net for the rebound under control, taking the ice in front of the goaltender and never the goaltender. On the coaching-consensus ranges in [Shooting](../technique/shooting.md) a slot shot is worth several times a perimeter one; the source names no dataset, so trust the size of the gap, not the decimals. The one limit is the crease: screen from outside it, with your skates off the red crease line and not merely out of the blue paint. What stepping in costs differs by rulebook, and is set out where you meet it.

**On the forecheck you are usually first in, and the job is the angle, not the hit.** Drive at the carrier on a line that pushes them toward one boards, so your strong-side winger knows where the puck is going. Steering is legal in every league; a push or a body check may not be in yours, and a long run at a carrier is how the rulebooks describe a charge. F1, F2 and F3 are taken in order of arrival, so if a winger gets there first you support. The 2-1-2 assumed here is a coaching choice — [Forechecking Systems](../systems/forechecking_systems.md) sets out the alternatives.

**Getting the puck to the outside survives every system.** The middle of your own zone is the most expensive place to lose it, and the neutral zone is where odd-man rushes are made — both **coaching consensus rather than a measured ranking**. Under pressure in either, move it to the boards or behind your own net first, and distribute from there.

**The draws are your defining skill, with one thing worth knowing first.** An NHL game has **roughly 56 faceoffs** — 56.6 per game in 2024-25 and 56.0 in 2025-26, **computed from NHL official statistics** — yet team faceoff percentage explains only about **6% of the variation** in points percentage across eleven seasons, and in 2016-17 the link went negative. So prepare for the ten or fifteen draws a game that carry real consequence — a coaching estimate, not a count. [Faceoffs](../systems/faceoffs.md) carries all three figures with their limits.

**One belief you will hear about the position is half wrong.** That the centre skates the furthest is coaching craft: **no published study separates centres from wingers on skating distance.** What has been measured points the other way — Lignell et al. (2018), 36 NHL players in one game, found that **defencemen covered 29% more skating in total than forwards and were on the ice 47% longer**, while forwards did 54% more high-intensity skating per minute. The surviving half is **territory, not mileage** — your job spans all three zones and the full width of the rink — and that is coaching craft too: no measurement of how much ice a position covers was found for this document.

**Every line, zone and area of the ice is defined in [Rink Map](../foundation/rink_map.md), and the vocabulary on top of it — high and low, strong and weak side, the slot, the half-wall, F1, F2 and F3 — in [Language and Glossary](../foundation/language_and_glossary.md).** Read those two once and everything below gets easier.

Three of those words do particular work here. **High** means toward the blue line you are facing and **low** toward the goal line — and you will be told to **swing low** more than anyone, which means below the hash marks, not merely below the puck. **Strong side** is the side the puck is on, and yours switches every time the puck does. And the **half-wall** — the boards roughly level with the faceoff dot — is your wingers' ice; drifting onto it is the commonest way a centre wrecks his line's spacing.

**A note on the headings below.** Each zone has the same four situations. *Without the Puck* means the puck is not on your stick **and** you are not pressuring whoever has it — a different job in each zone, so each of those headings is named for the job it describes.

## Defensive Zone

The center is normally given the most demanding defensive role among the forwards — coaching craft rather than a measured comparison, and it follows from the assignment below rather than from the position itself. You support the defenders and protect the most dangerous part of the ice — the slot, where most goals are scored from: close to half of NHL goals come from the inner slot and just over a quarter from the outer slot. [Shooting](../technique/shooting.md) has that figure, and the limit travels with it — it is Sportlogiq tracking of three seasons at five-on-five, and the tracking is not public.

---

### Defensive Zone: Carrying the Puck — Free Space

```facts
Position: Low in the defensive zone, below the hash marks, swinging toward the puck — the conventional swing breakout; under a centre fly you leave early up the middle instead
Action: Receive the puck from your defenceman and immediately look to exit the zone
Options: Carry up the middle if the lane is clearly open, or distribute to a winger on the boards
Goal: Be the reliable outlet that starts the breakout and gets the puck out of the zone
Key: On the conventional swing breakout your low positioning is what gives the defencemen a safe pass — if you don't swing low, they are stuck with the puck under pressure
```

**Where to be:** Low in the defensive zone, below the hash marks, swinging toward the puck. (Hash marks are the small alignment marks on the sides of the faceoff circles, roughly level with the faceoff dot. "Below" means in the area between them and the goal line.)

**What to do:** Receive the puck from your defenseman and immediately look to exit the zone. Carry up the middle if the lane is clearly open, or distribute to a winger on the boards. Your movement low in the zone is what makes the breakout (your team's controlled exit from the defensive zone) work.

**Goal:** Be the reliable outlet that starts the breakout and carries or passes the puck out of the zone.

**Key considerations:** Your low positioning is what gives the defensemen a safe pass. If you don't swing low, they are stuck with the puck under pressure. Your movement creates the option. **That low support job belongs to the conventional swing breakout, which is a coaching choice** — under a **centre fly** you leave the zone early up the middle instead and the weak-side winger drops in to do it in your place. Find out which your team plays — [Breakouts](../systems/breakouts.md) is the authority, and this document sets both out where it covers getting open for the breakout.

### Defensive Zone: Carrying the Puck — Under Pressure

```facts
Position: Low in the defensive zone near the boards or behind the net
Never: Carry through traffic in your own zone — the middle of your own end is the most expensive place to lose it, on coaching consensus reasoned from shot danger and recovery time, not measurement
Read: Rim it onward around the boards to beat pressure that is sealing you in; reverse it back against the flow, to a defenceman arriving behind you, to beat pressure that has over-committed
Priority: Getting the puck out of danger beats making a great play — get to a safe position first, then move it to a winger or rim it around the boards
Action: Make the rim-or-reverse read while the puck is still travelling to you — a look over your shoulder before it arrives, not after
Technique: When contact comes at the wall your skates should already be parallel to the boards — forearm and hip to the wall, not the point of your shoulder, and not the whole shoulder if you can help it
Never: Turn your back to the checker on a rim or a reverse — USA Hockey warns them: "Even a light hit from behind could inflict a severe head or neck injury". Skates parallel, forearm and hip, head up
Never: Duck to avoid a check — in a head-down collision with the boards or a goal post, your neck can break at walking speed; no speed is slow enough to make that safe. Head up, chin off your chest instead
Rule: Where body checking is barred it is barred both ways — the forechecker arriving behind you on a rim or reverse may not check you either, and it is at least a minor on him (USA Hockey 604(c), Hockey Canada 7.3(a)). Incidental board contact is legal everywhere, so never turn your back to the checker
Rule: A minor is the floor and not the ceiling on a check thrown where checking is barred — Hockey Canada 7.3(b) allows a major and game misconduct on the degree of violence and makes both mandatory if the check injures you; USA Hockey 604(d) is mandatory for reckless endangerment
```

**Where to be:** Low in the defensive zone near the boards or behind the net.

**What to do:** Don't try to carry through traffic in your own zone. Get to a safe position and quickly move the puck to a winger or rim it around the boards (a hard shot along the boards that follows the curve of the rink around to a teammate further along — typically from one corner, around behind the net, to the far corner). The priority is getting the puck out of danger, not making a great play.

A **rim** and a **reverse** are different plays and worth keeping straight: a rim sends the puck *onward* around the boards in the direction you're facing; a reverse sends it *back the way it came*, against the flow of the forecheck, usually to your defence partner or a defenceman arriving behind you. A reverse beats pressure that has over-committed; a rim beats pressure that is sealing you in.

**And both of those plays are made with a forechecker arriving behind you — so the posture you take the contact in is not a preference.** A rim is the play when one is sealing you against the boards; a reverse is the play when one has over-committed and skated past you. Either way: **never turn your back to the checker, and never duck.** They are two prohibitions against two different injuries. Turning your back converts the contact into a hit from behind, which you can neither see nor brace for, and USA Hockey's warning to the player delivering one is that *"Even a light hit from behind could inflict a severe head or neck injury."* Ducking tucks your chin to your chest and straightens the cervical spine, and USA Hockey records that the injury from that head-down, head-first collision with the boards or a goal post can occur at walking speed. Arrive with your skates already parallel to the boards, forearm and hip to the wall — never the point of your shoulder, and not the whole shoulder if you can help it — head up and chin off your chest. Facing the wall square is the last resort, for when you are already facing it and cannot turn parallel — and if you use it, get your stick and gloves up and spread on the glass to cushion the blow and spread the force. Never turn to face the wall with the checker behind you. [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md) covers the full three-case rule and the technique.

**Whether that forechecker is allowed to check you at all is a different question, and the answer is your classification rather than this play.** *May you check at all, and what a check from behind costs* below asks it of you as a backchecker; **the same two bans run the other way.** A body check on you in a USA Hockey Competitive Contact category is a minor on him under **Rule 604(c)**, and **Hockey Canada Rule 7.3(a)** assesses one to a player who *"intentionally body-checks, bumps, shoves, or pushes any opposing player"* at U13 and below, in female hockey and in any other division a Member has approved. ⚠️ **Neither of those minors is a ceiling**, and *May you check at all, and what a check from behind costs* below sets out the escalation in both books — the part worth carrying here is that Hockey Canada **7.3(b)** makes a major and game misconduct **mandatory** where a check that would otherwise be a minor injures you, which is not a price the player throwing it chooses. ⚠️ **That takes nothing off the posture, because neither book calls the boards quiet.** Hockey Canada writes into Rule 7.3 itself that *"[b]ody contact is incidental contact of two opposing players in pursuit of the puck on the ice in the same direction"* and that where it occurs *"no penalty will be assessed"*; the Note to USA Hockey 604(c) puts legal competitive contact in exactly the place a rim or a reverse happens — *"This most often occurs when two players are physically engaged (see Glossary) in front of the goal or along the boards."* **So the wall still arrives at every level. What changes by level is whether anybody is allowed to put you into it.**

**Make that read while the puck is still travelling to you, not after it's on your stick.** While your defenceman is retrieving it behind the net, take a look over your shoulder for the side the forecheck is sealing — that is the same look that lets you go straight into a rim or a reverse the instant you receive, instead of discovering the answer with a forechecker already on you. [Scanning and Anticipation](../hockey-iq/scanning_and_anticipation.md) covers the habit and the research behind it in full; it is not restated here.

![](diagram:centre-out-of-the-middle)

**Goal:** Relieve pressure and safely move the puck toward an exit.

**Key considerations:** The middle of the ice in your defensive zone is the worst place to lose the puck — **coaching consensus reasoned from shot danger and recovery time, not a measured ranking**, and no study ranking turnovers by where on the ice they happen was found for this document — read that as "none found here", not as proof that none exists. [Risk Management](../hockey-iq/risk_management.md) grades every area of the ice by what a turnover there costs you. If you are under pressure in the middle, move to the boards or behind the net first, then distribute.

### Defensive Zone: Pressuring an Opponent with the Puck

```facts
Position: Low in the zone, supporting the strong-side defender
Read: Puck in the corner or behind the net — join the strong-side defender; two of you on the puck usually wins it
Read: Puck at the net or in the slot — cover the high slot and mark any opposing forward set up there
Never: Take the goalmouth under a low zone collapse — it is the weak-side defender's, and yours is the layer one further out; under man-on-man or a hybrid it may be yours, so ask which your team plays
Goal: Help the defenders clear the zone — by winning the corner, or by denying the slot
Convention: Low zone collapse (zone) coverage is a coaching choice, not a rule — under man-on-man, or a hybrid of the two, the wingers follow their check into the corner, so find out which your team plays
Action: Under low zone collapse, when you leave the slot for the corner the weak-side winger covers it — call the rotation early so the slot is never empty; under man-on-man they stay with their check
Read: Strong-side winger already down with their man — the corner is covered, so hold the slot; weak-side winger already low with theirs — no collapse is coming, so weigh corner against slot and call it
```

**Where to be:** Low in the zone, supporting the strong-side defender. When the puck is in the corner or behind the net, go in and help win it. When the puck is at the net front or in the slot, cover the **high slot** — the layer between the faceoff dots and the top of the circles. **Under the low zone collapse assumed here** the goalmouth itself belongs to the weak-side defender, not to you; under **man-on-man** or a **hybrid** it belongs to whoever is matched to the net-front forward, and the centre is one of the low three who can be given that man.

**What to do:** Read where the puck is and provide the right support:
- **Puck in the corner or behind the net:** Join the strong-side defender. Two of you on the puck usually wins it. **Go in with your head up and your eyes off your own skates** — a corner is a 2-on-1 by default, and [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md#8-corner-battles) sets out the battle, the escape routes and the safety of it.
- **Puck at the net or in the slot:** Cover the high slot — the area between the faceoff dots and the top of the circles. Mark any opposing forward set up there. The weak-side defender stays glued to the player directly at the net (the goalmouth); your job is the player one layer further out.

The wingers stay high covering the points (the areas near the blue line where the opposing defensemen normally stand), and the weak-side defender holds the net front. **"Stay high" in your own end means level with the opposing point man, and it expires the moment your team wins the puck** — then the winger comes down the wall to the outlet spot. [Winger](winger.md) covers that instruction in full. You are the flexible low support that links the corner battle and the slot.

> **This describes a low zone collapse (zone) coverage system** — the one assumed throughout this document. It is a coaching choice, not a rule of hockey. Under **man-on-man**, every player tracks an assigned opponent and the wingers *do* follow their check into the corner; under a **hybrid**, it's man-on-man below the dots and zone above. Your job changes materially between them. Find out which one your team plays before your first shift — see [Defensive Zone Coverage](../systems/defensive_zone_coverage.md).

**Goal:** Help the defenders clear the zone — either by winning the puck in the corner, or by denying the slot to opposing forwards.

**Key considerations:** Your responsibility shifts with the puck. Watch the play and move with it. The wingers are not coming down to help in the corner unless they're following their own man — that role belongs to you. If you stay high in the slot when the puck is in the corner, the strong-side defender is alone against the puck carrier and is likely to lose the battle. When you leave the slot to help in the corner, the **weak-side winger** should collapse from the point to cover the high slot in your absence. **That rotation belongs to the low zone collapse rather than to every system** — under **man-on-man** the weak-side winger stays with their check and no rotation is coming, so find out which your team plays before you count on one. Anyone with a clear view can call the rotation (you, the weak-side defender, or the winger themselves) — speak up early so the slot is never empty.

![](diagram:centre-low-zone-collapse)

**Exception — strong-side winger has walked down with their man:** If the opposing strong-side defenseman has come down off the point and the strong-side winger has followed them, you have extra help low. Read the situation: if your defender and the winger have the puck battle covered, hold the slot rather than committing all the way to the corner.

**Exception — weak-side winger is already low:** If the weak-side opposing defenseman has come down off the point and the weak-side winger is following them, the high-slot collapse isn't available to you when you commit to the corner. Weigh whether the corner needs your help more than the slot needs coverage — and call clearly so the defenders know what to expect.

### Defensive Zone: Without the Puck — Getting Open for the Breakout

```facts
Position: Swinging low — inside the faceoff dots and below the hash marks, in the inside half of your strong-side faceoff circle
Never: Drift out onto the boards — that is the winger's ice, and one opponent can then cover you both
Action: Keep moving, stay open and communicate so the defencemen always have an option
Goal: Be the first and most reliable outlet for the defencemen when they gain possession
Convention: Low support belongs to the conventional swing breakout — a coaching choice; under a centre fly you leave early and the weak-side winger drops in instead
Action: Find out which breakout your team plays — a centre flying while the wingers hold high near the blue line leaves nobody underneath the puck
Key: If you are too high when your defenceman gets the puck, they have no safe outlet and come under pressure immediately
```

*This is the situation where your team is about to have the puck: your defencemen are winning it or have won it, and you are getting open to receive. It is not defensive coverage — that is the section above.*

**Where to be:** Swinging low in the defensive zone — **inside the faceoff dots and below the hash marks**, which in practice means the inside half of your strong-side faceoff circle rather than out on the boards. The boards are the strong-side winger's ice; if you drift out there, one opposing player can cover the two of you and the defenceman loses the middle-lane option entirely. Move toward where the puck is going. Full detail in [Breakouts](../systems/breakouts.md), which is the authority for breakout positioning.

> **Low support is a coaching choice, and the authority says so.** The five-role structure described here belongs to the **conventional swing breakout**, the version the coaching guides behind this document describe and the one assumed throughout — nobody has counted what teams actually run. The two alternatives you will actually meet: under a **centre fly** (also "centre high") you deliberately leave the zone early up the middle to stretch the ice, and the **weak-side winger drops in** to take the low support job instead; under a **flat or spread breakout** both wingers hold higher near the blue line and the exit is made with longer passes. **Find out which one your team plays** — the failure that produces most weekend breakdowns is a centre flying in a system where the wingers are holding high, leaving nobody underneath the puck at all. See [Breakouts](../systems/breakouts.md).

![](diagram:centre-swing-low)

**What to do:** Keep moving. Your constant movement low in the zone creates passing options for the defensemen. Stay open and communicate. When your team wins the puck, be in position to receive the first pass and start the breakout.

**Goal:** Be the first and most reliable outlet for the defensemen when they gain possession.

**Key considerations:** If you are too high when your defenseman gets the puck, they have no safe outlet and will come under pressure immediately. Swing low, stay open, and make their job easy.

## Neutral Zone

The transition zone is where the center connects defence and attack. You are the link between all five players.

---

### Neutral Zone: Carrying the Puck — Free Space

```facts
Position: Through the middle of the ice, leading or driving the rush
Action: Carry with pace through the centre of the ice and distribute to the best option
Read: If a winger is open, pass and let them enter in stride; if you have space, carry through yourself
Goal: Lead or direct a controlled transition through the neutral zone and into the offensive zone
Key: The centre of the ice is the best place to decide from — you can see both wingers and both defencemen
Never: Carry for its own sake. If a teammate is in a better position, move the puck instead
Never: Carry through the middle with your head down — you cannot brace for a hit you cannot see
Rule: Head up under every book: IIHF Rule 48.1 does not call a square, no-upward check on a carrier skating head down at the hitter a head hit — it can still be charging, and is illegal wherever checking is barred — and NHL 48.1(ii) and PWHL 48.3(i) weigh that posture against you
```

**Where to be:** Through the middle of the ice, leading or driving the rush.

**What to do:** Carry with pace through the center of the ice. Read the play — if a winger is open, pass and let them enter in stride. If you have space, carry through yourself. Distribute to the best option and keep the play moving at speed.

![](diagram:centre-nz-deciding-from-the-middle)

**Goal:** Lead or direct a controlled transition through the neutral zone and into the offensive zone.

**Key considerations:** The center of the ice is the best place to make decisions from — you can see both wingers and both defensemen. Use that view. Don't carry for its own sake; if a teammate is in a better position, move the puck. ⚠️ **And carry through the middle with your head up.** You cannot brace for a hit you cannot see, so the view you are carrying the puck for is the same thing that keeps you safe in it. The IIHF's book — the British reader's — is the only one read here that excuses, for the head-contact penalty alone, a square check on a carrier skating head down toward the hitter, where the hitter does not drive up into them and goes squarely through the body. The same hit can still be charging, and where checking is barred it is still illegal. The NHL and the PWHL weigh that posture against you too; the head-up rule and the reason for it are set out in full in [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md#keep-your-head-up-always--you-cannot-brace-for-a-hit-you-cannot-see).

### Neutral Zone: Carrying the Puck — Under Pressure

```facts
Position: Through the middle, or shifting to the boards if the middle is congested
Action: Move the puck to a winger or chip it off the boards
Never: Carry through heavy traffic in the middle of the neutral zone. Move the puck to a winger or chip it off the boards instead
Risk: Coaching material puts the making of odd-man rushes in the middle third — consensus rather than a tracked figure, because no public data classifies one by the play that created it
Priority: Quick decision-making is worth more than individual skill here — keep it simple and get the puck to the outside
Goal: Maintain possession through the neutral zone or safely advance the puck
```

**Where to be:** Through the middle, or shifting to the boards if the middle is congested.

**What to do:** If pressured in the middle of the neutral zone, move the puck to a winger or chip it off the boards. Don't try to carry through heavy traffic in the middle: coaching material puts the making of **odd-man rushes** — attacks where the attackers outnumber the defenders coming back, like a 2-on-1 or a 3-on-2, and a high-danger scoring chance — in the middle third of the ice. **That is coaching consensus rather than a tracked figure**: no public data classifies an odd-man rush by the play that created it. Quick decision-making is more valuable than individual skill here.

![](diagram:centre-nz-get-it-to-the-outside)

**Goal:** Maintain possession through the neutral zone or safely advance the puck.

**Key considerations:** The middle third is where coaching material puts the making of odd-man rushes against your team — **consensus rather than a counted ranking**, because nothing published classifies an odd-man rush by the play that created it. [Neutral Zone Systems](../systems/neutral_zone_systems.md) and [Risk Management](../hockey-iq/risk_management.md) both carry that limit. Keep it simple under pressure. Get the puck to the outside.

### Neutral Zone: Pressuring an Opponent with the Puck

```facts
Position: In the middle of the neutral zone — the closest forward initiates pressure, and that is usually you
Action: Drive at the puck carrier to disrupt their advance, or cut off the centre lane to force them wide
Read: Step up to challenge when the opportunity is clear; otherwise deny the middle and force the play to the boards
Goal: Deny a clean entry, or force a turnover before they reach your blue line
Convention: How hard you press is your team's neutral-zone system, not a law — containment under a 1-2-2, 1-2-2 trap or 1-3-1; two forwards hunting the puck under an aggressive press
Action: Find out which neutral-zone system your team plays and what the call is for switching — most teams run more than one
Key: If you don't initiate pressure, the opponent gets a free run at your defencemen
```

**Where to be:** In the middle of the neutral zone. You are the primary forechecker here — the closest forward to the puck initiates pressure, and that is usually you.

**What to do:** Drive at the puck carrier to disrupt their advance, or cut off the center lane to force them wide. The wingers support by blocking passing lanes on their sides, and the defensemen maintain their gap at the blue line. Step up to challenge when the opportunity is clear; otherwise, deny the middle and force the play to the boards.

> **How hard you press, and whether your wingers hold their lanes at all, is your team's neutral-zone system — a coaching choice, not a law of hockey.** What is described here is the **containment** job: under a **1-2-2**, a **1-2-2 trap** or a **1-3-1**, you are the lone forward taking the middle away and steering the carrier to a side, the wingers hold their lanes, and getting the turnover is a bonus rather than the objective. The realistic alternative is an **aggressive neutral-zone press** — the middle-third shape behind a 2-1-2 forecheck — where two forwards are gone hunting the puck, the wingers *do* leave their lanes to pressure, and the point is to take the puck back rather than wait for a mistake. Your job changes materially between them. **Find out which your team plays and what the call is for switching**, because most teams run more than one depending on the score and the shift. [Neutral Zone Systems](../systems/neutral_zone_systems.md) is the authority.

**Goal:** Deny the opposing team a clean entry through the neutral zone, or force a turnover before they reach your blue line.

**Key considerations:** You are the line's first wave of defence in transition. If you don't initiate pressure, the opponent gets a free run at your defensemen. Controlling the middle of the neutral zone limits what the opposing team can do — if the center lane is blocked, they are forced wide, where the wingers can support.

### Neutral Zone: Without the Puck — Supporting the Rush Through the Middle

```facts
Position: Through the centre of the neutral zone, level with or slightly behind the puck
Action: Be a moving target — keep pace with the play and stay connected to both wingers
Key: You set the line's pace into the zone — time your stride so the puck crosses first and arrive at full speed
Never: Coast to stay onside — a line that enters slowly is the one a defence stands up at the blue line, and that costs more than the whistle would
Never: Drop your head through the middle to gather a pass or to count your linemates out — you cannot brace for a hit you cannot see
Rule: You are offside only when both skates have completely crossed the blue line before the puck has; a trailing skate on the line keeps you onside (NHL Rule 83.1)
Rule: A trail skate held in the air is onside under NHL, IIHF and PWHL rules but offside under USA Hockey Rule 630(a) and Hockey Canada Rule 6.11, which require skate contact; CARHA never defines its "own side", so keep the blade down
Rule: USA Hockey Rule 630(d) grants delayed offside and tag-up only at Youth 15-Only and above, Girls 16U and above, High School and Adult — below those the whistle is immediate
Rule: Tag-up is a five-man act — the offside is nullified only when every player of the offending team makes skate contact with the blue line at the same instant (NHL Rule 83.3(i)), so count your linemates out
```

**Where to be:** Moving through the center of the neutral zone, level with or slightly behind the puck.

**What to do:** Be a moving target in the middle of the ice. Keep pace with the play so you are available to receive a pass and carry through. Stay connected to both wingers.

![](diagram:centre-nz-arrive-behind-the-puck)

**Goal:** Be the primary target for outlet passes and keep the rush moving at pace.

**Key considerations:** Stay onside — but the centre is the player who most often gives up speed to do it, and that costs the line more than a whistle would. You are offside only when **both** skates have completely crossed the blue line before the puck. **Keep your trailing blade down on the line**: that is onside in all six books, while a skate held in the air over the neutral zone is onside under the NHL, the IIHF and the PWHL (Rule 85.1) but not under USA Hockey (Rule 630(a)) or Hockey Canada (Rule 6.11), which require skate *contact*. ⚠️ **CARHA leaves you without an answer** — its Rule 72(c) Note 1 says *"on their own side of"* the blue line and never defines it, so it must not be read as following the NHL. In a CARHA-affiliated beer league, drag the blade. A delayed offside is recoverable by tagging up — **in the books that have one.** ⚠️ **Under USA Hockey Rule 630(d), delayed offside exists only at Youth 15-Only and above, Girls 16U and above, High School and Adult**; below those levels the whistle goes immediately and the entry has to be right first time. **Where tag-up exists it is a five-man act**: Rule 83.3(i) cancels the offside only when every player of the offending team has cleared the zone at the same instant, so as the centre, count your linemates out before you turn and go back in. The full rule is in [Rules Primer](../foundation/rules_primer.md).

The centre-specific version: **you set the line's pace into the zone.** You are attacking through the middle, the widest and most-watched lane, so it is tempting to slow down and be safe. Instead, time your stride so the puck crosses first and arrive at full speed. If you coast, both wingers arrive with you, and a line that enters slowly is exactly the one a defence stands up at the blue line. ⚠️ **Do all of it with your head up.** Arriving at speed through the middle, and counting your linemates out to tag up, both invite you to drop your eyes, and you cannot brace for a hit you cannot see. [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md#keep-your-head-up-always--you-cannot-brace-for-a-hit-you-cannot-see) sets out that rule, the reason for it, and the books that weigh a head-down posture against you — the British reader's IIHF book most of all, with the NHL and the PWHL.

**When your defence regroups the puck behind you, curl back with them.** Swing low through the middle of the neutral zone so you meet the pass already skating forward, rather than standing at the red line waiting for it — a pass taken standing still gives the other team time to set up in front of you. Teams differ on whether the centre or a winger curls back for it, so ask which yours wants.

### Offensive Zone: Carrying the Puck — Free Space

```facts
Position: In the slot — between the two faceoff circles, from the top of the circles down to the front of the net
Action: Look to shoot from the slot — on coaching-consensus conversion ranges a slot shot is worth roughly three to seven times a perimeter one
Key: The slot-versus-perimeter conversion ranges come from a source that names no dataset, so trust the size of the gap rather than the decimals
Action: Before you shoot, look for the pass that crosses in front of the net rather than one up your own side — a puck that crosses the middle makes the goalie push sideways and arrives before they are set
Options: If defenders close in, find a winger on the half-wall or in the circle, or a defenceman at the point
Goal: Create a shot on net, either directly or by finding a teammate in a better position
Key: With the puck in the slot and space in front of you, shoot — don't overthink it
Action: Keep moving and keep the puck moving
```

**Where to be:** In the slot — the high-danger area between the two faceoff circles, stretching from the top of the circles down to the front of the net.

**What to do:** Look to shoot from the slot. On the coaching-consensus conversion ranges in [Shooting](../technique/shooting.md) — roughly 10–15% from the slot against 2–4% from the perimeter, **on a source that names no dataset**, so trust the gap rather than the decimals — a slot shot is worth roughly three to seven times a perimeter one. If defenders close in, distribute to a winger on the half-wall (the boards area roughly level with the faceoff dot — between the corner and the point) or in the circle, or to a defenseman at the point for a shot. Keep moving and keep the puck moving.

**One pass does beat the shot, and it is the one across the middle.** A pass that crosses the **royal road** — the imaginary line running lengthwise down the centre of the ice, from the middle of one net to the middle of the other — immediately before a shot forces the goaltender out of small shuffles into a hard lateral push, so the puck arrives before they are set. In Alan Wells' analysis of Ryan Stimson's Passing Project data it is the highest-value pass type measured, finishing at **15.50%, roughly double the next one** — volunteer-tracked, 51,308 shots at five-on-five, counting all attempts rather than shots on goal. As a centre you are at both ends of it more often than anyone: the far-side receiver when a winger has the puck on the wall, and the passer when you come out from behind the net, because a pass from below the goal line up to the slot crosses the road almost by definition. So the pass to refuse is the extra one along your own side of the ice — never the one that changes which side of the net the goalie is looking at. [Offensive Zone Play](../systems/offensive_zone_play.md) owns the figure and the habits that create one.

![](diagram:centre-oz-slot-options)

**Goal:** Create a shot on net, either directly or by finding a teammate in a better position.

**Key considerations:** The slot is where the most goals are scored — close to half of NHL goals from the inner slot and just over a quarter from the outer slot, on Sportlogiq tracking of three seasons at five-on-five that is not public and cannot be re-derived (the shot-location figures are set out in [Shooting](../technique/shooting.md)). If you have the puck there with space, shoot. Don't overthink it. If you're covered, quick passing creates the next opportunity.

### Offensive Zone: Carrying the Puck — Under Pressure

```facts
Position: In the slot, behind the net, or along the boards near the circles
Technique: Use your body to protect the puck — forearm and hip to the wall, not the point of your shoulder, and not the whole shoulder if you can help it
Never: Turn your back to the checker on a cycle, or coming out from behind the net — USA Hockey warns them: "Even a light hit from behind could inflict a severe head or neck injury". Head up
Never: Duck to avoid a check — a head-down collision with the boards or a goal post can break your neck at walking speed, and behind the net the post is right there. Head up, chin off your chest instead
Options: A quick outlet to a winger on the half-wall, or to the defenceman at the point
Action: Use the back of the net as a pivot — come out the other side or find a late-arriving teammate
Goal: Maintain possession under pressure and keep the offensive play alive
Key: Behind the net you can see the whole ice and decide without risking a direct shot or a dangerous turnover
```

**Where to be:** In the slot, behind the net, or along the boards near the circles.

**What to do:** Use your body to protect the puck. Look for a quick outlet to a winger on the half-wall or the defenseman at the point. If you go behind the net, use it as a pivot point — come out the other side or find a late-arriving teammate.

**Cycling is how you hold the puck low in their end until the slot opens — a set-up, not a way to use up the shift.** A **cycle** is your line rotating around the low zone — corner, half-wall, behind the net — passing the puck short distances to each other while protecting it from the defence. The version you will use most is low-to-low: pass it backwards and down the boards to a teammate arriving behind you, then rotate up into the space they have just left. It works because the defenders are skating without the puck, cannot change lines, and shrink toward it as they tire — which opens the slot you want to arrive in. Four reads make it work:

- **Rim or reverse, and the checker tells you which.** If the checker is still in front of you, **rim** it — onward around the boards, past the player sealing you to the wall. If they have skated past you, **reverse** it — back against the flow, into the space they have just vacated. Picking the wrong one is a leading cycle turnover; **nobody has counted cycle turnovers by cause**, so take the ranking as coaching craft and the read itself as the point.
- **Bump it off the wall rather than onto a stick.** Fire the puck hard at a spot on the boards so it caroms to your teammate. A stick in the passing lane cannot block that, and a defender standing between you does not matter — but call it, because a bump nobody is expecting is a turnover.
- **Cut back when you run out of wall.** A defender who has committed their momentum toward the corner cannot change direction as fast as you can, so skating back up the boards you came down buys two or three seconds and usually opens the pass up to the point.
- **Scissor to catch a switch.** You and a supporting teammate skate at each other and cross, the support player going on the outside; you drop it to them or keep it. Defenders in the middle of swapping assignments are briefly covering nobody.

**And it has to end in something.** A cycle that does not finish in a shot from in front rather than the perimeter, a pass across the middle, a low-to-high with a screen, or a drive to the net has accomplished nothing — three tidy laps and no shot is the defence's result, not yours. [Offensive Zone Play](../systems/offensive_zone_play.md) owns the cycle, including when to stop cycling and attack the net instead.

**The same override applies here as in your own end: never turn your back to the checker, and never duck.** Turning your back makes the contact a hit from behind, and USA Hockey's warning to the player delivering one is that *"Even a light hit from behind could inflict a severe head or neck injury"*; ducking straightens the cervical spine, and USA Hockey records that the resulting injury can occur at walking speed. **Behind the net that second one matters twice over, because the goal post is right there** — USA Hockey names the post alongside the boards for exactly this collision. Skates parallel to the boards, forearm and hip into the contact — not the point of your shoulder, and not the whole shoulder if you can help it — head up and chin off your chest. [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md) covers the full three-case rule.

![](diagram:oz-behind-the-net)

**Goal:** Maintain possession under pressure and keep the offensive play alive.

**Key considerations:** Behind the net is a useful safe area. From there you can see the whole ice and make a decision without being at immediate risk of a direct shot or a dangerous turnover.

### Offensive Zone: Pressuring an Opponent with the Puck

```facts
Key: F1/F2/F3 are roles, not people — if a winger beats you to the puck they are F1 and you take the support role, immediately
Action: Be first in as F1 — forecheck through the middle at the opposing defenceman, or wherever the puck has gone
Technique: Drive at the carrier and take an angle, steering them to one side so your F2 knows where to support — steering is legal in every league; a push or a check may not be, so check whether you may check
Goal: Force a turnover or a bad outlet pass, and keep the opposing defence under constant pressure
Convention: The 2-1-2 with F3 on the weak side is one system among several, not a law — the stacked variant shades F3 to the puck side and concedes the far-side breakout, so find out which your team runs
Rule: Under NHL and IIHF rules the opposing goalie may play the puck behind the goal line only inside the trapezoid — the rules write one exception, skate contact with the crease; the IIHF Situation Handbook adds two more, at Situations 27.6 and 27.9 (NHL 27.8; IIHF 27.7)
Rule: USA Hockey, Hockey Canada and CARHA print no trapezoid, and the PWHL drops it in 1.8 and 27.8 though its 65.2(viii) keeps the minor, so their goalie can retrieve your dump-in and the dump-and-chase is worth much less — check your book, and whether your rink has the lines painted
Rule: Even with no trapezoid the forecheck still pays under USA Hockey — 614(c) bars their goalie freezing the puck outside the "goalkeeper's privileged" area, which excludes the corners, and 614(c)(1) bars it behind the goal line with the body entirely clear of the crease, so the puck has to move
Rule: Hockey Canada is blunter still — Interpretation 3 to Rule 10.1(a), clause v: "When a goaltender leaves their crease, they will not be allowed to freeze the puck. If they do, they will be assessed a Minor penalty. No warning will be issued."
Rule: The restricted area is IIHF Rule 27.7; nothing the Elite League publishes switches it off, but England Ice Hockey's Rules & Regulations 22.3 call it "not currently enforced" in its own competitions — in any British competition, plan for their goalie to come out
Action: Aim dump-ins away from the trapezoid so a defenceman must retrieve the puck with you arriving — how much rec and youth hockey marks one is a question about leagues, and this document has no count
```

**Where to be:** Forechecking through the middle — directly at the opposing defenseman or wherever the puck has gone in the opposition's defensive zone.

**What to do:** Be first on the forecheck (F1). Drive straight at the puck carrier, cutting off their options and forcing a quick decision. Take a deliberate angle — **steer** them toward one side (typically the strong-side boards) so your F2 winger knows where to support. ⚠️ **Steer is the verb the rulebooks use, and a push is a different act.** Hockey Canada's Interpretation 1 to Rule 7.3(a) lets a player *"steer or direct an opposing player into the boards, without actually touching or body-checking them"*. **In a non-checking classification the push is a penalty on its own** — a minor under Hockey Canada 7.3(a) and under the USA Hockey 604(c) Note — and neither minor is a ceiling: Hockey Canada 7.3(b) allows a major and game misconduct for a violent check and requires one if it injures, USA Hockey 604(d) requires one if it recklessly endangers, and both books reach a match penalty (7.3(c), 604(e)) — under USA Hockey plan on one for a reckless push, because Rule 602(a) says one *"shall be assessed"*. **And driving at a carrier from distance is how the books describe a charge**: Hockey Canada counts *"two or more strides immediately prior to making contact"* (7.4), USA Hockey *"more than two strides or … an excessive distance"* (607), and the NHL judges *"distance traveled"* (42.1) — so take the angle with your feet and arrive under control. **Before you plan a forecheck around contact, read [§May you check at all](#may-you-check-at-all-and-what-a-check-from-behind-costs) below** — the answer is your book and your classification rather than your coach.

**Goal:** Force a turnover or a bad outlet pass. Keep the opposing defence under constant pressure.

**Key considerations:** Forechecks are named by counting players from the goal line outwards — always three groups, always adding to five — so the common **2-1-2** is two forwards in on the puck, one in the middle, two defencemen high at the line. It is one system among several, not the only way. See [Forechecking Systems](../systems/forechecking_systems.md) for the alternatives (1-2-2, 1-3-1, 1-4) and when each is chosen.

In a 2-1-2:
- **F1 (usually you, as the center):** First in. Pressure the puck, take a deliberate angle.
- **F2 (the strong-side winger):** Second support on the strong side, sealing the puck wherever you've pushed it — usually the boards (corner) or behind the net.
- **F3 (the weak-side winger):** Stays higher to cover the breakout pass and any opposing forward leaking up the middle. **Which side F3 sits on is a genuine coaching split, not a law of hockey.** The weak-side default described here is the common one; in the **strong-side (stacked) variant** F3 shades to the puck side instead, so all three forwards overload one corner and the far-side breakout is conceded deliberately. Find out which your team runs — [Forechecking Systems](../systems/forechecking_systems.md) sets out both.

The defensemen hold the blue line at the points. Remember that these are **roles, not people**, defined by order of arrival rather than by position — if a winger beats you to the puck they are F1 and you take the support role, immediately, and without swapping back. [Language and Glossary](../foundation/language_and_glossary.md#f1-f2-and-f3) sets that out in full, including the part that catches people out: the numbering belongs to whichever team is forechecking, so on a breakout F1 is the opponent bearing down on you. Communicate clearly so the line moves as a unit and no one is left guessing.

![](diagram:forecheck-212)

**The trapezoid works in your favour here.** Under NHL and IIHF rules the opposing goalie may only play the puck behind their goal line inside the marked trapezoid, so they cannot come out to the corner and calmly move a dumped puck — a defenceman has to, with you arriving on them. That is exactly why dump-and-chase works, so aim your dump-ins away from the trapezoid. The rule writes one exception, a goalkeeper playing the puck *"while maintaining skate contact with"* the crease (NHL Rule 27.8; IIHF Rule 27.7). ⚠️ **The IIHF *Situation Handbook* adds two more, both narrow:** a puck stopped **on** the goal line is not in the restricted area (Situation 27.6), and a puck that merely hits a retreating goalkeeper is not one they played (27.9). **Neither moves where you aim** — they only tell you not to expect a whistle every time their goalie touches a puck out there.

**Check whether your league has a trapezoid at all.** USA Hockey, Hockey Canada and CARHA print none, and the PWHL switches it off (Rules 1.8 and 27.8) while its Rule 65.2(viii) still writes the minor, so that book contradicts itself. Against a goalie who can play the corner, dump-and-chase is worth much less. **How much rec, beer-league and youth hockey that covers is a question about leagues, and this document has no count for it**, so look behind the net and ask. **It is still worth something.** USA Hockey Rule 614(c) makes it a delay-of-game minor for a goalkeeper to fall on or gather the puck outside their privileged area — which excludes the corners — or behind the goal line with their body entirely outside the crease; Hockey Canada (Interpretation 3 to Rule 10.1(a), clause v) gives a minor, with no warning, to a goaltender who freezes the puck after leaving the crease. So their goalie may fetch your dump-in but may not sit on it — forecheck it as a puck-handler, not as a whistle. ⚠️ **But know where their cover is legal under USA Hockey**: in front of the goal line inside the privileged area (Casebook 614(c), Situation 12), and on a loose rebound outside the crease while you are pressuring them (Situation 11). **The whistle is the officials' call, not yours.** Play on through a cover you decided was illegal and you arrive into a goaltender: under USA Hockey a deliberate hit on one is charging, never two minutes alone (607(d) Note 1, 607(a)), and under Hockey Canada a charge on a goaltender is a mandatory major and game misconduct — set out under *Keep your body out of the crease* below. USA Hockey 607(d) adds that a goalkeeper is *"NOT 'fair game'"* outside the privileged area. Rule detail in [Rules Primer](../foundation/rules_primer.md).

**🇬🇧 In any British competition, plan for their goalie to come out for your dump-in.** The IIHF book carries the rule at 27.7 and nothing the Elite League publishes switches it off, but England Ice Hockey's *Rules & Regulations* 22.3 call the restricted area *"not currently enforced"* in its own competitions, and the In-House Rules never mention it — the published documents do not settle it, so the attacking team's safe assumption is a goalie who can play the corner. Ask your league, and treat an unmarked sheet as a hint rather than a ruling: IIHF 27.7 turns on *"the position of the puck"*, not on the markings.

### Offensive Zone: Without the Puck — Getting Open in the Slot

```facts
Position: In the slot between the two faceoff circles, just outside the crease — close enough to tip shots and pounce on rebounds
Technique: Work just outside the blue paint, off the red crease line rather than on it. Screen hard, keep your stick free for tips and keep your body out of the crease
Rule: Stay off the red crease line, not just the blue paint — every book that settles it counts that line as crease — IIHF 1.7, USA Hockey's 625(b) Note, Hockey Canada's Glossary, CARHA's Creases. The NHL's and PWHL's texts do not say; Table 14 in both, and the IIHF's (16 in 2025/26), allow a goal on it
Action: Screen the goaltender when your defenceman is about to shoot — plant, and be still at the moment of release, because shuffling gives the goalie flashes of the puck through the gaps
Never: Plant at the net front without knowing where the near post is — a defender walks you sideways by feel, not sight, with the post at one edge of that path and the goaltender at the other. Head up
Never: Duck away from the shot — a flexed neck in a head-first collision with a post can break your neck at walking speed. Chin off your chest, and meet a post with an arm or a leg, never head first
Never: Hold your spot into the goaltender — give ground against a goalie, even when it costs the screen
Goal: Be the primary scoring threat in the high-danger area and create chances for yourself and teammates
Key: Keep moving to get open, and slide with the point man as they walk the line — a stationary centre in the slot is easy to cover; the stillness belongs to the moment of release, not to the shift
```

**Where to be:** In the slot, between the two faceoff circles, just outside the crease (the painted area directly in front of the goalie). Stay close enough to tip shots and pounce on rebounds.

**What to do:** Get open in the slot. Look to position yourself for a shot, a tip-in, or a rebound. Screen the goaltender when your defenseman is about to shoot, and be still at the moment of release. Between shots, keep moving — a stationary center in the slot is easier to cover.

**The net front is three separate skills, not one place to stand.**

- **Screening.** Stand between the goalie's **eyes and the puck**, not between the goalie and the net — two different lines, and they move when the point man walks the line, so slide along with them. Chest up and square to the shooter, hips low and knees bent: the posture of a squat, not of a bow. Be still at the moment of release, because constant shuffling gives the goalie flashes of the puck through the gaps. **Track the puck, not the shot** — you are facing the shooter, so keep your eyes on the puck at their stick, ready to tip it, and do not turn to follow it toward the net. **Your eyes move; your chest does not.** Turning your body to follow the flight is what un-screens the goalie and takes you out of the tip and the rebound. Stand inside the faceoff dots, six to eight feet out from the goal line; the crease arc reaches six, so live at the far end of that range. ⚠️ **Know where the near post is before you plant, take the contact head up and chin off your chest, and never duck away from it** — everything above parks you still, six to eight feet out, with your eyes off the man beside you, so a defender walking you sideways is moving a body you are feeling rather than watching, along a path with a steel post at one edge and the goaltender at the other. The flexed neck is what turns that collision into a spinal injury, and USA Hockey's *Heads Up Hockey Program Guide* writes the goal post into that mechanism alongside the boards: *"almost all on-ice cervical spine injuries have been due to the head being slightly flexed (head down) while making head-on contact with the boards or goal post"*, and it *"can occur at walking speed"*. Meet a post with an arm or a leg, never your head first. **A box-out — legal or not in your book — walks you along the same path, so this is not a checking-league point.** **And the goaltender is the one body at the net front you never take that contact against — give ground rather than hold your spot into them.**
- **Tips.** **Blade on the ice, always** — the overwhelming majority of tippable pucks come in low, and a stick held off the ice at the net front is a stick doing nothing. You are redirecting, not shooting: ten to thirty degrees is enough to beat a set goalie, and a big swing usually misses the puck entirely. Angle the blade and absorb slightly rather than following through — hard hands send it into the glass. And present a target: hold the blade out in the lane you want the puck in, and shout for it.
- **Rebounds.** Decide to go **before** the shot rather than reacting after it. The defending team recovers roughly 60% of rebounds — Greg Revak's figure, read off a chart by Alex Novet that states no league, season or sample size — so take the direction and not the decimal. **Time the arrival by which job you have:** planted at the net front before the release if the net front is your assignment, and, if you are driving in from the wall or your own circle, arriving as the puck is released rather than well before it — go early and the defender has the time to establish position on you and turn you away from the puck. That split is coaching craft rather than a measured finding. When it comes out, shoot it straight back: do not settle it and do not look for a pass, because the window is a second or two while the goalie is still down. If you get tied up, go to the far post — nobody is covering it. Whichever way you arrive, arrive under control and around the goaltender, never into them.

[Offensive Zone Play](../systems/offensive_zone_play.md) owns all three, including the tip-height rules, which differ by book, and the limits on that 60%.

**Goal:** Be the primary scoring threat in the high-danger area and create chances for yourself and teammates.

**Key considerations:** The slot is the hardest area of the ice to defend — **coaching craft rather than a measured comparison** — and it is the most effective place to score from, which is the half with figures behind it: [Shooting](../technique/shooting.md) sets out the shot-location values, and on the coaching-consensus ranges it carries, a slot shot converts at roughly three to seven times a perimeter shot. Make the opposing center and defensemen work hard to keep you out of it. Movement and timing are your tools.

**When your defenceman pinches down the wall, somebody has to fill the point they left — and in many systems that is the highest forward, which is often you.** Read it as the puck goes low: if your defenceman steps in to keep the puck in the zone, slide up to the blue line on their side and play as a defenceman until they are back, rather than staying in the slot and leaving an odd-man rush going the other way. Some teams give that job to the weak-side winger or to whichever forward is F3, so ask your bench who covers the pinch.

#### Keep your body out of the crease — what it costs you differs by book

```facts
Rule: Screening from outside the crease without contact keeps the goal — NHL Table 14 and IIHF Table 14 (16 in 2025/26), Situations 5C and 5E, allow it. 69.1's positioning limb names no location for the attacker, so the tables decide it
Rule: No book voids a goal for a clean screen outside the crease on your body alone — for the NHL, IIHF and PWHL that rests on their tables, not rule text. USA Hockey writes no goalie-vision rule but counts your stick as you: Casebook 625 Sit. 10 voids it for a stick in the paint, goalie in the crease
Rule: Hockey Canada 8.5 is not flat — a teammate's goal from outside the crease stands if you stay passive in it, and is disallowed once you play the puck, interfere with the play or obstruct the goalie's view or movements; its two goalie-view references both reach only a player in the crease
Rule: Contact you initiate with a goalie in their crease kills the goal (NHL 69.3; IIHF 69.3 requires "a relevant contact"), and the NHL's own sentence signposts the exception — "(refer to Rule 69.7 for an exception)"
Rule: NHL and IIHF 69.7 and PWHL 71.7 allow a goal after "incidental contact" with the goalie on a rebound or a loose puck you both play, "whether inside or outside the crease". The EIHL Casebook disallows a goal for contact in the blue paint; Hockey Canada, USA Hockey and CARHA write no such carve-out
Never: Take your body inside the blue paint, or onto the red crease line — IIHF, USA Hockey, Hockey Canada and CARHA count that line as crease; the NHL does not say. Assume any contact there costs the goal
Never: Stay in the crease after being shoved in — get out, avoiding the goalie. NHL and IIHF 69.1 kill the goal for impairing position, no contact needed; USA Hockey 625(b) relieves position, not conduct
Rule: Being pushed, shoved or fouled into the goalie is not contact you initiated, "provided the attacking player has made a reasonable effort to avoid such contact" (NHL and IIHF Rule 69.1)
Rule: USA Hockey does not run 69.1's premise — 625(b) bars a goal with an attacking player in the crease whoever scored, unless the puck preceded them in, the goalie is out of it, or a defender's interference put them there; blocking a returning goalie is the goal off and a minor (Casebook Situation 9)
Rule: NHL and IIHF 69.4 protects a goalkeeper who has played the puck outside the crease and is then "prevented from returning" by "the deliberate actions of an attacking player" — a separate foul from 69.1's crease-position rule, turning on intent rather than on contact
Rule: Fouled into the goaltender, you can be penalised in every book — a mandatory minor under Hockey Canada 8.5(a) Interpretation 2 and CARHA 66(b) if you make no effort to avoid them, NHL, IIHF and PWHL tables without "a reasonable effort", USA Hockey's Casebook unless you "clearly made every attempt"
Rule: Hockey Canada's Interpretation 1 to 8.5(a) governs the goal rather than the penalty, and asks more of you — the goal stands only where the foul left you "unable to avoid contact with the goaltender", so it is no relief from that minor
Rule: Hockey Canada is not capped at a minor — 8.5(b) reaches a major plus game misconduct at the referee's discretion on the degree of violence of impact and mandatorily where the foul injures; 8.5(c) is a match penalty, and 8.5(d) leaves no rung between the minor and the ejection
Rule: Charging a goalie can eject in every book — mandatory in Hockey Canada 8.5(b), CARHA 52(b) in the crease or on injury, NHL, PWHL 42.5 and EIHL 42 if a major injures face or head, USA Hockey 607(b) if reckless, 607(c) has no bare minor in crease or privileged area; only the IIHF has no match penalty
```

You are in the goalie's kitchen more than anyone except a net-front winger, and playing too timidly here costs you goals. **Work just outside the blue paint, off the red crease line rather than on it. Screen hard, keep your stick free for tips, and keep your body out of the crease.** A screen from *outside* the crease without contact is legal and one of the most valuable things you do — but a goal is voided, with no contact needed, where an attacker who has entered the crease impairs the goalie's ability to move freely or defend the net (**NHL and IIHF Rule 69.1**). So keep your body out of the paint, and don't crowd the goalie so tightly they cannot move.

![](diagram:oz-net-front-screen)

> **The NHL's and the IIHF's own tables show where that line falls.** Rule 69 sends you to the *Interference on the Goalkeeper Situations* table — the NHL's **Reference Table 14**, and the IIHF's **Appendix IV, Table 16** in the 2025/26 book, which is **Table 14** in the 2026/27 book, so name the edition when you cite it. **Situation 5E** — an attacker who *"plants himself on the crease line or outside the goal crease"* to obstruct the goalkeeper's vision — and **Situation 5C**, a screen *"outside the crease"* that *"remains in motion"*, both answer **"Goal is allowed."** **Only 5B and 5D disallow the goal, and both put the attacker inside the crease** — so read those rows by **location**, not by whether you are moving.
>
> **The rule text alone does not draw that line, which is why the tables matter.** 69.1's positioning limb names no location for the attacker, and the sentences that do name one — 69.1's attacker who *"enters the goal crease"*, 69.3's who *"establishes a significant position within the goal crease"* — both need you in the paint. The IIHF's **Situation Handbook** agrees: its Situation 69.1 disallows the goal for a screener *"in the crease"*, and it publishes no situation disallowing one for a screen set outside it. The NHL publishes no comparable interpretation volume. **69.4 reaches further, and it is about a penalty:** a player whose *"deliberate actions"* keep a goalkeeper who has played the puck outside the crease from returning to it *"may be penalized"*, with no contact required. ⚠️ **Screening is not what that reaches; standing in the route a goaltender needs to get home is.** When their goalie comes out to play a puck, leave them their path back, and screen them once they are home.
>
> **One line genuinely is contested, and it is the one you stand nearest.** Situation 5E treats the red crease line as outside the crease, but **four books' wording counts it as crease**: IIHF Rule 1.7 — *"[t]he marked line belongs to the Goal Crease"* — USA Hockey's Note to Rule 625(b) and its Casebook (*The Rink*, Situation 4, which answers *"Yes."*), Hockey Canada's Glossary — *"The lines of the crease are considered part of the crease"* — and CARHA's definition of *Creases*. The NHL's own Rule 1.7 settles it neither way. [Rink Map](../foundation/rink_map.md) sets out what each book says. ⚠️ **So stand clear of the red line itself, not merely of the blue paint** — under USA Hockey a skate on the red is a skate in the crease, and 625(b) costs your teammate the goal on that alone, subject only to that rule's own carve-outs.
>
> **For a screen set from outside the crease, the other books reach only your stick.** USA Hockey writes no goaltender-vision provision at all (its 625(a)(1) *"protective screen"* is a screen set on a **checker**), Hockey Canada's two references to the goaltender's view both reach a player standing *in* the crease, and the **PWHL**'s Rule 71.3 keys its crease bar to the body. **Your stick is the exception, and it is USA Hockey's alone.** That book counts the stick as you — *Casebook* **Rule 625, Situation 10**: *"if a goal is scored while an attacking player has their stick and/or skates in the opponent's goal crease while the goalkeeper is in the crease, the Referee shall have no alternative but to disallow the goal."* ⚠️ **So the stick you are keeping free for a tip can cost the goal with both skates outside the line** in that book ([Language and Glossary](../foundation/language_and_glossary.md) sets this out under *the paint*).

Inside the blue paint, assume any contact costs you the goal — with one carve-out, and it covers exactly the rebound you are sent in to get. **NHL and IIHF Rule 69.7**, and the PWHL's 71.7, allow a goal after *"incidental contact"* with the goalkeeper *"In a rebound situation, or where a goalkeeper and attacking player(s) are simultaneously attempting to play a loose puck, whether inside or outside the crease"* — the NHL's wording — and the NHL's 69.3 signposts it inside the very sentence that disallows the goal, *"(refer to Rule 69.7 for an exception)"*. **Assume it does not survive into the EIHL.** The Motorpoint EIHL Casebook's addition at Rule 69 writes the test flat — *"Contact in the blue paint area calls for disallowing a goal"*, the last of three criteria it prints, after asking whether the contact was initiated by the defending side — and its interpretations supersede the IIHF's. ⚠️ **Do not carry it into a Hockey Canada, USA Hockey or CARHA game either — none of them writes a rebound carve-out.** Hockey Canada's Interpretation 1 to 8.5(a) disallows any goal where an attacker initiates contact with the goaltender *"regardless of whether the contact occurs inside or outside of the goal crease"*, its only exception a defender's foul; USA Hockey's Casebook at Rule 607, Situation 4 puts a player *"going hard to the goal"* who only then *"makes an honest attempt to avoid the goalkeeper"* at a minor plus misconduct. So play the rebound, and do not read 69.7 as room to lean on a goaltender who already has the puck.

**Being shoved in has one carve-out, and its limits matter as much as the carve-out does.** Under NHL and IIHF Rule 69.1, contact after you are *"pushed, shoved, or fouled"* into the goalkeeper is not contact you initiated, *"provided the attacking player has made a reasonable effort to avoid such contact."* The IIHF adds a sentence the NHL lacks: an attacker in the crease who *"does not leave immediately"* can have play stopped for a neutral-zone draw. **The reasonable-effort proviso is part of the carve-out** — pushed in and then making no attempt to get out of the way is not covered. And it decides only *who initiated the contact*, not 69.1's separate ground of impairing the goalie from inside the crease, so being shoved in is no licence to stay. Get out — *avoiding the goaltender*, because in every book read here an attacker fouled into a goalie can still be penalised for the contact. The test is *"a reasonable effort to avoid"* it in the NHL, IIHF and PWHL, *"clearly made every attempt"* under USA Hockey, and *"an effort"* under Hockey Canada or an *"attempt"* under CARHA, both of which make the minor mandatory where there was none.

**And price contact by the contact, not by the scoreboard.** Going through a goaltender is a foul whether or not the puck goes in, and whether or not either of you is in the crease. **NHL and IIHF Rule 69.2** give a minor or major for *"intentional or deliberate"* contact *"whether or not the goalkeeper is inside or outside the goal crease, and whether or not a goal is scored"*. **CARHA Rule 66(b)** writes a minor *"shall be imposed"* for deliberate contact with a goalkeeper *"whether in or out of the goal crease"*, with a major at the referee's discretion — and that major puts you out of the game (Rule 30(a)) with at least a one-game suspension (Rule 32(d)). ⚠️ **Make that shove with your stick shaft on a goaltender in the crease and CARHA gives the referee no choice:** its **Rule 54(c)** makes it a major and game misconduct, as 52(b) does for a charge there. **Nor does the contact have to be deliberate:** a goalie outside the crease is not *"fair game"*, and unnecessary contact with the goalkeeper is penalised in every book — NHL and IIHF 69.4, PWHL 71.4, Hockey Canada 8.5 (*"anywhere on the ice"*), the Note to CARHA 52(b), and USA Hockey 607(d). **Under USA Hockey a deliberate shove is charging, not interference** (Note 1 to 607(d)) — a minor plus a misconduct or a major plus a game misconduct (607(a) and (c)). **A reckless charge leaves the referee no choice** — 607(b) makes the major and game misconduct mandatory, 607(e) adds that a match penalty may also be assessed, and plan on one, because USA Hockey's general **Rule 602(a)** says one *"shall be assessed"* to any player who *"recklessly endangers or attempts to injure"* an opponent.

> **Under USA Hockey the governing rule is not 69.1, and it cuts both ways.** **Rule 625(b)** is a flat position rule — *"No goal may be scored with an attacking player in the goal crease unless the puck has preceded the player(s) into the goal crease or the goalkeeper is out of the goal crease area"* — which is **stricter** than the NHL's impairment test, and merely standing in the crease can stop play for a neutral-zone draw. But its push-in relief is **broader**: where a defender physically interferes with you and that puts you in the crease, *"any legal goal scored shall be allowed"*, with no reasonable-effort proviso.
>
> **Do not read "the goalkeeper is out of the goal crease area" as a licence.** The **USA Hockey Casebook 2025-29**, **Rule 625, Situation 9**, lets you precede the puck into the crease while the goalkeeper is completely out of it — and then takes it straight back: *"if the goalkeeper is attempting to return to the goal crease and there is any interference by the attacking player who prevents the goalkeeper from making a play, then the goal must be disallowed and an interference penalty shall be assessed."* So ⚠️ **never put your body between a scrambling goaltender and their net**: that is the goal off **and** two minutes for you. **And the push-in relief covers your position, not what you do next** — 617(c)(4) also disallows the goal for an attacker *"interfering with the goalkeeper"*, and **625(a)(8)** remains a minor for accidental or unavoidable contact that interferes with the goalie's movement; deliberate contact is charging, as above.
>
> **Hockey Canada's crease bar is not flat, and the difference lands on a play you will be standing in.** Rule 8.5 bars an attacker from standing in the crease unless the puck is there, then carves out the net-front case: if you are in the crease without interfering and a teammate *outside* it scores, *"the goal WILL BE ALLOWED provided that the player who was in the goal crease does not attempt to play the puck, interfere with the play or obstruct the Goaltender's view or movements."* **So on that play USA Hockey and Hockey Canada give opposite answers.** None of it changes the instruction: keep your feet out of the blue.
>
> **Make the effort to avoid the goaltender, whatever put you there.** ⚠️ **Every book read here writes failing to do so as a penalty on you.** The NHL's, IIHF's and PWHL's tables penalise an attacker who *"did not make a reasonable effort to avoid such contact"*; USA Hockey's Casebook (Rule 607, Situation 4) spares a player forced into the goaltender only where they *"clearly made every attempt to avoid the contact"*; and Hockey Canada's Interpretation 2 to Rule 8.5(a) says of a player tripped or cross-checked into the goaltender, *"If the player does not make an effort to avoid contact ... they must be penalized for interference with the goaltender"* — and the referee *should* penalise the defender too. **CARHA's Rule 66(b) writes the same minor twice**, for an attacker who fails to attempt to avoid the goalkeeper whether or not their team has the puck — and with possession, the goal is disallowed as well. ⚠️ **Do not read that as your goal surviving the other way**: an earlier paragraph of the same rule disallows the goal where an attacker *"has physically interfered with the goalkeeper, prior to or during the scoring of the goal"*. **The test asks for an *effort*, not a result.**
>
> **Hockey Canada's relief answers a different question — the goal, not the penalty.** Interpretation 1 to 8.5(a) lets the goal stand only where a defender's foul left you **unable** to avoid the goalie; the minor is escaped by **trying** to — a lower bar, in a separate limb. ⚠️ **Interpretation 1 does not relieve you of the minor**, and being fouled first does not either.
>
> **And Hockey Canada does not cap this rule at two minutes.** **8.5(a)** is the minor. **8.5(b)** reaches a major and game misconduct at the referee's discretion on the violence of the impact, and **mandatorily** for any player who *"charges the goaltender"* (its Note 1 sends you to Rule 7.4 for what counts as charging) or who *"injures an opponent"* by an interference that would otherwise be a minor. **8.5(c)** is a **match penalty** for attempting to injure or deliberately injuring a goaltender by interference. **8.5(e)** makes the game misconduct automatic on any 8.5(b) major, and **8.5(d)** rules out a misconduct, so there is no ten-minute rung between two minutes and the ejection. ⚠️ **For a centre working the slot: arriving into the goaltender is an ejection under this book, and a two-minute foul becomes one the moment it injures someone.**
>
> **Do not file that ejection under "Canadian rules" —** ⚠️ **charging a goaltender reaches an ejection tier in all six books.** **CARHA Rule 52(b)** makes a major and game misconduct mandatory for charging a goalkeeper *"within the goal crease"*, or injuring an opponent with a charge. The **NHL**'s and the **PWHL**'s **Rule 42.1** allow a minor, a major, or a major and game misconduct for charging a goalkeeper in the crease, and their **42.5** makes the game misconduct mandatory once a charging major injures the face or head; the **Elite League** Casebook's Rule 42 adds the same face-or-head limb to the IIHF book. **IIHF Rule 42.1** penalises the charge without naming tiers, and its **42.4** reaches a major and game misconduct for a charge that *"recklessly endangers"*, no injury required. **USA Hockey writes no bare minor for it**: **607(c)** is a minor plus a misconduct or a major plus a game misconduct for body checking or charging a goalkeeper in the crease or privileged area, and **607(b)** makes the major and game misconduct mandatory for a reckless charge.
>
> **And every book but the IIHF's** ⚠️ **goes on to a match penalty for charging.** NHL and PWHL **42.4** allow one where the player attempted to or deliberately injured the opponent, and their **Rule 21.1** imposes one for a deliberate injury *"in any manner"*; USA Hockey **607(e)** allows one, and **602(a)** says plan on it for reckless endangerment; Hockey Canada **7.4(c)** assesses one for a charge on a player unable to defend themselves or meant to injure; and CARHA's general **Rule 48(a)** reaches a charge meant to injure. The IIHF book has no match penalty at all — the phrase appears in no edition checked here — and its severest tier for charging is 42.4's major and game misconduct, with supplementary discipline under 42.5. *(Of the injury limbs, Hockey Canada writes two — 8.5(b), and its charging rule's own **7.4(b)**, where a charge that would otherwise be a minor injures a player — and CARHA's sits in 52(b); the NHL's and PWHL's 42.5 and the Elite League's Rule 42 reach only an injury to the face or head on a major, and the IIHF's Rule 42 and USA Hockey's Rule 607 write none.)*
>
> **Hit a goaltender from behind** ⚠️ **and the IIHF takes the two-minute option away.** Its **Rule 43.2** says *"there is no option to award a minor penalty"* for checking from behind, and the game misconduct **43.3** adds to the major is *"automatic"*. 43.1 names the goal frame — but **what puts you under that rule is where your contact lands**, on the back of a Player unable to protect themselves, so a goaltender facing you is not one you are behind. A charge that reaches the head goes to Rule 48 instead (42.1), which keeps a minor available; a hit from behind stays under 43. ⚠️ **None of this lifts the ceiling** — a major plus a game misconduct is still the most this book can do to you inside the game, and **20.4** ejects you on a second major of any kind in one game. *(That Rule 43 reaches a goaltender is a reading of how the book is assembled rather than a sentence the IIHF prints: **Rule 5.1** makes "Player" cover goalkeepers and 43.1 names the goal frame, but its Appendix II glossary defines a vulnerable position for "a skater", and its Situation Handbook prints no Situation under Rule 43.)*

The full rules are in [Rules Primer](../foundation/rules_primer.md) and [Offensive Zone Play](../systems/offensive_zone_play.md); the same situation from the goalie's side of it is in [Goaltender](goaltender.md).

## Other Situations

### Faceoffs — a defining responsibility of the center

```facts
Priority: Ten or fifteen draws a game matter enormously — your own end, special teams, the last minute, and after an icing; the count is a coaching estimate, not a measurement
Read: Own end — win it back to your defenders, ideally the boards-side one for a quick rim out; their end — to a defender for a one-timer, or back to a winger in the circle
Action: If you can't win it clean, lift or press their stick with yours — stick, not hands — then play the puck so your wingers can pounce; never hook it, hold it down or grab it with your hand
Action: Warn your wingers that their encroachment takes you off the draw, not them — true in all six books, and avoidable; under USA Hockey, Hockey Canada and CARHA their stick counts too
Rule: The draw after an icing is the faceoff-violation exception in four of the six books — the first violation is a warning rather than an ejection (NHL and IIHF 2026/27 Rule 76.4, PWHL 78.4; Hockey Canada Rule 6.5(a), Junior divisions only; neither USA Hockey nor CARHA writes such a carve-out)
Rule: Across the Home Countries the IHUK In-House Rules 2026-27 settle faceoff violations without the edition question — "the linesperson shall immediately replace the centre for all violations", face-offs following icings excepted (Section 10, Rule 76)
Rule: Pre-drop contact by a player facing off is a minor — discretionary under NHL and IIHF 76.7 and PWHL 78.7, mandatory under USA Hockey 613(e), Hockey Canada 6.2(c) and CARHA 57(b); the IIHF 2026/27 book replaces the centre too
Rule: A second violation on one draw is a bench minor under NHL and IIHF 76.7, PWHL 78.7, a minor on the offender under USA Hockey 613(d), and discretionary under Hockey Canada 6.2(e) and CARHA 57(d) — though Hockey Canada makes it automatic if the offender then contacts a linesperson clearing the dot
Never: Push your head over the dot — get low with knees and hips. Helmet to helmet before the drop is pre-drop contact in all six books; the 2026/27 IIHF handbook takes the centre responsible off the draw
Rule: The ninth spot is centre ice, where the visitor's blade goes down first; at the other eight, the defending centre (NHL and IIHF 76.4, PWHL 78.4). USA Hockey 613(a) reverses it to the attacker at all but centre ice, Hockey Canada 6.2(a) puts the visitor first at all nine, CARHA 57 writes no order
Rule: ⚠️ **Across the Home Countries there is no stick-down order** — IHUK scraps it ("Either centre can now put their stick down first for a face-off") and wants "the whole of their stick blades (not toe or heel)" in the white; CARHA 57(a) wants "the full blade of their sticks flat on the ice"
Rule: In the Elite League the stick-down order still binds — the In-House Rules never name the EIHL and the Casebook writes no Rule 76, so IIHF 76.4 governs: at the eight outer spots the defending centre puts their stick down first, the visiting centre at centre ice
```

You are the primary faceoff taker, and **most draws barely matter.** An NHL game has **roughly 56** — 56.6 per game in 2024-25 and 56.0 in 2025-26, **computed from the NHL's official statistics** — and team faceoff win percentage explains only about **6% of the variation** in a team's points percentage across eleven NHL seasons (r = +0.25); in 2016-17 the relationship was *negative*. **Ten to fifteen draws a game matter enormously** — a coaching estimate, not a counted figure: the draws in your own end, on special teams, in the last minute, and after an icing. Prepare for those. [Faceoffs](../systems/faceoffs.md) carries all these figures with their limits. Key principles by location:

- **Defensive zone:** Win the puck back to your defenders — ideally to the boards-side defender (positioned near the boards-side hash marks supporting your outer winger) for a quick rim out. If you can't win clean, lift or press your opponent's stick with your own — the stick, never their hands — then play the puck so your wingers can pounce on it — never hook it, hold it down, or grab it with your hand. A clean loss in your defensive zone puts a set opponent running a prepared play into the slot while your five are stationary and facing the wrong way. **And if you lose it, stay with their centre** — tie up his stick if you can, then go with him toward the slot rather than chasing the puck. That is the usual assignment off a defensive-zone draw, though some teams hand the centre a different man, so ask yours.
- **Offensive zone:** Win to a defender for a one-timer, or back to a winger in the circle for a quick chance. Read your linemates' positioning before the puck drops.
- **Neutral zone:** Win possession cleanly if you can; otherwise, a tie-up your wingers can chase down is fine too.

**General:** Study your opponent's tendencies (most centers favour one hand or one technique). Vary your own approach. **Technique, grip, the five-player alignments and set plays are all in [Faceoffs](../systems/faceoffs.md)**, which is the authority; the procedure and the violations as written are in [Rules Primer](../foundation/rules_primer.md).

![](diagram:faceoff-dzone-alignment)

**If you have just been moved here from the wing, this is the skill you have never taken seriously** — [Switching Positions](switching_positions.md) sets out what a new centre should work on first, and in what order.

**Staying in the dot — the part that is uniquely your problem.** Warn your wingers before every draw that matters, be set when the linesperson is ready, and never time your arrival to beat the clock — ⚠️ **a violation usually costs *you* the draw, whoever made it.**

**A linemate's violation takes you off the draw in all six books.** Hockey Canada's Rule 6.2(d) ejects the taker for any face-off violation; USA Hockey 613(d), CARHA 57(a), the NHL, the PWHL (Rule 78.6) and the IIHF's 2026/27 book (Rule 76.6 — its 2025/26 book only warned) each eject the offending team's taker when somebody else offends. **Your own failure to take position works differently.** The NHL, the IIHF 2026/27 book and the PWHL make replacement mandatory for a centre *"not positioned for the face-off"* (76.3(i), 76.3(I), 78.3(i)), and Hockey Canada's 6.2(d) ejects you for any violation; only USA Hockey (613(d)) and CARHA (57(c)) leave it to the official's choice — so plan on losing the dot. ⚠️ **Timing your arrival to game the five seconds** is a mandatory removal under NHL 76.4, PWHL 78.4 and IIHF 2026/27 76.4, except on the draw after an icing. **A premature move** has the NHL draw *"conducted again"* rather than ejecting you, CARHA writes no premature-movement violation at all, and whether the NHL removes a centre who is set and then twitches is not written down — **do not plan on either.** 🇬🇧 **Under Britain's In-House Rules 2026-27** the linesperson *"shall immediately replace the centre for all violations"* except on the draw after an icing — those rules are the *"overriding interpretation of how the IIHF Rule Book is to be applied domestically"* — so there you do lose the dot for a premature move.

**A second violation on the same draw** is a **bench** minor under the NHL, the IIHF and the PWHL (Rule 76.7; PWHL 78.7), and IHUK does not amend that. ⚠️ **The other three books route it elsewhere, so do not tell a replacement taker it is automatically two minutes off your bench.** USA Hockey 613(d) gives the minor to the player who commits the second violation; Hockey Canada 6.2(e) and CARHA 57(d) make it discretionary, CARHA's reaching only Section (a) violations — which include a linemate's physical contact with an opponent, as well as moving offside and encroaching. ⚠️ **Hockey Canada's discretion runs out in one case:** if the offender then contacts the linesperson before they have had reasonable time to clear the circle, the minor *"will be assessed"* (Rule 6.2(e)). So give the official room. *(Only IIHF Rules 76.3, 76.4, 76.6 and 76.7 have been read in the 2026/27 edition, and the In-House Rules apply in England, Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland and say nothing about the Elite League; [Faceoffs](../systems/faceoffs.md) carries the full comparison and the scope of both readings.)*

**Pre-drop contact with the other centre adds a minor.** It is the referee's choice under NHL and IIHF 76.7 and PWHL 78.7, where the linesperson also replaces you (the IIHF's 2025/26 edition warned instead); it is mandatory — *shall be assessed*, *will assess*, *shall assess* — under USA Hockey 613(e), Hockey Canada 6.2(c) and CARHA 57(b). ⚠️ **Do not rank those three against each other: under any of them, plan on the penalty.** So under the NHL, the IIHF and the PWHL, and in Britain, that contact **can** cost you the draw and two minutes; under USA Hockey, Hockey Canada and CARHA it **does**.

**The exception is on the draw you care most about — after an icing.** NHL Rule 76.4: *"When an icing infraction occurs, the first face-off violation by either team will not result in the center being removed."* The linesperson warns the team instead, any later violation by that team is a bench minor, and the warning is per team — one free attempt each. The IIHF's 2026/27 book and the PWHL's Rule 78.4 carry the same carve-out, and the IHUK In-House Rules except the post-icing draw too. **Hockey Canada gives it in Junior divisions only** (Rule 6.5(a)), and there it is wider, reaching *"any face-off violation, including but not limited to late arrival, encroachment, or early movement"*, with a later violation by the same team a minor (6.5(b)). **Outside Junior, Hockey Canada gives you nothing after an icing, and USA Hockey and CARHA give you nothing at all.** Read it as one attempt, not a free draw. Full detail in [Faceoffs](../systems/faceoffs.md).

So:

- **Which centre places his stick first depends on the book.** Under the NHL, the IIHF (Rule 76.4, unchanged in 2026/27) and the PWHL (78.4), **the defending centre puts his blade down first** at eight of the nine spots, and the **visiting** centre at centre ice — so you go first when you are defending that end. ⚠️ **USA Hockey reverses it** — Rule 613(a): *"The attacking player shall be the first to place their stick on the ice, except for a center ice face-off where the visiting team player shall be first to place their stick on the ice."* How much amateur hockey runs on that book is a question about leagues, and this document has no count for it. **Hockey Canada** puts the **visiting** player first at every spot (Rule 6.2(a) and Interpretation 1), and then relaxes it: *"[i]f the Home Team player wishes to put their stick down first, they are allowed to do so, if it does not cause a problem. If there is a problem, then apply the rule strictly."* ⚠️ **CARHA writes no order at all** — its Rule 57 was read in full. Find out which book you are in before you build a habit on it — [Faceoffs](../systems/faceoffs.md) covers this.

  **Under IHUK's In-House Rules none of those answers is yours.** The 2026-27 rules amend Rule 76: *"Either centre can now put their stick down first for a face-off."* So there the order tells you nothing. They also want both centres squared up *"with the whole of their stick blades (not toe or heel) in the white portion of the face-off circle"*, where IIHF Rule 76.7 requires only *"at least the toe of the blade of the stick"* in the white. ⚠️ **Put the whole blade down.** Both are British departures reaching games played *"in England, Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland"*; the Elite League is not named. CARHA Rule 57(a) also has both centres stand *"with the full blade of their sticks flat on the ice"*, so a CARHA centre is under the same instruction by a different route.
- **Your wingers can get you thrown out and never know it.** If a linemate creeps inside the circle or over the hash marks before the drop, **you** are the one ejected. Tell them what counts, because it differs by book. The **NHL**, the **IIHF** and the **PWHL** let a non-taker's stick inside the hash marks *"provided there is no physical contact with his opponent or his opponent's stick"* (NHL 76.7(ii); IIHF 76.7(II); PWHL 78.7(ii)). **USA Hockey, Hockey Canada and CARHA do not**: USA Hockey's Casebook (Rule 613, Situation 8) says *"Both players must be completely behind their respective hash marks. This includes all sticks and all skates"*, and Hockey Canada 6.2(a) and CARHA 57(a) require all other players to position *"themselves and their sticks on side"*. So in three books of the six a winger's blade ejects you as surely as their skate. It is one of the most avoidable ways a centre loses a draw — coaching judgement, not a counted ranking.
- **Get your depth from your knees, not by leaning your head in.** Low is right — you react faster and cannot be pushed off the dot — but bend your knees and drop your hips rather than pushing your head over the dot, or two centres finish helmet to helmet. **IIHF Rule 76.7(III) makes that a faceoff violation**, and its 2026/27 edition dropped the first-violation warning. The **IIHF Situation Handbook 2026/27** has the linesperson *"immediately eject the center that is responsible for the helmet to helmet contact"*, or *"eject both centers"* where they cannot tell (Situation 76.43), and a centre who will not move back from *"side by side, almost ear to ear"* is *"removed from the face-off"* (76.42) — the 2025/26 handbook said warn. The referee's minor sits on top: discretionary under NHL and IIHF 76.7, **mandatory under USA Hockey 613(e), Hockey Canada 6.2(c) and CARHA 57(b)**. **Move back when you are told to.**
- **Lift or press their stick with your own — the stick, never their hands — then play the puck; never hook it, hold it down or grab it with your hand.** ⚠️ **Catching a puck out of the air is not the offence** — no book penalises it provided you knock it straight down (NHL and IIHF **Rule 67.2**; USA Hockey's permission is a casebook interpretation rather than rule text) — but closing your hand on it to conceal it, or smothering it under your body, is a **minor penalty**. USA Hockey's one exception is a **caught** puck you fail to drop outside the crease, a stoppage and faceoff under its 618(a); covering a loose puck with your hand is still a minor there. Inside your own goal crease every book escalates it to a **penalty shot** — under USA Hockey a penalty shot or optional minor. The section below sets out both limbs, and the one restriction that falls on you and nobody else at the dot.

**When you get tossed:** a winger steps in. Agree in advance who takes it and what you are trying to do on that draw — usually a safe tie-up rather than anything clever. ⚠️ **Agree two names, because under Hockey Canada the player who caused the violation cannot be the replacement** (Rule 6.2(d)), and no change of players is allowed unless a penalty makes a team shorthanded. **So the replacement comes from the teammates already on the ice — not the goaltender, and not the one who put you out.** CARHA's **Rule 57(c)** writes the same no-substitution limb. [Faceoffs](../systems/faceoffs.md) carries the book-by-book position.

### Handling the puck — catching it is free, holding it is not

```facts
Rule: Catching the puck out of the air is not penalised in any of the six books — NHL, IIHF and PWHL (67.2 and 69.2) ask only that you "immediately place it or knock it down to the ice"; Hockey Canada 10.2(a)(iii) and CARHA Rule 61(a) exempt an immediate drop; USA Hockey's permission is casebook only
Never: Hold a puck you have caught, carry it, or drop it somewhere better. Knock it straight down where you caught it instead
Rule: At a live draw, though, the two centres may not play the puck with their hand at all — the note to NHL and IIHF 67.2 bars it "until such time as a third player (from either team) has at least touched the puck", and the PWHL carries the same note at 69.2
Rule: Closing your hand on the puck — at a draw or anywhere else — is a minor under the NHL and IIHF (67.2), and a penalty shot rather than a minor where you do it in your own goal crease (the note to 67.2(ii), and 67.4)
Rule: Hockey Canada 10.2(a)(iii) makes a closed hand a minor only if it "gains an advantage", and 10.2(a)(v) escalates to a penalty shot "when the infraction occurs inside the offending team's goal crease" — words that locate the infraction, not the puck; 4.11(a)(v), which it cites, locates the puck
Rule: Smothering the puck under your body is a minor under NHL 63.2(v), USA Hockey 614(a), Hockey Canada 10.2(a)(iv) and CARHA Rule 58(a), and escalates inside your own crease to a penalty shot — or, under USA Hockey 614(b), a penalty shot or optional minor. Neither limb is a faceoff violation
Rule: USA Hockey 618(a) makes a caught puck not dropped outside the crease a stoppage, not a penalty — but covering a loose puck with your hand is a minor (614(a) Note), and in the crease it is a penalty shot or optional minor, the other team's election (406(a))
Rule: With your net empty a hand closed on a puck in the crease is an awarded goal too, not only a body smother — USA Hockey through Rule 618(a) and Rule 614(b), but only where it prevented an obvious and imminent goal; the NHL, IIHF and PWHL through the note to Rule 67.2 or 69.2 and then 67.5 or 69.5
Rule: A skater other than the goaltender who covers a puck itself in the crease concedes a penalty shot — in five books the puck's location decides, not yours (NHL 67.4 and 63.6; IIHF 63.6; PWHL 65.6; CARHA Rule 58(c); USA Hockey 614(b))
Rule: With your own net empty, a skater covering a puck itself in the crease concedes an awarded goal, not a penalty shot (NHL and IIHF 67.5; PWHL 69.5; Hockey Canada 10.2(a)(vi); CARHA 58(c) Note 1, read flat; USA Hockey 614(b) only where it prevented a goal)
Rule: Hockey Canada gives a penalty shot for a puck in your crease (4.11(a)(v)), a goal with your net empty (10.2(a)(vi)); 10.2(a)(v) also names an infraction inside it, so treat a puck just outside covered while you stand in it as a possible shot or goal
```

**Catching the puck out of the air is not the offence, and five of the six books say so.** NHL and IIHF **Rule 67.2**, and the PWHL's 69.2: *"A player shall be permitted to catch the puck out of the air but must immediately place it or knock it down to the ice."* Hockey Canada's **10.2(a)(iii)** exempts a player who closes their hand on the puck *"to catch or stop the puck and immediately drops it to the ice without gaining or attempting to gain an advantage"*, and **CARHA Rule 61(a)** lets play continue in the same case. ⚠️ **USA Hockey's playing rules print no permission for a skater to catch the puck** — it is in the Casebook, Situation 1 to Rule 618: *"[p]rovided the player catches the puck and drops it to their stick in one continuous motion, play should be allowed to continue."* So under that book it is a casebook interpretation rather than rule text — the same permission in practice, not the same kind of evidence. Its **618(a)** makes a hand not immediately opened **outside the crease** a stoppage and last-play faceoff, not a penalty. ⚠️ **That exemption is for a puck you caught**: covering a loose puck on the ice with your hand is a minor under USA Hockey (the Note to 614(a); Casebook Situation 1 to Rule 614), and **inside the crease** a closed hand is a **penalty shot or optional minor** (618(a), 614(b)). So a puck dropping out of the air in front of you is not something you have to leave alone.

**The word carrying all six is "immediately".** Knock it straight down where you caught it. Carrying it, or dropping it somewhere more convenient, is not what any of them permitted — the IIHF's Situation Handbook works exactly that case: a defender whose own net is empty grabs the puck *"out of mid-air (around the height of the waist)"* and drops it **outside** the crease, and the answer is *"an Awarded Goal to Team B"* (Situation 67.6). Relocating the puck does not cure it. (The Handbook is a set of worked applications rather than rule text, and says so itself: *"in case of any contradictions, the valid IIHF Rulebook is always the basis."*)

**And the crease is a volume, not a patch of ice.** Every book runs it from the ice up to the level of the top of the goal frame — NHL and PWHL **1.7**, Hockey Canada **1.5(c)**, CARHA **Rule 5(c)**, and the Note to USA Hockey **Rule 625(b)**; the IIHF writes the same height in its own words at its own **1.7**, where the crease area *"extends vertically until the top of the crossbar."* ⚠️ **So a puck you catch out of the air above your own paint, below the bar, is a puck in the crease** — the height you took it at buys you nothing. Knock it straight down and play it with your skate or your blade.

![](diagram:the-puck-decides-not-you)

**And the permission is suspended for you, and only for you, while a draw is live.** The note to NHL and IIHF 67.2 says the same thing, each in its own house style, and the **PWHL** carries it at **69.2**. In the NHL's words: *"The two players involved in the actual face-off (the centers) are not permitted to play the puck with their hand without incurring a penalty under this rule until such time as a third player (from either team) has at least touched the puck."* The IIHF's is that sentence with a capitalised *"Players"* for the pair, a capitalised *"Player"* for the third, and *"face-off"* in quotation marks. ⚠️ **Between the drop and someone else touching the puck, your hand is out of it** — a restriction that falls on the two centres and on nobody else on the ice. **No equivalent was found in USA Hockey, Hockey Canada or CARHA.** Rule 613, Rule 6.2 and CARHA's Rules 57 and 61 were read for one; then all four volumes — USA Hockey's playing rules, its separate *Casebook*, the Hockey Canada book and CARHA's — were searched in full, including across line breaks, for *"third player"*, *"until such time"* and *"has at least touched"*, and for every face-off passage mentioning a hand. **Every hit in those books is something else** — a third player penalised while two teammates are already serving, a third man into a fight, a hand pass. **That is a search result and not an established absence**: a counterpart worded without any of those terms would still have escaped it.

**Now the price, and it is steeper than a faceoff violation.** Closing a hand over the puck to conceal it, or smothering it with your body, is a **minor penalty**. Inside your own crease it is a **penalty shot** (NHL Rule 67.4) — and **it is the puck that has to be in the crease, not you.** NHL and IIHF **Rule 63.6** make *"the location of the puck at the time it was held, grabbed or gathered into the body"* the test, and a puck outside the crease gathered in by a player inside it is a minor. The PWHL's 65.6 carries the same test, and **CARHA Rules 58(c) and 61(c)** award the shot for a puck *"within their goal crease"*. **Hockey Canada asks where the puck is too** (Rule 4.11(a)(v)), and rules that a player standing outside the crease who scoops the puck out and then covers it draws a minor, not a shot (Interpretation 5 to Rule 10.2(a)(v)). ⚠️ **But no Hockey Canada text rules on a puck outside the paint gathered in by a player standing inside it, and 10.2(a)(v) says only *"when the infraction occurs inside the offending team's goal crease"* — so treat that case as a possible penalty shot, and with your net empty a possible goal.** **USA Hockey does not make the shot automatic:** 614(b) reads *"a penalty shot/optional minor penalty"*, the non-offending team choosing (Rule 406(a)), and *"the decision as to whether the puck is in the crease is made at the moment the infraction occurs."*

**With your own net empty it is worse still.** If your goaltender has been pulled for an extra attacker, the falls-on-puck limb of NHL and IIHF **67.5**, and the PWHL's **69.5**, award the goal outright; USA Hockey **614(b)** does it where the act *"has prevented an obvious and imminent goal"*; and **Hockey Canada 10.2(a)(vi)** awards it for the infraction inside the crease with the goaltender substituted, routed through 4.11(a)(v) so that it too turns on where the puck is (Rule 4.12(a)). ⚠️ **CARHA looks flat here and is not.** Its Note 1 to 58(c) awards the goal whenever the goalkeeper has been removed, while the **Rule 36(b)** it points to carries USA Hockey's *"obvious and imminent goal"* condition and also wants a shot to have been taken. **Play the flat Note: with your own net empty, never cover a puck in your crease with your hand or your body.**

**And the body is priced separately from the hand.** Deliberately falling on or gathering the puck into your body is a minor under USA Hockey **614(a)** too, so the body smother is a penalty under every book — CARHA's is **Rule 58(a)**, for any player *"except a goalkeeper"*.

### Power Play (your team has the man advantage)

```facts
Position: In the 1-3-1 you most often play the bumper — the middle of the three across the slot, between the point man and the net front
Action: Be a passing target for both half-walls, take quick shots from the slot, screen the goalie and tip point shots
Key: A box has nobody assigned to the middle, which is exactly why a kill switches to a diamond against you
Position: In an umbrella or an overload you will usually be low or on the strong-side wall — there is no bumper in a true umbrella
Action: Drop back to receive the breakout pass and carry through the neutral zone, then transition into the bumper or slot
Never: Skate straight into the opposing defence, or dump into traffic. Enter with control instead, carrying into space or passing in
Key: Passing in is an alternative to carrying in, not an upgrade on it — the tracked figures have the two about level on the power play and carrying ahead at even strength; Zone Entries covers them
```

**The formations themselves — 1-3-1, umbrella, overload, spread, 2-3 — are set out in [Special Teams](../systems/special_teams.md).** What matters here is where they put you.

In the **1-3-1**, widely described as the modern default, the centre most often plays the **bumper**: the middle of the three across the slot, sitting in the soft spot between the single point man and the net-front player. Your job is to be a passing target for both half-walls, take quick shots from the slot, screen the goalie, and tip point shots. The bumper is the hardest player for a penalty kill to cover, because a **box** has nobody assigned to the middle — which is exactly why a kill switches to a **diamond** against you.

![](diagram:pp-131)

In an **umbrella** or an **overload** you will usually be low or on the strong-side wall instead. There is no bumper in a true umbrella, so don't go looking for the spot you're used to.

**On zone entries:** You often drop back to receive the breakout pass and become the puck carrier through the neutral zone (using drop passes or swing-entries with the wingers). Once in the offensive zone, you transition into the bumper or slot position. Avoid skating straight into the opposing defence, and avoid dumping the puck into traffic. Both hand the puck over; what you want is a **controlled entry** — carried into space, or passed in across the line.

**Passing in is an alternative to carrying in, not an upgrade on it.** The tracked figures put pass-ins and carry-ins about level on the power play and carrying clearly ahead at even strength, so pass in because the passing lane is the one that is open, not because passing beats carrying. [Zone Entries](../systems/zone_entries.md) covers those figures, the league they were measured in and their limits.

### Penalty Kill (your team is short-handed)

```facts
Position: In a box you are one of the two high players, each responsible for one point — there is no single "top" to drift into
Position: In a diamond you are usually the single apex, pressuring the point; in wedge+1 you are frequently the "+1"
Action: As the +1, pressure whoever has the puck; when the puck moves the nearest wedge player takes over as the new +1, so you never follow it round
Options: Your route out as the +1 is a coaching choice — straight out from the goal, sweeping across the top of the zone, or roaming to the wall and point; ask which one your team runs
Action: Take the high forward spot at the mid-to-high slot, block shooting lanes and clear it while you are still short-handed; Special Teams covers the shape
Never: Get pulled out of position chasing the puck around the perimeter
Rule: A shorthanded team may send the puck down the ice without being called for icing, but the exemption is conditional — USA Hockey Rule 624(b)(1) grants it only from Youth 15-Only, Girls 16U, High School and Adult up
Rule: The major-penalty clawback in NHL 81.6 and PWHL 83.6 stops a kill icing only once a major expires with the penalty bench empty, then only until the next stoppage — not during the major; Rule 20.3 wants the substitute seated at a stoppage before it ends, or immediately where minors are stacked on it
Rule: Shorthanded icing turns on the on-ice count, not on whether a penalty is running — expiry counts back only the player whose penalty ended, so a 5-on-3 kill is still below strength and still exempt, and a clear released before expiry draws no icing however late it lands (NHL and IIHF Rule 81.6)
Rule: Expiry is not the only thing that levels the sides — a penalty to them takes you to 4-on-4, which is not below their on-ice strength, so you can be iced while a major of yours is still running (NHL and IIHF Rule 81.6)
Key: Know which shape you are running before the puck drops — a box and a diamond ask very different things of you
```

A kill is four skaters, normally two forwards and two defencemen. **The shapes are described in [Special Teams](../systems/special_teams.md);** your job inside each one:

- **Box** — the standard. You are **one of the two high players**, alongside a winger, each responsible for one point. There is no single "top" of a box, so don't drift into the middle looking for one.
- **Diamond** — used against a 1-3-1. Here there *is* a single apex and it is usually you: pressure the point while the middle two take the half-walls and someone accounts for the bumper.
- **Wedge+1** — you are frequently the "+1", the single player working outside the triangle. That job is **pressure, not pursuit**. Get at whoever has the puck and make every possession uncomfortable. When the puck moves you stop, and the nearest wedge player steps out as the new +1 while you drop into the triangle. Following the puck around the perimeter is how this system is beaten, not how it is played. **The route you take to the puck is your coach's choice, not a law** — published descriptions differ, some sending the +1 straight out from the goal towards the carrier, some sweeping him laterally across the top of the zone above the circles, some letting him roam to the wall and the point. Ask which one you are running.

![](diagram:pk-diamond)

Whichever shape you're in: stay disciplined, don't get pulled out of position chasing the puck around the perimeter, block shooting lanes, and clear the puck whenever you get it — you may ice it while shorthanded, **but check your rule set before you build the kill on it.** USA Hockey grants the exemption only at Youth 15-Only and above, Girls 16U and above, High School and Adult (Rule 624(b)(1)); below those levels a shorthanded team is called for icing like anyone else. NHL Rule 81.6 and PWHL Rule 83.6 also withdraw it from a team that lets a *major* expire with nobody on the penalty bench to come out — ⚠️ **that bites at expiry, not during the penalty**, because Rule 20.3 lets that bench sit empty until a stoppage before the major ends, ⚠️ **unless minors are stacked on the major, when the substitute must be seated *"immediately"***. ⚠️ **What does not vary by book is the count.** Every book read here gives the exemption only to the side *below* its opponent's on-ice numbers, and NHL and IIHF Rule 81.6 settle it **at the instant a penalty expires** — the player whose penalty just ran out counts as back on the ice even while he waits at the box door. Killing a 5-on-3, the first expiry leaves you four against five and you may still ice it; the second stops you. ⚠️ **A penalty to *them* levels the sides too**: at 4-on-4 you can be iced while a major of yours is still running. **The relief is in the same sentence**: a puck *"released from the stick of the player shooting the puck down the ice prior to the penalty expiring"* draws no icing **however late it lands**, so do not stop making a legal play. [Special Teams](../systems/special_teams.md) covers this rule, and [Faceoffs](../systems/faceoffs.md) sets the books side by side. Know which shape you're running *before* the puck drops; a box and a diamond ask very different things of you.

#### Blocking shots on the kill

```facts
Rule: Blocking a shot is protected — a player who drops to their knees is not penalised if the puck is shot under them or lodged in their equipment, though using the hands to make it unplayable is (NHL 63.2(v) NOTE; IIHF 63.2(V), PWHL 65.2(v), USA Hockey 614(a))
Technique: Take a lane before the shot, close and on your feet, head out of it, hands behind your body, stick flat beside you and never leading — and never in front of your own goalie's eyes
Never: Block the shot on a 2-on-1 — as the lone defender back your job is the pass and your goalie has the shooter; the full list of when not to block is in Defender and Body Contact and Battles
Convention: How much your team blocks at all is a coaching choice, not a law — some teams want aggressive blocking, others want the lane taken away while you stay on your feet so the goalie sees everything. Find out which yours plays, ask your coach which they want, and ask your goalie what they want
```

**Blocking a shot is protected, and the limit is the part to remember.** The NHL, the IIHF, the PWHL and USA Hockey all write it: a player who drops to their knees is not penalised if the puck is shot under them or becomes lodged in their clothing or equipment — but **using your hands to make the puck unplayable is a minor** (NHL Rule 63.2(v) NOTE; IIHF 63.2(V); PWHL 65.2(v); USA Hockey 614(a)). Hockey Canada and CARHA print no such note, but their smothering minors reach only a player who *"deliberately falls on or gathers the puck"* (Hockey Canada 10.2(a)(iv); CARHA Rule 58(a)). So a blocked shot that dies under you is fine; reaching for it to hold it there is not.

**Taking a shooting lane away means a puck can hit you, so take it properly.** Be in the lane *before* the shot rather than sliding into it, block from close and on your feet, square to the shooter, head out of the lane and hands behind your body with the backs of the gloves out. **Never lead with your stick** — keep it flat on the ice beside you or out of the lane, because a puck off an outstretched blade tends to go upward, into your face (coaching craft, not a measured finding); **if you go down**, go down toward the shooter rather than sideways, hands behind your body or tucked in behind your shin pads — and **never from in front of your own goalie's eyes**, where all you do is deflect a puck they never saw. **How much your team blocks at all is a coaching choice, not a law** — some teams want you blocking aggressively, others want the lane taken away while you stay on your feet so the goalie sees everything. Neither is right in the abstract: find out which yours plays, ask your coach which they want, and ask your goalie what they want. [Defender](defender.md) and [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md) own the technique and the full list of when *not* to block. **Start with the hardest one on that list: never block the shot on a 2-on-1** — as the lone defender back your job is the pass, and your goalie has the shooter.

### Icing

```facts
Risk: Your icing risk is the lazy dump from behind the red line, not the defensive-zone clear
Technique: Carry the extra stride so the puck is past the red line on your stick before you dump it — legal however far it goes, unless your league splits the ice at the blue lines
Options: Or beat the icing the other way — make the dump soft enough and short enough that a winger would clearly reach the puck first, which only works where your book runs a hybrid-icing race
Rule: The linesperson judges which player would first touch the puck, not who first reaches the dot (NHL and IIHF Rule 81.1; PWHL Rule 83.1)
Rule: The hybrid-icing race is the NHL's, IIHF's and PWHL's (81.1; PWHL 83.1) — CARHA 65 has none, and USA Hockey is automatic outside sled hockey, its Rule 624 Note completing the icing "the instant the puck has completely crossed the goal line"
Rule: Hockey Canada runs the hybrid-icing race only in U18AAA and Junior, and in Senior at the Member's option (6.7(e))
Rule: After your own icing your line cannot change and your team cannot take a time-out under NHL, IIHF and PWHL rules (PWHL 83.4 and 89.1) — USA Hockey and CARHA impose neither and Hockey Canada is a third answer; an injured player can still be replaced
Rule: Hockey Canada 6.7(d) applies the no-change rule only in U18AAA, Junior and, at the Member's option, Senior — in every other category under that book, minor and female hockey included, your line changes after your own icing
Rule: Where Hockey Canada's no-change bar does apply, its time-out rule goes the other way from the NHL's — 6.7(d)(iii) permits either team to take a time-out after an icing, barring only the icing team from substituting during it
Action: Legs gone? Put the puck off the glass and out, not down the ice — never over it: from your own zone that is a minor in the NHL, IIHF and PWHL even by accident, and on purpose in all six
Key: When they ice it under NHL, IIHF or PWHL rules you take an offensive-zone draw against five who cannot change — have a set play. USA Hockey and CARHA let them change, as does most of Hockey Canada
```

**The rule itself — hybrid icing, the full wave-off list, "gaining the line", and how USA Hockey and rec leagues differ — is in [Rules Primer](../foundation/rules_primer.md).** What is specific to you is *which* icings are yours and what they cost you.

**Your risk is the neutral-zone dump, not the defensive-zone clear.** Your defencemen make most of the clears; what a centre throws away is a lazy dump from behind the centre red line. Two cheap fixes: carry the extra stride so the **puck is past the red line on your stick** before you let it go — that alone makes the dump legal, however far it goes — or make the dump soft and short enough that a winger would clearly reach the puck first, which, not who reaches the dot first, is what the linesperson is judging (NHL and IIHF Rule 81.1; PWHL Rule 83.1). ⚠️ **The second fix needs a race to exist, and under three of the books most hockey has none.** USA Hockey is automatic in every standing classification (the Rule 624 Note); Hockey Canada gives hybrid icing only to U18AAA, Junior and Senior at the Member's option (Rule 6.7(e)); and CARHA runs none at all (Rule 65). Under those, a soft dump that crosses the goal line is iced whoever would have reached it first. **A dump that stops short of the goal line works everywhere, and so does the first fix — unless you play in a CARHA league that divides the ice at the blue lines instead of the red** (Rule 65(b), (c)): **ask your league where its line is.** [Rules Primer](../foundation/rules_primer.md) sets out the book-by-book comparison.

![](diagram:icing-gaining-the-line)

**The penalty is aimed at you personally.** After your own icing the draw is in your defensive zone, and **under NHL, IIHF and PWHL rules** your line **cannot change** (an injured player can still be replaced) and your team cannot take a timeout — so a lazy dump leaves a tired centre taking a defensive-zone draw he badly needs to win. The IIHF imposes both (Rules 81.4 and 87.1), so British and European league hockey punishes you just as hard. Its 81.4 adds an exception for *"a Player with a broken skate"*, but ⚠️ **do not count on it without the official's say-so**: Rule 82.1 does not repeat it, and if the officials refuse the change, Rule 82.2(V) sends the players back and warns your team that any later violation in the game is a bench minor — one warning per team per game, shared with two other acts, so a bench already warned takes the penalty straight away. **USA Hockey imposes neither** (Rule 624 restricts no substitution, and 636(f) sets no icing condition on time-outs), so a tired line gets off — ⚠️ **at the ordinary speed**: Rule 204(a) gives each bench five seconds, then allows no more substitutions until the faceoff, and undue delay draws a warning and then a delay-of-game minor. ⚠️ **Hockey Canada applies the no-change rule only in U18AAA, Junior and, at the Member's option, Senior** (Rule 6.7(d)); everywhere else your line changes as after any whistle, and where it does apply either team may take its time-out but the icing team still cannot substitute during it (6.7(d)(iii)). **CARHA writes no bar at all** — Rule 65 sets no substitution limit and Rule 19(d) allows each team one change at any stoppage. [Rules Primer](../foundation/rules_primer.md) carries the book-by-book comparison. If your legs are gone, the puck goes off the glass and out, not down the ice. ⚠️ **Off the glass, never over it** — from your own zone, a puck that flies straight out over the glass is a delay-of-game minor in the NHL, the IIHF and the PWHL even when you did not mean it, and one put out on purpose is a minor in every book; [Rules Primer](../foundation/rules_primer.md) has the detail.

**When the other team ices it, that's one of your best draws of the night — wherever they cannot change.** Under the NHL, the IIHF and the PWHL, and under Hockey Canada in U18AAA, Junior and Senior-at-the-Member's-option, you are taking an offensive-zone draw against five players who are stuck out there and probably tired; under USA Hockey and CARHA, and in every other Hockey Canada category, they get a fresh line and the draw is an ordinary one. Know it's coming and have a set play ready. Remember too that under NHL, IIHF and PWHL rules, in Britain, and under Hockey Canada's in Junior, the **first** faceoff violation by either team on the draw after an icing does not eject a centre — so expect their centre to use his free one, and use yours.

One point on the matchup, because it is often misquoted: the bar falls only on the team that iced the puck. Rule 82.1 removes the home team's "any desired substitution" right *"in cases following an icing, which does not result in the delay of the game"*, and the rule's later paragraph — echoed by Rule 81.4 — identifies that exception as the bar on the team in violation of Rule 81. So the fresh-legs advantage is yours home or away when *they* ice it, and forfeited home or away when you do. See [Faceoffs](../systems/faceoffs.md).

### Backchecking — the middle lane is usually yours

```facts
Convention: The centre takes the middle lane and the wingers the outside — the assignment assumed here, but a coaching choice, not a law, and no count of which is commonest was found
Read: Not every team gives the centre the middle. Some give it to the first man back, others pick up man-on-man from the puck outward. Find out which yours plays
Action: Take the middle-lane driver — the forward arriving late and unmarked into the slot is usually more dangerous than the puck carrier, which is coaching emphasis rather than a counted ranking
Position: Come back on the defensive side of your man, between them and your own net — stick in the passing lane, not on his body, and not chasing behind their shoulder
Rule: The middle-lane driver has no puck — NHL and IIHF Rule 56.1 and PWHL Rule 57.1 grant body position on three conditions: stick not utilized, free hand not used, no otherwise illegal check off it
Rule: USA Hockey 625(a)(4) makes changing lane or foot speed to play the body of a man without the puck an interference minor — that rule stops at two minutes, but the same hit can be called under others that reach an ejection or a match penalty
Action: Skate first, then look, and don't decide mid-rush to chase the carrier — your defencemen have him, and swinging wide abandons the ice they are protecting
Options: Some teams coach the first forward back to pressure the carrier, with the defence holding inside — where that is your system, pressuring is your feet and your stick, never a hit from behind
Priority: Last forward back with your defence outnumbered — take away the pass, don't go for the hit
Action: Once you're set, become the centre again — cover the high slot and support the corner
```

When your team loses the puck going the other way, the centre normally has the single most important backcheck assignment in hockey: **the middle lane**.

> **Which backchecker owns which lane is a coaching choice, not a law.** The positional version described here — centre in the middle, wingers outside — is the default assumed throughout this guide, and **no count of which of the three is commonest has been found**. Other teams use **first man back takes the middle**, whoever that is, which is common when the centre is the one caught deep and common in rec hockey. Others pick up **man-on-man from the puck outward**. Find out which your team plays. [Defending the Rush](../systems/defending_the_rush.md) sets them out, along with the four named team backchecking systems that decide which assignment applies; under every one of them, somebody owns the middle and nobody assumes it is somebody else.

- **Take the middle-lane driver.** The most dangerous attacker on a rush is usually not the puck carrier — it's the forward filling the middle of the ice behind them, arriving late and unmarked into the slot. **That the middle is the lane most dangerous to leave unattended, and the middle-lane driver the assignment most often missed, is coaching emphasis and not a counted ranking**; [Defending the Rush](../systems/defending_the_rush.md) sets out that ordering and states the limit with it. Under the positional assignment that player is yours, the wingers have the outside lanes, and nobody else is going to pick this up. Under the other two variants you may end up on an outside man instead — but then somebody has called that they have the middle, and if nobody has, take it.
- **Get to the inside.** Come back on the defensive side of your man — between them and your own net — not chasing behind their shoulder; if you're trailing them, you can't defend anything. **Take the ice, not the body:** your stick goes in the passing lane rather than on him, because he has no puck. NHL Rule 56.1 — and IIHF 56.1 and PWHL 57.1 — grants body position only while your stick is not used to make yourself bigger, your free hand is not used, and you do not *"take advantage of his body position to deliver an otherwise illegal check"*; that third condition bites hardest. **Hockey Canada writes no such grant**: its Rule 8.3 makes it interference to impede *"the progress of an opponent, who is not in possession of the puck"*, so under that book skate your line inside him and never step across his path. **USA Hockey's Rule 625(a)(4)** makes it interference to change *"their skating lane or foot speed in an effort to play the body of an opponent who is no longer in control of the puck"*, and Rule 625 stops at a minor. ⚠️ **Two minutes is not the ceiling on the hit, though.** A body check with no effort to play the puck and the stick blade above the knees is roughing (640(d)), and a reckless one a major plus a game misconduct (640(g)); cross-check him and it is Rule 609, take him from behind and it is 608, throw it where checking is barred and it is 604 — and plan on a **602(a)** match if any of them recklessly endangers him or is meant to injure him.
- **Skate first, then look, and don't chase the carrier.** Your defencemen have the puck carrier; if you swing wide to help them, you have abandoned the exact area they are trying to protect. **Unless your team has told you otherwise:** some coaches want the first forward back — usually you — to pressure the carrier while the defence holds inside instead. That is a system, not an instinct, and [Defending the Rush](../systems/defending_the_rush.md) sets out both. What is always wrong is deciding it yourself in the middle of a rush — and pressuring is your feet and your stick, never a hit from behind. **Before you plan any of it around contact, read the subsection below:** whether you may body-check at all, and what a check from behind costs if you may, are answered by your rule book and your classification rather than by your coach — and the books do not agree.
- **Once you're set, become the centre again.** Transition into your normal defensive-zone role — cover the high slot, support the corner.

![](diagram:centre-backcheck-middle-lane)

If you are the last forward back and your defence is outnumbered, the priority is taking away the pass, not making a hit.

#### May you check at all, and what a check from behind costs

```facts
Rule: USA Hockey 604(a) bans body checking at 12 & under and below, in all Girls'/Women's age classifications and in all non-check Adult classifications
Rule: USA Hockey's age list is not exhaustive — 604(a) also lets a local governing body prohibit body checking in any classification, 604(b) bars it whenever a Competitive Contact team plays a Body Checking team, and its Blind Hockey rules bar it at every age. Ask your affiliate
Rule: Hockey Canada 7.3(a) bans INTENTIONAL body-checks, bumps, shoves and pushes at U13 and below, in female hockey and in any division a Member has approved — incidental contact is not a penalty
Rule: A barred body check escalates — Hockey Canada 7.3(b) allows a major plus game misconduct on violence and mandates it on injury, as USA Hockey 604(d) does for a reckless one; plan on a match under 602(a) for reckless endangerment, and 7.3(c)'s match is mandatory for an attempt to injure
Rule: The IIHF writes no classification ban on body checking; its women's Rule 101.1 allows it only with "a clear intention of playing the puck or attempting to 'gain possession'" of it, never to pin or push into the boards. An illegal hit is a minor, or a major and automatic game misconduct
Rule: British checking ages are set by IHUK's Rules of Competition 2026-27, one document per competition — junior U10 and U12 non-checking, U14, U16 and U19 checking, NIHL Division 1 and 2 checking, every WNIHL women's division non-checking. None covers the Elite League. Get yours in writing
Rule: CARHA 49(a) prices any player who "intentionally bodies, pushes, shoves" an opponent or "does not avert body contact" at a minor or a major, mandatory if intentional contact injures — and a major ejects (30(a)); an attempt to injure is a match penalty (48(a))
Rule: IIHF 101.1 excepts itself more than once — competing for possession you may not use the boards "to eliminate her from the play, push her into the boards, or pin her along the boards", you must skate around a stationary opponent, and any move to step or glide into an opponent is an illegal hit
Rule: The NHL, IIHF and PWHL write no minor for checking from behind at all (43.2 in all three) — what is left is a major, mandatory under NHL 43.3 and at the referee's discretion under IIHF 43.3 for a check that recklessly endangers, with a game misconduct on it (NHL 43.5)
Rule: From behind: USA Hockey's minor adds a misconduct (608(a)), CARHA's a game misconduct (53(a); major on injury). USA Hockey: at least a major and game misconduct if head first into boards or goal frame (608(b)) or forceful along the boards (Casebook); reckless, plan on the match (602(a))
Rule: Hockey Canada from behind, off the head or driven head first: minor/major and game misconduct, major a must if hurt, a match if driven into boards or goal frame defenceless, or meant to injure (7.5). If it also reaches the head, the harsher of this and head contact stands
Rule: Hockey Canada from behind, on the head: head contact, up to a match, a must if defenceless or meant to injure; major and game misconduct a must on high cross-check, in minor/female if hurt (7.6), in Junior/Senior if highly violent or head hurt off glass, ice or boards (7.7)
Rule: A hit from behind into boards or net on a player "unable to protect or defend themselves" is a mandatory match in Hockey Canada (7.5(c); 7.6(c) or 7.7(c) on the head) and CARHA 53(b); a reckless one with excessive force on a vulnerable or defenseless player is a match anywhere in USA Hockey 608(c)
Never: Finish a backcheck into the back of a player skating at his own end boards — take the puck, the ice, or angle him off it. Ask your book and classification before you plan any hit
```

**Check whether you may body-check at all.** USA Hockey Rule 604(a) bars it at 12 & under and below, in all Girls'/Women's and all non-check adult classifications, and **Hockey Canada Rule 7.3** at U13 and below, throughout female hockey, and in any division a Member of Hockey Canada has approved. ⚠️ **A checking classification is not the same thing as a checking game.** Under USA Hockey a local governing body may bar checking in any classification (604(a)), it is barred whenever a Competitive Contact team plays a Body Checking team (604(b)), and it is barred in all Blind Hockey games, adult rosters included, which the 604(a) list does not reach. Ask your affiliate, before the puck drops. **Both books escalate a barred check.** Hockey Canada 7.3(b) allows a major plus game misconduct on the degree of violence and makes it **mandatory** if the check injures; USA Hockey 604(d) makes the same pair **mandatory** for a check that *"recklessly endangers an opponent"* in a Competitive Contact category; and each reaches a match penalty, at 7.3(c) and 604(e). Under USA Hockey, plan on a match for a reckless check or one meant to injure, because its general **Rule 602(a)** says one *"shall be assessed"* to any player who *"recklessly endangers or attempts to injure"* an opponent.

**In a CARHA-affiliated adult league,** ⚠️ **plan no hit.** Its **Rule 49(a)** assesses a minor or a major to any player who *"intentionally bodies, pushes, shoves, stands in front of an opponent for the purpose of making contact, and/or does not avert body contact with an opponent"* — the major mandatory when intentional contact injures, and ending your game under **Rule 30(a)** — and a hit meant to hurt is a match penalty under **Rule 48(a)**.

**The IIHF book carries no classification ban**, and in women's hockey it writes a permission: **Rule 101.1** allows body checking *"when there is a clear intention of playing the puck or attempting to 'gain possession' of the puck"*, an illegal hit costing a minor or a major and automatic game misconduct (the 2025/26 and 2026/27 editions read identically). ⚠️ **In British men's, mixed and junior hockey neither rule book states a body-checking age** — IHUK publishes them in its **Rules of Competition 2026-27**: junior hockey **non-checking at U10 and U12** (*"Bodychecking is NOT allowed in U10 matches"*) and **checking at U14, U16 and U19**, **NIHL Division 1 and 2** checking, and **every WNIHL women's division non-checking, U16 and senior alike**. ⚠️ **None of those covers the Elite League**, whose Casebook does not mention body checking anywhere. ⚠️ **And they set the format without pricing a breach** — no IHUK regulation read here writes a penalty for a check thrown in a non-checking division. **Treat the band as the instruction and the price as unsettled**, and get your division's contact status in writing before your first shift. [UK and England Rules](../foundation/uk_rules.md) quotes all four.

**Where IIHF women's hockey allows checking, wanting the puck is not a licence.** Competing for possession you may not use the boards *"to eliminate her from the play, push her into the boards, or pin her along the boards"*; a stationary player *"is entitled to that area of the ice"*, and you must skate around her; and *"[a]ny move by a Player to step or glide into an opposing Player will be assessed at least a minor penalty (2') for an 'illegal hit'"*. ⚠️ **So a player who genuinely wants the puck and steps into a stationary opponent still takes the penalty**, and failing the test can be an ejection rather than two minutes. **The PWHL draws the line differently:** its **Rule 52.1** writes no boards limb, but makes it an *"illegal hit"* to make contact *"with opposite-directional force"*, to leave *"their skating lane to make contact"*, or to extend *"an arm, shoulder, or hip after angling an opponent"*. Ask which book and which classification you are in before you plan a shift around a hit.

**And where you may check, a check from behind is priced like nothing else — though not the same way in every book.** The **NHL**, the **IIHF** and the **PWHL** write no minor for it (Rule 43.2 in all three): what is left is a **major** — mandatory under NHL 43.3, at the referee's discretion under IIHF 43.3 for a check that *"recklessly endangers the fouled Player"* — with a game misconduct that NHL 43.5 says *"must be assessed"* and IIHF 43.3 calls *"automatic"*. **USA Hockey, Hockey Canada and CARHA write a minor, and it never travels alone.** USA Hockey **608(a)** is a minor plus a misconduct, or a major plus a game misconduct; **608(b)** makes the major and game misconduct mandatory for a check from behind that recklessly endangers or sends a player head first into the boards or goal frame; and **608(c)** is a match penalty — ⚠️ **with no boards condition** — for recklessly endangering, from behind and with excessive force, an opponent in a vulnerable or defenceless position. ⚠️ **And USA Hockey's own Casebook takes the minor off the hit you are most likely to throw.** **Rule 608, Situation 1** keeps the minor for a push on an opponent *"not near the boards"* and for *"minimal body contact"* producing a *"pinch"* against them, and directs that *"[t]he major plus a game misconduct penalty, or match penalty, must be called"* for a forceful check on a player standing along the boards, one *"thrust head first into the boards or goal frame"*, and a reckless check from behind *"regardless of whether or not board contact is made."* Its *"danger zone"* is *"approximately ten feet out from the boards"*. **Under USA Hockey, plan on a match for any reckless hit from behind**, because **Rule 602(a)** says one *"shall be assessed"*. **Hockey Canada 7.5(a)** is a minor and a game misconduct, **7.5(b)** a major and game misconduct on the violence of the impact — **mandatory** if the check injures — and **7.5(c)** a match penalty for a hit from behind into the boards or goal frame on a player *"unable to protect or defend themselves"*, or for deliberately injuring or attempting to injure by checking from behind, anywhere. **CARHA 53(a)** is a minor or a major, plus a game misconduct, for a hit from behind *"anywhere on the ice"*, the major mandatory on injury, and **53(b)** a mandatory match for propelling a player who cannot protect himself into the boards or goal net. ⚠️ **So no book lets you off with two minutes and a return to the bench** — under Hockey Canada for a hit that stays off his head, because one that reaches it is head contact under Rule 7.6 (7.7 in Junior and Senior), set out below.

**Never finish a check on a turned back — if you can see his numbers, pull up and play the puck.** A hit to the back that does not reach the head stays under Hockey Canada **Rule 7.5(a)**, a minor plus a game misconduct — a major plus a game misconduct if he is hurt (7.5(b)) — and one that drives him head first into the boards or goal frame costs at least that too — a **match** under 7.5(c) if he could not protect himself. **Once it reaches his head, Hockey Canada prices it as head contact**, because its head-contact rule *"supersedes any other rules, with the exception of Rule 7.10 – Fighting, and any foul that results in contact to the head must be penalized as Head Contact"*. But a head-first drive is the hit Hockey Canada's checking-from-behind rule names, so if it reaches his head as well, the harsher price stands. In minor and female hockey, under **Rule 7.6**, head contact is a minor if the contact was accidental and a double minor if it was intentional, and a third such penalty in one game is a game ejection. The referee may make it a major and game misconduct on the violence of the hit alone, and **must** if he is injured at all or if it was a cross-check above the shoulders; it is a **match penalty** if he was hit in a way that left him *"unable to protect or defend themselves"*, or if you tried to injure him. In Junior and Senior hockey, under **Rule 7.7**, minimal violence may be called as roughing, charging or another appropriate penalty, moderate violence is a minor plus a ten-minute misconduct, and high violence is a major and game misconduct — as is a cross-check above the shoulders, or an illegal hit that puts his head into the glass, the ice or the boards and causes a head injury — with a **match penalty** above that on the same two grounds.

**So take the puck, take the ice, or angle him off it — never finish into his back.**

### Line Changes

```facts
Action: When the line changes as a group you go off first — call it, and the wingers follow you
Convention: A line that changes as a group changes on the centre's legs, because the centre fades first — coaching craft rather than a measured finding
Never: Change into an offensive-zone faceoff you are needed to take
Rule: After your own icing a healthy skater cannot change under NHL and IIHF 81.4 and PWHL 83.4 — injury, a pulled goalie returning and a strength-changing penalty are the exceptions, plus an IIHF broken skate its own 82.1 omits; USA Hockey and CARHA write no icing bar
Rule: Hockey Canada restricts it only in U18AAA and Junior, and in Senior at the Member's option (6.7(d)), and there its third exception is wider than the NHL's — any penalty assessed to either team, whether or not it changes the on-ice strength
Rule: Try to change after the Referee signals the change closed — a late broken-skate swap included — and those players go back to the bench with a warning through your coach; a bench already warned takes a bench minor for delay of game (IIHF 82.2(V), which names no broken skate)
Key: Shift length, the safe and unsafe windows to change, and why a bad change concedes goals are all in Game Management
```

**You go first.** The centre's route covers more of the ice than either winger's and the centre fades first — **coaching craft rather than a measured finding** (see the note in the Overview) — so a line that changes as a group should change on *your* legs: call it and go, and the wingers follow you off. Two cautions that are a centre's: don't change into an offensive-zone faceoff you are needed to take, and after your own team's icing under the NHL, the IIHF and the PWHL you cannot change, so don't start drifting toward the bench. Rule 81.4's exceptions — injury, a pulled goalie going back in, a penalty that changes the strength on the ice, and in the IIHF book only a player with a broken skate — do not include a tired centre, and a broken-skate change that is refused gets your team warned, or a bench minor for delay of game if it has already been warned (IIHF Rule 82.2(V)). ⚠️ **USA Hockey and CARHA set no such bar** — USA Hockey's Rule 624 attaches none to an icing and its Rule 204(a) gives both benches their change at every stoppage, and CARHA's Rule 65 sets no substitution limit — so there you change after your own icing as after any whistle. ⚠️ **Hockey Canada applies it only in U18AAA, Junior and, at the Member's option, Senior** (Rule 6.7(d)), and there its penalty carve-out is **wider** than the NHL's: *"[w]hen a penalty or penalties have been assessed to either team, a change of players will be permitted"*, whether or not the strength on the ice changes. Find out which book and category you are in before you plan a shift around any of this. [Rules Primer](../foundation/rules_primer.md) carries the book-by-book comparison.

Shift length, the safe and unsafe windows to change, and why a bad change concedes goals are all in [Game Management](../systems/game_management.md).

## Common Mistakes

- **Staying high in the slot when the puck is in the corner — under a low zone collapse.** Go down and help your defenceman win it, and call for the weak-side winger to drop into the high slot behind you; stay high and your defenceman is left alone in a battle they will probably lose. **Under man-on-man — or a hybrid, which marks man-on-man low — the job changes**: you stay with your check, and holding the slot may be exactly right. Even in a collapse, hold the slot when your strong-side winger has already walked down with their man and the battle is covered.
- **Carrying the puck through the middle of your own zone.** Get to the boards or behind the net first, then distribute. Coaching consensus makes it the most expensive place on the ice to lose the puck — reasoned from shot danger, not counted: no study ranking turnovers by location was found for this document. [Risk Management](../hockey-iq/risk_management.md) grades every area of the ice by what a turnover there costs.
- **Turning your back to the checker on a rim or a reverse — and ducking when the check arrives.** Arrive with your skates parallel to the boards, forearm and hip to the wall, head up and chin off your chest. Moving the puck from the wall or behind your own net puts the forechecker behind you by design, so this is every shift. A turned back makes the contact a hit from behind you can neither see nor brace for — USA Hockey warns that *"Even a light hit from behind could inflict a severe head or neck injury"* — and ducking straightens the neck, which USA Hockey records can injure you against the boards or a post at walking speed. ⚠️ **Take the posture even where body checking is barred** — contact incidental to chasing the puck along the boards stays legal, and the wall does not care which. [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md) covers both halves.
- **Coasting through the neutral zone to avoid going offside.** Arrive with speed and time your stride so the puck crosses first, with your trail skate **touching** the line. A blade in contact is onside in every book; a raised one is offside under USA Hockey and Hockey Canada, and CARHA's book does not say.
- **Skating through the middle with your head down.** **Head up, chin off your chest** — at full speed and while counting your linemates out to tag up: you cannot brace for a hit you cannot see. ⚠️ **And no book fully protects a head-down carrier** — the IIHF does not count a square check on one skating head down toward the checker as a head hit, and the NHL and the PWHL weigh that posture against you. [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md#keep-your-head-up-always--you-cannot-brace-for-a-hit-you-cannot-see) sets out all of them.
- **Puck-watching on the backcheck** and losing the middle-lane driver. Find the middle-lane driver first and stay with him; if you cannot tell who has called him, pick him up. He is yours under the positional assignment assumed here — a coaching choice, not a law — and somebody's under every alternative.
- **Planning the backcheck around a hit you may not be allowed to throw.** **Take the ice, not the body**: angle with your feet, get inside him with your body between him and your net and your stick in the passing lane, and reach the spot ahead of him rather than stepping into him. It works whatever your division's contact status, and it is the only legal version against the middle-lane driver, who has no puck — body position is yours only while your stick and free hand stay off him and you do not use it to hit him. Step into him and it is interference — two minutes at the least, and it can go higher in five of the six books: under CARHA (Rule 66(a)) a major and a game misconduct, with at least a one-game suspension (Rule 32(d)); and even under USA Hockey, whose interference rule stops at two minutes, the same hit can be called under rules that reach an ejection or a match penalty. ⚠️ **Before you plan a shift around a hit on a puck carrier, get your division's contact status from your affiliate, in writing** — [the checking section above](#may-you-check-at-all-and-what-a-check-from-behind-costs) sets out who may hit. **On the forecheck, steer rather than push** — in a non-checking classification an intentional push is a penalty in its own right; a violent one can end your game, and one that injures (Hockey Canada, CARHA) or is reckless (USA Hockey) does.
- **Finishing a backcheck into a player’s back.** Take the puck, take the ice, or angle him off it — if you can see his numbers, pull up and play the puck. A forward skating at his own end boards has his back to you and is not braced for it. No book lets you off with two minutes: the NHL, the IIHF and the PWHL write no minor for it at all; CARHA’s and Hockey Canada’s minor carries a **game** misconduct, and a major and game misconduct is mandatory if he is hurt; USA Hockey’s minor carries a misconduct, with at least a major and game misconduct for a forceful check on a player along the boards, one that sends him head first into the boards or goal frame, or a reckless one anywhere — and plan on a match for a reckless one (602(a)). Once it reaches his head, Hockey Canada prices it as head contact, up to a match — a match if he could not protect himself — and if it also drove him head first, the harsher price stands. Put him into the boards when he cannot protect himself and it is a mandatory **match** penalty under Hockey Canada and CARHA, and under USA Hockey whenever it is reckless (602(a)).
- **Getting thrown out of the faceoff dot** for something avoidable — including a winger's encroachment you failed to warn them about. Warn your wingers before every draw that matters, and ⚠️ **agree two back-up draw takers, not one** — under Hockey Canada the player who caused the violation may not take the draw, so if your one replacement is the winger who just encroached, your plan is gone at the moment you need it.
- **Leaning your head in to get low at the dot.** Get your depth from the knees and hips; a head pushed out over the dot ends up against the other centre's. Helmet-to-helmet contact before the drop is pre-drop contact in every book: it can put you — or both centres — out of the draw, and the minor on top is discretionary under the NHL, the IIHF and the PWHL and mandatory under USA Hockey, Hockey Canada and CARHA.
- **Taking long shifts.** Change when your legs go, not when the play happens to stop: call it yourself, go first, and let the wingers follow you off. A well-managed shift is nearer **30–45 seconds** — coaching craft, not a measured band. The measured range is **roughly 30 to 80 seconds**, from **Lignell et al. (2018), Brocherie et al. (2018) and Bracko et al. (1998)** as collected in Vigh-Larsen and Mohr's 2024 physiology review, which summarises those three rather than measuring the band itself. The top of that range is where the wheels come off, and the centre is generally reckoned to fade first of the three forwards — coaching craft, not a measured comparison. [Conditioning and Recovery](../off-the-ice/conditioning_and_recovery.md) covers both figures.
- **Treating the slot as a place to stand — or standing still at the wrong moment.** **Keep moving to get open, slide with the point man as they walk the line, and plant only at the release** — shuffling then gives the goalie flashes of the puck through the gaps. A stationary centre in the slot is easy to cover.
- **Planting at the net front without knowing where the post is.** Know where the post is before you plant, and keep your head up and chin off your chest the whole time. A screen has you standing still with your eyes off the man beside you, so you get walked sideways — with the post at one edge of that path and the goaltender at the other. **The goaltender is the one body there you never take that contact against — give ground rather than hold your spot into them.** Never duck: the flexed neck turns a collision with a post into a spinal injury, and USA Hockey names the goal post in that mechanism alongside the boards, recording that the injury *"can occur at walking speed"*. Meet a post with an arm or a leg, never your head first. **A box-out, legal or not in your book, puts you on the same path.** [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md#9-net-front-battles) owns it in full.
- **Screening from inside the blue paint, or with a skate on the red line at its edge.** Screen from outside the paint and off the red line, and keep the blade you are saving for a tip out of there too — four books count that line as crease, and the NHL's and the PWHL's rule text does not say. ⚠️ **The cost is not only your own shot.** Under USA Hockey, which counts your stick as you, a goal scored with an attacker's stick or skates in the crease while the goalkeeper is in it is disallowed — whichever teammate shot it. ⚠️ **And when their goalie has come out to play the puck, leave them their path home** — under NHL and IIHF Rule 69.4 deliberately keeping them from getting back can be penalised as goalkeeper interference, with no contact at all.
- **Arriving into the goaltender instead of stopping in front of them.** Take the ice in front of the net, never the goaltender, and arrive under control. A screen is a place you stand; a charge is the speed you get there at, and off the forecheck or down from the slot those are one stride apart. Charging a goaltender risks a major plus a game misconduct in every book, and several make it mandatory: Hockey Canada for any charge on a goaltender, CARHA where the goalkeeper is in the crease or anyone is injured, USA Hockey for a reckless charge — plan on a match penalty there too — and the NHL, the PWHL and the Elite League once a charging major injures the face or head. ⚠️ **Arrive from behind and it is checking from behind:** the NHL, the IIHF and the PWHL write no minor at all, and Hockey Canada and CARHA make it a mandatory match penalty if it drives a goaltender who cannot protect themselves into the net or boards — and Hockey Canada wherever it reaches that goaltender's head.
- **Blocking a shot from in front of your own goalie's eyes.** Block from close and on your feet, head out of the lane, hands behind your body and stick flat beside you rather than out in front, or get out of the lane and let them see it. In a wedge you stand at the mid-to-high slot, on the line between the point and your own net. **And never block the shot on a 2-on-1** — as the lone defender back your job is the pass, and your goalie has the shooter. [Defender](defender.md) and [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md) own the technique and the list of when *not* to block.
- **Grabbing a loose puck in front of your own net and holding it.** Knock it straight down where you caught it and play it with your stick or your skate. Catching it is not the mistake — no book penalises the catch, provided you put it down immediately. ⚠️ **Holding on, carrying it, or dropping it somewhere better is what costs you**, and inside your own crease that is a penalty shot rather than a minor (USA Hockey lets the other team take a minor instead). With your own net empty, never cover a puck in your crease: it is an awarded goal on the bare fact in most books, and under USA Hockey wherever you prevented *"an obvious and imminent goal"*; CARHA writes it both ways, so treat any cover as the goal.

## Key Takeaways

1. **Play the middle of the ice in all three zones, and let your job move with the puck.** You are the link between the other four skaters: a winger owns a lane and a defenceman owns the back of the play, and you own the space between them.
2. **When the puck goes into your own corner, help your defenceman win it and call the weak-side winger into the high slot behind you; when it is at the net or in the slot, hold the high slot.** That is the low zone collapse, a coaching choice. Read who is already low first: if your strong-side winger came down with their man the corner may not need you, and if the weak-side winger is low with theirs, nobody is coming to fill the slot. Under man-on-man or a hybrid you track one opponent instead, so ask which your team plays.
3. **Swing low so your defenceman has a safe outlet** — that is the conventional swing breakout. Under a centre fly you leave early and the weak-side winger drops in, so find out which your team plays.
4. **Own the middle of the neutral zone, with your head up.** Under the containment systems assumed here — a 1-2-2, a 1-2-2 trap or a 1-3-1 — you are the lone forward taking the middle away and steering the carrier to a side. Under an aggressive press two forwards hunt the puck and your job changes, so ask which your team plays and what the call is to switch. And keep your head up: [you cannot brace for a hit you cannot see](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md#keep-your-head-up-always--you-cannot-brace-for-a-hit-you-cannot-see).
5. **On the backcheck, take the middle-lane driver and get inside him** — between him and your net, stick in the passing lane. The middle is yours under the positional assignment assumed here, a coaching choice; if you cannot tell who has called it, take it. **Take the ice, not the body:** he has no puck, so body position is yours only while your stick and free hand stay off him and you do not use it to hit him. Where your team sends the first forward back at the carrier, pressure him with your feet and stick. ⚠️ **If you can see his numbers, pull up and play the puck** — no book lets you off with two minutes; Hockey Canada's minor carries a game misconduct, a major is a must if he is hurt, and a hit that drives him head first or reaches his head is a match if he could not protect himself. Before you plan any contact, find out whether your division allows body checking at all.
6. **Prepare the draws that matter — your own end, special teams, the last minute and after an icing — and agree with your wingers beforehand where you are trying to win each.** ⚠️ **Get your depth from the knees and hips, not by pushing your head over the dot** — helmet-to-helmet contact before the drop can put you out of the draw and cost you a minor — automatically under USA Hockey, Hockey Canada and CARHA. **Warn your wingers that their encroachment puts you out, and agree *two* replacement takers** — under Hockey Canada the player who caused the violation may not take the draw.
7. **Under pressure anywhere, get the puck to the outside** — not through the middle of your own zone or the neutral zone. Rim it, chip it off the boards, or give it to a winger on the wall and support them. Simple beats clever.
8. **At the net front, screen from just outside the paint, then go for the rebound — taking the ice in front of the goaltender, never the goaltender.** A screen from outside the crease, without contact, is legal and valuable, so do not play it timidly; keep your skates off the red crease line and your stick out of the paint. Be on the rebound the moment it comes out, without touching the goalie. Shoved in? Get out at once and avoid the goaltender, or that contact can be penalised. ⚠️ **Arrive under control, never into the goaltender** — deliberate or unnecessary contact is a penalty in or out of the crease, goal or no goal; a charge risks ejection in every book — automatic under Hockey Canada, under CARHA when the goalie is in the crease or anyone is hurt, and under USA Hockey when reckless — and Hockey Canada also ejects you for any interference that injures a goalie.
9. **A puck in the air near your own net: knock it straight down and play it with your stick or your skate.** Catching it costs nothing if you put it straight down; holding on, carrying it or dropping it somewhere better costs a penalty shot inside your own crease, and an awarded goal with your net empty. Caught above your own paint, below the bar, it counts as in the crease. **At a live draw, keep your hand off the puck altogether** until a third player has touched it — the NHL, the IIHF and the PWHL penalise the centre who does not.
10. **Cross the blue line at full speed with your trailing blade down on the line or the ice behind it.** Time your stride so the puck crosses first rather than coasting to stay onside. A blade in contact is onside in every book; a raised one is not under USA Hockey or Hockey Canada, so keep it down.
11. **Never block the shot on a 2-on-1.** As the lone defender back, your job is the pass — your goaltender already has the shooter, on the penalty kill and off it. [Defender](defender.md) and [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md) carry the technique and the other times not to block.
12. **Before you shoot, look for the pass across the front of the net.** A pass across the **royal road** — the line down the middle of the ice from net to net — forces the goaltender into a hard lateral push, so the puck arrives before they are set. You are at both ends of that pass more than anyone: the far-side receiver when a winger has it on the wall, and the passer coming out from behind the net. Refuse the extra pass along your own side. [Offensive Zone Play](../systems/offensive_zone_play.md) owns the figure behind it.
13. **On a 1-3-1 power play you are most often the bumper.** Sit in the soft spot in the middle of the slot, be the target for both half-walls, and shoot quickly; in an umbrella or an overload you are low or on the strong-side wall, so ask which your team runs. Enter the zone with control — carry into space or pass across the line — rather than skating into the defence or dumping into traffic.
14. **On the penalty kill, know your shape before the puck drops and hold it.** In a box you are one of the two high players with a point each; in a diamond usually the apex pressuring the point; as the +1 in a wedge you pressure the puck and hand over when it moves. Never chase it around the perimeter. Clear it the length of the ice when you get it — icing is waived only while you are a man short on the ice. Once the sides are level, after a major expires with your box empty under NHL and PWHL rules, or under USA Hockey below Youth 15-Only, Girls 16U and High School, get it to the neutral zone instead.
15. **Carry the extra stride before you dump it.** Past the red line on your stick, the dump is legal however far it goes — unless your league divides the ice at the blue lines, as some CARHA leagues do. The icing a centre gives away is the lazy dump from behind the red line, and under NHL, IIHF and PWHL rules, and at Hockey Canada's top levels, a tired line then cannot change for the defensive-zone draw. When the other team ices it, have a set play ready.
16. **Change on your legs, and go first.** When the line changes as a group, you call it and the wingers follow you off — the centre is generally reckoned to fade first, which is coaching craft rather than a measured finding. Don't change into an offensive-zone faceoff you are needed to take.
17. **On the forecheck you are usually first in: drive at the carrier on an angle and steer them to one side so your winger knows where to seal.** Steer with your feet and stick rather than pushing — in a non-checking division the push is a penalty on its own — and arrive under control, because a long run at a carrier is how the rulebooks describe a charge. If a winger gets there first, they are F1 and you support. How many forwards go in is your team's system, so ask.
