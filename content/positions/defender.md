## Key focus

**Stay on the defensive side of the puck.** Stay between the puck and your net, goal side of the attacker you have. Give that up only on purpose — a pinch, a step-up, joining a rush — once you have called it and someone is covering behind you. How often your coach wants that is their call, so ask.

**Work as a pair: one of you on the puck, one at the mouth of your goal.** Swap every time the puck crosses the middle, and say it out loud. Both of you on one puck leaves the net front empty. This is the low zone collapse; man-on-man teams split it differently, so ask which yours plays.

**At the net front, get inside and lift their stick.** Beat the forward to the spot between them and your goalie. As the shot or pass comes, lift their stick up, low on the shaft, then let go. **Hold the spot by position, not by shoving** — pushing a forward without the puck can be called interference in any game. **If a body moves there, take it toward the corner, never across your goalmouth.**

**Never carry or pass the puck across the front of your own goal. Go behind the net.** A puck lost behind the net is a battle; lost in the slot it is a point-blank chance. Some teams exchange high above the circles instead, so ask which route yours uses.

**Win the gap with your feet, not with a hit.** Gap control — the room you leave a puck carrier — is the defining skill of the position. Start skating backwards early, take the middle away, watch chest and hips rather than the puck, and stay backwards as long as you can. How hard you step up is your coach's call.

**Alone against two, take the pass away and leave the shot to your goalie.** Stay on your feet in the lane, stick flat on the passing side. Some teams coach a slide instead, but a slide that takes the carrier down is tripping even if you reach the puck first — only Hockey Canada and CARHA excuse that — so ask which your team plays. Once no pass is realistically on, close on the puck.

**Move a retrieved puck early — look up the ice first.** Shoulder-check on the way back. The seconds after you win the puck are when their forecheck is least organised, so take the first option that is on. With nothing on, use the glass — off it, never over it: over it from your own zone can cost two minutes even by accident.

**At the offensive blue line, keep the puck in and do not get caught inside.** Hold the line rather than backing in, keep your skates inside it, and walk it to find a lane. Only one of you goes below the puck; the other holds the line unless your coach has called an exception.

**From the point, get the shot through low.** Find the lane first by moving along the line, then shoot at knee height with pace so a teammate can tip it or play the rebound. No lane? Move the puck until there is one. That a point shot is for tips and rebounds is coaching craft; [Shooting](../technique/shooting.md) owns the method.

## Overview

![](diagram:defender-pair-overview)

```facts
Never: Let an attacker get between you and your own net, unless you are pinching, stepping up or joining the rush on purpose and have called it
Action: Work as a pair — one of you plays the puck, the other guards the ice at the mouth of your own goal, and you swap those jobs whenever the puck crosses the middle
Convention: One defenceman on the puck and one on the net front is a low zone collapse — a coaching choice, not a rule. Man-on-man gives the net front to whoever's man is there, so find out which your team plays
Key: Gap control — how much room you leave an oncoming puck carrier — is the defining skill of playing defence, and it is a skating problem before it is a decision
Action: Start skating backwards early, and take the middle away so the only route left runs toward the side boards
Never: Turn to skate forwards while you can still stay backwards — once you turn you have given up your ability to react
Never: Carry the puck or pass it across the ice in front of your own goal. Go behind the net instead, or high above the circles if that is what your team plays
Convention: Alone against two attackers, take the pass away and let your goalie have the shot — a convention rather than a law, which stops applying if the shooter is walking in unpressured with no realistic pass available
```

You and your partner are the last players between the puck and your goal, so **staying on the defensive side of the puck** sits under everything below. A forward who gets between you and your net has already beaten you. You give that up on purpose — a pinch, a step-up, joining a rush — and only once somebody has taken your place above the puck. So before you go, know **who** is above the puck, not what.

**The pair fails most often when both of you go to the puck** and the net front is left empty. **Gap control is a skating problem first:** you cannot close a gap from a standstill, so start backwards early and stay backwards as long as you can. **Go behind your own net, not across the front of it** — behind, a lost puck is a battle; across, it is a scoring chance. Some teams exchange high above the circles instead, so ask which route yours uses. **Alone against two**, taking the pass away is a convention, and it stops applying when the shooter walks in unpressured with no pass on.

**The goalmouth job has two steps.** Get inside the net-front forward before he gets there, then lift his stick as the shot or pass comes — up, low on the shaft, below his bottom hand, and let go, so it is not a hold, hook or slash. **Keep the inside with position, not your body:** shoving a forward without the puck off his spot can be called interference in any game, and CARHA's adult book puts a duty on you to avert contact. **Whenever a body moves there, take it toward the corner, not across your goalmouth.** The *Net-front technique* sections below have the books; [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md) owns the technique.

**Your first touch on a retrieval decides most of what follows.** You retrieve pucks under pressure more than anyone, and the breakout starts there. Look up the ice before you look down it: in the two or three seconds after your team wins the puck, the forecheck is at its least organised. Take the first option genuinely on; [Breakouts](../systems/breakouts.md) owns the options. With nothing on, use the glass: off it and along it is fine, but over it from your own zone is two minutes under the NHL, IIHF and PWHL books even by accident, and a deliberate one is two minutes from anywhere in all six.

**In the offensive zone you have two jobs: keep the puck in, and stay able to get back.** Hold the line rather than backing in, and keep your skates inside it. With the puck, find a lane first by walking the line, then shoot low, knee height or below, so a teammate can tip it or play the rebound; [Shooting](../technique/shooting.md) owns the method. Only one of you goes below the puck while the other holds the line — **the same *who*, not what, question, at the far blue line**. That is a near-universal convention that coaches do break — a full-ice press, a delayed penalty, 6-on-5 — so assume it unless told otherwise.

**Where the words are.** Every line, zone and area of the ice is defined in [Rink Map](../foundation/rink_map.md). The working vocabulary — high and low, strong and weak side, the point, the goalmouth against the high slot, rim against reverse — is in [Language and Glossary](../foundation/language_and_glossary.md). Most instructions below are location words, so read those two first.

**Two words carry most of it.** **High** means toward the blue line you face, **low** toward the goal line — so hold the line, stay above the puck, and do not drop below the circles until somebody has your job at the blue line. **Strong side** is the puck's side, **weak side** the other, and for a pair those are **job titles**: strong side plays the puck, weak side holds the goalmouth, and you swap when the puck crosses the middle.

**The headings below.** Each zone has the same four situations. The fourth, *Without the Puck*, means the puck is not on your stick **and** you are not the one pressuring it. That is a different job in each zone, so each of those headings names it.

## Defensive Zone

The most important zone for a defender. Your job is to protect the area in front of your net.

---

### Defensive Zone: Carrying the Puck — Free Space

```facts
Position: Behind your own net or along the boards
Action: Move the puck quickly and purposefully to a winger on the boards, the centre coming low or your defensive partner — don't hold it longer than you need to
Options: Quick-up, up, wheel, D-to-D, reverse, rim, off the glass — take the first genuinely on; that order is a decision aid under pressure, and Breakouts picks by the read, not off a list of favourites
Read: Take the quick-up in the two or three seconds after your team wins the puck, before their forecheck exists — look up ice first, then down
Goal: Transition the puck out of danger and start your team's breakout
Never: Carry or pass D-to-D through your own slot. Go behind the net instead. Only the alternative route varies: some teams allow the exchange above the tops of the circles, outside the slot
Rule: Shooting the puck over the glass and out of play from inside your own defensive zone is a two-minute delay-of-game minor whether or not you meant it (NHL Rule 63.2(iii))
Rule: Putting the puck deliberately outside the playing area is a two-minute delay-of-game minor from anywhere on the ice, not only from your own end (NHL and IIHF 63.2(ii), USA Hockey 610(c), Hockey Canada 10.1(ii), CARHA 75(b))
Rule: The NHL and IIHF write that deliberate limb for the leg, foot and arm as well as the hand and stick, and reach it after a stoppage as well as during play (63.2(ii)); USA Hockey 610(c) reaches the after-whistle one and 601(c)(10) makes it a misconduct
Rule: The NHL's out-of-play minor has three carve-outs — boards with no glass, a puck shot into the players' bench, and one out directly off a faceoff, which no book reconciles with 63.2(ii)'s deliberate limb, so never aim it; over the glass behind the bench is penalised (NHL Rule 63.2 and its NOTE)
Rule: The IIHF carries the NHL's out-of-play minor and its three carve-outs, so it binds a British player — but its bench clause is wider: the glass "surrounding" the bench, not "behind" it (IIHF Rule 63.2(III)). Its Handbook exempts only a puck shot straight into the bench (Situation 63.24)
Rule: Do not rely on a skate clear under the IIHF — its Rulebook 63.2(III) names only "hand or stick" in 2025/26 and 2026/27 alike, but 63.2(II) reaches a deliberate one by foot from anywhere; its Handbook answers Yes to a kick over the glass (Situation 63.34, all three editions), so clear with the stick
Rule: USA Hockey penalises putting the puck out of play only where it is deliberate — 610(c) is a minor for deliberately shooting or batting it outside the playing area — and 631(d) makes an accidental one a last-play faceoff in the zone it was shot from, with no penalty at all
Rule: Hockey Canada writes no strict over-the-glass minor for a skater — Rule 10.1(ii) reaches only a player who "deliberately shoots, bats, or throws the puck outside the playing surface" — but an accidental one costs you the faceoff, and in Junior and Senior the line change too (Rule 6.4(a))
```

**Where to be:** Behind your own net or along the boards.

**What to do:** Use the space to compose yourself and start the breakout (your team's exit from the defensive zone with controlled possession). Look for a winger on the boards, the center coming low, or your defensive partner. Move the puck quickly and purposefully — don't hold it longer than you need to.

**Goal:** Transition the puck out of danger and start your team's breakout.

**Your breakout options.** Read this as a **decision hierarchy for a retriever under pressure with no time to think**, **not as a ranking of breakout plays.** Run down it and take the first thing that is genuinely on. [Breakouts](../systems/breakouts.md) is explicit that the correct breakout is chosen by reading the shape of the forecheck in front of you, not picked off a list of favourites, and it is the authority. Full detail there.

- **Quick-up** — the counter, before their forecheck exists. In the two or three seconds right after your team wins the puck, the opposition is the least organised it will ever be; if a teammate is available up the ice, move it on your first touch. [Breakouts](../systems/breakouts.md) calls this the lowest-risk high-reward play available, and it is the one most defencemen never look for because their eyes go down instead of up. **First look up ice, then down.**
- **Up** — a direct pass to your winger on the boards — in the conventional swing breakout, **between the hash marks and the goal line**, though other breakouts set the winger elsewhere, so learn where yours stands. [Breakouts](../systems/breakouts.md) calls the up pass the fastest of the nine breakout options when the lane is open, though no count ranks breakout plays by speed. Take it when it's there.
- **Wheel** — skate the puck yourself around behind the net and up the far side. Good against a slow or one-man forecheck.
- **D-to-D** — pass across to your partner to change the angle of attack (see below).
- **Reverse** — send it back against the flow to a teammate arriving behind you. Beats an over-committed forechecker.
- **Rim** — hard around the boards to the far winger. Safe, but concedes possession more often than the others.
- **Off the glass and out** — no possession, but no goal against either, and always available when you are in trouble. **Three instructions go with it: off the glass and along it, never over it; treat the players' bench as a trap, not a gap; and under the IIHF book, clear with your stick, not your skate.** Over the glass from inside your own defensive zone is a two-minute delay-of-game minor under the NHL (Rule 63.2(iii)), the PWHL (65.2(iii)) and the IIHF (63.2(III)) whether you meant it or not, and **a deliberate one is the same minor from anywhere on the ice** (NHL 63.2(ii), IIHF 63.2(II), PWHL 65.2(ii)). Amateur defencemen take it constantly — coaching observation rather than a counted statistic. ⚠️ **USA Hockey (610(c)), Hockey Canada (10.1(ii) with 10.1(a)) and CARHA (75(b), CARHA-affiliated adult leagues) write only the deliberate half**, so under them a clear that gets away from you is not the automatic minor, and one you put out on purpose still is. **The strict limb has three written carve-outs:** boards with no glass above them, a puck *"shot into the players' bench"*, and one that goes out *"directly off a face-off"*. **The bench carve-out is the narrow one.** The NHL and PWHL penalise a puck *"shot over the glass 'behind' the players' bench"*, and the IIHF writes *"surrounding"* for *"behind"*, which appears to reach the glass at the sides too, though no source settles the reading. The IIHF Situation Handbook exempts only a puck shot *"**directly** into the Players' Bench (where there is no glass)"* (Situation 63.24): one that goes over the glass and drops into the bench is still a minor, so is a glove scoop over the glass behind it (63.28), and only a puck that clears lower penalty-bench glass is excused (63.3). The Handbook ranks below the Rulebook on its own Foreword, so read it as how the rule is officiated. **Under the IIHF, your skate is not outside the rule:** 63.2(III) names only the hand and stick, but the Handbook calls a kicked puck over the glass a minor (Situations 63.34 and 63.19, in its 2026/27 edition as in 2025/26), and 63.2(II) reaches a deliberate one *"(using their hand, leg, foot, arm, stick etc.)"* from anywhere. Whether the two documents agree turns on what *"deliberately"* attaches to, and the Handbook's answers have moved between editions — [Risk Management](../hockey-iq/risk_management.md) carries that pair in full. *(No PWHL or NHL situation handbook is held here, so the Handbook points are claimed for the IIHF only.)*

**D-to-D passing — and the one rule about it.** Moving the puck to your partner makes the forecheck reset and shift across, which is often all you need. But: **never pass D-to-D through the slot.** Go behind your own net so the puck travels below the goal line, where a lost puck is a battle behind the goal line rather than a shot from the slot. **Some teams instead exchange high in the zone, above the tops of the circles**, on the reasoning that an interception up there is not a slot chance — though it is still a loose puck at the top of your slot, which is not nothing. Those are two different routes, not a hard version and a soft one; ask your coach which yours uses. A D-to-D pass in front of the net that gets picked off is a point-blank chance against, and it is one of the most punished mistakes in the game — coaching emphasis, not a counted ranking.

![](diagram:defender-d-to-d-behind-the-net)

**When your partner has the puck, you are the second option and usually the voice.** Take a spot where a pass behind the net reaches you cleanly — usually the other side of the net, level with or just above the goal line — so that when the first option fails the puck has somewhere to go. You can see the forecheck arriving behind your partner when they cannot, so the calls — "up", "over", "wheel", "reverse", "rim", "time", "man on" — are mostly yours. Where you stand and who calls it are coaching choices; [Breakouts](../systems/breakouts.md) teaches this job in full, including where screening the net front for a wheel is and is not legal.

**Key considerations:** Don't carry through the slot either. If no pass is on, skate behind the net to the other side rather than going through the middle.

### Defensive Zone: Carrying the Puck — Under Pressure

```facts
Position: Along the boards or behind your own net, protecting yourself
Technique: Shield the puck with your body — don't panic
Options: Rim to your partner or the far-side winger, reverse it, or hold it tight behind the net until a teammate gets open
Read: Rim to beat a forechecker sealing you against the boards; reverse to beat one who has over-committed and skated past you
Never: Carry through the slot under pressure — a turnover in the highest-danger area is a direct scoring chance against
Never: Turn your back to the checker — USA Hockey warns him: "Even a light hit from behind could inflict a severe head or neck injury". Skates parallel, forearm and hip, head up, chin off your chest
Never: Duck when the check comes. Head up and chin off your chest instead
Risk: A tucked chin straightens the cervical spine, and the injury from that head-down, head-first collision with the boards or a goal post can occur at walking speed
```

**Where to be:** Protect yourself along the boards or behind the net.

**What to do:** Shield the puck with your body. Rim the puck (a hard shot along the boards that follows the curve of the rink around to a teammate further along) to your partner or the far-side winger, reverse it (send it back the way it came, against the direction the forecheck is flowing), or hold it tight behind the net until a teammate gets open. Don't panic.

![](diagram:breakout-rim)

![](diagram:breakout-reverse)

**Goal:** Maintain possession or safely exit the puck from danger.

**One thing overrides all of it: never turn your back to the checker, and never duck.** Going back for a puck with pressure behind you is routine for you, so this hazard is squarely yours, and these are two prohibitions against two different injuries. A turned back makes the contact a hit from behind that you can neither see nor brace for — USA Hockey's warning to the player delivering one is that *"Even a light hit from behind could inflict a severe head or neck injury."* Ducking tucks your chin to your chest and straightens the cervical spine, and USA Hockey records that the injury from that head-down, head-first collision with the boards or a goal post can occur at walking speed. **So arrive with your feet already turned:** skates parallel to the boards, contact on your forearm and hip — never on the point of your shoulder, and not the whole shoulder if you can help it (USA Hockey's checklist asks for *"No shoulder contact on the boards, if possible"*) — head up and chin off your chest. If you are already square to the wall and cannot turn in time, the last resort is to get your stick and gloves up and spread on the glass. [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md) covers the full three-case rule and the technique.

**Key considerations:** Never try to carry through the slot under pressure. The slot is the highest-danger area — a turnover there is a direct scoring chance for the opponent.

**Rim vs. reverse — pick by where the pressure is.** A rim beats a forechecker who is sealing you against the boards, because it goes past them to the next teammate around. A reverse beats a forechecker who has over-committed and skated past you, because it goes back into the space they just vacated. Reading which one you're facing is a core defenceman skill.

**Make that read while you're still skating back for the puck, not once it's on your stick.** You retrieve pucks under forecheck pressure more than anyone else on the ice, so the glance over your shoulder — checking which side the forecheck is arriving from while you're still moving toward the puck, behind the net or along the boards — is the one that turns rim-or-reverse into a decision you have already made rather than one you make with a forechecker already on you. [Scanning and Anticipation](../hockey-iq/scanning_and_anticipation.md) covers the habit and the research behind it; the figures are not repeated here.

#### The trapezoid — which books paint one

```facts
Rule: Under NHL and IIHF rules your goaltender may play the puck behind the goal line only inside the marked trapezoid; outside it is a two-minute delay-of-game minor (NHL 27.8, 63.2(viii); IIHF 27.7) — the rule text's one exception is skate contact with the crease, and the IIHF Handbook adds two more
Rule: The painted trapezoid begins eight feet outside each post at the goal line and reaches eleven feet outside each post at the end boards (NHL Rule 1.8) — Rink Map has the full geometry
Key: Measure the trapezoid from NHL Rule 1.8 and never from NHL 27.8 — name the book, because the IIHF numbers its own restricted area 27.7 and writes Rule 1.8's dimensions in metres
Key: NHL 27.8's "lines that begin six feet … from either goal post" is the narrower trapezoid painted before 2014-15, and those two feet a side are exactly the band where a rim dies
Key: NHL and IIHF play a trapezoid (the KHL too, unverified); USA Hockey, Hockey Canada and CARHA do not, so there the dead rim is your goalie's; the PWHL contradicts itself, so play as if it binds
Rule: No trapezoid still leaves your goalie a freezing bar — a minor under USA Hockey 614(c)(1) for covering the puck behind the goal line with the body clear of the crease, and under Hockey Canada's Interpretation 3 to Rule 10.1(a), clause v, once they leave the crease, "No warning will be issued"
Rule: CARHA, the one adult rec book held here, marks no restricted area at all — but 37(e) is a minor for a goalkeeper intentionally taking part in the play beyond the centre red line, and 58(b) for deliberately falling on the puck behind the goal line with the body clear of the crease
Rule: CARHA's 58(b) needs the puck behind the goal line; 61(b) does not — a deliberate hold anywhere causing an unnecessary stoppage is also a minor, reaching the corner in front of the line too
```

> **The trapezoid — this constrains everything you do behind your net.**
>
> Your goaltender may only play the puck behind the goal line **inside the marked trapezoid**; outside it, behind the line, it is a two-minute minor for delay of game (Rules 27.8 and 63.2(viii)). The NHL and IIHF have it — verified at NHL 1.8 and 27.8 and IIHF 27.7 — and the KHL is commonly said to as well, **though that one is unverified here: no KHL rulebook was available to check it against.** **Neither North American governing body's book has one** — the *USA Hockey Playing Rules 2025-29* and the *Hockey Canada Playing Rules 2026-2028* mark no goalkeeper's restricted area — and where there is no trapezoid the retrievals below stop being yours. [Rules Primer](../foundation/rules_primer.md) has where it does and does not apply. ⚠️ **Nor does the one adult recreational book held here, the *CARHA Hockey Official Rule Book*, 2026 edition — but it still stops your goaltender.** **Rule 37(e)** is a minor for one who *"intentionally participates in the play in any manner beyond the centre red line"*, **Rule 58(b)** a minor for deliberately falling on or gathering the puck behind the goal line with the body entirely outside the crease (the same shape as USA Hockey's 614(c)(1)), and ⚠️ **Rule 61(b)** a minor for a goalkeeper who *"deliberately holds the puck and in the opinion of the Referee, is causing an unnecessary stoppage of play"* — with no location condition, so it reaches the corner in front of the goal line too. CARHA governs CARHA-affiliated leagues only. ⚠️ **"Beer league" is not a rule set, so ask yours which book it runs, by name.** How much rec and youth hockey runs on any of these books is a question about leagues, and this document has no count for it.
>
> **What is actually painted.** Eight feet from each goal post at the goal line, widening to eleven feet — a 28-foot span — at the end boards (NHL Rule 1.8). NHL Rule 27.8 still reads the older *"six feet (6') from either goal post"*, from before the trapezoid was widened two feet a side for the 2014-15 season — so measure from 1.8, never from 27.8. [Rink Map](../foundation/rink_map.md#the-trapezoid-goalkeepers-restricted-area) owns the geometry. **That two-foot band on each side is exactly where a rim now dies** that would once have carried through.

**Under NHL and IIHF rules your goalie may play the puck behind the goal line only inside the marked trapezoid**, so a puck that dies in the corner is yours — plan the route before it arrives. **The rule carries one exception in its own words:** the minor is not assessed *"when a goalkeeper plays the puck while maintaining skate contact with his goal crease"* (NHL Rule 27.8; IIHF Rule 27.7 says it in materially identical words, writing *"their goal crease"* where the NHL writes *"his"*). There is no exception for being under pressure and none for a teammate being nearby. ⚠️ **That is the whole of the exception in the rule text — but the IIHF's *Situation Handbook* publishes two more, and both help your goalie.** A puck that comes off the glass and hits them out there is no penalty, because *"the goalkeeper must play the puck to be penalized"* (Situation 27.9); and the goal line itself is not in the restricted area, so a puck stopped on the line is legal — *"the puck must be over the goal line to be considered to be in the restricted area"* (Situation 27.6). Both are in the 2025/26 v1.1 and the 2026/27 Handbooks. **There is no NHL casebook to check the same question against**, so treat those two as IIHF rulings rather than as shared ones.

**Three of the six books paint no trapezoid at all, and that reverses the instruction above.** The *USA Hockey Playing Rules 2025-29*, the *Hockey Canada Playing Rules 2026-2028* and the *CARHA Hockey Official Rule Book* mark no goalkeeper's restricted area anywhere. ⚠️ **In a PWHL game play as though the trapezoid binds, because the book contradicts itself:** the *PWHL Official Rule Book 2025-2026* switches it off at **Rule 1.8**: *“No restricted Goalkeeper’s Area, which will be known as a ‘trapezoid’ will be enforced”*, repeated at its Rule 27.8 — *“The PWHL does not apply a restricted area for goalkeepers.”* But its **Rule 65.2(viii)** still makes it a minor for a goalkeeper who *"plays the puck outside of the designated area behind the net"*, and its goalkeeper summary table still lists *“Playing puck in restricted area”*, so treat the PWHL corner rim as yours. Under the other three the dead corner rim is your goalie's to fetch rather than yours — and a defenceman who sprints into that corner out of NHL habit is racing their own goaltender for a puck under forecheck pressure. ⚠️ **Find out which book your league runs before you plan a single retrieval.** **All six books bar a goalkeeper from taking part in the play beyond the centre red line** (CARHA's is Rule 37(e)), but they measure it differently: the NHL, the IIHF and the PWHL go on the position of the puck (NHL 27.7, IIHF 27.6, PWHL 27.7), USA Hockey and Hockey Canada on the goaltender's **skates** (USA Hockey Casebook Rule 407 Situation 13; Hockey Canada Interpretation 2 to Rule 4.13(c)) — so your goalie reaching back over the line for a puck still on your side is legal under the NHL and IIHF books and two minutes under USA Hockey or Hockey Canada. **And "no trapezoid" is not "no restriction":** under USA Hockey, Hockey Canada and CARHA your goalie may *play* that corner puck but may not freeze it, so expect it to come back to you moving — be an outlet, not a spectator. [Rules Primer](../foundation/rules_primer.md) covers both rules in full, and the retrieval section below sets out what each one does to your job.

#### The trapezoid — what it changes about your retrievals

```facts
Rule: Without a trapezoid your goalie may still not freeze the puck in the corner — USA Hockey Rule 614(c) confines that to the "goalkeeper's privileged area", which excludes the corners, and 614(c)(1) bars it anywhere behind the goal line with the body entirely outside the crease
Rule: Hockey Canada goes further than USA Hockey — a goaltender who leaves their crease "will not be allowed to freeze the puck… a Minor penalty. No warning will be issued" (Interpretation 3 to Rule 10.1(a), clause v) — but clause ii of the same Interpretation permits covering after a save
Rule: USA Hockey's Casebook draws the line your rim sits on — Situation 6 makes it a minor for your goalie to beat an attacker to a loose puck and fall on it, and Situation 12 says a cover behind the goal line or outside the privileged area "must be assessed"
Rule: A save is the other side of the freezing line — USA Hockey Casebook Rule 614(c) Situations 8, 10 and 11 answer no penalty for a cover taken in front of the goal line after stopping a shot while pressured, so do not shout at a goalie who has smothered a rebound
Rule: The IIHF numbers the trapezoid 27.7, not 27.8; in Britain assume it binds — England Ice Hockey's 2024-25 suspension in its own competitions ("not currently enforced", R&R 22.3) may no longer stand, and nothing the Elite League publishes switches it off
Rule: Past the centre red line the books split on measurement — NHL 27.7, IIHF 27.6 and PWHL 27.7 go on the puck's position, USA Hockey and Hockey Canada on your goalie's skates (USA Hockey Casebook 407 Situation 13; Hockey Canada 8.3(a)(ii)), so never call your goalie up for a loose puck there
Rule: All six write an intent word — "intentionally" glosses the NHL's and PWHL's 27.7 and the IIHF's 27.6, and sits in the bar itself in Hockey Canada 4.13(c) and CARHA 37(e); USA Hockey 407(c) says "deliberately", and its Casebook clears one "accidentally struck by the puck" (407, Situation 12)
Rule: Without a trapezoid the dead rim is your goalie's — but all six books make it a minor for them to take part beyond the centre red line with intent, and the NHL, IIHF and PWHL add that a check counts too (NHL and PWHL 27.7, IIHF 27.6, USA Hockey 407(c), Hockey Canada 4.13(c), CARHA 37(e))
Action: Plan your retrieval route before the puck arrives, and aim rims where a teammate can meet them, not into the corner
Convention: Without a trapezoid you can both legally reach a dead corner rim, so agree the call in the room — "I've got it" means your goalie takes it and you go to your breakout position, "leave it" means they stop it and leave it for you; the words vary by team
```

> What it means for you, on every retrieval:
> - **Your goalie cannot get a rim that dies in the corner.** You have to, under forecheck pressure. Plan your retrieval route before the puck arrives, not after — that decision is the difference between a breakout and a battle you lose facing your own end boards.
> - **A rim aimed straight into the corner is a gift to the forecheck.** Aim rims to arrive where a teammate can meet them, or keep them tight enough that they carry all the way around.
> - **Your goalie can still help you, just not out there.** They may stop a puck directly behind the net and leave it dead for you, or play it inside the trapezoid. A rim that stays close to the net is therefore retrievable with no race at all. Agree which of those you want — the call is **"leave it"**, and it has to come while you're still coming back, not as you arrive. See [Goaltender](goaltender.md) and [On-Ice Communication](../foundation/on_ice_communication.md).
> - **🇬🇧 If you play in Britain, find out before you plan any of this.** The IIHF book has the rule and numbers it **27.7**, not the NHL's 27.8 — but England Ice Hockey's own *Rules & Regulations* **22.3** say the Goalkeepers' Restricted Area is *"not currently enforced to allow ice rinks the necessary time to make the changes to implement this rule"*, and neither the current 2026-27 In-House Rules nor the 2025-26 edition before them mention it at all. That edition of the *Rules & Regulations* is 2024-25, so the published documents do not settle whether the suspension still stands — and nothing the Elite League publishes takes it out, since its *Casebook* leaves the rule to the 2026-27 IIHF book. **Play as though it binds, and ask your league.** **Look behind the net to see whether the lines are painted at all** — 22.3's own stated reason for the suspension is giving rinks time to repaint, so an unmarked sheet is a strong hint. It is a hint and not a ruling: IIHF 27.7 turns on *"the position of the puck"*, not on the markings. [UK and England Rules](../foundation/uk_rules.md) records the same uncertainty.
> - **If you play without a trapezoid, rebuild the habit.** Your goalie becomes a genuine third puck-handler behind the net, and the retrievals you have been sprinting to are no longer yours. Find out before your first shift. **"Behind the net" is the limit, though:** all six books bar a goalkeeper from participating *"in the play in any manner"* beyond the centre red line. ⚠️ **The NHL, IIHF and PWHL gloss that phrase as *"(intentionally plays the puck or checks an opponent)"*** (NHL 27.7; IIHF 27.6; PWHL 27.7), so a goalie who checks an opponent past the line takes the minor without touching the puck. USA Hockey 407(c) (*"deliberately participates"*) and Hockey Canada 4.13(c) put the intent word in the bar itself, and USA Hockey's *Casebook* clears a goalie *"accidentally struck by the puck"* (Rule 407, Situation 12). Those two judge it on the goalie's skates, but neither rule contains the word "skates": USA Hockey's test is in its *Casebook* (**Rule 407, Situation 13**: *"the determining factor is the position of the goalkeeper's skates and not the puck."*), and Hockey Canada's is **8.3(a)**, a minor for a goaltender who *"plays the puck with their skates over the centre red line"*, restated at Interpretation 2 to Rule 4.13(c).
>
>   **Under both North American books your goalie may play that corner puck but may not freeze it.** USA Hockey **614(c)** confines freezing to the *"goalkeeper's privileged"* area, which excludes the corners, and *"(1) The puck is behind the goal line and their body is entirely outside of the goal crease"* makes a smothered puck behind your own net a minor too. ⚠️ **Its *Casebook* cuts along the line between a retrieval and a save.** On the retrieval side — your rim — it runs against your goalie: rushing a loose puck to beat an attacker and falling on it is a minor (**Situation 6**: *"[t]he goalkeeper has an obligation to play the puck with their stick, just like any other player."*), and for a cover *"behind the goal line or outside of the privileged area"* the minor *"must be assessed"* (**Situation 12**). **On the save side it runs the other way:** a goalie who comes out and catches or traps a shot takes no penalty unless they had time to play it with no attacker near (**Situations 8 and 10**), and a dive on an uncontrolled rebound outside the crease is no penalty while attackers can pressure them (**Situation 11**). **So pressure decides the save side and location decides yours — never tell a goaltender to release a puck smothered after a save.** **Hockey Canada is blunter:** under its Interpretation 3 to Rule 10.1(a), once a goaltender leaves the crease *"they will not be allowed to freeze the puck. If they do, they will be assessed a Minor penalty. **No warning will be issued.**"* ⚠️ **But clause ii of the same Interpretation permits coming out *"to cut down the angle and catch the shot or, after stopping the shot, cover the puck"*, and nothing in the book says which governs a cover after a save outside the crease** — read together they restrict manufacturing a stoppage rather than a save, which is a reading, not a Hockey Canada ruling. What clause iv penalises with no warning is racing an attacker to a loose puck and *"simply jumps on the puck"* — the case your rim creates.
>
>   **Because you can both legally reach that puck,** ⚠️ **one of you calls it before you are both in the corner.** **"I've got it"** means your goaltender is taking it, so turn and go to your breakout position; **"leave it"** means they stop it and leave it for you. Either has to come while you are still coming back — and "I've got it" is not "I can sit on it". Agree both in the room; see [Goaltender](goaltender.md) and [On-Ice Communication](../foundation/on_ice_communication.md). Expect the puck back moving, and be an option rather than a spectator.

### Defensive Zone: Pressuring an Opponent with the Puck

```facts
Position: Wherever the puck is on your side — the corner and behind the net included
Action: Pressure the puck carrier directly, or angle them away from the net and the slot
Technique: Pin the puck, not the player, with your skate or stick for a second or two until help arrives — then keep it moving or play it off the wall, never hold it for a whistle
Rule: Freezing the puck on the boards for a whistle is a minor in every book — NHL and IIHF 63.2(i), PWHL 65.2(i) if deliberate; USA Hockey after three seconds (Casebook 632); HC and CARHA excuse it while you are being checked (10.1, 74(b)) but not to get the whistle (HC 6.12(b), CARHA 55(a) bench minor)
Rule: Pinning the player, not the puck, is priced — USA Hockey's Note to Rule 622 calls arms that pin an opponent to the boards holding, and IIHF women's Rule 101.1 bars using the boards to eliminate, push or pin her
Goal: Win the puck or force a bad play that your team can recover from
Convention: Carrier behind your net — hand him over at the post rather than chasing him round; it is a common default, not the only one, so ask which your team plays
Convention: Under low zone collapse the wingers stay high and the low battle is yours and the centre's; under man-on-man they follow their check into the corner — one of several coverage systems, and a coaching choice rather than a law of hockey
Action: Talk to your partner so you always know who is strong-side and who is weak-side, and find out which system you play before the puck drops
```

The two defenders work as a pair. The **strong-side defender** (the one on the same side as the puck) engages the puck carrier; the **weak-side defender** covers the net. As the puck moves around the zone, the two of you switch roles. This section describes the strong-side role; the next section describes the weak-side role.

**Where to be:** Wherever the puck is on your side — including in the corner or behind the net.

**What to do:** Pressure the puck carrier directly. **Pin the *puck* against the boards, not the player** — that is the distinction [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md) draws, USA Hockey's Note to **Rule 622** calls wrapping an arm round an opponent to pin them against the boards holding, and **IIHF women's Rule 101.1 prohibits using the boards to eliminate an opponent from the play, push her into the boards, or pin her along the boards** — or angle them away from the net and the slot. Angle them with your body and an active stick; pin the puck with your skate or stick blade for a second or two, until help arrives, then keep it moving or play it off the wall — never hold it for a whistle, because that is a minor in every book. The NHL, IIHF and PWHL call it when you deliberately cause the stoppage, USA Hockey after three seconds whether or not you are pressured, and Hockey Canada and CARHA excuse a puck tied up while you are being checked but still penalise a freeze made to get the whistle (CARHA as a bench minor). Never gather it into your body, a minor in all six books and a penalty shot when the puck is in your own crease (Hockey Canada 10.2(a)(v), 4.11(a)(v)). The center will support you in the corner — two of you against one puck carrier is a battle you should win.

**Goal:** Win the puck or force a bad play that your team can recover from.

**Key considerations:** Communicate with your defensive partner so you always know who is the strong-side and who is the weak-side defender.

**When their carrier takes the puck behind your net, do not chase him all the way round.** A defenceman trailing him out of the far side leaves him the slot. The common approach is a handover: the defenceman on the side he is heading for meets him at that post, stick on the ice, while the one who followed him in peels off to the front of the net, and your goaltender seals the post against the wraparound. Some teams keep one defenceman on him all the way round and send the partner to the net front instead. Ask your bench which yours plays, and call the handover out loud either way.

**Whether the wingers help you in the corner depends on the system your team plays** — this is a coaching choice, not a law of hockey. See [Defensive Zone Coverage](../systems/defensive_zone_coverage.md) for the alternatives. The two you'll meet most often:

- **Low zone collapse (zone coverage)** — the default assumed throughout. Wingers stay high covering the points, and the low battle belongs to the strong-side defenceman and the centre. Don't expect winger help in the corner.
- **Man-on-man** — wingers follow their assigned check wherever it goes, including into the corner. Here you *will* get help low, but the points may be left open when their man goes down.

Under either system, two things hold: if the puck escapes up the wall toward the strong-side winger, that's their breakout opportunity and they should engage it — that's not a corner battle. And if an opposing player drops off the point into the low battle, the winger covering them is responsible for tracking them down.

**Know which system you're playing before the puck drops.** On the coaching account a defensive-zone breakdown is not an effort problem but two players operating on different assumptions about who has the corner — **coaching emphasis, not a counted ranking**, because the public tracking that breaks goals down does it by the play the *offence* made rather than by the defensive breakdown that let them in. [Defensive Zone Coverage](../systems/defensive_zone_coverage.md) owns this and carries that limit. Communication is everyone's job; call out switches as you see them, regardless of position.

### Defensive Zone: Without the Puck — Weak-Side Coverage at the Goalmouth

```facts
Position: At the goalmouth, between any opposing forward and your goalie
Convention: Under low zone collapse you guard the goalmouth and the centre covers the high slot — two layers in front of the net, and a coaching choice rather than a rule; under man-on-man each of you takes your assigned man and either layer may be anyone's; other systems differ again, so ask which yours is
Never: Get drawn to the puck before somebody has the goalmouth — both of you there leaves the screen, the tip-in and the tap-in unmarked; some systems do send you both low, so know which yours is
Action: When the puck crosses the middle you become the strong-side defender and your partner the weak-side, so adjust together and keep one of you on the puck and one at the goalmouth
```

*The opposition still has the puck here. This section describes the **weak-side defender** role — the defender on the opposite side from the puck — which is the other half of the pair job described immediately above.*

**Where to be:** At the goalmouth — the immediate front-of-net area, between any opposing forward and your goalie. You and the center form two layers of coverage in front of the net: you guard the goalmouth (the screen, the tip-in threat), and the center covers the high slot (the player one layer further out, between the dots and the top of the circles) — until the center goes into the corner battle, when the weak-side winger drops in to take it, as *The high-slot rotation* below sets out. **That split is a low zone collapse — the coverage assumed throughout this document, and a coaching choice rather than a rule.** Under **man-on-man** each of you takes the man you are assigned and either layer may be anyone's, and other systems answer it differently again — [Defensive Zone Coverage](../systems/defensive_zone_coverage.md) sets out who has the net front under each of the systems it names — so ask which your team plays.

**What to do:** Cover the goalmouth. Don't get drawn to the puck — your partner and the center are handling it.

**Goal:** Make sure no opponent is sitting unmarked at the goalmouth.

**Key considerations:** When the puck moves to the other side of the zone, you become the strong-side defender and your partner becomes the weak-side. Both of you must adjust together so there is always one defender on the puck and one at the goalmouth. The mistake to guard against is both defenders being drawn to the puck and leaving the net front empty — nobody on the screen, nobody on the tip-in, and an unmarked tap-in at the goalmouth to follow.

#### Net-front technique — holding the inside

```facts
Technique: Never walk a screener out — they have no puck, so it can be called interference in any game. Hold the inside, no lean, no push; lift the stick low, well below the hands as the puck comes, let go
Convention: "Position beats strength" is a tendency, not a law — coaching consensus rather than a measured finding. Plan the shift around lifting their stick, not out-leveraging somebody much bigger
Rule: No body checking is not no contact — USA Hockey's Note to 604(c) protects players "focused on gaining possession of the puck" and "simply maintaining established body position", but penalizes "deliberate physical contact … with no effort to legally play the puck": hold your spot, push nobody
Rule: Driving an opponent off a position they have established is what is barred — USA Hockey 604's Note 1 makes intentional contact using "overt hip, shoulder, forearm or torso action" an illegal check, at least a minor at 12U and below, all girls'/women's and all non-check adult classes (604(a), (c))
Rule: USA Hockey's Competitive Contact list is a floor — 604(a) lets a local governing body bar body checking in any classification, 604(b) bars it whenever a Competitive Contact team plays a Body Checking team, and its Blind Hockey rules bar it at every age. Check the game, not the age group
Rule: A body check in a Competitive Contact category is not capped at a minor — USA Hockey 604(d) mandates a major plus a game misconduct where it "recklessly endangers an opponent", so plan on a match (602(a)); 604(e) allows one for a reckless check there or in a non-check Adult classification
Rule: Hockey Canada prices the push that moves a screener at U13 and below, in female hockey and in any division a Member has approved — 7.3(a) is a minor, and 7.3(b) is a major plus a game misconduct on the degree of violence with nobody hurt, mandatory on injury
Rule: Hockey Canada 7.3(c) is a match penalty for a player who "deliberately attempts to or deliberately injures an opponent by body-checking" — the attempt alone reaches it, and nobody need be hurt
Rule: The walk-out can be called interference even where checking is legal, because a screener has no puck: NHL and IIHF 56.1 and 56.2(i), PWHL 57.1 and 57.2(i), Hockey Canada 8.3, USA Hockey 625(a)(4), and Rule 625 announces no Competitive Contact scope in its title the way Rule 604 does
Rule: A walk-out is not capped at a minor: a major on violence under NHL 56.4 and PWHL 57.4 (a game misconduct if it injures), IIHF 56.4 if reckless, major plus game misconduct under Hockey Canada 8.3(b) and CARHA 66(a); USA Hockey as cross-checking or roughing (609(b), 640(g)) — plan on a match (602(a))
Rule: In CARHA adult leagues 49(a) is a minor or, on intent and violence, a major for failing to "avert body contact with an opponent", mandatory if intentional contact injures; a major ejects you with at least a one-game ban (30(a), 32(d)). Take the position, lift the stick, lean on nobody
Rule: In British women's hockey any major ends your game, at every age and adult included — the IIHF's bare majors, net-front interference under 56.4 among them, draw an additional game misconduct "in all cases" (In-House Rule 101, which states no age scope)
Rule: In women's hockey a player may "hold their ground" any time they have "established their position on the ice" (IIHF 101.1), so get there first; it bars the move onto them: "any move by a Player to step or glide into an opposing Player" is at least a minor, with a major and game misconduct above it
```

"Clear the front of the net" is the instruction every defenceman hears and almost nobody is taught, and it is where your position and the rulebook meet most often. **The technique is owned in full by [Body Contact and Battles → Defending the net front](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md)** — the six-step order of operations, Mike Corbett's coaching on controlling sticks rather than bodies, and the cross-check rule you are most likely to break here by accident.

**The two steps that do the work apply wherever you play:** take the inside and lift the stick. ⚠️ **Driving them off their spot is never one of the steps** — it can be called interference wherever you play, checking game or not, because the screener has no puck. ⚠️ **Where checking is barred it is an illegal check too, and the minor is not the ceiling.** USA Hockey **604(c)** makes it a minor in every Competitive Contact classification — 12 and under and below, all girls'/women's, all non-check adult (604(a)) — with **604(d)** a major plus a game misconduct for a player who *"recklessly endangers an opponent with a body check in a Competitive Contact category"*, and **604(e)** allowing a match for reckless endangerment there or in a non-check Adult classification. Hockey Canada **7.3(a)** is a minor for a player who *"intentionally body-checks, bumps, shoves, or pushes any opposing player"* at U13 and below, in female hockey and in any division a Member approves; **7.3(b)** a major plus a game misconduct on the degree of violence, with nobody hurt, **compulsory where the check injures**; and **7.3(c)** a match. In women's hockey it is an illegal hit under **IIHF Rule 101.1**, *"at least a minor penalty (2')"*, with a major (5') plus an automatic game misconduct above it. It matters most at the goalmouth, because the player you shove falls into your own goaltender or the post.

The picture below is that screener case, and nothing in it moves: stand beside them on the inside, lift the stick, and move nobody into or across the shaded goal frame and goalie. The corner direction further down is for a different moment — a loose-puck battle, or a legal check on a forward who has the puck.

![](diagram:net-front-walk-out-direction)

**Where checking is legal the walk-out can still be called interference, because the player you would be moving has no puck.** A body check is delivered on an opponent *in control of the puck*; a net-front screener is not, so what reaches you is **interference**. **NHL and IIHF 56.1** and **PWHL 57.1** give you the ice you stand on — the NHL's *"[a] player is allowed the ice he is standing on (body position) and is not required to move in order to let an opponent proceed"* — and let you block a path only *"provided he is in front of his opponent and moving in the same direction"*, penalising *"Moving laterally and without establishing body position, then making contact with the non-puck carrier"*. **56.2(i)** (PWHL **57.2(i)**) is the minor for impeding an opponent *"who is not in possession of the puck"*. The quoted wording is the NHL's; the IIHF and PWHL differ in pronouns, and the IIHF puts quotation marks round its defined terms. **USA Hockey 625(a)** is a minor for the same act, and **625(a)(4)** names this exact play — *"[a] defending player who changes their skating lane or foot speed in an effort to play the body of an opponent who is no longer in control of the puck."* ⚠️ **Unlike Rule 604, Rule 625 announces no Competitive Contact scope in its title, so it reaches you in a checking game too.**

**CARHA leaves a defenceman worse off.** Its **66(a)(1)** minor covers the same act, and the **Note 2** under it entitles defenders to *"'stand their ground' or 'shadow' the attacking players"* — ⚠️ **but says nothing about the body you put on them, and Rule 49(a) prices that:** a minor or a major, *"based upon the intent and the violence of the impact"*, for a player who *"intentionally bodies, pushes, shoves, stands in front of an opponent for the purpose of making contact, and/or does not avert body contact with an opponent"*, the major mandatory *"[w]hen injury results from intentional body contact"*, and the casebook adds *"a Match penalty, if warranted"*. A CARHA major ends your game (Rule 30(a)). ⚠️ **That is a duty to avert contact, not a licence to hold your spot by it** — take the position, lift the stick, and expect no protection for the body you put on them.

**What it costs.** ⚠️ **None of these stops at a minor.** USA Hockey's Rule 625 has no tier above its minor, but that caps the rule, not the act: a walk-out that becomes a body check can be roughing under **Rule 640(b)** (every classification except Adult Male), where **640(g)** is a major plus a game misconduct for reckless endangerment; a two-handed shaft shove is a cross-check, with the same tier under **609(b)**; and a reckless walk-out of any kind is a match under the general Rule **602(a)**. NHL **56.4** and PWHL **57.4** allow a major *"based on the degree of violence"*, with a game misconduct where it injures (NHL 56.5; PWHL 57.5). The IIHF's **56.4** is a major where the player *"recklessly endangers the fouled Player"* — a bare major that leaves you in the game — with the game misconduct (56.5) only where a major would not be sufficient. ⚠️ **In British women's hockey that bare major ends your game anyway, at every age and adult included:** In-House Rule 101 adds a game misconduct to any major *"in all cases"* and states no age scope. **CARHA 66(a)** allows a major and a game misconduct at the referee's discretion, and **Hockey Canada 8.3(b)** a major plus a game misconduct on the degree of violence — **compulsory where it injures** — with **8.3(c)** a match. **So take the position first:** an established position is what 56.1 protects, and driving from one you never established is what it penalises. [Body Contact and Battles → Defending the net front](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md#9-net-front-battles) owns the full text.

**USA Hockey's list is not the whole of it.** **604(a)** lets a local governing body prohibit body checking in any classification, and **604(b)** prohibits it any time a Competitive Contact team plays a Body Checking team — so a checking-age team can be in a no-checking game for the night purely because of who it drew. ⚠️ **Its *Disabled Hockey* section also prohibits body checking in all Blind Hockey games**, with no age or classification limb, and a Blind Hockey roster can be all-adult. ⚠️ **Check the game, not the age group, and ask your affiliate.** ⚠️ **In Britain neither the IIHF book nor the In-House Rules sets a checking age — your competition's Rules of Competition does, so ask your league:** IHUK's junior leagues play U10 and U12 non-checking and U14, U16 and U19 checking, and its women's league is non-checking.

**Read that prohibition with the permission beside it, because the net front is where the rulebook singles it out.** USA Hockey's Note to **604(c)** says Competitive Contact *"does not mean 'no contact'"*: legal competitive contact *"occurs when players are focused on gaining possession of the puck and are simply maintaining established body position"*, most often *"in front of the goal or along the boards"*. ⚠️ **But *"deliberate physical contact with an opponent, with no effort to legally play the puck, shall be penalized."*** So the lift is yours in every league while it stays a lift, and the box-out is written as legal in four of the six books — this Note, NHL and IIHF 56.1, and PWHL 57.1. **IIHF Rule 101.1** draws the same line for women's hockey: players may *"'hold their ground' any time that they have established their position on the ice"*. What none of the six permits is driving somebody off a spot they already hold, checking game or not. ⚠️ **Under Hockey Canada the box-out is unwritten rather than written-legal**, and a box-out that stops a forward getting where he is going can be called: Rule 8.3 makes it interference to impede *"the progress of an opponent, who is not in possession of the puck"*, and the book mentions body position only in passing, in Rule 8.1. **CARHA runs the other way** — Rule 49(a) is a duty to avert contact. ⚠️ **Do not stop defending the net front because you may not check there — an unmarked screener is the danger this whole section is about.**

Read that section before you next play this role, and read [Body Contact and Battles → Which Contact Rules Are You Actually Playing Under?](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md#1-which-contact-rules-are-you-actually-playing-under) with it, because that is where which of these books you are under gets settled. One thing in it that a defenceman in particular gets wrong:

- **"Position beats strength" is a tendency, not a law** — [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md) says so explicitly, flags it as coaching consensus rather than a measured law with nothing in the ice hockey biomechanics literature quantifying it, and holds that against a large enough strength gap it fails. Do not plan a shift around out-leveraging somebody much bigger; plan it around tying up their stick.

#### Net-front technique — which way a body goes

```facts
Never: Let a battle or a legal check carry a body across your own goalmouth — that path has a goal post at one edge and your goaltender at the other. Take it toward the corner instead
Never: Lift a net-front opponent off their edges, even in a legal check on a forward who has the puck — a player whose skates are off the ice cannot brace or choose what meets the post first
Rule: CARHA 53(a) ejects for a hit to his back, a mandatory match into boards or goal if he can't protect himself (53(b)); USA Hockey 608(b) ejects if head first, and if reckless plan on a match (602(a)); NHL and PWHL 43.3 and 43.5 too; IIHF 43.3 at discretion
Rule: Kept off his head, or head first into boards or frame, a hit to his back ejects under Hockey Canada 7.5(a) and (e), a mandatory match into boards or goal if he can't protect himself (7.5(c)); on his head, it is head contact (7.6; 7.7 in Junior/Senior), a match if he can't protect himself
Rule: Hockey Canada head contact from behind: a match if he can't protect himself; a mandatory major and game misconduct for a high cross-check at any level, if he's hurt in minor and female hockey (7.6), or in Junior/Senior if highly violent or head-injured off glass, ice or boards (7.7)
```

**You never walk a screener out, but bodies still move at the goalmouth** — in a loose-puck battle, or a legal check on a forward who has the puck. ⚠️ **Whenever a body moves there, take it toward the corner and not across the front of your own net.** That path has a steel post at one edge and your goaltender at the other, and the player on it is going sideways or backwards and sees neither. USA Hockey writes the goal post into the head-first spinal-injury mechanism alongside the boards, and says that injury *"can occur at walking speed"*. **The corner direction is coaching caution rather than a rule**; what the rules reach is the worst version, a hit on his back, and that is never two minutes. **USA Hockey 608(b)** is a major plus a game misconduct where a check from behind *"causes them to go head first into the boards or goal frame"*, **608(c)** a match once it recklessly endangers a defenceless opponent with excessive force, and if it is reckless at all, plan on the match (**602(a)**). **Hockey Canada 7.5** is at least a minor plus a game misconduct (7.5(a)), and a match if it puts him into the boards or goal frame unable to protect himself (7.5(c)); **once it reaches his head it is head contact too (Rule 7.6; 7.7 in Junior and Senior), and the harsher price stands** — up to a match, as [Gap control — the method](#gap-control--the-method) sets out. **CARHA 53(b)** is a match where he cannot protect himself, and referees are *"instructed not to substitute other penalties when a player is checked from behind in any manner"*. **The NHL, IIHF and PWHL have no minor for checking from behind (43.2 in each).** The NHL and PWHL leave a major with a mandatory game misconduct (**43.3**, **43.5**) — though their 43.1 covers a player *"unable to protect or defend himself"* (the PWHL's *"herself"*), so a push on a player who could brace may be called boarding or cross-checking instead, each with a discretionary major (NHL **41.3** and **59.3**, PWHL **41.3** and **60.3**). The IIHF's major and game misconduct are the referee's call on reckless endangerment (**43.3**), and boarding or charging (**41**, **42**) still reach it otherwise. **In most books it ends your night.** ⚠️ **And whatever contact happens there, their skates stay on the ice** — a player whose edges are gone cannot brace or choose what meets the post first. **No book here penalises that lift on its own:** the nearest, IIHF **Rule 48.1(III)** on *"[a] Player who extends their body upward or outward in order to reach their opponent"*, is a limb of the head-contact rule and reaches you only *"when a Player checks an opponent"* — and, read from the rule's title and preamble rather than the sub-clause itself, only where the head or neck is contacted — so a box-out is outside it. **The reason to keep them on their edges is the collision, not the tariff.** [Body Contact and Battles → Receiving a Hit Safely](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md#6-receiving-a-hit-safely) is written for the player on the other end of it, and [Goaltender](goaltender.md) for the one behind you.

#### Net-front technique — the stick

```facts
Technique: Body position first, stick second — a stick lift beats a cross-check at everything, including staying on the ice. Lift up, not sideways, low on the shaft, below their bottom hand, then let go
Rule: A two-handed shaft shove is a cross-check whatever your blade is doing — NHL 59.1, IIHF 59.1, PWHL 60.1 and the Hockey Canada and CARHA glossaries carry no blade-on-ice element, and only USA Hockey Rule 609's Note builds "no portion of the stick on the ice" into the definition
Rule: USA Hockey's "no portion of the stick on the ice" element is no licence to shove with the blade down — Rule 609(b) makes a major plus a game misconduct mandatory where a cross-check recklessly endangers an opponent, so plan on a match too (609(c), 602(a))
Rule: Lift the stick as the shot or pass comes, and let it go — the NHL, IIHF and PWHL make you "immediately release the stick". Holding one is a minor in five books, and Hockey Canada 8.1(b) makes a hold that injures a major plus a game misconduct
Rule: In CARHA adult leagues holding a stick is at least a minor — 63(a) may allow a major (its text is unclear), 63(b) makes a hold that injures one, and either major ejects you (30(a))
Rule: A stick lift at or near the hands is hooking in three of the six books — on location under IIHF 55.1, whose next sentence exempts stick-to-stick-only contact while checking; on effect under Hockey Canada 8.2(a) and in USA Hockey 623's own Note. Take it low, well below their bottom hand
Rule: A misjudged lift is not only hooking — a forceful chop on the stick or at the hands is slashing in every book here: the NHL, IIHF 61.1, PWHL 63.1 and USA Hockey 634 name the hands, and Hockey Canada 9.3 and CARHA exempt only a tap on the puck-carrier's stick, which a screener is not
Rule: USA Hockey 634's Note has no attempt-to-play-the-puck let-out on a chop at the hands, which the NHL, IIHF and PWHL leave to the referee. A reckless slash is a 634(b) major plus game misconduct — plan on a match (602(a)) — and CARHA 80(a) allows an ejecting major on any slash (30(a))
Rule: No book caps hooking at a minor — a major plus a game misconduct on injury under NHL 55.3 and 55.4, Hockey Canada 8.2(b) and (e), PWHL 56.3 and 56.4 and CARHA 64(b) with 30(a), and on reckless endangerment under IIHF 55.3 and USA Hockey 623(b), plus a USA Hockey match to plan on (602(a))
Rule: A CARHA major for a stick foul ejects you with at least a one-game ban (30(a), 32(d)) — 63(b) injurious holding, 64(b) hooking and 80(a) slashing each reach one; an accidental high stick escapes the ban, and the ejection unless it injures (62(b)) (CARHA-affiliated adult leagues only)
```

Two more things a defenceman gets wrong at the net front are both about the stick, and **neither depends on whether checking is legal in your game** — USA Hockey's cross-checking, holding and hooking rules (**609**, **622** and **623**) carry no classification clause where its body-checking rule, **604(a)**, writes one in terms. These reach you whatever you are allowed to do with your body.

- **The order really is body position first and stick second.** A stick lift beats a cross-check at everything, including staying on the ice — **timed for the shot or pass, lifted up rather than sideways, and taken low on the shaft, well below their bottom hand**. ⚠️ **And it is a lift, not a hold — let the stick go once it is up.** The lift itself is good defensive play: USA Hockey’s casebook answers *"Yes. A player may lift the opponent’s stick at any time provided they do not impede their progress"*, and Hockey Canada allows it *"for the purpose of making a play on the puck"*.

  **Holding it is a different offence, and all six books price it.** The NHL’s and IIHF’s **54.2** and the PWHL’s **55.2** let you defend against a stick, but the NHL’s wording is that *"[h]e must immediately release the stick and allow the player to resume normal play"*, and the other two say the same; Hockey Canada **8.1** and USA Hockey’s Note to **622** bar holding it too. **CARHA 63(a)** makes it a minor or, at the referee’s discretion, a major, and a CARHA major ends your game (Rule 30(a)) — though ⚠️ **63(a)’s next sentence says a hold of the stick is *"recorded and announced"* as a minor, and the book does not say whether that caps the tariff or only fixes what is announced.** ⚠️ **A hold that injures is a mandatory major under CARHA 63(b), which ejects you, and a mandatory major plus a game misconduct under Hockey Canada 8.1(b).**

  **At the hands.** ⚠️ **Three of the six books reach a lift at the hands as hooking.** The **IIHF** does it on location alone — *"If the stick goes against the opponent's hands / or near the opponent's hands, it shall be penalized as 'hooking'"* (Rule 55.1), and that is the book a British player is under. ⚠️ **But 55.1’s next sentence exempts a player checking another with only stick-to-stick contact, which is what a lift under the shaft is.** The book does not reconcile the two and no IIHF interpretation reconciling them is held here, so the caution may be stricter than the rule; low is still the answer. **Hockey Canada and USA Hockey turn on effect:** a lift that contacts the hands and impedes a pass or shot is hooking under Hockey Canada (Interpretation 1 to Rule 8.2(a)), and USA Hockey’s Note to **Rule 623** includes *"a stick placed on the hand/arm that takes away the ability for the opponent to pass or shoot the puck with a normal amount of force"* — hooking there whether you meant it or not.

  **The NHL’s hooking rule names no opponent’s hands,** ⚠️ **but slashing covers them:** **NHL 61.1**, **IIHF 61.1** and **PWHL 63.1** make *"[a]ny forceful or powerful chop with the stick on an opponent’s body, the opponent’s stick, or on or near the opponent’s hands"* slashing when it is not an attempt to play the puck. ⚠️ **USA Hockey’s Note to Rule 634 is stricter — it has no attempt-to-play-the-puck let-out at all.** **Hockey Canada 9.3** and **CARHA**’s glossary exempt only a tap on the **puck-carrier’s** stick to take the puck — ⚠️ **and a screener is not the puck-carrier, so that exemption is not yours.**

  **What it costs.** ⚠️ **Neither offence stops at a minor in any of the six.** Hooking reaches a major plus a game misconduct on injury (NHL **55.3** and **55.4**; Hockey Canada **8.2(b)** and **8.2(e)**; PWHL **56.3** and **56.4**; **CARHA 64(b)** with **30(a)**) or on reckless endangerment (IIHF **55.3**; USA Hockey **623(b)**). Slashing reaches a major on the severity of the act alone under the NHL (**61.3**, with a game misconduct, **61.5**), the PWHL (**63.3**, compulsory on injury, with **63.5** a game misconduct and **63.4** a match) and Hockey Canada (**9.3(b)**, a major plus a game misconduct, compulsory on injury, with **9.3(c)** a match); and on reckless endangerment under the IIHF (**61.3**, with an automatic game misconduct) and USA Hockey (**634(b)**, a major plus a game misconduct). Under USA Hockey, plan on a match on top for a reckless hook or slash (**623(c)**, **634(c)**, **602(a)**). ⚠️ **CARHA is not the cheap one:** **80(a)** allows a major at the referee’s discretion on an ordinary slash with nobody hurt, **80(b)** makes it mandatory on injury and **80(d)** is a match — and a CARHA major ends your game with at least a one-game suspension (**30(a)**, **32(d)**). **Take the lift low on the shaft, well below their bottom hand, in every book**: no contact at the hands for the hooking sentence to catch, and no chop for the slashing one. [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md) owns this.
- **Blade on the ice does not make it legal — in five of the six books.** **NHL 59.1**, **IIHF 59.1** and **PWHL 60.1** define cross-checking as using *"the shaft of the stick between the two hands to forcefully check an opponent"*, with no blade element. Hockey Canada’s and CARHA’s glossaries leave out *"forcefully"*, and Hockey Canada’s **Interpretation 1 to Rule 9.2(a)** puts a test back: *"a striking motion must be penalized."* Under those five a two-handed shaft shove is a cross-check whatever your blade is doing. ⚠️ **USA Hockey is the exception, in its own definition:** the Note to **Rule 609** adds *"with no portion of the stick on the ice."* ⚠️ **That is not permission to shove with the blade down** — **609(b)** makes a major plus a game misconduct mandatory where a cross-check *"recklessly endangers an opponent"*, so plan on a match too (**609(c)**, **602(a)**), and a shove is also reachable as boarding (**603**), checking from behind (**608**) or roughing (**640**). In any book, do not build a net-front habit on the shaft: position first, then lift the stick.

#### The high-slot rotation

```facts
Read: Under low zone collapse, puck in the strong-side corner and the centre gone to the battle — the weak-side winger collapses to the high slot; under man-on-man they stay with their check
Action: Find out which system you play; in a collapse, call for the weak-side winger's rotation, and if it doesn't come, hold the goalmouth anyway and call louder
Read: If their weak-side defenceman has come off the point and your weak-side winger is following him, the rotation is not available — you and your partner cover the slot and call out anyone arriving
```

Stay disciplined and hold your position. **This rotation belongs to a low zone collapse rather than to every system.** When the puck is in the strong-side corner and the center has gone to help in the battle, the weak-side winger should collapse from the point to cover the high slot (unless that winger is already following their own man down — see exception below). Anyone with a clear view should call for the rotation — you, the center moving away, or the winger themselves. If for any reason it doesn't happen, hold the goalmouth anyway and call louder — an unmarked tap-in at the goalmouth is more dangerous than a high-slot shot through traffic. **Under man-on-man that winger stays with their check and no rotation is coming**, so find out which your team plays before you wait for one.

![](diagram:dz-collapse-high-slot)

**Exception, still inside the collapse — weak-side winger is already low:** If the weak-side opposing defenseman has come down off the point and the weak-side winger is following their man, the high slot rotation isn't available. In that case, the responsibility falls to you and your partner to be more vigilant about anyone arriving in the slot — call out anything you see entering the high-danger area.

## Neutral Zone

The transition zone. Your job is to prevent the opponent from entering your zone cleanly.

---

### Neutral Zone: Carrying the Puck — Free Space

```facts
Position: Moving up through the middle of the ice or along the boards
Action: Carry with confidence and look to pass to a forward to push play into the offensive zone
Options: Pass to a forward, or use the boards to chip the puck ahead to a forward in stride
Goal: Transition the puck through the neutral zone efficiently
Never: Stickhandle through the middle under pressure
Mindset: Simple and safe beats clever and risky in the neutral zone
Read: If no forward is open up the ice, regroup — pass D-to-D so your forwards can swing back and come again with speed
```

**Where to be:** Moving up through the middle of the ice or along the boards.

**What to do:** Carry with confidence and look to make a pass to a forward to push play into the offensive zone. Alternatively, use the boards to chip the puck ahead to a forward in stride.

**Goal:** Transition the puck through the neutral zone efficiently.

**Key considerations:** Don't stickhandle through the middle under pressure. Simple and safe beats clever and risky in the neutral zone.

**When every forward is covered, regroup rather than force it.** A D-to-D pass across the ice at your blue line resets the play: it gives your forwards time to swing back and come again with speed, and it makes their forecheckers turn and skate. Forcing a pass into a lane that is already shut is how a neutral-zone turnover becomes a rush against you with you as the last man back. Where your forwards swing on a regroup is a coaching choice, so ask what your team runs.

---

### Neutral Zone: Carrying the Puck — Under Pressure

```facts
Position: Near the boards or just inside your own blue line
Action: Move the puck quickly
Options: A bank off the boards, a pass to a forward, or a dump-in
Goal: Keep possession or relieve pressure safely
Never: Try to carry through heavy pressure
Risk: The neutral-zone middle is an expensive place to lose the puck — coaching material puts the making of odd-man rushes there, though nothing published classifies them by the play that created them
Mindset: When in doubt, go simple
```

**Where to be:** Near the boards or just inside your own blue line.

**What to do:** Move the puck quickly. A bank off the boards, a pass to a forward, or a dump-in are all good options. Don't try to carry through heavy pressure.

**Goal:** Keep possession or relieve pressure safely.

**Key considerations:** The neutral zone middle is a dangerous place to lose the puck, and it is where coaching material puts the making of odd-man rushes — where the attackers outnumber the defenders coming back, e.g. 2-on-1 or 3-on-2, a high-danger scoring chance. **That is coaching consensus rather than a tracked figure: nothing published classifies odd-man rushes by the play that created them**, and not every one starts here — [Defending the Rush](../systems/defending_the_rush.md) owns how they are made and lists five causes, one of which is in the offensive zone. When in doubt, go simple.

---

### Neutral Zone: Pressuring an Opponent with the Puck

```facts
Position: Between the puck carrier and your net, facing the oncoming play, closing the distance as they come
Read: Step up at the blue line only if you are confident you can win the puck or force a turnover — otherwise back up under control and deny a clean entry
Goal: Prevent a clean zone entry; force a dump-in or a turnover
Risk: A step-up that misses leaves your partner alone in a 2-on-1
Never: Step up in the neutral zone unless you are confident you can win the puck — confidence, not hope
Never: Read a head-down carrier as a licence to arrive harder — it tells you they cannot change direction, and nothing more
Convention: How aggressive "confident" should be is a coaching choice — an aggressive neutral-zone press wants you challenging the line, a passive trap wants you holding
Rule: A step-up finished as a hit is what the charging rules are written for — USA Hockey's Casebook rules the head-down case charging (607, Situation 2) and a check you accelerate through without the two fast strides (Situation 3)
Rule: Charging binds every division, checking or not — USA Hockey 607 and Hockey Canada 7.4 name no division — and none of the six books caps it at a minor: USA Hockey's floor is a minor plus a misconduct (607(a)), a reckless charge a mandatory major plus game misconduct (607(b)): plan on a match (602(a))
```

**Where to be:** Between the puck carrier and your net, facing the oncoming play, closing the distance as they come.

**What to do:** Step up at the blue line to challenge only if you are confident you can win the puck or force a turnover. Otherwise, back up under control and deny a clean entry. Support from the neutral-zone forwards is important here.

**Goal:** Prevent a clean zone entry. Force a dump-in or a turnover.

**Key considerations:** Don't step up in the neutral zone unless you are confident you can win the puck — confidence, not hope. If you step up and miss, you leave your partner alone in a 2-on-1 (two attackers against one defender, with the defender forced to choose between covering the shot or the pass). When in doubt, maintain your position and back up under control. **How aggressive "confident" should be is a coaching choice** — an aggressive neutral-zone press wants you challenging at the line, a passive trap wants you holding and steering the carrier wide. [Defending the Rush](../systems/defending_the_rush.md) sets out the trade-off in full; ask which risk your coach wants to carry.

**That trade-off is about rushes against you. It is not the whole of what "challenge" costs, and the other half is a rulebook one.** A step-up is a closing of space — your feet keep moving backwards and the attacker stays in front of you. ⚠️ **Where one is finished as a hit instead, that is the geometry the charging rules are written for**, and a carrier with their head down is the picture they name. **The read is about their vision, not about how hard you arrive:** it tells you they cannot change direction, so your body gets there first, and nothing more. The books, the stride counts that disagree, and what charging actually costs are set out under ["Step up" and "pinch" are two different plays](#step-up-and-pinch-are-two-different-plays) below and in full in [Defending the Rush → Standing up (stepping up)](../systems/defending_the_rush.md#standing-up-stepping-up).

#### Gap control — the method

```facts
Key: Gap control is the defining skill of the position — your gap is the distance between you and the puck carrier
Risk: Too big a gap gives them a free run at you with speed and options; too small and one move puts them past you
Action: Start your feet before they reach you — you cannot close a gap from a standstill, then tighten as they cross the red line and again at your own blue line; Defending the Rush covers the distances
Read: Watch their chest and hips, not the puck, hands or head — the torso has to point where the body is actually going
Action: Take away the middle and force them outside, where the wall becomes a second defender — but what the boards take away is the carrier's ice, not the carrier
Rule: Steer the carrier to the boards with your route and add no bump, push or shove — legal in terms under Hockey Canada's Interpretation 1 to 7.3(a) (no bump), USA Hockey's Glossary "Angling" and PWHL 52.1, an inference elsewhere; IIHF women's 101.1 bars pinning her there
Rule: In women's hockey IIHF 101.1 makes "Any move by a Player to step or glide into an opposing Player" at least a minor — carry the angle with your feet and never finish it with a step into her
Rule: Riding them into the wall is barred outright in a great many classifications — USA Hockey 604(a) at 12U and below, in all Girls'/Women's and all non-check Adult, and Hockey Canada 7.3(a) at U13 and below, female hockey and any Member-approved division; steering with your route stays legal there
Rule: Riding them into the wall with your body is a different act, and defenceless is not the whole test: USA Hockey 603's base sentence asks no such question, and the NHL, IIHF and Hockey Canada each name unnecessary contact in an icing or off-side race as boarding
Rule: The onus is on the checker in five of the six books, and the sixth, CARHA, penalises failing to avert contact (49(a)); none caps boarding at a minor — USA Hockey's floor is a minor plus a misconduct (603(a)), a reckless one a mandatory major plus game misconduct (603(b)): plan on a match (602(a))
Rule: CARHA 49(a) makes failing to avert contact a minor or major, the major mandatory if intentional contact injures; a major ejects and suspends you (30(a), 32(d)); a minor that puts an opponent into the boards doubles, two of the three penalties that eject you (49(b), 32(a)) (CARHA adult leagues only)
Rule: Never hit a turned back at the wall — pull up, play the puck. Ejects: NHL, PWHL, IIHF, USA Hockey if forceful or head first (reckless: plan on a match, 602(a)), CARHA, Hockey Canada off the head or head first (on head: minor to match); CARHA, Hockey Canada: mandatory match if defenceless
Technique: Stick in the passing lane, on the ice, out front — it takes the pass away without committing your body; and pivot late, because once you turn to skate forwards you have given up your ability to react
```

**Gap control is the defining skill of the position, so here is the actual method.** Your "gap" is the distance between you and the puck carrier. Too big and they get a free run at you with speed and options; too small and one move puts them past you. See [Defending the Rush](../systems/defending_the_rush.md) for the full treatment, including 2-on-1s and 3-on-2s.

![](diagram:rush-gap-and-angle)

- **Start your feet before they reach you.** The most common defenceman error is standing still and letting the attacker build speed into you. Begin skating backwards early so you're already matching their pace when they arrive — you cannot close a gap from a standstill.
- **Target roughly a stick-and-a-half of space between you and the carrier** as they cross the red line, closing to about a stick length by your blue line. [Defending the Rush](../systems/defending_the_rush.md) owns those distances and calls them a guide rather than a law. The blue-line stick length is published, though a second page anchored to that line prints two; the red-line figure is disputed — the only page anchoring a distance to the red line prints two stick lengths, and a stick and a half is published for the neutral zone rather than the line. Treat both as coaching convention. Close enough to reach the puck with a poke check, far enough that a single deke doesn't beat you.
- **Read their chest and hips, not the puck, hands or head.** A skilled attacker fakes with the puck, the stick, the hands and the head — those are the tools of deception. Their torso and hips have to point where their body is actually going. Watch the middle of them.
- **Take away the middle, force them outside.** Angle your body so the inside lane is closed and the only route is toward the boards, where the wall becomes a second defender. ⚠️ **What the boards take away is the carrier's ice, not the carrier.** Steer him there with the route you skate, add no bump, push or ride, and play the puck — where each book draws the line between steering and contact is set out straight after this list.

  **And how much the wall actually helps depends on the width of the sheet you are standing on** — on a wide rink an attacker forced outside still has room to keep skating, so gap and stick position matter more than steering; [Rink Map](../foundation/rink_map.md) owns the dimensions and what they change.
- **Stick in the passing lane, on the ice, out front.** An active stick on the ice takes away the pass without committing your body.
- **Pivot late.** Once you turn to skate forwards you've given up your ability to react. Hold your backward skating as long as you can.

**Where the steer becomes contact.** Steering a carrier toward the boards with your route is legal, and **three of the six books say so in terms.** Hockey Canada (Interpretation 1 to Rule 7.3(a)) lets a player *"steer or direct an opposing player into the boards, without actually touching or body-checking them"*, as long as neither player uses their body to *"intentionally body-check, bump, push, or shove an opponent"* — ⚠️ **so even a bump is out.** USA Hockey’s Note to **604(c)** puts **angling** inside legal competitive contact even where body checking is banned. **The PWHL’s Rule 52.1** goes furthest — players *"may angle their opponent by using their body in order to separate her opponent from the puck"* — but it is an *"illegal hit"* to make contact *"with opposite-directional force"*, to leave your skating lane, or to extend an arm, shoulder or hip after angling; ⚠️ **its penalties block also makes *"[a]ny move by a player to step or glide into an opposing player"* at least a minor**, and prices moving into a carrier who is trying to avoid contact. So carry the angle with your feet and add no step at the end. Rule 52.1 binds the PWHL only; in Britain’s Women’s NIHL, non-checking at U16 and senior, angle with your route and plan no hit at all. ⚠️ **The NHL, IIHF and CARHA write no angling permission**, so under those the route is legal only by inference from the absence of a prohibition. **In women’s hockey the IIHF is not silent:** **Rule 101.1** bars players competing for the puck from using the boards *"to eliminate her from the play, push her into the boards, or pin her along the boards"*, and makes a step or glide into an opponent at least a minor — so under the book British women’s hockey runs on, the steer is legal and finishing it against the wall is not. ⚠️ **USA Hockey’s Note to 604(c) is the one permission that reaches contact** — players *"physically engaged"* at the net or along the boards — but only two players in pursuit of the puck with possession their sole objective, and the same Note penalises *"deliberate physical contact with an opponent, with no effort to legally play the puck"*. ⚠️ **So no book here lets you ride a carrier who already has the puck along the wall** — the PWHL’s 52.1, the only permission that reaches a carrier in possession, stops the moment you leave your lane, extend a limb, or step into her.

**Riding him into the wall with your body is a different act, and in many classifications it is itself a penalty.** Body checking is barred at 12 & under and below and in all Girls’/Women’s and non-check Adult classifications under **USA Hockey 604(a)** (a minor under 604(c); a mandatory major plus a game misconduct under 604(d) where it recklessly endangers), and at U13 and below, throughout female hockey and in any other division a Member approves under **Hockey Canada 7.3(a)** — so a U15 side in an approved non-check division is inside it too. ⚠️ **That list is a floor:** 604(a) lets a local governing body bar checking in any classification, and 604(b) bars it whenever a Competitive Contact team plays a Body Checking team. Check the game, not the age group. ⚠️ **Where checking is legal the ride is still limited, and *defenceless* is not the whole test.** NHL, IIHF and PWHL 41.1 and Hockey Canada 7.2(a) write boarding around *"a defenseless opponent"*, but **USA Hockey 603’s base sentence asks no such question** — boarding is pushing, tripping or checking an opponent *"causing them to go dangerously into the boards"* — and the NHL's *"[a]ny unnecessary contact with a player playing the puck on an obvious 'icing' or 'off-side' play"*, which the IIHF, Hockey Canada and PWHL also write, makes it boarding when it puts him into the boards, though he can see you perfectly well. ⚠️ **Five of the six books put the onus on the checker**; CARHA instead penalises a player who *"does not avert body contact with an opponent"* (**49(a)**). ⚠️ **None caps boarding at a minor.** USA Hockey **603(a)** writes no bare minor — a minor plus a misconduct, or a major plus a game misconduct — **603(b)** makes the major and game misconduct mandatory where it recklessly endangers, and plan on a match on top (**602(a)**). **PWHL 41.3** and **41.4** put a major and a match above its minor. **CARHA 49(a)** is a minor or major at the referee’s discretion, the major mandatory *"[w]hen injury results from intentional body contact"*, and a CARHA major ejects you with at least a one-game suspension (**30(a)**, **32(d)**); a minor that puts him into the boards doubles, counting as two of the three penalties that eject you (**49(b)**, **32(a)**). Your worst case is an ejection and, in some books — USA Hockey (404(b)) and CARHA (32(d)) among them — a suspension after it. [Rules Primer](../foundation/rules_primer.md) has each book’s tiers.

**Boarding is not the only rule this instruction can put you under, and the other one is worse.** Forcing a carrier outside turns him: he shields the puck on the wall, so his back is half round to you as you arrive. ⚠️ **A check finished on those numbers is a check from behind — the head, neck and spine class. If you can see his numbers, pull up and play the puck.**

**USA Hockey 608** reaches a check *"directly from behind, or diagonally from behind"* and puts the onus on the checker. **608(b)** is a major plus a game misconduct where it recklessly endangers or sends him head first into the boards or goal frame; **608(c)** is a compulsory match where it recklessly endangers him from behind with excessive force while he is vulnerable or defenceless; and if it is reckless at all, plan on the match (**602(a)**). ⚠️ **Its Casebook removes the reckless test for your exact picture** (**Rule 608, Situation 1**): the major plus game misconduct, or a match, *"must be called"* whenever a player *"forcefully checks an opponent who is standing along the boards (back toward the middle of the ice)"*.

**Hockey Canada 7.5 names your picture** — a player along the boards *"with their head down looking for the puck or 'digging the puck out,'"* hit from behind and driven head first. A hit to his back is priced under 7.5: **7.5(a)** is a minor **plus a game misconduct**, **7.5(b)** a major plus a game misconduct on a violent hit — mandatory if he is hurt — and **7.5(c)** a match where he *"is unable to protect or defend themselves"*. ⚠️ **A head-first drive costs at least 7.5’s price, and once your stick or body reaches his head it is head contact too (Rule 7.6, which supersedes other rules) — if both apply, the harsher price stands.** In minor and female hockey (**7.6**), head contact is a minor if accidental and a double minor if intentional, and a third in one game is an ejection (**7.6(a)**); the referee may give a major plus a game misconduct on violence alone, and ⚠️ **must if he is injured or the hit was a cross-check above his shoulders (7.6(b)), with a match if he could not protect or defend himself or you tried to injure him (7.6(c)).** In Junior and Senior (**7.7**), minimal violence may be called as roughing, charging or another penalty and moderate violence is a minor plus a ten-minute misconduct (**7.7(a)**), but ⚠️ **high violence, a cross-check above the shoulders, or an illegal hit that puts his head into the glass, ice or boards and injures it is a major plus a game misconduct (7.7(b)), with the same match (7.7(c)).**

**The NHL, IIHF and PWHL** ⚠️ **write no minor for it at all** (43.2 in each), so what is left is a major with a game misconduct (NHL and PWHL **43.5**; IIHF **43.3**). The NHL’s and PWHL’s major is automatic (**43.3**); the IIHF’s is at the referee’s discretion and only where it *"recklessly endangers the fouled Player"* — but that discretion is over whether to call it, not what to call it. ⚠️ **Do not finish a check on "he turned into me":** NHL and PWHL **43.1** assess no penalty where a player intentionally turns his back into the contact, but **IIHF 43.1** removes only the checking-from-behind call — *"However, other penalties may still be assessed"* — and asks more of the turn, so the hit still has to stand on its own. ⚠️ **CARHA is harsher still:** **53(a)** is a minor plus a game misconduct, **53(b)** a compulsory match where a player is propelled from behind into the boards or goal net *"in such a way that the player is unable to protect or defend themselves"*, and its referees *"are instructed not to substitute other penalties"*. **Under the NHL, IIHF, PWHL and CARHA books and Hockey Canada’s 7.5, a checking-from-behind call ends your game.** So when the numbers come round, take the puck and not the man — the steer has done its work, and the wall is holding him for your stick.

#### Backward skating, and which way to pivot

```facts
Key: Backward skating is the physical foundation of gap control — you cannot hold a gap you are not fast enough to hold
Technique: Generate speed with C-cuts and backward crossovers rather than pushing straight back
Convention: Pivot to the boards side rather than the middle, so that when you are beaten you are beaten toward the wall — a coaching default, not a law
Read: Some coaches want a middle pivot when turning away would take your eyes off the puck, or when you have inside help and the greater danger is the attacker cutting behind you
Action: Ask which your coach wants and drill both directions — the correct pivot is decided by where the attacker is
Key: If you have just been moved up from forward, backward skating is the barrier — the switch is a technical rebuild, not a set of new decisions
```

**Backward skating is the physical foundation of all of this**, and it is the skill that separates defencemen who can play a tight gap from those who can't — you cannot hold a gap you are not fast enough to hold. **Go and work through [Skating](../technique/skating.md); it is not optional reading for this position.** The two things it will tell you that change your gap immediately: generate speed with **C-cuts and backward crossovers** rather than pushing straight back, and **pivot to the boards side rather than the middle** — so that when you are beaten you are beaten toward the wall instead of into your own slot. Note that this is a **coaching default, not a law**: some coaches want a middle pivot on specific reads, such as when turning away would take your eyes off the puck, or when you have inside help and the greater danger is the attacker cutting behind you. Ask which your coach wants, and drill both directions — the correct pivot is decided by where the attacker is, not by which way you happen to be competent. See [Skating → Which way to pivot](../technique/skating.md#which-way-to-pivot--a-coaching-default-not-a-law). **If you have just been moved up from forward, this is the barrier** — [Switching Positions](switching_positions.md) is honest that the forward to defence switch is a technical rebuild rather than a set of new decisions, and takes correspondingly longer.

#### "Step up" and "pinch" are two different plays

```facts
Key: Stepping up happens in the neutral zone or at your own blue line — you move forward to challenge an oncoming attacker early rather than retreating
Key: Pinching happens at the offensive blue line — you step down into the offensive zone to stop a clearance and keep the puck in
Risk: A missed step-up leaves nothing but open ice behind you
Risk: A missed pinch puts the puck behind you going the other way — but your forwards are still in the zone to help
Key: A pinch is generally the safer of the two, because you have teammates behind you; a neutral-zone step-up has no safety net — that compares rushes against you, not injury risk
Never: Build speed into a step-up to finish it as a hit, or read a head-down carrier as a licence to arrive harder — it tells you they cannot change direction, and nothing more
Rule: A step-up finished as a hit is what the charging rules are written for — USA Hockey's Casebook rules the head-down case charging (607, Situation 2) and a check you accelerate through without the two fast strides (Situation 3)
Rule: The stride count runs the wrong way for anyone who learns the American number — Hockey Canada 7.4(ii) makes "two or more strides" the offence, USA Hockey's 607 Note and CARHA's 52(a) Note "more than two", IIHF 42.1 only "multiple strides", NHL 42.1 none
Rule: Charging binds every division, checking or not — USA Hockey 607 and Hockey Canada 7.4 name no division — and none of the six books caps it at a minor: USA Hockey's floor is a minor plus a misconduct (607(a)), a reckless charge a mandatory major plus game misconduct (607(b)): plan on a match (602(a))
```

- **Stepping up** happens in the neutral zone or at your own blue line: you move *forward* toward an oncoming attacker to challenge them early rather than retreating. Risk: if you miss, there is nothing but open ice behind you.
- **Pinching** happens at the *offensive* blue line: you step *down* into the offensive zone to stop a clearance and keep the puck in. Risk: if you miss, the puck is behind you going the other way — but your forwards are still in the zone to help.

A pinch is generally the safer of the two, because you have teammates behind you. A neutral-zone step-up has no such safety net. **Read what that compares, though: rushes coming back at you, not what the two plays do to a body.**

![](diagram:defender-step-up)

![](diagram:pinch-centre-high)

**A step-up has a second cost the tactical one does not.** It is a closing of space. ⚠️ **Finished as a hit, it meets the charging rules** — and the picture they name is a carrier who has not seen you. USA Hockey's Casebook (**Rule 607, Situation 2**) calls it charging when a player sees *"an unsuspecting opponent skating up the ice with their head down"* and *"skates across the ice at full speed to deliver a hard body check"*, because that is punishing a defenceless opponent rather than trying to win the puck. ⚠️ **No two-stride run-up is needed:** **Situation 3** answers *"Yes"* for a player who *"delivers a check without taking the two fast strides, but instead accelerates through the check"*.

**The stride count runs the wrong way for anyone who learns the American number.** **Hockey Canada 7.4(ii)** makes it charging to build *"up speed by taking **two or more** strides immediately prior to making contact"*, while the Notes to **USA Hockey 607** and **CARHA 52(a)** need *"more than two"* — and Situation 3 shows the count is no safe harbour under USA Hockey either. **IIHF 42.1** writes no number (*"multiple strides"*, in its 2025/26 and 2026/27 editions alike), and **NHL 42.1** and **PWHL 42.1** judge it *"as a result of distance traveled"*, the NHL's opening on a player who *"skates, jumps into or charges an opponent in any manner"* — so leaving your feet is part of the offence. ⚠️ **None of the six caps charging at a minor.** USA Hockey **607(a)** writes no bare minor — a minor plus a misconduct, or a major plus a game misconduct — with **607(b)** a mandatory major plus game misconduct for reckless endangerment and **607(e)** a match. ⚠️ **Hockey Canada makes that pair compulsory if the charge injures (7.4(b))**, the game misconduct automatic on any charging major (**7.4(e)**), and **7.4(c)** a match for a charge on a player *"unable to defend themselves"*. **CARHA 52(a)** allows a major at the referee’s discretion — an ejection in that book (Rule 30(a)) — and makes a major and a game misconduct compulsory *"[i]f injury results"*; **PWHL 42.3** and **42.4** put a major and a match above its minor.

**And charging binds every division, checking league or not:** USA Hockey **607** and Hockey Canada **7.4** carry no division clause, where their body-checking rules (**604(a)**, **7.3**) do. ⚠️ **So in a league where you may not check, a step-up finished as a hit breaks two rules rather than one** — which a referee calls, neither book says. Whether you may check at all is settled in [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md). [Defending the Rush → Standing up (stepping up)](../systems/defending_the_rush.md#standing-up-stepping-up) owns the step-up and carries this rule in full.

### Neutral Zone: Without the Puck — Holding the Line as the Safety Valve

```facts
Position: At your own blue line, in the middle of the ice — one defender each side of the middle, roughly between the two neutral-zone faceoff dots
Action: Stay in position and watch the play develop
Action: Be a safety valve if your forwards need to pass back
Technique: Keep your stick active to intercept any passes in your lane
Goal: Be ready to either support the attack or pivot to defence instantly
Never: Drift too far forward into the neutral zone — if your team turns the puck over you must be in position to protect against the rush
```

**Where to be:** At your own blue line, in the middle of the ice — one defender on each side of the middle, roughly between the two neutral-zone faceoff dots. Together you form the last line before your zone.

**What to do:** Stay in position. Watch the play develop. Be a safety valve if your forwards need to pass back. Keep your stick active to intercept any passes in your lane.

![](diagram:defender-holding-the-line)

**Goal:** Be ready to either support the attack or pivot to defence instantly.

**Key considerations:** Don't drift too far forward into the neutral zone. If your team turns the puck over, you need to be in position to protect against the rush.

## Offensive Zone

You are the last line of defensive responsibility, so your role in the offensive zone is to support without taking risks.

---

### Offensive Zone: Carrying the Puck — Free Space

```facts
Position: Both defenders normally just inside the blue line
Action: Look to shoot, pass to a winger in the circle, or find your partner for a one-timer — in a CARHA league, keep it to a short backswing, because past fifteen inches it is a slap shot and a penalty
Technique: Walk the line — skate laterally along the blue line to create better shooting angles
Technique: Get the shot through rather than shoot it hard — find the lane first, then put it on net at knee height or below, with pace, so a teammate can tip it
Read: The strong-side defender may step down to the top of the faceoff circle to support a cycle or create a shot, but only when there is no immediate threat of a turnover
Goal: Create a shot on net or maintain offensive-zone possession
Key: Three depths — just inside the line is the default, the top of the circle is activated and still recoverable, below the circles is always a commitment
Never: Go below the circles unless somebody has taken your job at the blue line first. When in doubt, stay at the line
```

**Where to be:** Both defenders normally play just inside the blue line. The strong-side defender may step down to the top of the faceoff circle (the edge of the circle closest to the blue line) to support a cycle (a play where your forwards rotate around the offensive zone passing to each other to keep possession) or create a shot — but only when there is no immediate threat of a turnover.

![](diagram:defender-three-depths)

**What to do:** Look to shoot, pass to a winger in the circle, or find your defensive partner for a one-timer (a shot taken directly off an incoming pass without stopping to handle the puck — devastating when timed well; in a CARHA league keep the backswing under fifteen inches, because that book makes anything longer a slap shot and a penalty). Walk the line (skate laterally along the blue line) to create better shooting angles. **Find the lane before you decide to shoot, and when you have it, get the puck through rather than shoot it hard** — on net at knee height or below, with enough pace that a small deflection still carries, so your net-front teammate can tip it and so the rebound comes out to them rather than into the goaltender's glove. A point shot is there to make a tip, a rebound or a scramble rather than to beat a goalie clean; when there is no lane at all, the play is to move the puck — D-to-D, or down to the half-wall and back — until there is one. [Shooting](../technique/shooting.md) owns the method and the order of operations.

**Goal:** Create a shot on net or maintain offensive zone possession.

**Key considerations:** Picture three depths in the offensive zone: (1) just inside the blue line — the default position; (2) the top of the faceoff circle — the activated position, acceptable for supporting a cycle, and still recoverable; (3) **below the circles** — not always wrong, but **always a commitment**. At depth (3) you are a fourth forward, you cannot recover if it turns over, and someone must have taken your job at the blue line *before* you go, not after. The "step down" mentioned above is only to depth (2). Going to depth (3) is a separate, deliberate decision covered under *Activating* below. When in doubt, stay at the line. [Offensive Zone Play](../systems/offensive_zone_play.md) covers the offensive-zone structure and is the authority on these three depths.

### Offensive Zone: Carrying the Puck — Under Pressure

```facts
Position: At or near the blue line — if you had stepped down to support the cycle, retreat to the line now
Priority: Keeping the puck in the zone is priority one
Options: Under severe pressure, a reverse pass back to your partner or a chip out of the zone to reset
Never: Panic inward. Hold the blue line instead
Never: Drift out over the line, or you are no longer available as an option
Goal: Maintain the zone; don't give up the blue line
Risk: Losing the line and getting caught in the zone lets the opponent exit cleanly. If you cannot hold it, exit and reset rather than gamble
```

**Where to be:** At or near the blue line. (If you had stepped down to support the cycle, retreat to the line now.)

**What to do:** Hold the blue line — don't panic inward. If the pressure is severe, consider a reverse pass back to your partner or a chip out of the zone to reset. Keeping the puck in the zone is priority one — but keep your own skates inside the line too, because if you drift out and the puck stays in, you're no longer available as an option.

**Goal:** Maintain the zone. Don't give up the blue line.

**Key considerations:** Losing the blue line and getting caught in the zone means the opponent can exit cleanly. If you cannot hold the line, exit and reset — don't gamble.

### Offensive Zone: Pressuring an Opponent with the Puck

```facts
Position: Near the blue line, with the strong-side defender closer to the action
Action: When the opponent retrieves the puck after a turnover, the strong-side defender pinches to challenge the carrier and the weak-side defender holds back against an odd-man rush
Read: If the puck is deeper in the zone, both hold position at the top and intercept rather than chase
Goal: Prevent the opponent from exiting the zone cleanly
Technique: Pressure from the top, not from below. If you were stepped down when it turned over, recover to the line first, read, then decide whether to pinch
Convention: Only one defender pinches at a time — a near-universal coaching convention rather than a rule, with real exceptions coaches do call
Risk: If you both step up and the play breaks the other way, the opponent has a clean break against your goalie
Never: Cut across into or hit a winger who has chipped the puck past you — turn and skate to the puck
```

**Where to be:** Near the blue line, with the strong-side defender (the one on the same side as the puck) closer to the action.

**What to do:** When the opponent retrieves the puck after a turnover, the strong-side defender pinches (steps down off the blue line into the zone) to challenge the carrier. The weak-side defender holds back to protect against an odd-man rush. If the puck is deeper in the zone, both hold position at the top and intercept rather than chase.

**Goal:** Prevent the opponent from exiting the zone cleanly.

**Key considerations:** An offensive-zone pinch is safer than a neutral-zone one because your forwards are still in the zone to help recover — safer in rushes against you, not in what the play does to a body. Only one defender pinches at a time, a near-universal convention with real exceptions coaches do call; if you both step up and the play breaks, they have a clean break on your goalie. If you were stepped down when the puck turned over, recover to the line first, read, then decide — don't pressure from below. [Forechecking Systems](../systems/forechecking_systems.md) has the timing test most teams use — go only if you can arrive on contact, and only when a forward is high enough to cover the point you vacate — and sets out what you may do to the winger once you reach the wall, which is where a pinch becomes a penalty. The commonest failure is the winger chipping the puck past you: **turn and skate to the puck — never cut across into the winger or hit them once it has gone.** The hit does not get the puck back, and the window for a legal one is almost nothing: in women's hockey none at all, because IIHF 101.1 and PWHL 52.1 allow a check only with *"a clear intention of playing the puck"*; elsewhere the NHL and IIHF allow it only *"immediately following"* the loss of the puck, and USA Hockey 625(a)(4) makes it interference to change your skating lane or foot speed *"to play the body of an opponent who is no longer in control of the puck."*

### Offensive Zone: Without the Puck — Supporting the Cycle from the Line

```facts
Position: Both defenders just inside the blue line, the strong-side one potentially stepped down to the top of the circle
Action: Read the play below you, offer yourself as an outlet, and move laterally to find shooting lanes
Convention: One defender pinches, the other holds the line as a safety — near-universal coaching convention, not a rule; coaches call exceptions for a full-ice press, a delayed penalty and 6-on-5
Never: Activate unless a forward can back-fill above the puck for you
Convention: Activate off the weak side into the back door — the common default, not the only route; some teams activate strong side on purpose, so ask which yours does
Read: Activate on possession, not on hope. Jump when your team has clear control below the goal line, and arrive late as the second wave rather than early
Action: Call it, so your partner slides over to cover the middle and your forwards rotate high
Convention: Somebody is above the puck — on a defence pair normally one of the two of you, but a five-man rotation covers the line with whichever player is highest, and some teams keep only one player high and make that the weak-side winger; ask which yours plays
```

*Your team has the puck here. This section describes your role when a forward has it in the offensive zone and you don't — supporting the cycle from above.*

**Where to be:** Same as when carrying — both defenders just inside the blue line, with the strong-side defender potentially stepped down to the top of the circle to support the cycle. If the puck turns over while you're stepped down, recover to the line first before reacting (see "Pressuring an Opponent with the Puck" above).

**What to do:** Read the play below you and offer yourself as an outlet. The forwards will look up to you when their options below are covered. Move laterally to find shooting lanes and stay open. Keep the puck alive in the zone — if a clearance attempt comes up the boards, step in and keep it in (a "pinch" — see below).

**Goal:** Keep the puck in the offensive zone and feed the forwards' attack from above.

**Key considerations:** Pinching in the offensive zone (stepping inside the line to keep the puck in) is part of the role here — but only one defender pinches at a time. The other holds the blue line as a safety. **That is a near-universal coaching convention rather than a rule**, and coaches do call exceptions — a full-ice press, a delayed penalty, 6-on-5. Unless you have been told one is on, assume the convention: if the puck turns over and you have both stepped in, there is nobody in position.

**Activating — joining the attack as a fourth (or fifth) man.** The conservative positioning above is the default, not a prohibition. Modern defencemen create a lot of offence by jumping into the play, and the difference between a good activation and a bad one is entirely the read:

- **Only activate when a forward can back-fill for you.** Someone has to be the safety valve above the puck. If all three forwards are below the circles and you jump, there is nobody home.
- **Activate off the weak side — the common default, not the only route.** The undefended arriving player is the dangerous one, so coming down the weak side into the back door generally beats driving into a crowded strong-side battle. Teams that run a five-man rotation, or that use the defenceman as the strong-side wall support in a cycle, activate strong side on purpose. Ask which yours does; [Offensive Zone Play](../systems/offensive_zone_play.md) covers the choice.
- **Activate on possession, not on hope.** Jump when your team has clear control below the goal line, not while the puck is still 50-50 in a corner.
- **Call it.** Shout so your partner knows to slide over and cover the middle, and your forwards know to rotate high.
- **Late, not early.** Arriving as the second wave into the slot is what beats coverage. Arriving early just means you're another body to mark.

The rule of thumb: **somebody is above the puck.** On a defence pair that is normally one of the two of you, and which one it is changes through a shift. **Hear it as a person rather than a position, though.** Teams running a **five-man rotation**, or giving both defencemen a standing green light, cover the line with whichever player is highest, and under a **single-high** offensive-zone structure — one player high rather than two — the safety is the weak-side **winger**. [Offensive Zone Play](../systems/offensive_zone_play.md) and [Puck Support and Spacing](../hockey-iq/puck_support_and_spacing.md) set out those alternatives — ask which one your team plays. The habit that survives all of them: before you go below the puck, know **who** is above it, not what.

### Faceoffs

```facts
Key: The centre takes the faceoff; your position depends on the zone of the faceoff and your team's setup
Position: Defensive zone — one defender supports the boards-side winger near the hash marks, ready to chase pucks to the corner or get to the back of the net
Position: Defensive zone — the second defender is in the slot, ready to block a point shot or cover the net front
Position: Offensive zone — one at the strong-side point ready to shoot on a clean win, the other holds the weak-side point
Position: Neutral zone — both defenders back at your blue line, ready to defend a rush if the opposing centre wins it forward
Convention: Faceoff alignments are a common default rather than a rule of hockey — Faceoffs owns them and its defaults assume the house zone system, so ask what your team runs
Action: Be ready to react instantly to a tie-up or a loose puck
```

The center takes the faceoff. **The alignments here are a common default rather than a rule of hockey** — [Faceoffs](../systems/faceoffs.md) owns them, and the defaults there assume the house zone system used throughout this guide. Your specific position depends on the zone of the faceoff and on what your team actually runs, so find out before your first shift:

- **Defensive zone faceoff:** One defender (the "boards-side defender") supports the winger at the outside hash mark (the boards-side winger) — positioned near the hash marks, ready to chase pucks to the corner or get to the back of the net. The other defender is in the slot, ready to block a point shot or cover the net front. Be ready to react instantly to a tie-up or a loose puck.
- **Offensive zone faceoff:** One defender at the strong-side point, ready to shoot on a clean win — a set play off a clean draw is one of the few chances in hockey where you get to shoot with your feet already set and traffic already in place. The other holds the weak-side point.
- **Neutral zone faceoff:** Both defenders back at your blue line, ready to defend a rush if the opposing center wins it forward.

### Power Play (your team has the man advantage)

```facts
Key: 1-3-1 — one defenceman only, playing the single point as the quarterback; if you are the second defenceman you are on the second unit, not on the ice
Action: As the quarterback, move the puck quickly, walk the line laterally to open shooting lanes, and use the threat of your shot to pull the box out of shape
Key: Umbrella — you are one of two defencemen high, so you have a point partner and can play give-and-go across the top
Key: 2-3 — both defencemen at the points; the older structure, and still common in rec, beer-league and youth hockey — an impression of how those levels play rather than a measured share
Key: Overload — you are usually the lone weak-side outlet, the release valve when the strong side gets sealed
Priority: Keeping the puck in the zone is your first job — a cleared power play has to re-enter against four killers already set in their structure
Action: Shoot for tips and rebounds rather than trying to beat the goalie clean through four bodies
```

**The formations are set out in [Special Teams](../systems/special_teams.md).** The question they answer for you is a blunt one — *whether you are on the ice at all, and whether you have a partner up there.*

- **1-3-1 (widely described as the modern default):** only **one** defenceman, playing the single point as the "quarterback". If that's you, you are the distributor: move the puck quickly, walk the line laterally to open shooting lanes, and use the threat of your shot to pull the box out of shape. If you're the second defenceman, you're on the second unit, not on the ice.
- **Umbrella:** you are one of two defencemen high, so you *do* have a point partner and can play give-and-go across the top.
- **2-3:** both defencemen at the points — the older structure, still common in rec, beer-league and youth hockey, and where you are most likely to have a partner. ⚠️ **That prevalence is an impression of how those levels play rather than a measured share** — [Special Teams](../systems/special_teams.md), which sets out the formations, has no count for it.
- **Overload:** you are usually the lone weak-side outlet, and your job is to be the release valve when the strong side gets sealed.

Whichever you're in: your first job is **keeping the puck in the zone**. A power play that gets cleared has to re-enter against four killers already set in their structure, which is a great deal harder than holding the line was. Your second job is shooting for tips and rebounds rather than trying to beat the goalie clean through four bodies.

### Penalty Kill (your team is short-handed)

```facts
Key: A kill is four skaters, normally two forwards and two defencemen — where you and your partner stand depends on which of the three shapes your team kills in
Key: Box — you and your partner low, owning the net front and the two low corners; it is the shape almost everyone is taught first, and no public count of what teams actually run has been found
Key: Diamond — usually one of you takes the low point at the net front and the other a middle spot; run against a 1-3-1, because a box leaves the bumper — their player in the middle — unmarked
Key: Wedge+1 — you are almost always in the triangle, not the "+1" who pressures whoever has the puck
Action: In wedge+1 the handover can make you the +1 — when the puck moves, the nearest wedge player steps out and the previous +1 drops into the triangle
Action: Hold the box — two of you just off the crease edges — hold the inside at the net front, block shots, and clear on every opportunity; Special Teams covers the shape and how it slides
Convention: The route the +1 takes out — straight from the goal, sweeping across the top, or roaming to the wall and the point — is a coaching choice and published descriptions differ, so ask which yours runs
Rule: You may ice the puck freely while below their on-ice count — not merely while a penalty runs, so a penalty to them taking you to 4-on-4 ends it mid-major — but USA Hockey grants it only from Youth 15-Only, Girls 16U, High School and Adult up (NHL and IIHF 81.6; USA Hockey 624(b)(1))
Rule: The NHL and PWHL take the shorthanded icing exemption away once a major expires with the penalty box empty, and only until the next stoppage (NHL 81.6, 20.3; PWHL 83.6)
Rule: Shorthanded icing turns on the on-ice count, not the clock — expiry counts only that one player back on, so on a 5-on-3 your pair is still below strength and still exempt, and a clear released before expiry draws no icing however late it lands (NHL and IIHF Rule 81.6)
Never: Get pulled out of position chasing the puck around the perimeter — the power play wants you to chase, because that is what opens the seam
```

A kill is four skaters, normally two forwards and two defencemen. **The shapes are described in [Special Teams](../systems/special_teams.md);** where you and your partner stand differs between them:

- **Box** — you and your partner low, owning the net front and the two low corners. It is the shape almost everyone is taught first and the baseline the other two are described against — though [Special Teams](../systems/special_teams.md), which owns these shapes, records that no public count of how many NHL teams run each as their base shape has been found, so treat any "most teams use X" claim as an impression from coaching sources.
- **Diamond** — usually one of you takes the low point of the diamond at the net front and the other becomes one of the two middle players, though which spots go to the defence is your coach's call. Run against a 1-3-1, because a box leaves the bumper — their player in the middle — unmarked.
- **Wedge+1** — you are almost always in the triangle, not the "+1". The single player outside the triangle pressures whoever has the puck; when the puck moves he stops and the nearest wedge player — which can be you — steps out as the new +1 while he drops in. Following the puck around the perimeter is how this system is beaten, not how it is played. **The route the +1 takes out — straight out from the goal, sweeping across the top of the zone, or roaming to the wall and the point — is a coaching choice, and published descriptions differ; ask which one your team runs.**

![](diagram:pk-box)

In all of them: stay tight, hold the inside at the net front, block shots, and clear the puck at every opportunity — under-pressure clears are fine, and where your book allows it you may ice the puck while shorthanded, ⚠️ **but only conditionally: the exemption belongs to the side below its opponent's on-ice numbers**, and what ends it is set out under [On the kill](#on-the-kill--where-the-exemption-applies-and-what-ends-it) below. Don't get pulled out of position chasing the puck around the perimeter; let it come to you. The power play *wants* you to chase, because that's what opens the seam.

### Icing

```facts
Action: Aim your clears wide and high off the glass rather than flat up the middle
Risk: The straight-line clear up the middle is the easy one to pick off at the blue line, and a rim that carries all the way down the ice is what gets whistled
Never: Put it over the glass — from your own zone that is the delay-of-game minor under NHL, IIHF and PWHL rules, not a clear, and a deliberate one is a minor from anywhere on the ice
Rule: The minor that needs no intent is narrower than it sounds — the NHL's 63.2(iii), the IIHF's version of it and the PWHL's reach only a direct, non-deflected shot out of play from your defending zone, and each exempts boards with no glass above them
Rule: Putting the puck out of play deliberately is a minor from anywhere on the ice, in the NHL, IIHF, USA Hockey, Hockey Canada and CARHA books alike (NHL and IIHF 63.2(ii), USA Hockey 610(c), Hockey Canada 10.1(ii), CARHA 75(b))
Rule: After your own icing, under NHL and IIHF rules (81.4, 87.1) you cannot change lines and your team cannot take a time-out — with narrow substitution exceptions (three in the NHL, four in the IIHF), an injured player among them but never a tired one
Rule: NHL Rule 81.4's three exceptions to the no-change bar after an icing are replacing an injured player, returning a goalie pulled for an extra attacker, and a penalty that changes either team's on-ice strength; the IIHF adds a broken skate its own 82.1 omits, so do not plan a shift around it
Rule: USA Hockey bars neither the line change nor the time-out after an icing — nothing in Rule 624 restricts substitution, and Rule 636(f) puts no icing condition on time-outs
Rule: The USA Hockey change after an icing is ordinary, not free — Rule 204(a) gives each team five seconds, then "no additional player substitutions … until the face-off has been completed legally and play has resumed, except when a penalty … affect[s] the on-ice strength of either or both teams"
Rule: Undue delay in placing your lineup or changing players draws a warning first, and a minor for delay of game on any subsequent delay by the same team (USA Hockey Rule 204(a))
Rule: The PWHL bars the change after an icing as the NHL does (83.4); Hockey Canada bars it only in U18AAA, Junior and Member-opted Senior (6.7(d)); CARHA writes no bar, and its change window at a stoppage is ten seconds (19(g))
Key: When you are gassed, the glass is the answer — the punishment for a tired clear is being kept out there tired for a defensive-zone draw
```

**The rule itself — hybrid icing, the full wave-off list, "gaining the line", and how USA Hockey and rec leagues differ — is in [Rules Primer](../foundation/rules_primer.md).** Read it once properly, because you ice the puck more often than anyone else on your team and the outs are worth knowing.

**Glass, not flat.** You ice it most often on a straight-line clear up the middle from your own end, and that same clear is the easy one to pick off at the blue line. Aim **wide and high off the glass** instead: a glass clear rarely ices, is very hard to pick off, and gets the puck out even when it doesn't get it out cleanly. A rim that carries all the way down the ice is what gets whistled. (Off the glass, yes — *over* it, no: under NHL and IIHF rules that is a two-minute delay-of-game minor, where USA Hockey's Rule 610(c) and Hockey Canada's Rule 10.1(ii) reach only a deliberate shoot-out. Hockey Canada still makes an accidental one bite in Junior and Senior, though: no penalty, but no line change either (Rule 6.4(a)). The book-by-book detail is under your breakout options above.)

**The cost lands on your pair.** After your own icing the draw is in your zone, and **under NHL and IIHF rules** you **cannot change** and your team cannot take a timeout. The bar has exceptions — NHL Rule 81.4 permits a substitution *"to replace a goalkeeper who had been substituted for an extra attacker, to replace an injured player, or when a penalty has been assessed which affects the on-ice strength of either team"* — ⚠️ **so never leave a hurt teammate out there for the draw; the rule does not ask you to.** None covers a tired defenceman, so a tired clear keeps you out there tired, for a faceoff in your own end. When you are gassed, the glass is the answer. ⚠️ **The IIHF's 81.4 adds a fourth exception, *"a Player with a broken skate"* — but do not plan a shift around it:** its Rule 82.1 restates the bar with only the other three, no published ruling says which list controls, and the British In-House Rules amend neither. Expect to be kept out there. **USA Hockey imposes neither restriction after an icing** — nothing in Rule 624 restricts substitution, and Rule 636(f) puts no icing condition on time-outs — ⚠️ **but it is the ordinary change, not a free one:** Rule 204(a) gives each team five seconds at a stoppage, then freezes substitutions until the faceoff, and an undue delay draws a warning and then a delay-of-game minor. CARHA writes no bar either, with a ten-second window (Rule 19(g)); Hockey Canada bars it only in U18AAA, Junior and, where its Member opts in, Senior (Rule 6.7(d)); and the PWHL bars it as the NHL does (Rule 83.4). [Rules Primer](../foundation/rules_primer.md) covers this.

#### On the kill — where the exemption applies, and what ends it

```facts
Rule: The exemption is a count, not a clock — while you are below their on-ice count your clear is not icing (NHL and IIHF 81.6, Hockey Canada 6.7(b)), so a penalty to them taking you to 4-on-4 ends it even with your own major running; USA Hockey grants it only above its classification floor
Rule: A major with nobody seated to serve it is a bench minor under NHL and IIHF 68.2; the deadline differs: immediately under the IIHF, before the major expires under the NHL, and its 20.3 is tighter still, wanting a stoppage. The NHL (81.6) and PWHL (83.6) also cost you the clear until the next stoppage
Rule: A clear released while you are still below their on-ice count is not icing however late it lands, even if your penalty expires in flight — NHL and IIHF 81.6, Hockey Canada 6.7(b), which takes the count at the shot, and USA Hockey 624(b)(1), but only in the classifications that sub-clause names
Rule: A penalty expiring counts the player who was serving it back onto the ice from that instant, even if he is still sitting in the box — so on a 5-on-3 the first expiry leaves you below strength and still exempt, and only the second ends it (NHL and IIHF 81.6)
Rule: USA Hockey grants the shorthanded exemption only from Youth 15-Only, Girls 16U, High School and Adult up (624(b)(1)) — below those levels a shorthanded team is called for icing like anyone else
Action: Where your book grants it, use the exemption rather than trying to make a play you do not need to make — and on the last shift of a kill, get the clear away before the penalty expires
```

**On the kill you may ice it freely — where your rule set allows it.** Where it applies it is the biggest structural advantage a penalty kill has, so use it rather than trying to make a play you do not need. It has limits. **USA Hockey** grants it only at Youth 15-Only and above, Girls 16U and above, High School and Adult (Rule 624(b)(1)) — below those a shorthanded team is called for icing like anyone else. **NHL Rule 81.6** and **PWHL 83.6** withdraw it from a team that lets a *major* expire with nobody on the penalty bench to come out — at expiry, not during the penalty, and until the next stoppage. ⚠️ **The IIHF carries neither carve-out, so a British kill keeps the exemption — but an empty box still costs you.** **IIHF Rule 20.3** says *"the offending team must place a substitute in the penalty box immediately"*, and NHL and IIHF **68.2** both treat a failure as *"an illegal substitution under this rule calling for a bench minor penalty"*. The NHL's 68.2 deadline is *"before the major penalty expires"*, but its Rule 20.3 wants the substitute *"at a stoppage of play prior to the expiration of the major penalty"*, or *"immediately"* where minors are stacked on the major — **work to 20.3's, the tighter.** A bench minor while you are already down on a major puts you two down.

**The test.** ⚠️ **What counts is the number of skaters, not whether a penalty is running.** NHL and IIHF 81.6 grant the exemption to the side *"below the numerical strength of the opposing team"*, and the player whose penalty expires counts back on from that instant even if he is still in the box: *"[t]he action of the penalized player remaining in the penalty box will not alter the ruling."* So killing a 5-on-3, the first expiry leaves you still short and still exempt; the second ends it. ⚠️ **And a penalty to *them* that takes you to 4-on-4 ends it too, even with your own major still running.** **The other half matters on the last shift of a kill:** a puck *"released from the stick of the player shooting the puck down the ice prior to the penalty expiring"* draws no icing, however late it lands. USA Hockey writes the same test into 624(b)(1): *"if the puck was shot prior to the penalty time expiration, regardless as to the position of the penalized player, no icing shall be called."* ⚠️ **Hockey Canada takes the count at the shot rather than at expiry** (Rule 6.7(b), *"at the instant the puck is shot"*), with the same result for a 5-on-3 and for a clear released in time, and its Note 1 says a team is no longer shorthanded once the penalty time expires, *"irrespective of whether or not the penalized player has left the Penalty Bench."* [Special Teams](../systems/special_teams.md) owns these rules, and [Faceoffs](../systems/faceoffs.md) sets the books side by side.

### Blocking Shots

```facts
Key: Blocking shots is a technique, not an act of courage — done badly it hurts you and doesn't stop the puck, so it wants shin guards covering shin and ankle and gloves not worn through in the palm
Technique: Get close — ten feet beats thirty, because you cover more net and the puck has less time to rise; that it is "safer" for you is coaching craft, not a measured finding
Technique: Present the biggest possible surface square to the shooter — shin pads facing the shot, stick down on the ice beside you
Action: Stay on your feet if you can, hands behind your body. If you go down, go toward the shooter, not sideways. Head up and out of the shot lane; once down, hands behind your shin pads
Never: Turn your back, turn sideways or lift a leg — each exposes what armour does not cover. Never lead with your stick either: a puck off the blade can go up into your face (coaching craft)
Never: Block from a screen position — you deflect a puck your goalie never saw
Read: On a 2-on-1, blocking the shot means abandoning the pass — your goalie wants the shot; going down at a shot is not the same act as a timed slide across a passing lane
Rule: Leaving your feet and sliding into an opponent and causing them to fall is a trip, "regardless of who gains possession of the puck" — the one exception is dropping to your knees to block a shot, where your momentum then carries you into the shooter (USA Hockey Casebook, Rule 639, Situation 4)
Convention: How much your team blocks at all is a coaching choice — ask your coach which they want and ask your goalie what they want
```

Blocking shots is a defenceman's job, but it is a *technique*, not an act of courage — done badly it hurts you and doesn't stop the puck. Full detail in [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md).

![](diagram:shot-block-get-close)

- **Get close.** The further away you are, the more the shooter can adjust around you and the more time the puck has to rise or change direction. Ten feet beats thirty: you take away more net and give the puck less time. ⚠️ **That it is *safer for you* is not measured** — no study in this document's sources sets a blocker's injury risk against distance from the shooter, and [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md)'s own comparison, ten feet against *"three feet in front of your own goalie, where a deflection is a goal"*, is about the goal rather than you. Treat the distance as coaching craft, like the ranking of body parts below.
- **Take away the ice, don't dive at the puck.** Present the biggest possible surface square to the shooter — shin pads facing the shot, stick down on the ice beside you to seal the low gap.
- **Stay on your feet if you can.** A standing block keeps you in the play; a dropped block takes you out of it for two seconds, which is a lifetime if you miss.
- **Stay on your feet until the puck is actually released.** **Committing early is exactly what a shot fake is designed to draw** — go down before the puck is gone and you are out of the lane you were standing in while the shooter still has it. Close the distance with your feet rather than lunging. A lunge is a slide, and a slide that misses is a defender out of the play with no way to recover. [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md) owns this.
- **If you go down, go down toward the shooter, not sideways.** **Head up and out of the shot lane**, hands behind your body while you are up and behind your shin pads once you are down, backs of the gloves out, and feet on the ice — that much comes from [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md). ⚠️ **The *direction* is this document's own, and it is coaching craft rather than a measured or sourced finding** — no study, coaching manual or rulebook held here tests it. It rests on the prohibition in the next bullet: turning sideways opens the unpadded flank and loses sight of the puck. **This is for blocking a *shot*, and it does not transfer** to a timed slide across a passing lane on a 2-on-1, a different action on a different axis (see §Defending the Rush — quick reference below). **The rulebooks draw the same line:** USA Hockey's Casebook makes leaving your feet and sliding into an opponent a trip *"regardless of who gains possession of the puck"*, with one exception — *"when a player drops to their knees to block a shot and their momentum carries them into the player shooting the puck, causing them to fall. In that event, no penalty is to be assessed"* (Casebook, Rule 639, Situation 4). That excuses the block, not the slide. The parts to protect are the ones armour does not cover — feet, hands and face. *(Which of them gets hurt most often is not established: no injury-surveillance study breaks shot-blocking injuries down by body part, and the only published paper on the mechanism — a three-player case series of lower-leg and ankle fractures from puck impacts in professional players — calls puck impact "a frequent and **underreported** cause of injury" (Foot & Ankle Specialist, 2026). Treat the ranking as coaching craft.)*
- **Never turn your back, never turn sideways, never lift a leg, and don't lead with your stick.** Turning your back or sideways opens the unpadded flank and loses sight of the puck, a raised leg puts the gap between shin pad and pant into the shot lane, and a stick thrust out ahead deflects pucks upward at your own face and your goalie. ⚠️ **That the deflection goes upward is coaching craft, widely taught and measured by nothing this corpus holds.** Present padded surfaces, keep both feet on the ice, keep the stick down beside you.
- **Don't block from a screen position.** Half-blocking a shot your goalie can't see is worse than not blocking at all; you've deflected an unseen puck.
- **Know when not to.** On a 2-on-1, blocking the shot means abandoning the pass. Your goalie wants the shot.

**One precondition sits underneath all of it, and it is about your kit rather than your technique.** ⚠️ **Blocking puts your shins, feet and hands in the puck's path deliberately**, so [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md) attaches a check on the equipment doing the absorbing: **shin guards that actually cover the shin *and* the ankle, and gloves not worn through in the palm.** That is where the only published clinical paper on the mechanism lands too — the lower-leg and ankle fractures in the case series cited below. [Equipment](../off-the-ice/equipment.md) covers fit.

**How much your team blocks at all is a coaching choice** — some systems want defenders collapsing and blocking aggressively in front of the goalie, others want the lane taken away on your feet with the goalie seeing everything. Neither is right in the abstract. **Ask your coach which, and ask your goalie what they want**, before you decide a teammate who stayed up was being soft. See [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md) and [Goaltender](goaltender.md).

### Line Changes

```facts
Key: Your shift is legitimately a little longer than a forward's, because more of it is gliding, reading and skating backwards rather than sprinting
Key: A longer shift does not mean less ground covered — the measured direction is the other way round, and Center covers that finding
Key: The shift-length gap is real and smaller than you think — 1.7 seconds a shift, under two seconds, computed for this document from the NHL's own time-on-ice report and not a published figure
Key: The "40–50 seconds for a D against 35–45 for a forward" figures are coaching talk with no source behind them, and the forward band does not even reach what the league actually does
Action: Change your pair on its own clock, not with a forward line. Let the forwards go and take your change on the next safe window
Never: Change while you are the last man back. Say it out loud: "last man"
```

**Your shift is legitimately a little longer than a forward's**, because you spend more of it gliding, reading and skating backwards rather than at a full sprint. **Do not read that as covering less ground** — the measured direction is the other way round. Lignell and colleagues, tracking 36 NHL players through a game, found that *"defensemen (D) covered **29% more** (p ≤ 0.05) skating in total than forwards (F) and were on the ice **47% longer**"*, while forwards performed *"**54% more** (p ≤ 0.05) high-intensity skating per minute"*. Less sprinting per minute, more skating in total. [Center](center.md) covers that finding.

**The gap is real, and it is smaller than you think.** In the 2025-26 NHL regular season defencemen averaged **49.3 seconds per shift against 47.6 for forwards** — a difference of **1.7 seconds**, under two seconds a shift. That is computed from the NHL's own time-on-ice report (total ice time divided by total shifts, all situations, every skater who took a shift: 325 defencemen, 615 forwards); it is not a published figure, and the same calculation on 2024-25 gives **48.7 s for defencemen against 47.4 s for forwards**. [Conditioning and Recovery](../off-the-ice/conditioning_and_recovery.md) computes that same 2024-25 season from that same report and gets **48.8 s for defencemen and 47.2 s for forwards**. It restricts the population to the 597 skaters who played 41 or more games and averages the per-skater figure, where this document keeps every skater who took a shift and pools total ice time over total shifts. **Those two choices are the whole of the difference — a tenth or two of a second, and in opposite directions for the two positions — and neither figure is a correction of the other.** Both are this corpus's own arithmetic, not something the NHL publishes.

The specific "40–50 seconds for a D against 35–45 for a forward" figures you hear are coaching talk with no source behind them, and neither band is centred anywhere near what the league actually does — the forward band does not reach it at all, and only the very top of the defence band does. ([How to Watch Hockey](../off-the-ice/how_to_watch_hockey.md) flags the related broadcast figure of "40–45 seconds" as not coming from the physiology literature — though it also notes that figure is *not* baseless, being close to the mean of **47 seconds** that [Playing Without the Puck](../hockey-iq/playing_without_the_puck.md) computes from 8,325 shifts across an 11-game convenience sample of 2024-25. That is a different endpoint, a different population and a mean over all skaters rather than a split by position, so it is not comparable with the positional pair from the time-on-ice report. It does not address positional bands at all; that comparison is this document's.) The measured *range* for an individual skater is **roughly 30 to 80 seconds** — measured by **Lignell et al. (2018), Brocherie et al. (2018) and Bracko et al. (1998)**, and collected in Vigh-Larsen and Mohr's 2024 physiology review, which reports the band rather than having measured it; [Conditioning and Recovery](../off-the-ice/conditioning_and_recovery.md) covers that figure. What the extra second or two buys you is one more rotation of the puck, not a ninety-second shift.

Two things are yours specifically. **Your pair changes on its own clock, not with a forward line** — five players stepping over the boards at the same moment is a rush against nobody, so let the forwards go and take your change on the next safe window. And **you are frequently the last man back**, in which case you do not change at all until somebody relieves you. Say it out loud: "last man."

Shift length, the safe and unsafe windows to change, and why a bad change concedes goals are in [Game Management](../systems/game_management.md).

### Defending the Rush — quick reference

```facts
Action: 1-on-1 — force them outside, stick in the passing lane, don't lunge. Steer them to a bad shooting angle rather than making a highlight hit — take the angle, not the man
Convention: 2-on-1 — take away the pass and give up the shot, sitting in the passing lane between the two attackers with your stick flat on the passing side; a timed slide to force the shot is the realistic alternative — ask which your team plays, how they want you down, and make sure your goalie agrees
Never: Slide before the pass is released on a 2-on-1 where your team plays the passing lane, or carry the shot-blocking drop into a timed slide. If you go down, it is head up and out of the shot lane
Rule: A slide is not just timing — USA Hockey calls it tripping when you leave your feet and slide an opponent down, "regardless of who gains possession of the puck" (Casebook 639, Situation 4); a reckless one is a mandatory major plus game misconduct (639(b)) — plan on a match (602(a))
Rule: A slide catching an opponent at or below the knees reads as clipping rather than tripping — "throwing the body across or below the knees of an opponent from any direction" (NHL and IIHF 44.1), though neither names the play; Hockey Canada 8.7 draws its line at the hips, and so catches more
Rule: Clipping triggers differ — the NHL's major needs injury and brings a mandatory game misconduct (44.3, 44.5), its match penalty an attempt to injure (44.4); the IIHF needs neither (44.3, discretionary major plus automatic game misconduct); USA Hockey's 639(b) needs no injury and hangs off tripping
Rule: Hockey Canada, PWHL and CARHA price the low hit — Hockey Canada 8.7(a) a minor, 8.7(b) a major plus game misconduct on violence, a must on injury, 8.7(c) a compulsory match for an attempt to injure; PWHL 44.3 and 44.5 the same pair on injury; CARHA, with no clipping rule, uses 86(a) and 48(a)
Rule: A low hit from behind is checking from behind, not clipping — IIHF 43.1, no minor; USA Hockey 608(a) at least a misconduct; CARHA 53(a) and, off the head, Hockey Canada 7.5 a game misconduct — on it, head contact (7.6; 7.7) up to a match, a must if defenceless. NHL, PWHL: unclear, not more lenient
Read: If the second attacker has been picked up by a backchecker and the shooter is walking into the slot unpressured with no realistic pass, it is no longer a 2-on-1. Close on the puck
Convention: 3-on-2 — "two wide, middle to the backchecker" is the common default; "strong side takes the puck, weak side splits" the alternative
Action: Ask your coach which of the two 3-on-2 systems you are playing — it is a coaching choice, not a law
Action: Under both 3-on-2 systems, retreat together and keep the middle of the ice sealed as long as possible
Never: Let an attacker get between you and your net while you are defending — unless you are pinching, stepping up, or activating into the rush on purpose. Stay on the defensive side of the puck otherwise
```

Full detail in [Defending the Rush](../systems/defending_the_rush.md). The three situations you must have automatic:

![](diagram:defender-two-on-one)

- **1-on-1:** Gap control (see the neutral zone section above). Force them outside, keep your stick in the passing lane, don't lunge. Your goal is to steer them to a bad shooting angle, not to make a highlight hit. ⚠️ **Take the angle, not the man** — steering with the route you skate is one act and riding a carrier into the wall is another, and the rulebook limits on the second are set out under [Gap control — the method](#gap-control--the-method) above.
- **2-on-1:** **Take away the pass, give up the shot.** Sit in the passing lane between the two attackers, stick flat on the passing side. The shot is your goalie's — they can see it and are set for it; a cross-ice pass to an open forward is a chance they cannot save. Do not slide until the pass is released. **This is one of two legitimate systems, not a law:** the alternative is a **timed slide** across the lane to remove the pass and force the shot — but go early and you are out of the play, and you cannot contest the rebound. **Ask your coach which you play**, and make sure your goalie gives the same answer. **Do not carry the shot-blocking drop from §Blocking Shots into it** — a slide across a passing lane is a different action on a different axis. How you go down is not settled teaching here, so ask the coach who wants you to slide, and keep your head up and out of the shot lane. ⚠️ **The exposure is not just timing.** **USA Hockey's Casebook rules on this play** (Rule 639, Situation 4): *"When a player leaves their feet and slides into an opponent, thereby causing them to fall, a penalty for tripping must be called regardless of who gains possession of the puck."* Getting to the puck first buys you nothing there. ⚠️ **Hockey Canada writes the opposite exception, so do not carry one book's answer into the other:** its **Rule 8.6** makes the slide tripping *"unless they make contact with the puck prior to contacting their opponent"*, and the Note under **CARHA Rule 86(a)** ignores the trip if you knock the puck away before making body contact.

  **A slide that catches them low** ⚠️ **is clipping, not tripping, and every book can eject you for it.** NHL and IIHF Rule 44.1 define clipping as *"the act of throwing the body across or below the knees of an opponent from any direction"*; **Hockey Canada 8.7** draws its line at the **hips**, the widest definition here. The triggers differ: the **NHL and PWHL** make the major mandatory with a game misconduct if it injures (44.3, 44.5) and allow a match for an attempt to injure (44.4), and their **Rule 21.1** makes a match compulsory for any deliberate attempt to injure; the **IIHF** needs no injury — a discretionary major plus an **automatic** game misconduct where it recklessly endangers (44.3); **USA Hockey** needs no clipping label and no injury — **639(b)** makes a major plus game misconduct mandatory for recklessly endangering an opponent *"as a result of tripping, clipping or leg checking"*, and **plan on a match too (602(a))**, as its Casebook Situation 5 says one *"must be considered"*; **Hockey Canada** allows the major plus game misconduct on the degree of violence, compulsory on injury (8.7(b)), and **8.7(c)** a compulsory match for an attempt to injure or a deliberate injury. **CARHA has no clipping rule** but is not free: 86(a) above is a minor or a discretionary major and game misconduct, **56(a)** reaches a foul with the knee on the same tiers with **56(b)** a major where it injures, any CARHA major ejects you (30(a)), and **48(a)** makes a match compulsory for a deliberate injury. *(That the NHL's and IIHF's words reach a slide across a passing lane, and not only a check, is a reading of them — neither book names the play. USA Hockey's Note 2 names leaving the feet only where that was the purpose.)*

  **A slide that arrives from behind** ⚠️ **is checking from behind, which starts higher:** **IIHF 43.1** reaches a hit from behind *"on open ice in any manner"* and **43.2** leaves no minor; **Hockey Canada 7.5(a)** and **CARHA 53(a)** attach a **game misconduct** to the minor. **The NHL and PWHL write no equivalent catch-all — their silence is not permission to deliver the hit.** [Defending the Rush](../systems/defending_the_rush.md) covers both systems and this exposure in full. **The one honest caveat:** if the second attacker has been picked up by a backchecker and the shooter is walking into the slot unpressured with no realistic pass, it is no longer a 2-on-1. Close on the puck.
- **3-on-2:** There are **two named systems here and you need to know which one your team plays** — this is a coaching choice, not a law. The common default is **"two wide, middle to the backchecker"**: each defenceman takes an outside attacker, nobody chases the puck carrier, and the middle attacker belongs to whoever is backchecking the middle lane — usually the centre, though *which* backchecker owns the middle is itself a coaching choice, with "first man back takes the middle" and man-on-man-from-the-puck-outward both in use (see [Center](center.md) and [Winger](winger.md)). The realistic alternative is **"strong side takes the puck, weak side splits"**: the strong-side defenceman plays the carrier as a 1-on-1 and the weak-side defenceman drops off to split the middle and the far attacker, sitting in the passing lane exactly as on a 2-on-1. The default concedes the middle entirely if the backcheck does not arrive; the alternative never concedes the middle but leaves the far attacker more room. **Ask your coach which one you are playing** — two defencemen running different versions on the same rush is worse than either. Under both: retreat together and keep the middle of the ice sealed as long as possible.

The idea underneath all three situations above: **stay on the defensive side of the puck.** Anything that puts an attacker between you and your net while you are defending has already gone wrong — though a pinch, a neutral-zone step-up or activating into the rush all put you there on purpose, which is why this is the default rather than a law with no exceptions.

## Common Mistakes

- **Watching the puck and losing your man.** Stay goal side of him and keep checking where he is: a forward who gets behind you has beaten you without touching the puck. Give that position up only on purpose, with somebody covering behind you.
- **Both defencemen drawn to the same puck**, leaving the goalmouth empty. One of you on the puck, one at the mouth of your goal, swapping out loud every time the puck crosses the middle. Coaches name this among the commonest causes of goals against — **coaching consensus, not a statistic**, because public tracking counts how goals were made, not which breakdown let them in. [Defensive Zone Coverage](../systems/defensive_zone_coverage.md) states it the same way.
- **Not defending the net front because you think you may not check there.** **Take the inside and keep it, and as the shot or pass comes, lift their stick low on the shaft, well below their bottom hand, and let it go.** The lift is yours in every league while it stays a lift: holding the stick is a penalty in all six books, and a forceful chop at the hands is slashing. **The box-out is legal in four of the six books**, but Hockey Canada can call one that stops a forward getting where he is going as interference, and CARHA puts a duty on you to avert body contact — under those two, hold the spot without putting your body into him. [Net-front technique — holding the inside](#net-front-technique--holding-the-inside) has the books.
- **Clearing the net front by shoving the screener off their spot.** **Get there first instead, then lift the stick.** Driving him off a spot he already holds is a push, not a box-out, and **it can be called interference wherever you play**, because he has no puck. Where checking is barred it is an illegal check too — **check the game, not the age group**, because a checking-age team can be in a no-checking game for the night. [Net-front technique — holding the inside](#net-front-technique--holding-the-inside) has what each book charges.
- **Letting a body travel across the front of your own net.** **Whenever a body moves at your goalmouth, take it toward the corner, not across your crease, and keep their skates on the ice** — that path has a goal post at one edge and your goalie at the other, and a player going backwards along it sees neither. USA Hockey puts the goal post in the head-first spinal-injury mechanism alongside the boards, at what it says can be walking pace. The corner direction is coaching caution rather than a rule, and it applies wherever you play. [Net-front technique — which way a body goes](#net-front-technique--which-way-a-body-goes) carries the tiers.
- **Taking a retrieval hit square to the wall with your back to the checker — or ducking.** **Arrive with your skates parallel to the boards and take it on your forearm and hip** — never the point of your shoulder — **head up and chin off your chest.** A turned back makes it a hit from behind you can neither see nor brace for; USA Hockey warns that *"Even a light hit from behind could inflict a severe head or neck injury."* A tucked chin straightens the cervical spine, and USA Hockey records that the head-first injury into the boards or a post can happen at walking speed. [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md) covers the full rule.
- **Reading "force them outside" as a licence to ride somebody into the wall.** ⚠️ **Take the angle with your route, then play the puck — and if his numbers come round as you arrive, pull up.** Steering him with your route is fine. Riding him along the wall with your body is barred where checking is barred, and boarding where it is legal once it puts him into the wall dangerously; no book caps boarding at a minor, and four of the six cover a player racing you for the puck on an icing or off-side too. ⚠️ **A carrier forced outside turns to shield the puck, so a hit as you arrive lands on his back** — checking from behind, which ends your game in the NHL, IIHF, PWHL, CARHA and Hockey Canada, and under USA Hockey if forceful at the boards, head first or reckless (plan on the match, 602(a)); and Hockey Canada and CARHA make it a mandatory match where it puts him into the boards or goal unable to protect himself. **Under Hockey Canada one that drives him head first still carries that ejection and match, and one that reaches his head is head contact too, up to a match.** [Gap control — the method](#gap-control--the-method) quotes all six books.
- **Standing still at the blue line** and letting an attacker skate into you with speed. You cannot close a gap from a standstill. Start your feet early.
- **Standing tall while skating backwards.** Stay low or you get beaten wide.
- **Turning to skate forwards while you can still stay backwards.** Once you turn you cannot react, so stay backwards as long as you can. When you pivot, pivot to the boards side, so you are beaten toward the wall rather than into your slot — a coaching default; some coaches want a middle pivot on specific reads, so ask, and drill both. [Backward skating, and which way to pivot](#backward-skating-and-which-way-to-pivot) has both.
- **Finishing a step-up as a hit, or arriving harder at a head-down carrier.** **A step-up closes space — close it and play the puck.** A head down means he cannot change direction, so your body gets there first, and nothing more. ⚠️ **Accelerate into him and it is charging** — whether your league checks or not, no stride count makes it safe, and no book caps it at a minor. [Step up and pinch are two different plays](#step-up-and-pinch-are-two-different-plays) has the books and their stride counts.
- **Diving or sliding on a 2-on-1 before the pass is released**, where your team plays the passing lane — you take yourself out and open both options. Stay on your feet in the lane, stick flat on the passing side, until the pass has gone. Some teams coach a timed slide; the mistake is sliding in a system that did not ask for it, and it is not the drop you use in front of a shot. **Even when asked for, a slide is not free:** under USA Hockey, sliding into an opponent so they fall is tripping whoever gets the puck, and a slide that catches them low can be clipping — a bigger offence, with the line drawn differently in each book. [Defending the Rush — quick reference](#defending-the-rush--quick-reference) has the exposure in full.
- **Carrying the puck through your own slot.** Go behind the net instead.
- **Putting your head down on a retrieval and missing the quick-up.** Look up the ice before you look down it: the forecheck is least organised in the two or three seconds after you win the puck, and that is the pass most defencemen never look for. Take the first option genuinely on. [Defensive Zone: Carrying the Puck — Free Space](#defensive-zone-carrying-the-puck--free-space) has the order.
- **D-to-D through the slot.** Never. Go behind the net, or above the tops of the circles if your team uses that route — a different route, not a softer version of this one.
- **Flat clears up the middle** — the classic icing and the classic interception. Aim wide and off the glass, never over it, so the puck runs along the boards.
- **Putting the clear *over* the glass rather than off it.** **Off the glass and along it, fine; over it, and your team is a man down.** From inside your own zone that is two minutes under the NHL, IIHF and PWHL books whether you meant it or not, and a deliberate one is two minutes from anywhere in all six. **Never aim at the players' bench:** over the glass behind it is still penalised, and the IIHF exempts only a puck shot directly into an open bench. [Defensive Zone: Carrying the Puck — Free Space](#defensive-zone-carrying-the-puck--free-space) has the carve-outs.
- **Rimming into the corner and assuming the goalie will get it.** **Under the NHL and IIHF books that rim is yours — your goalie cannot legally play it there — so plan the route before it arrives.** In Britain play as though the trapezoid binds too: England Ice Hockey's suspension of it may no longer stand, and nothing the Elite League publishes switches it off. **In a book with no trapezoid, the opposite mistake is sprinting for a rim that is now your goalie's** — somebody calls it before you are both in the corner, and "I've got it" from your goalie means take your breakout position. Your goalie may fetch it, not sit on it. [The trapezoid — which books paint one](#the-trapezoid--which-books-paint-one) names them.
- **Blocking a shot from a screening position**, deflecting a puck your goalie never saw. If the shooter is yours, get out and block square, shin pads facing the shot, head up and out of the lane; if not, get out of your goalie's eyeline. A half-block of a shot your goalie cannot see is worse than none.
- **Going down to block before the puck is released.** That is what a shot fake is designed to draw, and once you are down you are out of the lane. Stay on your feet until it is gone, and close with your feet rather than lunging. [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md) owns the technique.
- **Pinching with no read.** An offensive-zone pinch with forwards behind you is fine; a neutral-zone pinch that misses is a 2-on-1 against your partner. Only one of you pinches, while the other holds the line — a near-universal convention, so assume it unless your coach has called an exception. If the winger chips it past you, turn and skate to the puck — do not cut across and hit them, because they no longer have it.
- **Panicking inward at the offensive blue line.** Keeping the puck in is priority one; if you cannot hold the line, exit and reset rather than gamble and get caught inside.
- **Drifting over the offensive blue line while the puck stays in.** Keep your skates inside the line, or you are no longer an option for the forwards below you.
- **Activating with no forward covering you.** Call it, and go only once a forward has taken your place above the puck.
- **Going below the circles with nobody back-filling.** Down there you are a fourth forward and cannot recover — fine if somebody has your blue line, an odd-man rush against if not. The error is the missing back-fill, not the depth.

## Key Takeaways

1. **Stay on the defensive side of the puck, and work as a pair.** Stay between the puck and your net, goal side of your man, and give that up only on purpose. One of you plays the puck, the other guards the mouth of your goal, and you swap out loud each time the puck crosses the middle. That is a low zone collapse; other systems split it differently, so find out which your team plays. [Defensive Zone Coverage](../systems/defensive_zone_coverage.md) owns it.
2. **When a carrier comes at you, win the gap with your feet and take the puck, not the man.** Start backwards early, take the middle away, watch his chest and hips, and pivot late. Step up only when you are confident of winning the puck; otherwise back off under control and deny the clean entry. ⚠️ **Never finish it as a hit:** charging, boarding and a hit from behind can each end your game, and charging applies whether your league checks or not. [Gap control — the method](#gap-control--the-method) has the books.
3. **When a forward parks at your net, get inside first and lift his stick.** Hold the spot by position, and as the shot or pass comes lift his stick low on the shaft, then let go. ⚠️ **Never drive him off a spot he holds:** he has no puck, so it can be interference anywhere, and a reckless one is a match under USA Hockey (602(a)). ⚠️ **Move any body toward the corner, never across your goalmouth, and never push his back** — that is checking from behind, which ends your game in most books. [Net-front technique — which way a body goes](#net-front-technique--which-way-a-body-goes) has the tariffs.
4. **On a 2-on-1, stay on your feet until the pass is released.** Sit in the passing lane, stick flat on the passing side, and leave the shot to your goalie — a convention; some teams slide instead, so ask, and agree it with your goalie. ⚠️ **A slide is not free:** under USA Hockey, sliding an opponent off his feet is tripping whoever gets the puck, and catching him low can be clipping, which can end your game. Once no pass is realistically on, close on the shooter.
5. **Never carry or pass the puck across the front of your own goal. Go behind the net**, where a lost puck is a battle, not a point-blank chance. Some teams exchange above the tops of the circles instead, so find out where your coach draws the line. The middle is never a route.
6. **When the puck is dumped in behind you, read the forecheck on the way back.** Shoulder-check as you go. A **rim** beats a forechecker sealing the boards; a **reverse** beats one who has over-skated. Take the first breakout option genuinely on. ⚠️ **With nothing on, go off the glass, never over it** — over it from your own zone can be two minutes even by accident, and a deliberate one from anywhere, and the players' bench is a trap, not a gap. [Breakouts](../systems/breakouts.md) owns the options.
7. **When a rim dies in your corner, know whose puck it is — and that depends on your league's book.** Under the NHL and IIHF trapezoid it is yours; play as though it binds in the PWHL and in Britain too. **USA Hockey, Hockey Canada and CARHA paint no trapezoid**, so it can be your goalie's: agree in the room who calls it. Your goalie may fetch it but not sit on it, so be an outlet. [The trapezoid — which books paint one](#the-trapezoid--which-books-paint-one) has each book.
8. **In the offensive zone, keep the puck in, get your shot through, and know *who* is above the puck before you go below it.** Hold the line with your skates inside it. Find a lane, then shoot low with pace for a tip or a rebound. Below the circles you are a fourth forward — fine when somebody has back-filled the blue line, an odd-man rush against when nobody has. [Offensive Zone Play](../systems/offensive_zone_play.md) has the depths.
9. **Block shots with technique, not courage.** Get close, shin pads square to the shooter, and stay on your feet until the puck is released. Head up and out of the lane, hands behind your body. **Never turn your back or side, lift a leg, lead with your stick, or block from a screening position.** Wear shin guards that cover the shin and ankle. How much your team blocks is a coaching choice, so ask your coach and your goalie.
10. **On a retrieval with a forechecker behind you, turn side-on before he arrives — never your back to him, and never duck.** Skates parallel to the boards, contact on forearm and hip, head up, chin off your chest. A turned back makes it a hit from behind you cannot brace for; a tucked chin straightens your neck, and USA Hockey records that the head-first injury that follows can happen at walking speed.

However, deliberate physical contact with an opponent, with no effort to legally play the puck, shall be penalized"*; **604(d)**, *"A major plus a game misconduct penalty shall be assessed to any player who recklessly endangers an opponent with a body check in a Competitive Contact category"*; and **604(e)**, *"A match penalty for reckless endangerment may also be assessed for body checking in a Competitive Contact category or non-check Adult classification"* — so both turn on reckless endangerment, which the *Casebook*'s **Rule 604, Situation 3** reads as one tier (*"Rule Reference 604(d and e)"*), and 604(e) is *"may"* rather than *"shall"*. ⚠️ **One further USA Hockey prohibition was found on 2 September 2026 outside Rule 604's own structure**, in the book's *Disabled Hockey* section and so not reachable by reading Rule 604 alone: *"Body checking is prohibited during all Blind Hockey games. Body checking shall be penalized under the guidelines within Rule 604 in the Official Rules of Ice Hockey."* It writes **no age or classification limb**, and the same section provides that Blind Hockey *"[r]osters can comprise all youth-aged players (players under 18), all adult-aged players (18 and older), or mixed youth and adult-aged players"* — which is the whole of the basis for saying it reaches adult rosters that 604(a)'s list does not. ⚠️ **Only the Blind Hockey body-checking provision was read.** The Sled Hockey rules carry a separate **Non-Contact Player** provision — a player *"wearing a jersey pullover or brightly colored helmet"* who *"cannot be contacted in any fashion (other than incidental contact), nor can they deliberately make contact with another player"*, a minor either way — which is a different act from body checking and is **not** described anywhere in this document. **Hockey Canada Rule 7.3**, whose **(a)** is a minor to a player who *"intentionally body-checks, bumps, shoves, or pushes any opposing player"*, **(b)** a major plus a game misconduct *"At the discretion of the Referee, based on the degree of violence of the impact"* and that same pair mandatory — *"must be assessed"* — where *"a player is injured, as the result of a body-checking infraction that would otherwise call for a Minor penalty"*, and **(c)** a match *"to any player who deliberately attempts to or deliberately injures an opponent by body-checking"*. **IIHF Rule 101.1**, read in full for both halves — *"Players are allowed to 'hold their ground' any time that they have established their position on the ice. No Player is required to move out of the way of an oncoming Player to avoid a collision"*, against *"Any move by a Player to step or glide into an opposing Player will be assessed at least a minor penalty (2') for an 'illegal hit'"*, the rule's stated penalties being *"(I) Minor penalty (2')"* and *"(II) Major penalty (5') and automatic game misconduct penalty"*. ⚠️ **101.1 opens by allowing bodychecking in women's hockey** — *"In Women's Hockey 'bodychecking' is allowed when there is a clear intention of playing the puck or attempting to 'gain possession' of the puck with the exception from the situation described in this rule"* — which is why the women's-hockey facts above are scoped to the walk-out rather than written as a checking ban. **USA Hockey Rules 609 and 623 were re-read for a classification clause and carry none**, where 604(a) writes one in terms; that is the whole of the basis for saying the cross-checking and hooking rules are not scoped to checking leagues.*
