## Key focus

**Crash the net every time a shot goes toward it.** Rebounds and tips are where a winger's goals come from — that is coaching craft rather than a counted ranking, and the ice in front of the net does convert several times better than the perimeter, on figures whose own source names no dataset. **Time it by which of the two jobs you have.** If the net front is your assignment you are planted there *before* the release, on the line between the goalie's eyes and the puck. If you are driving in from the wall or your circle, arrive as the puck is released rather than well before it, or a defender has the time to tie you up and turn you away. Work just outside the blue paint — and off the red crease line itself, which every book that settles the question counts as part of the crease — with your stick on the ice: take the goaltender's eyes, not their crease. Know where the near post is before the shot goes, and take a defender's shove braced — wide base, knees giving, head up and chin off your chest. Never take it braced against the goaltender: give ground the moment they lean into you, then re-set the screen.

**Shoot when the look is yours, instead of hunting for a better one.** Passing up good shots, not taking bad ones, is the habit that costs wingers goals — coaching craft rather than a counted finding. **"Shoot first" is a bias and not a rule**: a teammate with a clear look at an open side is always worth the pass. When a teammate is already at the net front, the shot from your own circle or the half-wall — the boards roughly level with the faceoff dot — beats the extra pass that gives the defence time to close the seam. What you are breaking is the habit of *always* looking for one.

**Hold your lane, and stay wide.** Your lane is the outside third of the ice on your side, and holding it is what stretches the defence: defenders cannot cover three lanes with one flat line of skaters unless they spread thin, and the moment you drift to the middle one of them covers two of you. Your width is also what opens the middle for your centre. Under an aggressive press the weak-side winger does leave the lane to hunt the puck, so find out which your team plays.

**In your own end, stay high on your point — then come down the wall the instant your team wins the puck.** "Stay high" means level with the opposing defenceman on your side, not halfway to the corner, and the job expires the moment the puck is yours: you are then the first outlet, down the wall to between the hash marks and the goal line — the short red lines level with the faceoff dot, so that band sits at or just below the dot rather than above it. Staying out of the corner belongs to a low zone collapse. Under man-on-man you follow your check in, and under a hybrid of the two only once he crosses the line your team draws between man and zone. Ask which one your team plays.

**Win the puck on the wall, and move it early.** Keep the puck on the far side of your body from the checker, get your backside into them, feet wide and still moving — a stationary player on the wall gets pinned. Your first look is always up ice, and if nothing is on, rim the puck or reverse it rather than forcing a pass through the middle of your own zone. Head up and chin off your chest while you do it, and never your back to the wall and never duck.

**Get back through your lane, and pick up the late forward rather than the puck.** The opponent who hurts you off a rush is their third forward arriving behind the play, and nobody picks him up if all five of you watch the puck carrier. Sprint back inside your lane and stay between him and the puck — body in his lane, stick in the passing lane rather than on him, because he has no puck and a stick across a puckless man is interference rather than a backcheck. The standard assignment gives the wingers the outside lanes and the centre the middle; others give the middle to the first man back, and others pick up man-on-man from the puck outward. No count of which is commonest has been found, so ask which yours plays.

## Overview

```facts
Action: Crash the net on every shot at it, stick on the ice, just outside the crease. Driving in from the wall or circle, arrive as the shot is taken; on the net-front job, planted before the release
Key: Rebounds and tips are where a winger's goals come from — coaching craft, and the ice in front of the net converts several times better than the perimeter on figures whose source names no dataset
Rule: The red line marking out the crease is crease, not the safe edge of it: IIHF 1.7 has "The marked line belongs to the Goal Crease", USA Hockey's Note to 625(b) "including crease lines", and Hockey Canada and CARHA say the same. Keep your skates outside that line, not on it
Convention: Your lane is the outside third of the ice on your side, and holding it is a containment structure's spacing job, not a law — drift into the middle and one defender covers two of you. Under an aggressive press the weak-side winger does leave it, so find out which your team plays
Position: "Stay high" in your own end means level with the opposing defenceman on your side, not halfway to the corner — and it expires the moment your team wins the puck
Convention: When your team wins the puck, come down the side boards to receive, between the hash marks and the goal line, at or just below the faceoff dot — that is the conventional swing breakout; a flat/spread structure holds you higher and a stretch sends a winger up ice, so find out which you're in
Convention: Under a low zone collapse — a coaching choice not a rule, assumed throughout — you never go into the corner; under man-on-man you follow your man anywhere, corner included; under a hybrid, only below the line your team draws between man and zone — so find out where it is
Technique: Puck on the far side of your body from the checker, backside into them, feet wide and still moving — head up and chin off your chest, never your back to the wall, and never duck
Action: On the backcheck, take your own lane and pick up the opponent's late forward rather than watching the puck — stay between him and the puck all the way back, stick in the lane and not on him
```

**Two patches of ice decide what a winger is worth: the one in front of the opposing net, and your own lane.** Start with the net front, because that is the one that scores. A shot that arrives unchanged from the point is the shot a goaltender is most comfortable with; what beats them is a tip, a screen, or a puck that comes back out — and all three need a body in front of the net at the moment the shot is taken. So the timing matters more than the effort — and it runs opposite ways for the two jobs. **On a drive from the perimeter**, arrive early and a defender has the time to tie you up and turn you away from the puck; arrive late and the rebound is already covered. **On the screen it reverses** — though only the arrival does: both are decisions taken before the shot. The net-front job is one you are planted in *before* the release, and [Offensive Zone Play](../systems/offensive_zone_play.md), which owns the zone, treats getting there deliberately ahead of the shot as the whole of that job: the defending team recovers most rebounds, so the way you win one is by deciding to go before the shot rather than reacting to it afterwards — that document owns the figure and the qualifications on it. Go every time, including on the shots you are sure will be saved, because a rebound nobody went for was never a chance at all. And when the shot is the one on your own stick from the half-wall, take it — most wingers pass up good shots rather than take bad ones. **"Shoot first" is a bias rather than a rule**: a teammate with a clear look at an open side is always worth the extra pass, and what you are breaking is the habit of *always* looking to pass, which lets a defence sit in the seam and wait.

**Holding your lane is a spacing job before it is a scoring one** — in the containment structure this document assumes, which is a coaching choice and not a law: hold your lane and a flat line of defenders cannot cover all three of you unless they spread thin; drift out of it and one of them covers two of you. Width does the same work coming up the ice, because the seam your centre uses — the gap between two defenders' coverage, and the pass that goes through it — is the one your width opened. Under an aggressive press the weak-side winger does leave the lane, so find out which your team plays. In the offensive zone the job changes, and that section says so.

**In your own end the same discipline pays twice.** Staying high denies the opposing defenceman the time to shoot *and* leaves you one short trip from the spot you take the breakout pass in, so a winger who drifts into the corner loses both at once: the point opens up, and there is nobody on the wall to exit to. The read is when to go, not whether. Leave before your team actually has the puck and you are cheating for offence, and the pass to a winger already at the blue line is long, flat, intercepted, and arrives in your back. Leave late and you have wasted the short trip you were staying high for. Turn your feet as the battle is being won, and go as the puck becomes yours.

**Winning the puck on the wall is the defining physical skill of a winger** — a coaching ranking rather than a measured one — and "shield it with your body" is not instruction. The instruction is the puck on the far side of your body from the checker, your backside into them, a wide low base, feet that never stop, and a decision about where the puck is going made before you get to it. Pick the rim when the checker is sealing you against the boards and the reverse when they have over-committed and skated past you. **Two things override all of it, and neither is a rule for fast checkers only: never turn your back to the wall, and never duck.** A turned back converts the contact into a hit from behind, which you can neither see nor brace for; a tucked chin straightens your neck and puts your head into the boards or a post first, and that injury happens at speeds you would not think twice about. Skates parallel to the wall, forearm and hip into the contact, head up and chin off your chest. [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md) covers the technique, the quoted warnings behind it and what your free hand may legally do while you fight for the puck.

**The last of the five is the one nobody watches you do.** When the puck turns over you are often the forward best placed to get back, and under the family of systems this document assumes — the forwards take lanes and men while the defence takes the puck — the job on the way is not to chase the puck but to find the opponent arriving behind it. Sprint into your own lane, pick up their third forward, and get to your covering position before the puck does. **Other teams coach the reverse on purpose, and the system has a name — *hound the puck*:** the first forward back pressures the puck carrier all the way in, and the defencemen hold inside and support rather than engaging. That is a legitimate system with its own logic, so find out which your team plays, and ask for it by name. **What pressuring the carrier never means is finishing into the back of a player skating at their own end boards** — take the puck, take the ice, or angle him off it. That is the checking-from-behind geometry, and [Defending the Rush](../systems/defending_the_rush.md#what-hounding-never-means) sets out what it costs. What is wrong under *both* systems is deciding in the middle of a rush to abandon your man and double the puck, because that is the version nobody else on the ice is expecting. [Defending the Rush](../systems/defending_the_rush.md) sets them both out. A trailer sneaking in unmarked while everyone watches the carrier is named across coaching material as one of the cheapest goals a team gives up off a rush; public tracking does split goals by how they were produced, but not by the defensive breakdown that allowed them, so take that as coaching consensus rather than a tracked share. Then settle into the defensive-zone job above: cover your point, and be ready to break out again.

**Every line, zone and named area of the ice is defined in [Rink Map](../foundation/rink_map.md), and the working vocabulary layered on top of it — your lane, high and low, strong side and weak side, stay high, the point, the half-wall, rim and reverse — in [Language and Glossary](../foundation/language_and_glossary.md).** Read those two once and everything below gets easier.

Two of those words are worth carrying in now, because this document leans on both constantly. **High** means toward the blue line you are facing and **low** toward the goal line. And **strong side** is the side the puck is on, **weak side** the other: as a winger you are usually one or the other for a whole sequence rather than switching every few seconds, and the two jobs are genuinely different — the strong-side winger takes the puck up the wall, the weak-side winger holds the weak-side point and, under the low zone collapse this document assumes, fills the high slot while the centre is low.

**A note on the headings below.** Each zone is broken into the same four situations. The fourth — *Without the Puck* — means what you do when the puck is not on your stick **and** you are not the player pressuring whoever has it. That is a genuinely different job in each zone.

## Defensive Zone

Your job is to help your team exit the zone cleanly and to limit what the opposing defenseman can do on your side.

---

### Defensive Zone: Carrying the Puck — Free Space

```facts
Position: On the boards on your side, between the hash marks and the goal line, in the conventional swing breakout — Breakouts is the authority on this landmark
Action: Come down from your point coverage as the breakout starts; don't stand there while the other team has the puck
Convention: The hash-mark spot belongs to the conventional swing breakout — flat/spread holds you higher, stretch sends a winger up ice, so find out which you're in
Action: If you receive the puck, look to move immediately — carry out along the boards, or pass to the centre
Goal: Exit the defensive zone cleanly and transition to offence
Never: Hold the puck in your own zone longer than you need to — your first look is always up ice
```

**Where to be:** On the boards on your side, **between the hash marks and the goal line** — roughly level with the faceoff dot or just below it. You get there by coming *down* from your point coverage as the breakout starts; you do not stand there while the other team has the puck. [Breakouts](../systems/breakouts.md) is the authority on this landmark.

> **Wall position is system-dependent, and the authority says so.** The hash-mark spot belongs to the **conventional swing breakout** — the structure assumed throughout this document. Teams running a **flat/spread** structure ask wingers to hold higher toward the blue line; teams running an aggressive **stretch or flying-winger** structure send one winger up ice entirely. **Find out which one you're in** — the failure mode of a winger standing at the hash marks in a stretch system is exactly as bad as the reverse. See [Breakouts](../systems/breakouts.md).

![](diagram:winger-wall-depth-by-system)

**What to do:** You are one of the primary outlets when your team starts a breakout (the controlled exit from your defensive zone). The defenseman may pass to you, the center, or their D-partner depending on who is open. If you receive the puck, immediately look to move — either carry it out along the boards, or pass to the center if they are in a better position to exit.

**Goal:** Exit the defensive zone cleanly and transition to offence.

**Key considerations:** Don't hold the puck in your defensive zone too long. Get it moving quickly. Once you have control on the boards, your first look is always up ice.

### Defensive Zone: Carrying the Puck — Under Pressure

```facts
Position: Against the boards, with the puck on the far side of your body from the checker
Technique: Backside into them, feet wide and low, feet still moving — a stationary player on the wall gets pinned. Chest to the boards is the fallback for when you are already facing the wall, not the target
Never: Turn your back to the wall — USA Hockey warns your checker: "Even a light hit from behind could inflict a severe head or neck injury". Skates parallel, forearm and hip, head up, not the shoulder tip
Never: Duck to avoid a check — in a head-down collision with the boards or a goal post, your neck can break at walking speed; no speed is slow enough to make that safe. Head up, chin off your chest instead
Never: Turn into pressure the instant you receive it
Read: Rim to beat a checker sealing you against the boards; reverse to beat one who has over-committed and skated past you
Rule: The free hand may fend off an opponent or their stick, but may not hold their stick or body — NHL and IIHF 56.1, PWHL 57.1; Hockey Canada 8.1 writes it as blocking an opponent's stick with the hands, arms or body, and the arm in a strength move where you have body position, but never holding
Rule: USA Hockey writes the fend-off permission in its Casebook only, and conditions it — control the puck (Standard of Play Situation 6) or be reaching for it, engaged in a corner or in front of the goal (Situation 7), hold a position you "legally established", and never push off to create space
Rule: Fingers closing on a jersey or a stick is a holding minor, and not the ceiling — Hockey Canada 8.1(b) makes a major plus a game misconduct mandatory where a holding infraction injures
Action: Know your out before you get there, and don't force a pass through traffic in your own zone
Goal: Don't lose the puck in a dangerous position. Get it to safety
```

**Where to be:** Against the boards, protecting the puck with your body.

**What to do:** Shield the puck from the pressuring opponent. Look for your center swinging low or your defenseman as a safe outlet. If no pass is available, **rim** the puck (a hard shot along the boards that follows the curve of the rink onward to a teammate further around) away from danger, or **reverse** it (send it back the way it came, against the direction the pressure is flowing) to a teammate arriving behind you.

**Rim and reverse are opposite plays and you pick by where the pressure is.** A rim beats a checker who is sealing you against the boards, because it goes *past* them. A reverse beats a checker who has over-committed and skated past you, because it goes back into the space they just left.

**Make that pick before the puck is on your stick, not after.** You are receiving into a wall reception, which is exactly the case where a late look costs the most — so take a glance over your inside shoulder while the pass is still travelling, and let the last look land back on the puck just before it arrives. [Scanning and Anticipation](../hockey-iq/scanning_and_anticipation.md) covers the habit and the research behind it in full; it is not restated here.

![](diagram:winger-dz-rim)

![](diagram:winger-dz-reverse)

**Puck protection on the wall — the technique.** Winning 50-50 battles along the boards is the defining physical skill of a winger — **a coaching ranking rather than a measured one** — and "shield it with your body" is not instruction. Full detail in [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md); the essentials:

- **Puck on the far side of your body from the checker.** Your body is the wall between them and the puck. If the puck is on the same side as the pressure, you've already lost it.
- **Get your backside into them and your feet wide.** A wide, low base is almost impossible to move. Standing tall and narrow is why players get knocked off pucks.
- **Do not turn into pressure the instant you receive it.** Take it, absorb the contact, then spin off. But note the ranking in [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md): the orientation you are aiming for at the wall is **skates parallel to the boards, forearm and hip to the wall, head up and chin off your chest**. **Not on the point of your shoulder, and not the whole shoulder if you can help it.** USA Hockey's *Introduction To Body Contact* rules the tip out in terms, and that sentence is absolute: *"Never hit the boards or glass with the tip of your shoulder – it can cause injury"*. But it sits in the manual's "Angling In" demonstration list, and the teaching points written for this very case — a player *"checked while skating parallel to the boards"* — are broader and softer: beside the forearm and hip is *"[a]void hitting the boards with the shoulder"*, all of it, and the word there is *avoid*, not *never*; and the checklist USA Hockey gives for taking a check, printed in *Introduction To Body Contact* and again in its *Heads Up Hockey* guide, asks for *"No shoulder contact on the boards, if possible"*. **So the tip never, and no part of the shoulder if you can help it — hedged the way USA Hockey hedges it, not softened here.** Your shoulder does face the *checker*; it is the *boards* it should not meet. Facing the boards chest-first is the *fallback* for when you are already facing the wall and cannot turn — and if you use it, get your stick and gloves up and spread on the glass, with no head contact at all. **Never your back to the wall, never duck** — two prohibitions against two different injuries. Turning your back makes the contact a hit from behind, and USA Hockey's warning on those is that *"Even a light hit from behind could inflict a severe head or neck injury"*; ducking straightens the cervical spine, and USA Hockey records that the resulting injury can occur at walking speed.
- **Keep your feet moving.** A stationary player on the wall gets pinned; a player with momentum can escape along the boards or spin out toward the middle.
- **Your free hand: fend off, never hold — and all five books write that permission, each in its own words and on its own conditions.** **NHL and IIHF Rule 56.1** permit the free hand to *"fend off"* an opponent or their stick but *"not… to hold an opponent's stick or body"* — and **PWHL Rule 57.1** is that sentence in the feminine. **Hockey Canada 8.1** writes it in words no search for *"fend off"* finds — *"players are permitted to block an opponent's stick with their hands, arms, or body"*, though *"not… to hold an opponent's stick"*. **USA Hockey writes it in its Casebook rather than its playing rules**, where Standard of Play Situation 6 allows *"the player with the puck [to use] the free arm to fend off the opponent and maintain their body position"* only while you are in control of the puck and holding a position you *"legally established"* — Situation 7 extending it to a player reaching for the puck in a corner or net-front battle — and withdraws it the moment you *"grab the stick, sweater or arm"* or *"use the free arm to push off and create space"*; its **Rule 622 alone carries no permission**, which is why reading the playing rules by themselves gets this wrong. The arm in a strength move to block is permitted **provided you have body position** under NHL and IIHF 54.2 and Hockey Canada 8.1, in the same sentence word for word in all three; **USA Hockey writes no strength-move sentence**, and its Rule 622 names *"using a free arm/hand to restrain or impede the opponent's progress"*, and *"wrapping one or both arms around the opponent along the boards in a manner that pins them against the boards **and prevents them from playing the puck or skating**"*, as holding — while its Casebook Situation 5 stops a defender's arm short of *"an extended manner that 'pushes off' the opponent and creates space between you"*. What is penalised everywhere is using that hand to **hold, pull, tug or grab**: forearm and open hand to keep them off you, fine; fingers closing on a jersey or a stick, two minutes — and a major plus a game misconduct under Hockey Canada 8.1(b) if it injures. **The habit that needs no condition met in any book is keeping the free hand on your own shaft and winning the wall with your feet and hips.**
- **Know your out before you get there.** Decide where the puck is going while you're still skating to it, not after you're pinned.

**Goal:** Don't lose the puck in a dangerous position. Get it to safety.

**Key considerations:** Rimming the puck around the boards is a completely valid play. It resets possession and gives your team time to regroup. Don't try to force a pass through traffic in your own zone.

### Defensive Zone: Pressuring an Opponent with the Puck

```facts
Position: High in the defensive zone under a low zone collapse, covering the opposing defenceman at the point on your side, between them and the slot
Convention: Staying high out of the corner is the low zone collapse job, not a law — under man-on-man you follow your check in, under a hybrid only below the man/zone line, which teams draw at the dots, the hash marks or the tops of the circles, so learn your team's version
Action: Find out which defensive-zone system your team plays before your first shift — on the coaching account a breakdown here is two players on different assumptions rather than an effort problem
Goal: Deny shots from the point and stop the opposition setting up a clean cycle
Read: If your man walks down off the point, follow a few feet, stay connected, and call the switch
Read: If the puck squirts up the strong-side boards to you, engage it — that is your breakout, not a corner battle
Read: Under low zone collapse, the centre gone into the strong-side corner — the weak-side winger collapses to the high slot until the puck leaves; under man-on-man they stay with their check
Risk: Under a low zone collapse, chase into the corner and the opposing defenceman walks in free, with nobody positioned for the breakout
```

**Where to be:** High in the defensive zone, covering the opposing defenseman at the point on your side. (The "point" is the area just inside the blue line where defensemen normally stand — an area, not the line itself. Covering the point means denying that defenseman the space to operate.)

**What to do:** Deny the opposing defenseman time and space at the point. Position yourself between them and the slot/net — this blocks the most dangerous shooting and passing lanes at once. Stay on your side of the ice.

> **Whether you stay high or go to the corner depends on your team's system.** This is a coaching choice, not a law of hockey — see [Defensive Zone Coverage](../systems/defensive_zone_coverage.md).
>
> - **Low zone collapse / zone coverage** — the system assumed throughout, and the one coaching material most often describes as taught first in North American minor, junior and rec hockey. **That is consensus from coaching material rather than a survey, and no count of what teams run has been found.** You stay high on the point. The low battle belongs to the strong-side defenceman and the centre. **Do not go into the corner.**
> - **Man-on-man** — you follow your assigned check wherever they go, including into the corner. The points get covered by whoever's man is there.
> - **Hybrid** — man-on-man below the dots, zone above. You take your man down only once he crosses that line — and **where the line sits is a team decision**, some saying the dots, some the hash marks, some the tops of the circles, so learn your team's version.
>
> The rest of this section assumes low zone collapse. **Find out which one your team plays before your first shift** — on the coaching account a defensive-zone breakdown is two players operating on different assumptions rather than an effort problem, which is coaching emphasis and not a counted ranking; [Defensive Zone Coverage](../systems/defensive_zone_coverage.md) owns it and carries the limit.

**Goal:** Deny shots from the point and prevent the opposing team from setting up a clean cycle (a play where their forwards rotate around the offensive zone passing to each other to maintain possession and create chances).

**Key considerations:** It is tempting to chase the puck into the corner when you see your team battling there. In a collapse system, resist it. If you go to the corner, the opposing defenseman is free to walk in for a shot, and your team has no one positioned for the breakout. Staying high is your job, not just your option.

![](diagram:winger-leaves-the-point)

**Exception — when your man walks down:** If the opposing defenseman you are covering moves down off the point to join the puck battle, follow them down a few feet to stay tight on them. Don't chase all the way into the corner. But stay connected and call out the switch so the center or defender knows you've moved with your man.

**Exception — when the puck comes up the wall to you (strong-side winger):** If a corner battle squirts the puck up the strong-side boards toward you, engage it. That's not a corner battle — it's your breakout opportunity. Grab the puck, get your feet moving, and start the exit.

**Exception — when the center is in the corner (weak-side winger only):** If the puck is in the strong-side corner and the center has gone in to help your defender, the **weak-side winger** collapses from the point to the high slot to cover the area the center has vacated. Anyone with a clear view should call the rotation — you, the center, or the weak-side defender. The strong-side winger holds their own point coverage. As soon as the puck moves out of the corner, the weak-side winger rotates back up to their point.

### Defensive Zone: Without the Puck — Ready to Break Out

![](diagram:winger-high-then-down-the-wall)

```facts
Position: High and level with your man while they have the puck; down the wall between the hash marks and the goal line the moment your team wins it, in the conventional swing breakout
Action: Watch the battle rather than the puck and read who is coming out of it with possession
Action: Turn your feet before the puck is won, but don't leave the point until it actually is
Technique: Present yourself as an outlet — stick on the ice, feet already moving, calling for it
Convention: The trip down the wall to the outlet belongs to the conventional swing breakout — flat/spread holds you higher, stretch sends a winger up ice, so find out which you're in
Key: Anticipating while still onside and able to get back is coached; abandoning coverage on a hope is not — the test is whether you can recover
Goal: Be the first line of the breakout and get into the neutral zone with speed
```

*The section above is you denying an opponent who has the puck. This one is the moment either side of that: the puck is loose or your team is winning it, and your job flips from coverage to outlet.*

**Where to be:** This is the one job on the ice that is defined by a *move* rather than a spot, so hold both ends of it:

- **While they still have the puck** — high on your side, roughly level with the opposing defenceman you are covering. Unchanged from the section above.
- **As your team wins it** — down the wall on your side, to **between the hash marks and the goal line**. That is the outlet position, and it is **a little under 44 to 64 feet below** where you started — the blue line is 64 ft out from the goal line and **the point sits just inside it rather than on it**, while the outlet band runs from level with the faceoff dot, 20 ft out, down to the goal line ([Rink Map](../foundation/rink_map.md) covers both figures). **In the conventional swing breakout**, that is — under a **flat/spread** structure you hold higher toward the blue line, and under a **stretch or flying-winger** structure one winger leaves up ice altogether. Find out which you're in (see the note under *Defensive Zone: Carrying the Puck — Free Space* above, and [Breakouts](../systems/breakouts.md)).

Staying high is what makes that trip short and makes you the first outlet available; it is not where you receive the pass. A winger who never leaves the point is as wrong as one who left it early — the pass to a winger standing at the blue line is long, flat and intercepted, and it arrives in your back. Come down as the puck becomes yours.

**What to do:** Watch the battle rather than the puck, and read who is going to come out of it with possession. The instant it is yours, start down the wall and present yourself as an outlet — stick on the ice, feet already moving, calling for it. Do not leave the point before your team actually has it; do turn your feet so that when they do, the first stride is already going.

**Goal:** Be the first line of the breakout. Get into the neutral zone with speed the moment your team has possession.

**Key considerations:** Staying high does two jobs at once — it covers the opposing point on defence *and* it leaves you one short trip down the wall from the outlet spot. Both are lost the moment you drift into the corner, which is why the discipline of staying high pays twice. There is a real distinction to hold onto here, and it separates a good winger from a cheating one: **turning your feet in anticipation while you are still onside and still able to get back is coached and valuable; abandoning your coverage on a hope is not.** The test is whether you can recover if you read it wrong. Full treatment in [Breakouts](../systems/breakouts.md), which is the authority for breakout positioning.

## Neutral Zone

The transition zone. Your job is to carry the puck through your lane and support the breakout.

---

### Neutral Zone: Carrying the Puck — Free Space

```facts
Position: In your lane along the boards, on your own side of the ice
Action: Skate hard through the neutral zone — carry into the offensive zone if the path is open, or set up an entry play with the centre
Goal: Move the puck through the neutral zone efficiently and enter the offensive zone with speed
Never: Drift into the centre of the ice — that's the centre's lane, and staying wide is what keeps the ice open for your line
```

**Where to be:** In your lane along the boards, carrying through the neutral zone.

![](diagram:neutral-zone-lanes)

**What to do:** Skate hard through the neutral zone in your lane. Look to carry into the offensive zone if the path is open, or set up for a zone entry play with the center. Stay on your side of the ice.

**Goal:** Move the puck through the neutral zone efficiently and enter the offensive zone with speed.

**Key considerations:** Don't drift to the center of the ice — that's the center's lane. Staying wide keeps the ice open and creates proper spacing for your line.

---

### Neutral Zone: Carrying the Puck — Under Pressure

```facts
Position: Along the boards in your lane
Options: Hard rim, soft area dump, chip past the defenceman, cross-corner dump, or a flip over a trap
Read: Chip past a defenceman who is stepping up; soft-dump when you'll win the race; flip when a trap has closed every route along the ice
Technique: Angle your chase to take away their easier escape rather than skating straight at the puck
Never: Land a flip from your own side of the centre red line past the goal line. Land it behind their defence but short of the line instead, or you have iced it
Never: Fight through neutral-zone pressure with the puck — coaching material puts the making of odd-man rushes here; nothing published classifies them by the play that created them
Read: Dump when nobody is arriving with you, or when your support is late — a read, not the default when in doubt
Key: NHL tracking at 5-on-5 put a carry-in at about twice the value of a dump-in; the one-third break-even is modelled from one team's games and an assumed shooting percentage — Zone Entries covers both
```

**Where to be:** Along the boards in your lane.

**What to do:** If pressured, make a quick decision: dump the puck deep into the offensive zone and chase it, or pass back to a defenseman to reset. Don't try to fight through pressure in the neutral zone with the puck.

**Dump-ins are not one play. Pick the right kind.** You are your team's most frequent dump-in taker, so this is a core skill, not a last resort. See [Zone Entries](../systems/zone_entries.md) for the full treatment.

- **Hard rim** — around the boards behind the net to the far corner. Beats a defence that collapses to the middle, and arrives where your first forechecker is heading. Commonly the default of the five — an impression rather than a counted one, and [Zone Entries](../systems/zone_entries.md), which owns them, ranks none of them by frequency.
- **Soft area dump** — a gentle chip into a specific empty area, aimed to *stop* rather than carom. Used when you'll win the race; a hard dump just gives it to their defenceman.
- **Chip past the defenceman** — flick it off the boards past a defender who is stepping up or standing up at the line, and skate onto it yourself. This is a *possession* play, not a surrender, and it's the best option against a defenceman who is stepping up.
- **Cross-corner / north-south dump** — into the far corner away from where their D is retreating, forcing the longest possible retrieval skate.
- **Flip / area dump over a trap** — lift the puck high so it travels *over* a congested neutral zone and lands in open space behind their defence. This is the answer when a trap has closed every route along the ice. From your own side of the centre red line, land it past the defence but **before the goal line**, or you have iced it — past the red line it is not icing however far it travels (see *Icing* below).

![](diagram:dump-soft-area)

![](diagram:dump-chip-past)

![](diagram:dump-cross-corner)

![](diagram:dump-flip-over-trap)

**Chasing it properly matters more than the dump itself.** Don't skate straight at the puck — that lets the retriever pick either side. Angle your route to take away their easier escape (usually the strong-side wall) so they're forced to the side your support is arriving on. A dump-in with nobody chasing on an angle is just giving the puck away politely.

**Goal:** Keep possession or dump the puck safely into the offensive zone.

**Key considerations:** A turnover in the neutral zone is expensive, and it is where coaching material puts the making of odd-man rushes — where the attackers outnumber the defenders coming back, e.g. 2-on-1 or 3-on-2, a high-danger scoring chance against your team. **That is coaching consensus rather than a tracked figure: nothing published classifies odd-man rushes by the play that created them**, and not every one starts here — [Defending the Rush](../systems/defending_the_rush.md) owns how they are made and lists five causes, one of which is in the offensive zone. So do not try to stickhandle through two players at the line.

**But that is not an argument for dumping it in by default.** The tracked evidence runs the other way: at 5-on-5 a carry-in was worth about twice a dump-in, a failed carry cost far less than it feels like it does, and the study's own conclusion was that players give the puck up at the blue line too easily. That was hand-tracked NHL play, not your league, and it says nothing about amateur hockey — so take the direction rather than the decimals. The dump is a **read**: put it in when nobody is arriving with you, or when your support is late and the alternative is forcing it into two defenders. [Zone Entries](../systems/zone_entries.md) covers the figures, including the roughly one-third confidence in a carry that is the break-even — a threshold modelled from one team's tracked games and an assumed shooting percentage, so take its shape rather than its number.

### Neutral Zone: Pressuring an Opponent with the Puck

```facts
Position: Under a containment structure, in your lane, blocking the passing lanes through your side
Action: Cut off the passing options and force the puck carrier wide toward the boards
Convention: Lane-blocking belongs to a containment structure — a 1-2-2, a 1-2-2 trap or a 1-3-1 — and is not a law of hockey
Options: Under an aggressive press the lane-blocking instruction reverses: forwards hunt the puck and the weak-side winger does leave the lane
Action: Find out which neutral-zone system your team plays and what the call is for switching — most run two or three depending on the score and the shift
Risk: Leave your lane under containment and you open a seam the single defenceman behind their three-man line cannot cover
Goal: Prevent a clean entry — force a difficult pass, force them wide, or force a dump-in
```

**Where to be:** In your lane, blocking passing lanes through your side.

**What to do:** When the opposing team is moving the puck through the neutral zone, stay in your lane and cut off the passing options. Force the puck carrier wide toward the boards. In a containment structure, don't leave your lane to chase. Block the lane instead and let the pressure develop naturally.

> **Which of those two things you do is your team's neutral-zone system, not a law of hockey.** The lane-blocking job described here belongs to a **containment structure** — a **1-2-2**, a **1-2-2 trap** or a **1-3-1** — where the whole design is to deny the middle and wait for a mistake, and where one forward leaving the wall to chase opens the seam instantly. The realistic alternative is an **aggressive neutral-zone press**, the middle-third shape behind a 2-1-2 forecheck: there the instruction reverses, forwards hunt the puck, and the weak-side winger *does* leave the lane to pressure, because the point is to take the puck back rather than wait for it. **Find out which one your team plays — and what the call is for switching**, because most teams run two or three depending on the score and the shift. [Neutral Zone Systems](../systems/neutral_zone_systems.md) is the authority and sets out all of them.

![](diagram:nz-1-2-2-containment)

![](diagram:nz-1-3-1)

![](diagram:nz-pressure)

**Goal:** Prevent a clean entry into your defensive zone. Force the opponent to make a difficult pass, go wide, or dump the puck in.

**Key considerations:** Under a containment structure, blocking lanes is more effective than chasing: if you leave your lane to pressure the puck, you create a gap that the opponent will exploit, and the single defenceman behind their three-man line cannot cover two lanes. Stay disciplined. Under a press that trade is reversed deliberately — you accept the gap in exchange for winning the puck back sooner — so the discipline there is to pressure *together* rather than one player freelancing.

### Neutral Zone: Without the Puck — Filling Your Lane on the Rush

```facts
Position: In your lane, moving toward the offensive zone
Action: Time your skating so you arrive at the blue line in stride, and be a moving target for the centre or defenceman
Technique: Drag a trail skate along the line while your body, stick and momentum get ahead of it — and keep the blade touching
Never: Drag the trail skate with your head down — your body, hands and stick are in the zone before your skate is, and that is where a defender standing up at the line will be
Rule: A dragged, touching skate keeps you onside wherever you play; a lifted trail skate is onside in the NHL, IIHF and PWHL but offside under USA Hockey Rule 630(a) and Hockey Canada Rule 6.11
Rule: Tagging up is a five-man act — the arm stays up until every teammate has cleared, in all six books; four of them word it "at the same instant" (NHL 83.3(i); PWHL 85.3(i))
Rule: Hockey Canada 6.11(b)(ii) and CARHA 72(e)(2) word the tag-up requirement as skate contact from every attacker and as a result — the zone "completely clear of attacking players", CARHA's in the Note under the clause — rather than as an instant, which comes to the same thing
Rule: ⚠️ First check you have a tag-up at all — USA Hockey Rule 630(d) grants the delayed off-side only at Youth 15-Only and above, Girls 16U and above, High School and Adult; below those the whistle is immediate
Action: Tag, then look before you go — as the winger usually first to the line, you are the one who re-enters too early
Never: Coast at the blue line to be safe — arriving late and slow costs your line more than a whistle would
Read: On your off wing the lane is the same, but the puck sits on your inside hand — it helps entering the zone and costs you on a breakout pass up your own wall
```

**Where to be:** In your lane, moving toward the offensive zone.

**What to do:** Skate hard through the neutral zone in your lane. Be a target for a pass from the center or defenseman. Time your skating so you arrive at the offensive zone blue line in stride — ready to attack.

**Goal:** Be a moving target and create a passing option through the neutral zone.

**Key considerations:** Timing matters, and the offside rule is far more generous than most players assume — you are offside only when **both** skates have completely crossed the blue line before the puck has, so a trailing skate on the line keeps you onside — and one held in the air over the neutral zone does too **under the NHL, IIHF and PWHL books, but not under USA Hockey, whose Rule 630(a) requires skate *contact* with the neutral zone or the blue line, and not under Hockey Canada, whose Rule 6.11 counts *"only the player's skate(s) that are in physical contact with the ice surface"***. A delayed offside is recoverable too — but **tagging up is a five-man act, not a solo one.** NHL Rule 83.3(i) requires that *"all players* of the offending team clear the zone at the same instant (skate contact with the blue line)", and the IIHF, USA Hockey and the PWHL say the same — **PWHL Rule 85.3(i) is that sentence in the feminine**; **Hockey Canada Rule 6.11(b)(ii) (the tag-up clause) and CARHA Rule 72(e)(2) ask instead for skate contact from every attacker and state it as a result — the zone *"completely clear of attacking players"*, CARHA's in the Note printed under the clause — rather than as an instant, and come to the same thing**, so playing it as one act keeps you legal wherever you have a tag-up at all. ⚠️ **And check that you do, because USA Hockey is the one book here that rations it by age:** under **USA Hockey Rule 630(d)** the delayed off-side and the tag-up exist only *"(For all Youth 15-Only levels and above, all Girls 16U levels and above, High School and Adult classifications only)"* — below those the whistle is immediate and there is nothing to tag up to. No other book here sets such a floor. Skate contact with the line is enough for you individually, so one foot on the line does your part; but the arm stays up and nobody is re-entered until the last teammate has also tagged. Tag, then **look** before you go — re-entering while a teammate is still deep is what gets the play whistled off, and as the winger who is usually first to the line you are the one who does it. **The rule in full, including tagging up and what turns a delayed offside into a whistle, is in [Rules Primer](../foundation/rules_primer.md) and [Zone Entries](../systems/zone_entries.md).**

**The winger-specific version is a technique, and it is worth drilling.** You are the player who attacks the blue line widest and fastest, so you are the one the rule rewards most: **drag a trail skate along the line while your body, stick and momentum get ahead of it.** Done properly you are a full stride into the zone, at speed, and still legal. **One thing in the trail-skate drag is not free, and it is your head.** The offside rule does not care where your body is, and it says nothing about what is in front of it: you arrive at top speed with your body, hands and stick already across the line, and that is the ice a defenceman standing up at their own blue line is defending. **A head up is the condition of the technique, not an extra** — [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md) owns that rule and writes it without exceptions, because you cannot brace for a hit you cannot see and a body that is surprised does not absorb contact. **Keep the blade in contact with the ice or the paint.** A dragged, touching skate keeps you onside wherever you play; a *lifted* trail skate is onside under the NHL, the IIHF and the PWHL but **offside under USA Hockey Rule 630(a) and Hockey Canada Rule 6.11** — two books, and you need to know which is yours. **How much North American rec and youth hockey runs on USA Hockey's book or Hockey Canada's is a question about leagues rather than about books, and this document has no count for it, so find out which book your league runs.** The alternative — coasting at the line to be safe — is the classic way a winger arrives at an offensive-zone battle with no speed and loses it, on the coaching account rather than a counted ranking. Do not slow down to be safe. Arriving late and slow costs your line more than a whistle would.

![](diagram:winger-arriving-at-the-line)

**If you are playing your off wing** — a left shot on the right side, or the reverse — your lane is the same but the puck now sits on your inside hand, which helps you entering the zone and costs you on a breakout pass up your own wall; [Switching Positions → Playing your off wing](switching_positions.md) covers the trade and the fix.

## Offensive Zone

Your natural home as a forward. Your job is to create scoring chances, win battles in the corners, and put pressure on the opposing defence.

---

### Offensive Zone: Carrying the Puck — Free Space

```facts
Position: Along the half-wall, or driving toward the net from the wing
Targets: Shoot, feed the centre in the slot, or find the defenceman at the point
Action: If you have space to drive the net, use your speed and get to the front of it
Convention: "Shoot first" is a bias, not a rule — a teammate with a clear look at an open side is always worth the extra pass
Never: Stand still — movement is what creates the opportunities
Goal: Create a shot on net, or force the defence to react and open space for teammates
```

**Where to be:** Along the half-wall (the boards area roughly level with the faceoff dot — between the corner and the point), or driving toward the net from the wing.

**What to do:** If open on the half-wall, look to shoot, pass to the center in the slot, or find the defenseman at the point. If you have space to drive the net, do it — use your speed and get to the front of the net.

**Goal:** Create a shot on net, or force the defence to react and open space for teammates.

**Key considerations:** Don't stand still. Movement creates opportunities. If you're open, look to shoot first — most wingers pass up good shots rather than take bad ones. That said, "shoot first" is a bias, not a rule: a teammate with a clear look at an open side is always worth the extra pass. What you're avoiding is the habit of *always* looking to pass, which lets a defence sit back and take away the seam.

---

### Offensive Zone: Carrying the Puck — Under Pressure

```facts
Position: In the corner or along the boards
Technique: Use your body to protect the puck and fight to maintain possession — forearm and hip to the wall, not the point of your shoulder, and not the whole shoulder if you can help it
Never: Turn your back to the wall — USA Hockey warns your checker: "Even a light hit from behind could inflict a severe head or neck injury". Skates parallel, forearm and hip, head up, chin off your chest
Never: Duck to avoid a check — in a head-down collision with the boards or a goal post, your neck can break at walking speed; no speed is slow enough to make that safe. Head up, chin off your chest instead
Options: Pass back to the defenceman at the point, or hold possession until the centre can support you
Read: Look up before passing to the point — the strong-side defender may have stepped down to support the cycle
Never: Force the puck out of the corner through heavy traffic — a rushed pass turns over possession
Goal: Maintain possession in the offensive zone and keep the pressure going
```

**Where to be:** In the corner or along the boards.

**What to do:** Use your body to protect the puck. Fight to maintain possession — but the same override applies here as in your own end: never turn your back to the wall and never duck. Turning your back makes the contact a hit from behind, and USA Hockey's warning on those is that *"Even a light hit from behind could inflict a severe head or neck injury"*; ducking straightens the cervical spine, and that injury can occur at walking speed. Skates parallel to the boards, forearm and hip into the contact, head up and chin off your chest. See [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md), which covers it. Look for a pass back to the defenseman at the point, or hold possession until the center can support you. Don't try to force the puck out of the corner through heavy traffic.

![](diagram:winger-corner-and-the-empty-point)

**Goal:** Maintain possession in the offensive zone and keep the pressure going.

**Key considerations:** The corners are a battle area. Protecting the puck and waiting for support is better than a rushed pass that turns over possession. Your defenseman is usually an option at the point — but the strong-side defender may have stepped down (moved from the blue line toward the top of the faceoff circle) to support the cycle, so look up before passing blindly to the point.

---

### Offensive Zone: Pressuring an Opponent with the Puck

```facts
Position: As F2, in behind F1 on the strong side; as F3, higher in the zone covering the middle and the weak side in the 2-1-2 default, or puck-side in the stacked variant
Key: F1, F2 and F3 are roles set by order of arrival — if you dumped the puck in and got there first, you are F1
Read: Take the next-most-likely escape route from wherever F1 has pushed the carrier — typically the strong-side boards or behind the net
Technique: Pin the puck against the boards, not the player
Rule: IIHF women's Rule 101.1 prohibits using the boards to eliminate an opponent from the play, push her into the boards, or pin her along the boards
Rule: Do not hear the boards-pinning prohibition as a women's-hockey rule only — USA Hockey's holding rule reaches the same act for every player, naming "wrapping one or both arms around the opponent along the boards in a manner that pins them against the boards" (Rule 622 Note)
Convention: A 2-1-2 sends two forwards deep and a 1-2-2 sends one, and F3 sits weak-side by default or puck-side in the stacked variant — find out which your team runs
Goal: Force a turnover or a bad outlet pass by trapping the carrier with no escape route
Key: Forechecking is about angles, not speed. Stay in your role and trust your linemates to be in theirs
```

**Forechecking** means putting pressure on the opposing team in their own defensive zone (your offensive zone) — trying to win the puck back rather than retreat.

> **F1, F2 and F3 are roles, not positions — and not people.** They are defined purely by **order of arrival**: whoever gets to the puck first is F1, the second support is F2, the highest is F3. If you dumped the puck in and you're first on it, **you are F1**, even though you're a winger and the centre is on the ice. Don't wait for the centre to arrive. Take the role your position on the ice gives you and let your linemates fill in around you.
>
> How many forwards go deep also depends on the system your team runs — a 2-1-2 sends two in, a 1-2-2 sends one. See [Forechecking Systems](../systems/forechecking_systems.md). The description below assumes a **2-1-2** with the centre arriving first, which is the house default throughout but not a law of hockey.

Your role depends on which side of the ice the puck is on, and on who got there first.

**Where to be:**
- **Strong-side winger (F2):** Coming in behind F1 on the strong side to seal the puck. Read where F1 has pushed the carrier and take the next-most-likely escape route — typically the strong-side boards (corner) or behind the net.
- **Weak-side winger (F3):** Higher in the zone, covering any opposing forward leaking up the middle or a cross-ice pass to the weak side. **Which side F3 sits on is a genuine coaching split, not a law of hockey.** The weak-side default described here is the common one; in the **strong-side (stacked) variant** F3 shades to the puck side instead, so all three forwards overload one corner and the far-side breakout is conceded deliberately. Find out which your team runs — [Forechecking Systems](../systems/forechecking_systems.md) sets out both.

![](diagram:forecheck-212)

![](diagram:forecheck-212-stacked)

**What to do:**
- **As F2:** Be F1's second support on the strong side. Seal the puck wherever F1 has angled it. **Pin the *puck* against the boards, not the player** — that is the distinction [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md) draws, and it matters legally: **IIHF women's Rule 101.1 prohibits using the boards to eliminate an opponent from the play, push her into the boards, or pin her along the boards.** ⚠️ **That is the plainest wording of it, not the only book that reaches it:** USA Hockey's holding rule names *"[w]rapping one or both arms around the opponent along the boards in a manner that pins them against the boards and prevents them from playing the puck or skating"* as holding, for every player (Rule 622, Note), which is the same sentence quoted in *Defensive Zone: Carrying the Puck — Under Pressure* above. See [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md).
- **As F3:** Stay higher and read the play. Cover the weak-side breakout option and be ready to transition the moment the puck is won.

**Goal:** Force a turnover or a bad outlet pass by trapping the puck carrier with no escape route.

**Key considerations:** Effective forechecking is about angles, not just speed. Read where F1 has pushed the puck and seal the next-most-likely escape. Stay in your F2 or F3 role and trust your teammates to be in theirs — if everyone chases the puck, the opponent will pass around you.

### Offensive Zone: Without the Puck — Getting Open and Crashing the Net

```facts
Position: On the half-wall, in your side's faceoff circle, or at the side of the net and the goalmouth
Never: Crowd the high slot, between the dots and the top of the circles, when another player is already there. Which player that is depends on the system, so find out rather than assuming it is the centre
Technique: For a one-timer, open your stance toward the passer with your weight back, start the backswing as the pass is released, and hit the ice just behind the puck
Read: If the pass is bad, don't force the one-timer. Take the extra touch, because a miss ends up in the neutral zone going the other way
Action: Crash the net on every shot toward it. Driving in from the wall or your circle, arrive as the shot is taken rather than well before it; on the net-front job you are planted before the release
Technique: At the net front a defender you feel, not watch, moves you, so know where the near post is before the shot and take the shove braced — wide base, knees and hips giving — never against the goaltender
Never: Meet a goal post head first, and never duck. Head up, chin off your chest instead. A head-down, head-first hit on a goal post is the same injury as one on the boards, and can happen at walking speed
Goal: Be a threat in your half of the zone, particularly from the side of the net for rebounds
```

**Where to be:** On the half-wall, in your side's faceoff circle, or at the side of the net and the goalmouth. **Stay out of the *high* slot — between the dots and the top of the circles — unless your team's structure sends you there. The goalmouth, meaning the immediate net front, is a different patch of ice and it is very often yours** (see *Net-front and the crease* below). [Offensive Zone Play](../systems/offensive_zone_play.md), which owns the zone, gives that upper layer to no position: it has one forward supporting in the high slot to form a triangle with the point, a defenceman who has activated down to the top of the circle receiving there, and the late-arriving trailer using it. Which of those your team asks for is a coaching choice, so find out. Two layers, two jobs; running them together is how two forwards end up standing on the same square metre.

![](diagram:winger-offensive-zone-patches)

**What to do:** Get open for a pass. From the half-wall, be ready to shoot or feed the center in the slot. From your faceoff circle, be ready for a one-timer off a cross-ice pass. From the side of the net, look for rebounds and tip-ins. Crash the net on every shot toward it, timed by which of the two jobs you have — the drive in from the wall or your circle, or the net-front assignment.

**The net front is the one place you take contact you never see coming, and it is worth learning before you learn the rules that govern it.** Your eyes are deliberately on the puck — that is what makes you a tip and rebound threat — so a defender working under your arms to walk you sideways is a body you are *feeling* rather than watching, and you can be moved several feet without ever looking up. The path they walk you along has a steel goal frame at one edge and a set goaltender at the other. Two habits cover it. **Know where the near post is before the shot is taken**, rather than finding out where it is by hitting it — USA Hockey's *Heads Up Hockey Program Guide* lists *"avoiding goal posts while driving to the net"* among the answers it expects when it asks players what playing safe hockey means. And **take the shove braced**: wide base, knees bent, weight low, knees and hips giving rather than a locked frame. **Head up, chin off your chest, and never duck.** The goal frame sits *beside* the boards in USA Hockey's sentence on how these injuries happen, not below it — a head *"slightly flexed (head down) while making head-on contact with the boards or goal post"* — and that injury *"can occur at walking speed"*, so a slow, crowded net front is not a safe one. USA Hockey's *Introduction To Body Contact* writes the instruction plainly: *"Hit the boards or goal posts with an arm, a leg, or anything but your head first."* **None of this is scoped to checking leagues** — a legal box-out, a legal seal and an illegal shove all put the same body in the same place, so it applies wherever you play. **And there is one body at the net front you must not take braced — the goaltender's.** Give ground the moment a goalie leans into you, then re-set the screen. [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md) covers the net-front battle from both sides.

**The one-timer — the basics of actually hitting one.** Full detail in [Shooting](../technique/shooting.md).

- **Set your feet before the pass arrives.** Open your stance toward the passer with your weight already on your back foot. Everything after this is just falling forward.
- **Start your backswing as the pass is released**, not when it arrives. The miss to guard against is being late.
- **Hit the ice just behind the puck**, the same as a slap shot — let the flex of the stick do the work rather than trying to smash it.
- **Aim low far side or for a tip.** A one-timer's value is that the goalie has to move across; you don't need to pick a corner.
- **If the pass is bad, don't force it.** A missed one-timer usually ends up in the neutral zone going the other way. Take the extra touch.

**Goal:** Be a threat in your half of the zone — particularly from the side of the net for rebounds.

**Key considerations:** Crashing the net for rebounds is one of the most effective ways for a winger to score — **coaching consensus rather than a ranked measurement**, and the ice in front of the net does convert several times better than the perimeter, on figures whose own source names no dataset ([Shooting](../technique/shooting.md) owns them and that qualification). **Time your arrival by which of the two jobs you have.** Driving in from the wall or your own circle, arrive as the shot is taken rather than well before it — go early and the defender has time to establish position on you and turn you away from the puck. **If the net front is your assignment it runs the other way**: you are planted there before the release, because a screen you are still walking into is not a screen. That split is coaching craft rather than a measured finding.

#### Net-front and the crease

```facts
Position: At the net front, work just outside the blue paint, off the red line, get in the goalie's eyes, keep your stick free for tips, keep your body out of the crease — head up, and know where the post is
Rule: NHL, PWHL and IIHF Situation 5 E allows the goal to a screener planted "on the crease line or outside the goal crease" obstructing the goalie's vision — a no-contact screen there is legal; but that is an appendix table, and every book that settles it counts that line as crease, so stay off it
Rule: The screen test is NHL and IIHF Rule 69 and PWHL Rule 71 only — USA Hockey, Hockey Canada and CARHA reach no screen set from outside the crease; USA Hockey and CARHA write no goaltender-vision provision, and Hockey Canada's two references to the goaltender's view reach only a player in the crease
Rule: Hockey Canada 8.5 is not flat — a teammate's goal from outside the crease stands if you stay passive in it, and is disallowed once you play the puck, interfere with the play or obstruct the goalie's view or movements
Never: Stay in the crease after a defender shoves you in. Get out instead; under NHL and IIHF 69.1, PWHL 71.1, the goal dies if you impair the goalie from in there, whoever started the contact
Rule: USA Hockey does not run 69.1's premise — 625(b) bars a goal with an attacker in the crease whoever scored, unless the puck preceded them in, the goalie is out of it, or a defender put them there; and its Casebook Situation 10 disallows the goal for a stick in the paint, goalkeeper in the crease
Rule: Two of USA Hockey's crease reliefs are narrower than they read — Casebook Rule 625, Situation 9 disallows the goal and adds an interference minor where you stopped a returning goalkeeper making a play; and a defender pushing you in relieves your position only, not what you do next
Rule: NHL and IIHF 69.4 and PWHL 71.4 protect a goalkeeper who has played the puck outside the crease and is then "prevented from returning" by "the deliberate actions of an attacking player" — a separate foul from 69.1's crease-position rule, turning on intent rather than on contact
Rule: Hockey Canada splits the two consequences — Interpretation 2 to 8.5(a) governs the penalty, and a minor on you is mandatory if you are fouled into the goaltender and then make no effort to avoid them
Rule: Hockey Canada's Interpretation 1 to 8.5(a) governs the goal instead, and asks more of you — the goal stands only where the foul left you "unable to avoid contact with the goaltender", so it is no relief from that minor
Rule: Hockey Canada is not capped at a minor — 8.5(b) reaches a major plus game misconduct at the referee's discretion on the degree of violence of impact and mandatorily where the foul injures, and above that 8.5(c) is a match penalty for attempting to injure or deliberately injuring
Rule: Charging a goaltender is a mandatory ejection in two books — Hockey Canada 8.5(b) anywhere on the ice, and CARHA 52(b) both in the crease and for any charge that injures an opponent
Rule: The other four leave it to the referee — IIHF 42.4, NHL and PWHL 42.1, and USA Hockey 607(c), which adds the privileged area and writes no bare minor; the NHL, the PWHL, USA Hockey and Hockey Canada add a match penalty on top
```

You are the net-front player more often than anyone, so the working rule: **work just outside the blue paint and off its red edge, get in the goalie's eyes, keep your stick free for tips, and keep your body out of the crease.** Screening from *outside* the crease without contact is legal and one of the most valuable things you can do — and under the **NHL, the PWHL and the IIHF** you do not have to reason it out from the shape of the rule, because all three books print the answer to this exact play. Their reference tables allow the goal where an attacking player *"plants himself on the crease line or outside the goal crease, as to obstruct the goalkeeper's vision and impair his ability to defend his goal, and a goal is scored"* — *"Goal is allowed."* (NHL Reference Table 14, **Situation 5 E**; the IIHF prints the same row in the plural at Table 16 of its 2025/26 Appendix IV; the PWHL prints it in its own pronouns — *"plants herself on the crease line or outside the goal crease"* — under *Screening situations* in its own Table 14, which it heads **Rule 71**, because the PWHL numbers goalkeeper interference 71 where the other two number it 69.) **The rule text is built the same way:** 69.1's disallowing sentence reaches an attacker who *"enters the goal crease"*, and 69.4, the rule for outside it, takes the **goal** for contact only. ⚠️ **The goal is not the whole of 69.4, though — it also reaches a penalty with no contact in it anywhere.** Where a goalkeeper has played the puck outside the crease and is then kept from returning to it *"due to the deliberate actions of an attacking"* player, that player *"may be penalized"* for goalkeeper interference — both books, at the same number. **That is not a screen, and it is not the legal play above**: planting yourself in the route a goaltender needs to get home is deliberate by definition, so take their eyes and leave them their path. ⚠️ **And do not carry the crease reading further than it goes in the other direction either** — 69.1's clause (1) reaches a player whose positioning *"impairs the goalkeeper's ability to move freely within his crease or defend his goal"*, and that last phrase names no location at all, which is why the crease keying is a reading of how the rule is assembled rather than something either book states in terms. All three add that the rule is *"enforced exclusively in accordance with the on-ice judgement of the Referee(s)"* — so it is not a call you will argue from the ice. ⚠️ **Only the NHL's and the IIHF's then make it challengeable**, *"but may be subject to a Coach's Challenge"*, the IIHF's version also naming a Video Review; the **PWHL's 71.5 reads *"Coach's Challenge — Not applicable"***, sending it to a *"Central Situation Room Review"* instead, so in that league a goal disallowed on you is not one your bench can challenge. ⚠️ **And keep off the red line itself** — every book that settles the question in its rule text or glossary counts the line as crease, but each in a different layer of its book: the IIHF in its rule text at **Rule 1.7**, USA Hockey in the **Note** to **Rule 625(b)** and again in its *Casebook*, Hockey Canada in its **Glossary** rather than in its own Rule 1.5, and CARHA likewise in its **Glossary** entry *Creases* rather than in its own **Rule 5(c)**, both of which use the NHL's looser wording. Against those, the Situation 5 E row above allows the goal to an attacker planted *"on the crease line"* in both books, so the IIHF answers that play in two directions. ⚠️ **No book tells you which of its own layers wins, and that is the reason to stay off the line rather than a reason to doubt it** — off the line is the reading that holds under every one of them. **Everything in this subsection is the legal half of the job. The physical half — knowing where the near post is before the shot, taking a shove from a defender you are feeling rather than watching, head up and chin off your chest — is set out under *Getting Open and Crashing the Net* above, and it outranks all of it.**

**That crease keying belongs to the NHL, the PWHL and the IIHF, and to no other book on disk.** For a screen set from *outside* the crease, **USA Hockey writes no goaltender-vision provision at all, in its playing rules or its Casebook** — its 625(a)(1) *"protective screen"* is a screen set on a **checker**, not on a goaltender — **CARHA writes none either**, and Hockey Canada's only two references to the goaltender's view both reach a player who is standing *in* the crease. ⚠️ **So out there those three books are the more permissive**, and the crease line does all the work.

Inside the blue paint, assume any contact costs you the goal. **Being shoved in is not a licence to stay there.** Under **NHL and IIHF Rule 69.1**, and the **PWHL's 71.1** in the same words, a defender pushing you into the goalie is not contact you initiated — *"provided the attacking player has made a reasonable effort to avoid such contact"* — but the goal still dies if, once you are in the crease, your position impairs the goalie's ability to defend it. Get out, and let the officials sort out who put you there.

**The IIHF book adds a sentence neither the NHL's nor the PWHL's does:** an attacking player in the crease who *"does not leave immediately"* can have play stopped for a neutral-zone draw — so internationally the instruction to get out has an explicit rule behind it.

> **⚠️ Under USA Hockey the governing rule is not 69.1, and it cuts both ways.** **Rule 625(b)** is a flat position rule — *"No goal may be scored with an attacking player in the goal crease unless the puck has preceded the player(s) into the goal crease or the goalkeeper is out of the goal crease area"* — **and that second exception runs out the moment you are what stops the goalkeeper getting back to it** — which is **stricter** than the NHL's impairment test, and merely standing in the crease can stop play for a neutral-zone draw. But its push-in relief is **broader**: where a defender physically interferes with you and that puts you in the crease, *"play shall not be stopped and any legal goal scored shall be allowed"*, with no reasonable-effort proviso attached. ⚠️ **And USA Hockey is the one book of the six that counts your stick as you.** 625(b) writes its stoppage for an attacking player who *"stands, **holds their stick**, or skates through the goal crease"*, and its *Casebook* at **Rule 625, Situation 10** takes the goal off on the same reach: *"if a goal is scored while an attacking player has their stick and/or skates in the opponent's goal crease while the goalkeeper is in the crease, the Referee shall have no alternative but to disallow the goal."* **So the tip you are reaching for can cost the goal with both skates outside the line** — the other five books key their crease bar to your body ([Language and Glossary](../foundation/language_and_glossary.md) sets this out under *the paint*).
>
> ⚠️ **Do not stop reading 625(b) at "the goalkeeper is out of the goal crease area."** For a net-front winger that clause reads as a licence — the goalie has come out, so get to the paint ahead of the puck — and it is only half the rule. The **USA Hockey Casebook 2025-29** puts that exact play at **Rule 625, Situation 9**: the goalkeeper leaves the crease to stop a shot, an attacker enters it before the puck, a teammate scores. The goal counts — *"If the goalkeeper is completely out of their crease, an attacking player may precede the puck into the crease and any legally scored goal in this situation shall be allowed."* **And the same situation takes it back if you are what stopped them getting home:** *"However, if the goalkeeper is attempting to return to the goal crease and there is any interference by the attacking player who prevents the goalkeeper from making a play, then the goal must be disallowed and an interference penalty shall be assessed."* So the crease is open only while the goaltender is genuinely out of the play. Beat the puck in if you like — but do not stand between a scrambling goaltender and their net, because that is the goal wiped out **and** two minutes for you. **But that relief is to your position, not to what you do next.** 617(c)(4) disallows the goal both for an attacker *positioned in the crease* **and** for one *"interfering with the goalkeeper"*, and the push-in parenthetical is written into the first limb only — while **625(a)(8)** remains a minor for physical contact that interferes with the goalie's movement.
>
> **Hockey Canada's crease bar is not flat, and the difference lands on a situation you will be standing in front of.** Rule 8.5's Goal Crease Area paragraph begins the same way — *"Unless the puck is in the goal crease area, a player of the attacking team may not stand in the goal crease. If the puck should enter the net while such conditions prevail, the goal will NOT BE ALLOWED"* — and then carves out the net-front case in terms: *"However, if an attacking player is in the goal crease but does not interfere with the Goaltender and another attacking player (who is outside the goal crease) scores, the goal WILL BE ALLOWED provided that the player who was in the goal crease does not attempt to play the puck, interfere with the play or obstruct the Goaltender's view or movements."* **So on a body in the blue and a teammate scoring from outside it — a situation you will meet at the net front — the two books give opposite answers.** What Hockey Canada asks instead is what you *did* in there: its Interpretation 3 to Rule 8.5(a) disallows the goal where a player standing in the crease attempts *"to play the puck, interfere with the play, or impede the goaltender's vision or movements"* — an impairment test that names vision, and the counterpart of NHL 69.3 rather than 69.1. None of that changes the instruction. Keep your feet out of the blue.
>
> **⚠️ Hockey Canada is the fourth answer and the strictest on this exact point.** Its Interpretation 2 to Rule 8.5(a) keeps an effort requirement and makes failure a penalty **on you**: where a player is tripped or cross-checked into the goaltender, *"there must be an effort by the attacking player to avoid making contact with the goaltender. If the player does not make an effort to avoid contact ... they must be penalized for interference with the goaltender"* — and the referee *should* penalise the defender too. **Read that test for what it asks: an *effort*, not a result.**
>
> **The relief Hockey Canada writes sits in a different clause and answers a different question — the goal, not the penalty.** Interpretation 1 to 8.5(a) governs whether the puck counts: *"Any goal scored on a play where an attacking player initiates contact with the goaltender will be disallowed, regardless of whether the contact occurs inside or outside of the goal crease. The only exception to this is where the attacking player is fouled by a defending player and, as a result, is **unable to avoid** contact with the goaltender."* So the goal survives only where the foul left you **unable** to avoid the goalie, while the minor is escaped by **trying** to — a lower bar, in a separate limb. ⚠️ **Interpretation 1 does not relieve you of the minor**, and being fouled first does not either.
>
> **⚠️ And Hockey Canada does not cap this rule at two minutes — everything above decides the goal, and 8.5's lettered clauses decide whether you are still in the game.** **8.5(a)** is the minor. **8.5(b)** reaches *"A Major penalty and Game Misconduct penalty"* at the referee's discretion *"based on the degree of violence of impact"* — and **mandatorily**, *"will be assessed"*, in two named cases: *"to any player who **charges the goaltender**"* (its Note 1 refers the definition of charging to Rule 7.4(b) Interpretation 2), and *"to any player who **injures an opponent** by an Interference with the Goaltender infraction that would otherwise call for a Minor penalty."* **8.5(c)** is a **match penalty** *"to any player who attempts to injure or deliberately injures a goaltender by Interference."* **8.5(e)** makes the game misconduct automatic on any 8.5(b) major, and **8.5(d)** rules a misconduct out entirely — *"No Misconduct penalty may be assessed for interference with the goaltender"* — so there is no ten-minute rung between the two minutes and the ejection. **The practical consequence for the player who stands at the net front more than anyone: arriving into the goaltender is an ejection under this book, and a foul that would have been two minutes becomes one the moment it injures someone.** ⚠️ **Do not file that ejection under "Canadian rules", though — charging a goaltender reaches an ejection tier under the NHL, the PWHL, the IIHF, USA Hockey, Hockey Canada and CARHA alike, and two of the six make it mandatory.** **CARHA Rule 52(b)** is the second: *"A Major penalty and a Game Misconduct shall be assessed to any player who charges a goalkeeper while the goalkeeper is within the goal crease or who injures an opponent as a result of a charge."* **NHL Rule 42.1**: *"A minor, major or a major and a game misconduct shall be imposed on a player who charges a goalkeeper while the goalkeeper is within his goal crease."* **PWHL Rule 42.1** prints that sentence in its own pronouns, *"within her goal crease"*. **IIHF Rule 42.1** writes that sentence without the tiers — *"A penalty shall be imposed on a Player who charges a goalkeeper while the goalkeeper is within their goal crease"* — and its **42.4** reaches *"a major penalty and a game misconduct penalty"* for a Player who *"recklessly endangers their opponent by 'charging'"*, with no injury required. **USA Hockey writes no bare minor for it at all**: Rule **607(c)** assesses *"[a] minor plus a misconduct or a major plus a game misconduct penalty shall be assessed to a player who body checks or charges a goalkeeper while the goalkeeper is within the goal crease or privileged area"*, and its Note 1 sends *"[a]ny deliberate body contact or check that is delivered to the goalkeeper"* to that rule. ⚠️ **And four of those six go past the ejection to a match penalty for charging.** **NHL Rule 42.4** lets the Referee assess one *"if, in his judgment, the player attempted to or deliberately injured his opponent by charging"*, and **PWHL Rule 42.4** the same in its own pronouns; **USA Hockey Rule 607(e)** that *"[a] match penalty for reckless endangerment may also be assessed for charging"*; **Hockey Canada Rule 7.4(c)** that one *"will be assessed to any player who charges an opponent in such a way that they are unable to defend themselves or who deliberately attempts to injure an opponent by charging."* **The other two are outside it for different reasons.** The IIHF's book has no match penalty at all — the phrase appears nowhere in any edition on disk, and its severest tier for charging is 42.4's major plus game misconduct, with supplementary discipline beyond it under 42.5. CARHA does use the tier, but not here: its Rule 34 governs match penalties and its Rule 53(a) reaches one for checking from behind, while Rule 52 writes none into charging at all. *(The charging limb was read in all six books named; the "injures an opponent" limb above has been read in Hockey Canada and CARHA, and nothing here says whether the other four write it.)*

Full rules in [Rules Primer](../foundation/rules_primer.md) and [Offensive Zone Play](../systems/offensive_zone_play.md); the same situation from the goalie's side is in [Goaltender](goaltender.md).

## Other Situations

A quick reference for situations outside the zone-by-zone framework above.

### Faceoffs

```facts
Position: At the hash marks — one winger on the inside hash facing the slot, one on the outside hash facing the boards
Convention: The winger whose natural side matches the faceoff location usually takes the outside hash, but assignments vary by team, so ask
Action: Read the result of the draw and react instantly — pounce on a loose puck, support the centre, or get to your defensive position
Never: Creep inside the circle or over your hash marks, or lean on an opponent, before the drop — skates in all five books, stick in three, body in four; your centre is thrown out of the dot, not you
Rule: Your stick counts as well as your skates in three books of five — NHL 76.7(ii) and IIHF 76.7(II) let a blade cross the hash marks if it touches neither your opponent nor their stick, but USA Hockey Casebook Situation 8 to Rule 613, Hockey Canada 6.2(a) and CARHA 57(a) do not
Rule: Physical contact with an opponent before the drop is a faceoff violation in its own right in four books — Hockey Canada 6.2(a) and CARHA 57(a) name it for a player off the dot and eject your centre, NHL 76.7(iii) and IIHF 76.7(III) list it unscoped; USA Hockey 613(d) asks about position
Rule: A second violation on the same draw is a bench minor under NHL and IIHF 76.7; USA Hockey 613(d) puts the minor on the offender, and Hockey Canada 6.2(e) and CARHA 57(d) leave it to the referee — but Hockey Canada makes it automatic if that player then contacts a linesperson clearing the dot
Rule: On a winger's encroachment the IIHF 2025/26 book warned the team and ejected nobody, but its 2026/27 book ejects the draw-taker as the NHL does (IIHF Rule 76.6, both editions)
Rule: Across the Home Countries IHUK settles the faceoff-violation question rather than the IIHF edition — "the linesperson shall immediately replace the centre for all violations", face-offs following icings excepted (In-House Rules 2026-27, Section 10, Rule 76)
Rule: The Elite League sits outside the In-House Rules, which never name it, and the EIHL Casebook writes no Rule 76 at all — so an Elite League draw runs IIHF 2026/27 Rule 76.6, which ejects the centre too, and IIHF 76.4, which warns instead on the first violation after an icing
Action: Know in advance whether you are the backup draw taker, and aim for a clean tie-up — but under Hockey Canada 6.2(d) the player who encroached may not step in, so it cannot be you if it was you
Rule: Step in for an ejected centre and you are a "Player taking the face-off", so pre-drop contact is a minor, not only a violation — discretionary under NHL and IIHF 76.7, mandatory under USA Hockey 613(e), Hockey Canada 6.2(c) and CARHA 57(b); helmet-to-helmet contact is named at IIHF 76.7(III)
```

The centre takes the draw; you line up at the hash marks — one winger on the **inside** hash (facing the slot), one on the **outside** hash (facing the boards). The usual default is that the winger whose natural side matches the faceoff location takes the outside hash, but assignments vary by team and situation, so ask. **The nine locations, the full five-player alignments and the set plays off each are in [Faceoffs](../systems/faceoffs.md).** Your job in every one of them is the same: read the result of the draw and react instantly — pounce on a loose puck, support the centre, or get to your defensive position.

**Start with two things about faceoffs that are specifically your responsibility:**

- **Don't encroach.** If you creep inside the faceoff circle or over your hash marks before the puck drops, **your centre gets thrown out of the dot** — you don't. Your team then takes the draw with its second-best option, and a second violation on the same draw is a bench minor — **under USA Hockey Rule 613(d) it is a minor on the individual instead, and under Hockey Canada Rule 6.2(e) and CARHA Rule 57(d) it is discretionary rather than automatic**. ⚠️ **Hockey Canada's discretion has a limit, though, and it is one a winger walking back to the hash marks can trip over.** The second paragraph of **Rule 6.2(e)** takes the choice away where *"the offending player subsequently contacts the Linesperson before the Linesperson has had reasonable time to vacate the face-off location"* — there the minor *"will be assessed"*, under Rule 10.1(a). **Give the official room to get out of the circle.** ⚠️ **The IIHF used to be the one book that ejected nobody, and it no longer is.** Its **2025/26** edition made your winger's encroachment a warning to the team, with the centre staying for the re-drop; its **2026/27** edition, published June 2026, replaced that sentence with the NHL's — *"the offending team's player taking the face-off shall be ejected from the face-off circle"* (IIHF 2026/27 Rule 76.6). ⚠️ **And in Britain you do not need to know which IIHF edition your league runs, because IHUK has written the answer into its own rules**: the **IHUK In-House Rules 2026-27** amend Rule 76 to say that *"with the exception of face-offs following icings, the linesperson shall immediately replace the centre for all violations."* **So plan on losing your centre for it** — on every draw except the one after an icing. ⚠️ **🇬🇧 The Elite League is outside that answer, and lands in the same place by a different road.** The In-House Rules never name it, and the *Motorpoint EIHL Casebook 26-27* writes no Rule 76 at all — its Preamble leaves every rule it does not mention to *"the official 2026 - 2027 IIHF Rulebook"*. So an Elite League draw runs **IIHF 2026/27 Rule 76.6**, which ejects your centre for your encroachment exactly as the NHL does, together with **IIHF Rule 76.4**, under which *"[w]hen an icing infraction occurs, the first face-off violation by either team will not result in the center being removed"* — a warning first, and a bench minor for the next one. A **second** violation by the same team on the same draw is a **bench minor** for delay of game under both IIHF editions (Rule 76.7), and the In-House Rules do not amend that, so it was never free either way. ⚠️ **And in three of the five books your stick counts as well as your skates.** The **NHL** and the **IIHF** expressly permit the blade: *"A player's stick may be inside the area between the hash marks provided there is no physical contact with his opponent or his opponent's stick"* (NHL Rule 76.7(ii); IIHF Rule 76.7(II) is materially identical, differing in pronoun — it writes *"their opponent or their opponent's stick"* where the NHL writes *"his"* — and is unchanged between its 2025/26 and 2026/27 editions). **USA Hockey answers the same question the other way, and it is the Casebook that answers it** — asked *"[m]ay a player have their stick between the hash marks that are meant to separate them from their opponent during a face-off?"*, Situation 8 to Rule 613 replies *"No… Both players must be completely behind their respective hash marks. This includes all sticks and all skates."* **Hockey Canada and CARHA put it in the rule**, in near-identical words: Hockey Canada 6.2(a) and CARHA 57(a) each require that *"[a]ll other players on the ice must position themselves and their sticks on side."* So under those three books a blade reaching over the line ejects your centre exactly as a skate would, and under the other two it does not — find out which is yours. *(Only IIHF Rules 76.3, 76.4, 76.6 and 76.7 have been read in the 2026/27 edition for this document, and the In-House Rules govern England, Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland and say nothing about the Elite League; [Faceoffs](../systems/faceoffs.md) carries the full comparison and the scope of both readings.)* Hold your position — and, in three of the five books, your blade — until the puck hits the ice.
- **Be ready to take the draw.** Centres get tossed several times a game. Know in advance whether you're the backup taker, and know what you're trying to do — usually just a clean tie-up rather than anything ambitious. ⚠️ **But if you are the one who caused the violation, Hockey Canada is the book that bars you from stepping in.** **Rule 6.2(d)** requires that *"[t]he player who encroached must be identified clearly as the player causing the violation and that player is not eligible to take the face-off"*, and that *"[n]o change of players will be permitted, even if the puck has been physically dropped, unless a penalty has been assessed to make a team shorthanded"* — so one of the teammates already on the ice has to take it instead, and a plan that names only you leaves your team with no plan at all on the very draw you cost them. **CARHA writes the no-substitution half of that** at **Rule 57(c)**: *"[n]o substitution of players shall be permitted until the face-off has been completed and play has been resumed, except when a penalty is imposed which will make a team short-handed."* **So ask your centre for a second name, and step aside if the first one is you.** **The rules that apply to you change when you step in; the subsection below sets out how.**

#### Contact before the drop — including helmet to helmet

**Crossing a line is not the only way to give a faceoff away, and the first bullet above is only half of what counts.** Touching an opponent before the puck drops is a violation in its own right, and it does not require you to be over your hash marks at all. **Hockey Canada and CARHA write it most plainly, and they name the offence as yours even though the punishment lands on your centre**: where a player *"other than the player taking the face-off, moves off-side, makes physical contact with an opponent or encroaches on the face-off circle prior to the dropping of the puck, then the offending team's player taking the face-off will be ejected from the face-off"* (Hockey Canada Rule 6.2(a); CARHA Rule 57(a) carries the same sentence, writing *"shall be ejected"* where Hockey Canada writes *"will be"*). Three separate triggers, and the middle one is a glove or a shoulder on the winger opposite you. **The NHL and the IIHF list the same limb without scoping it to the centre** — *"[a]ny physical contact with an opponent prior to the puck being dropped"* (NHL Rule 76.7(iii); IIHF Rule 76.7(III), whose opening sentence is identical in its 2025/26 and 2026/27 editions — the rest of that item is not, and the change is set out below). ⚠️ **USA Hockey is the one book that answers differently, and it is built differently:** the clause under which a player other than the draw-taker costs their team its centre in the dot reaches one who *"fails to maintain their proper position"* (Rule 613(d)), which asks where you are standing rather than what you touched, and the contact rule USA Hockey does write is aimed at *"any player facing-off"* (Rule 613(e)) — the two in the dot, not you on the hash marks. So in four books of the five, leaning on the player across from you costs your team its centre in the dot exactly as a skate over the line does. ⚠️ **And under CARHA it is not free the second time either, which is easy to miss because the penalty clause sits next door.** CARHA's second-violation minor at **Rule 57(d)** reaches a second violation *"of any of the provisions of Section (a)"* — and your contact is one of Section (a)'s three limbs for a player off the dot, alongside moving offside and encroaching — so it feeds that minor exactly as your skate would. ⚠️ **What it is not is the mandatory minor at 57(b)**, which opens *"no player facing-off shall make any physical contact"* and so reaches the two in the dot and not you. Stand still and keep your hands to yourself until the puck lands.

**And the moment you step in for an ejected centre, the rule set around you changes — which is the part the second bullet above does not tell you.** You are then a *"Player taking the face-off"* in the IIHF's words, and contact stops being only a violation and becomes a penalty as well. ⚠️ **The books split on whether that penalty is discretionary, and the IIHF — the book a British reader is under — sits with the NHL.** Under the **NHL** the referee *"may, at his discretion impose a minor penalty or penalties on the player(s) whose action(s) caused the physical contact"* (Rule 76.7), and **IIHF** Rule 76.7 prints that sentence in its own words — *"the Referee may, at their discretion impose a minor penalty or penalties on the Player(s) whose action(s) caused the 'physical contact'"* — in its 2025/26 and 2026/27 editions alike; under **USA Hockey**, **Hockey Canada** and **CARHA** there is no discretion — Rule 613(e) provides that *"[a] minor penalty for interference shall be assessed to any player facing-off who makes any physical contact with their opponent's body by means of their own body or by their stick"*, Hockey Canada 6.2(c) that *"the Referee will assess a Minor penalty or penalties to the player(s) whose action(s) caused the physical contact"*, and CARHA 57(b), which carries no discretion clause at all, that *"the Referee shall assess a Minor penalty on the player(s) whose action(s) caused the physical contact."* **The IIHF is also the only one of these books whose faceoff rule names your head.** Its Rule 76.7(III) provides that if *"either Player taking the 'face-off' makes helmet-to-helmet contact with their opponent"* that is a faceoff violation — and its **2026/27** edition deleted the softener its **2025/26** edition gave that sentence, which was that the offending player *"must receive a first 'face-off' violation warning"*. ⚠️ **That does not leave the contact free, and it is the discretionary minor above that reaches it**: the helmet sentence is the second sentence of an item whose opening words are *"[a]ny physical contact with an opponent prior to the puck being dropped"*, so 76.7's own penalty paragraph applies to it. The **2026/27** edition also gives the contact a consequence the older one did not — it *"shall be deemed as a 'face-off' violation and the Linesperson shall order the center of the offending team replaced"*, where the 2025/26 edition had the centre warned. **The IIHF Situation Handbook is harder still, and it changed in the same direction.** In the **2026/27** handbook the linesperson should *"immediately eject the center that is responsible for the helmet to helmet contact"* and, where they cannot tell who caused it, *"eject both centers"* (Situation 76.43); the **2025/26** handbook said *"warn"* in both limbs. Its **Situation 76.42** catches the position before the contact ever happens — two heads *"side by side, almost ear to ear"* — and has the linesperson instruct both players to move back, with one who will not comply now *"removed from the face-off"* where the older handbook warned. *(The handbook writes "center" throughout, because that is who is normally in the dot; the rule it interprets is written for whoever is taking the draw, which on those shifts is you.)* 🇬🇧 **And do not read the older edition's warning as the British answer, even though the older edition is the one the In-House Rules' Introduction names** — the In-House Rules 2026-27 replace the centre for every violation bar a post-icing draw, and a helmet-to-helmet is a violation under 76.7(III) in both editions. **Get your depth from your knees and hips, not by pushing your head out over the dot** — that is the whole of the remedy, and [Center](center.md) owns the stance cue in full.

⚠️ **And take one point past the penalty sheet.** Two players who both get low the safe way still finish with their heads a few inches apart, on every draw either of them ever takes, and **no rulebook or handbook read for this document speaks to that residue** — Situation 76.42 above reaches two centres who lean in over the dot into each other's space, which is a different thing from the inches a safe stance cannot avoid. The 2026/27 change raises what helmet-to-helmet contact costs you — the rule deleted the first warning and the handbook moved from warning the centre responsible to ejecting them. It does not make the space over the dot safe, and nothing here should be read as saying it does.

### Power Play (your team has the man advantage)

```facts
Position: Almost always the half-wall or the net front — the formations are in Special Teams
Targets: From the half-wall you have four options: the point, the bumper, the net front, and the far half-wall through the seam
Technique: Walk down toward the goal line to drag a killer with you, then use the space you just made
Action: From the net front, screen, tip and hunt rebounds — get in the goalie's eyes and keep your body out of the paint and your skates off the red crease line
Rule: Don't initiate contact and don't crowd the goalie. Keep your body out of the crease (NHL and IIHF Rule 69, PWHL Rule 71) — and off the red line around it, which every book settling it in rule text or glossary counts as crease, while the NHL's, PWHL's and IIHF's own tables allow a goal on it
Rule: The don't-crowd-the-goalie test is NHL, PWHL and IIHF only — neither USA Hockey nor Hockey Canada reaches a screen set from outside the crease, and inside it those two differ from each other; Net-front and the crease sets out both
Never: Stay in the crease after a defender puts you there. Get out instead; the books split on what that costs you, and under Hockey Canada it is a minor on you
Convention: The bumper is usually the centre, but plenty of units put a winger there — find out whether that spot is yours
Key: Quick puck movement is what beats a kill — it can cover any single option, but not three in two seconds
```

**The formations are set out in [Special Teams](../systems/special_teams.md).** Wherever they put you, it is almost always one of two spots: the **half-wall** or the **net front**.

**From the half-wall you are the primary distributor on your side.** Your four options are the point, the bumper, the net front, and the far half-wall through the seam — and the reason the half-wall is the best job on a power play is that a kill can take away any one of them but not all four. Walk down toward the goal line to drag a killer with you, then use the space you just made.

![](diagram:pp-131)

**From the net front your job is screening, tips and rebounds.** Get in the goalie's eyes; keep your body out of the crease, don't initiate contact, and don't crowd them (**NHL and IIHF Rule 69**, which the **PWHL** numbers **71** and writes in the feminine). **That crease test is the NHL's, the PWHL's and the IIHF's only** — neither USA Hockey nor Hockey Canada reaches a screen set from outside the crease, and inside it those two differ from each other, so read *Net-front and the crease* above before you play under either. ⚠️ **And keep off the red line around the crease — that is a different rule from Rule 69, and every book that settles the question in its rule text or glossary writes it.** The line counts as crease under **IIHF Rule 1.7** (*"[t]he marked line belongs to the Goal Crease"*), under the Note to **USA Hockey Rule 625(b)** (the crease area is all the space outlined by the semi-circular crease lines *"(including crease lines)"*), under **Hockey Canada's Glossary** (*"[t]he lines of the crease are considered part of the crease"*) and under **CARHA's Glossary** entry *Creases* (*"[t]he lines which designate these areas are to be considered as part of the creases"*); ⚠️ **the NHL's rule text leaves it unsaid, but its Reference Table 14 allows the goal to an attacker planted *"on the crease line"* — and the IIHF's own Appendix IV prints that same row, so that book answers the question in two directions.** Neither book's Rule 69 says it either way, and off the line is the reading that holds whichever way the IIHF's is resolved.

One thing worth asking rather than assuming: **the bumper is *usually* the centre, but plenty of units put a winger there**, especially a left-shot winger who can one-time a feed from the right half-wall. Find out whether that spot is yours.

Move the puck quickly, look for one-timer setups, and crash the net for rebounds. Quick puck movement is what beats a penalty kill; a kill can cover any single option, but not three in two seconds.

### Penalty Kill (your team is short-handed)

```facts
Position: In a box you are one of the two high players covering a point; in a diamond you take a middle spot on a half-wall; in a wedge+1 you are often the "+1"
Action: As the +1, pressure whoever has the puck; when the puck moves the nearest wedge player takes over as the new +1, so you never follow it round
Options: Your route out as the +1 is a coaching choice — straight out from the goal, sweeping across the top of the zone, or roaming to the wall and point; ask which one your team runs
Action: Stay tight, block shots, and force the opposing point into low-percentage shots
Technique: If you block, do it from close and on your feet — square to the shooter, head out of the shot lane, hands behind your body, backs of the gloves out; behind the shin pads is the going-down form
Never: Block from in front of your own goalie's eyes — Defender and Body Contact and Battles cover the technique and the full list of when not to block
Action: Clear the puck whenever you get it — you don't need to be fancy
Rule: A shorthanded team may ice the puck freely under NHL and IIHF rules, but the exemption is conditional; USA Hockey Rule 624(b)(1) grants it only from Youth 15-Only, Girls 16U, High School and Adult up
Rule: Under the NHL the shorthanded icing exemption survives a running major — Rule 81.6's clawback bites only once the major expires with nobody on the penalty bench to come out, and only until the next stoppage — but it runs on the on-ice count, so a penalty to them taking you to 4-on-4 ends it
Rule: Shorthanded icing is an on-ice count, not a clock — expiry counts back only the player whose penalty ended, so a 5-on-3 kill is still exempt, and a clear released before expiry draws no icing however late it lands (NHL and IIHF 81.6; Hockey Canada 6.7(b) takes the count at the shot)
Never: Chase the puck around the perimeter and pull the shape apart — that is exactly what the power play wants
```

A kill is four skaters, normally two forwards and two defencemen. **The shapes are described in [Special Teams](../systems/special_teams.md);** your job inside each one:

- **Box** — the standard. You are **one of the two high players**, alongside your centre, each responsible for one point.
- **Diamond** — the centre usually takes the apex and you take one of the two middle positions, covering a half-wall.
- **Wedge+1** — you are often the "+1", the single player working outside the triangle. That job is **pressure, not pursuit**: get at whoever has the puck and make every possession uncomfortable. When the puck moves you stop, and the nearest wedge player steps out as the new +1 while you drop into the triangle. Following the puck around the perimeter is how this system is beaten, not how it is played. **The route you take to the puck is your coach's choice, not a law** — published descriptions differ, some sending the +1 straight out from the goal towards the carrier, some sweeping him laterally across the top of the zone above the circles, some letting him roam to the wall and the point. Ask which one you are running.

![](diagram:pk-box)

![](diagram:pk-diamond)

Stay tight and force the opposing point into low-percentage shots. Block **from close, on your feet, square to the shooter, head out of the lane and hands behind your body with the backs of the gloves out** — and not from in front of your own goalie's eyes. ⚠️ **Tucked in behind your shin pads is the going-down form, not a standing one:** on your feet you cannot get your hands there without folding forward, and that puts your head down into the low shot lane. If you do go down, [Defender](defender.md) has that technique; it and [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md) own the rest of it, and the list of when *not* to block. Clear the puck whenever you get it — you don't need to be fancy, and a shorthanded team may ice the puck freely — **under NHL and IIHF rules, and conditionally even there.** USA Hockey gives the exemption only *"For all Youth 15-Only and above, Girls 16U and above, High School and Adult classifications only"* (Rule 624(b)(1)), so outside those classifications your clear can still be iced; and NHL Rule 81.6 takes it away from a team killing a *major* — ⚠️ **but at expiry, not during the penalty.** It reaches a team that has *"neglected to ensure there is a player on the penalty bench to exit upon the expiry of the penalty"*, and Rule 20.3 lets that bench sit empty until a stoppage before the major ends. So clear it through the five minutes: it is only from expiry with nobody there, and only until the next stoppage, that your clear comes back as an icing. ⚠️ **Both of those vary by book. The condition that does not is the count:** every book read here gives the exemption to the side *below* its opponent's on-ice numbers, and NHL and IIHF Rule 81.6 settle the call **at the instant a penalty expires**, adding that *"[t]he action of the penalized player remaining in the penalty box will not alter the ruling."* **That is the player whose own penalty just ended, not a teammate:** he is counted back onto the ice from that instant even if he is still standing at the box door, so the expiry costs you the exemption only if it makes the sides even. On a 5-on-3, the first penalty ending leaves you four against five, still below strength and still free to ice it. ⚠️ **And expiry is not the only thing that can level the sides: a penalty to *them* takes you to 4-on-4, so you can be iced while a major of yours is still running.** **The same sentence gives it back at the other end, too, and this is the half to know:** a puck *"released from the stick of the player shooting the puck down the ice prior to the penalty expiring"* is not icing **however late it lands**. Get it away in time and it is a legal clear even if you are back to full strength before it arrives. ⚠️ **Hockey Canada asks the same question at a different moment.** Its **Rule 6.7(b)** provides that *"[i]cing will not be called and play will continue if the team shooting the puck is below the numerical strength of the opposing team **at the instant the puck is shot**"* — the shot rather than the expiry — with its **Note 1** adding that *"[a] team will no longer be considered shorthanded once the penalty time expires, irrespective of whether or not the penalized player has left the Penalty Bench."* **So the head count is common ground across the books; the moment at which they take it is not.** [Special Teams](../systems/special_teams.md) covers it. [Faceoffs](../systems/faceoffs.md) sets the books side by side, the rec-league one included. Don't chase the puck around the perimeter and pull the shape apart; that's exactly what the power play is trying to make you do.

### Icing

```facts
Rule: The dot is a decision deadline, not a finish line — NHL and IIHF 81.1 and PWHL 83.1 judge which player would first touch the puck, decided by the time a skate reaches the dots; USA Hockey 624 runs no such race, and Hockey Canada 6.7(e) only in U18AAA, Junior and Senior at the Member's option
Action: Carry the extra stride so the puck is on your stick as it crosses the centre red line — that makes the dump legal however far it travels
Options: Or beat the icing by winning the race to the puck yourself — where your book runs a race at all; under automatic icing only a dump that stops short of the goal line beats it
Action: Sprint the first half of the chase — that is where the call is decided
Read: If you are clearly beaten to the dot, ease up; you gain nothing and the end boards are unforgiving
Risk: An icing sticks your line on for a defensive-zone draw with no change and no timeout under NHL, IIHF and PWHL rules — USA Hockey blocks neither, but its change window still closes at every stoppage
Rule: The post-icing no-change bar has exceptions — NHL and IIHF 81.4 and PWHL 84.1 allow a substitution for an injured player, for a pulled goalie going back in, and on a penalty that changes on-ice strength; the IIHF adds a broken skate that its own 82.1 omits
Rule: The USA Hockey change after an icing is ordinary, not free — Rule 204(a) gives each team five seconds, then "no additional player substitutions … until the face-off has been completed legally and play has resumed, except when a penalty … affect[s] the on-ice strength of either or both teams"
Rule: Undue delay in placing your lineup or changing players draws a warning first, and a minor for delay of game on any subsequent delay by the same team (USA Hockey Rule 204(a))
Rule: Hockey Canada is a third answer on the change — 6.7(d) bars it only in U18AAA, Junior and Senior at the Member's option, and where it applies 6.7(d)(iii) expressly permits either team's time-out, barring only the icing team from substituting during it
Key: The rule in full — hybrid icing, the wave-off list and "gaining the line" — is in Rules Primer
```

**The rule itself — hybrid icing, the full wave-off list, "gaining the line", and how USA Hockey and rec leagues differ — is in [Rules Primer](../foundation/rules_primer.md).** Icing touches a winger from both ends, which is why it is worth your attention more than most players'.

**You take more dump-ins than anyone, and dumping from behind the red line is the classic accidental icing.** Two fixes: carry the extra stride so the **puck is on your stick** as it touches the centre red line, which makes the dump legal however far it travels; or make the dump soft enough that you win the race yourself. ⚠️ **That second one needs a race to exist, and two of the four books do not run one** — USA Hockey never, and Hockey Canada outside three categories, both set out below. The first fix, and a dump that stops short of the goal line, work in all four.

![](diagram:icing-gaining-the-line)

**You are also the one chasing, and under hybrid icing that makes you the reason it gets waved off.** Be precise about what the race actually is, because the common version is wrong. The dot is a **decision deadline, not a finish line**. Rule 81.1: icing *"is completed upon the determination as to which player **(attacking or defending) would first touch the puck**"*, and that call *"will be made by no later than the instant the first player reaches the end zone face-off dots **with the player's skate being the determining factor**."* (That closing clause matters: it is what fixes the deadline to a skate crossing the dot line.) So the linesperson is judging who would get to **the puck** first, and simply has to decide by the time someone reaches the dot. Beating the defender to the dot does not by itself wave anything off — being clearly on course to reach the puck first does. Sprint the first half of that race, because that is where it is decided. If you are clearly beaten to the dot, ease up: you gain nothing and the end boards are unforgiving. ⚠️ **And check that your book runs a race at all.** Hybrid icing is the NHL's and the IIHF's. **USA Hockey runs none** — its Rule 624 Note completes the icing *"the instant the puck has completely crossed the goal line"*, and the only place that book writes "hybrid icing" is its Sled Hockey section. **Hockey Canada applies hybrid icing *"[i]n categories of U18AAA, Junior, and at the option of the Member in Senior"* only** (Rule 6.7(e)), and in every other category under that book — the rest of minor and female hockey included — icing is automatic under Rule 6.7(a) the moment the puck crosses the goal line, so there is nothing to chase and nothing to wave off by beating a defender to it. [Rules Primer](../foundation/rules_primer.md) owns the four-book comparison, and [Rink Map](../foundation/rink_map.md) the definition.

**The cost when it does get called is your line stuck on the ice for a defensive-zone draw with no change and no timeout — but only under three of the five books.** **NHL Rule 81.4** bars the substitution — *"[a] team that is in violation of this rule shall not be permitted to make any player substitutions prior to the ensuing face-off"* — and **NHL Rule 87.1** bars the time-out: *"No time-out shall be granted to the defensive team following an icing…"* **IIHF Rules 81.4 and 87.1 impose both**, so a British or European league punishes the chase just as hard, and **PWHL Rules 84.1 and 89.1** do the same in the feminine. ⚠️ **The substitution bar is not absolute, and the limb that matters is the injury one:** Rule 81.4's very next sentence permits a substitution *"to replace a goalkeeper who had been substituted for an extra attacker, to replace an injured player, or when a penalty has been assessed which affects the on-ice strength of either team."* So a team with a hurt player on the ice for its own icing does not have to leave them out there. **The IIHF book writes a fourth exception the NHL's does not — *"a Player with a broken skate"* — but its own Rule 82.1 states the same ban and lists only the NHL's three, and neither rule says which controls, so treat the broken skate as unsettled rather than as an entitlement.** None of the exceptions is a winger who is tired. **⚠️ USA Hockey imposes neither** — nothing in Rule 624 restricts substitution, Rule 204(a) gives both benches their change *"immediately following any stoppage of play"*, and Rule 636(f) puts no icing condition on time-outs. ⚠️ **Read the rest of those rules before you treat that as an open door**: the visiting team gets **five seconds** to complete its substitutions and then the home team five, after which *"no additional player substitutions shall be permitted until the face-off has been completed legally and play has resumed, except when a penalty or penalties are imposed that affect the on-ice strength of either or both teams"*, and *"any undue delay by either team in placing their lineup on the ice or changing players"* is a warning and then a minor for delay of game. **The time-out is bounded by the same window**: Rule 636(f) requires that *"[t]ime-outs must be requested during a stoppage of play prior to the conclusion of the line change procedure."* So under USA Hockey your line gets off; under the book you play in Britain it does not. ⚠️ **Hockey Canada is the fourth answer and it is not either of those.** Its no-change rule applies *"[i]n categories of U18AAA, Junior, and at the option of the Member in Senior"* only (Rule 6.7(d)), so below U18AAA your line changes after its own icing; and **where the bar does apply, the time-out goes the opposite way from the NHL's** — Rule 6.7(d)(iii) provides that *"[e]ither team is permitted to take their time-out, as per Rule 6.18 – Time of Game, following an icing, but during that time out, the team in violation of icing will not be permitted to make player substitutions."* So that bench may stop the clock; it just cannot use the stoppage to get you off. Either way it is exactly the shift you don't want after a full-speed chase. **How much North American rec and youth hockey runs on USA Hockey's book is a question about leagues rather than about books, and this document has no count for it, so find out which book your league runs.** [Defender](defender.md) and [Rules Primer](../foundation/rules_primer.md) carry the rule.

### Line Changes

```facts
Convention: Wingers change as a group led by the centre — coaching craft rather than a measured finding; Center sets out what is and is not established
Action: When the centre calls the change, go with him rather than staying out for one more look
Risk: A line that dribbles off one player at a time plays a stretch of every shift a man short in the middle of the ice
Never: Change while the puck is still in your defensive zone and not under your team's control — you are the point coverage
Key: Shift length, the safe and unsafe windows to change, and why a bad change concedes goals are in Game Management
```

**Wingers change as a group, and the group is led by the centre.** His route covers more of the ice than yours and he fades first — **coaching craft rather than a measured finding**; [Center](center.md) sets out what is and is not established here — so when he calls it you go with him rather than staying out for one more look. A line that dribbles off one player at a time plays a stretch of every shift a man short in the middle of the ice, which is exactly the cost a change is supposed to avoid.

The one that costs wingers goals: **changing while the puck is still in your defensive zone and not under your team's control.** You are the point coverage. If you leave, the opposing defenceman is unmarked at the blue line with the puck coming to him.

Shift length, the safe and unsafe windows to change, and why a bad change concedes goals are in [Game Management](../systems/game_management.md).

### Backchecking

```facts
Action: Sprint back to your defensive zone the moment your team loses the puck — often faster than the opposing forwards
Convention: Wingers take the outside lanes and the centre the middle under the standard assignment — a coaching choice, not a law
Options: Other backcheck schemes give the middle to the first man back, or pick up man-on-man from the puck outward — find out which, and note somebody always owns the middle
Read: Inside your lane the man to find is the opposing F3, the late-arriving forward — stay between them and the puck
Rule: A trailer has no puck: the body position is yours, the stick is not — IIHF 56.1 allows lengthening an opponent's path "provided their stick is not utilized", NHL 56.1 and PWHL 57.1 in their own pronouns; USA Hockey 625(a)(4) reaches a change of skating lane or foot speed to play the body
Never: Puck-watch. Watch your assignment instead, and arrive at your covering position before the puck does
Never: Finish a backcheck into the back of a carrier skating at their own end boards — take the puck, take the ice or angle him off it instead, whichever book you are under
Action: Once you are set, transition into your normal defensive-zone role: cover the point, be ready to break out
```

When your team loses the puck in the offensive or neutral zone, your job as a winger is to sprint back to your defensive zone — often faster than the opposing forwards. Backchecking well is one of the most important defensive responsibilities of a winger, and one of the most commonly missed — **coaching emphasis rather than a counted ranking**.

- **Take the outside lanes — under the standard assignment.** The assignment assumed throughout gives the centre the middle lane and the two wingers the outside lanes. **That is a coaching choice, not a law, and no count of which of the three schemes is commonest has been found.** Other teams use **first man back takes the middle**, whoever that is — so as a winger you can end up in the middle. Others pick up **man-on-man from the puck outward**. Find out which your team plays; [Defending the Rush](../systems/defending_the_rush.md) sets them out, along with the four named team backchecking systems that decide which assignment applies. Under every variant, somebody owns the middle and nobody assumes it is somebody else.
- **Pick up the trailing forward.** Inside your lane, the man to find is the opposing F3 — the late-arriving forward. **Stay between them and the puck with your feet and your body, and keep your stick in the passing lane rather than on them.** The trailer has no puck, so the body position is yours to use and the stick is not: a stick laid across a puckless man is interference rather than a backcheck, and so is changing your skating lane or your foot speed to take their body instead of their ice. [Playing Without the Puck → Backchecking](../hockey-iq/playing_without_the_puck.md#backchecking) quotes the interference rule in full, alongside the checking-from-behind tiers for the carrier. A trailer sneaking in unmarked while three defenders watch the carrier is named across coaching material as one of the cheapest goals a team gives up off a rush — **coaching consensus, not a tracked share**: public tracking splits goals by how they were produced, not by the defensive breakdown that let them in. [Defending the Rush](../systems/defending_the_rush.md) and [Neutral Zone Systems](../systems/neutral_zone_systems.md) state it the same way.
- **Watch your assignment, not the puck**, and arrive at your covering position before the puck does. **And when you get there, never finish the backcheck into the back of a carrier skating at their own end boards** — take the puck, take the ice, or angle him off it, whichever book you are under. That is the checking-from-behind geometry, and [Defending the Rush](../systems/defending_the_rush.md#what-hounding-never-means) sets out what it costs. Once you're set, transition into your normal defensive-zone role: cover the point, be ready to break out.

## Common Mistakes

- **Drifting into the corner in your own zone** when your team plays a collapse system. The point opens up and nobody is positioned for the breakout.
- **Cheating for offence** — leaving the zone early looking for a breakaway pass before your team actually has the puck. Anticipating is coached; abandoning coverage on a hope is not, and the test is whether you can still get back if you read it wrong.
- **Coasting at the offensive blue line** to avoid offside instead of dragging a trail skate and entering with speed.
- **Dragging the trail skate with your head down.** The drag puts your body, hands and stick over the line before your skate, into the ice a defenceman standing up at their own line is defending. Aggressive body position is free under the offside rule and nowhere else, so the head stays up — head up and chin off your chest here as at the boards. [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md) writes that rule without exceptions.
- **Encroaching on a faceoff — or leaning on the opponent beside you** — and getting your own centre thrown out of the dot. **Get your depth from your knees and hips, not by pushing your head out over the dot, and keep your hands and shoulder off the opponent beside you — and have a second name ready for the draw.** Contact with an opponent before the puck drops is a faceoff violation in its own right, named for a player other than the draw-taker by **Hockey Canada 6.2(a)** and **CARHA Rule 57(a)**, and listed unscoped by **NHL Rule 76.7(iii)** and **IIHF Rule 76.7(III)**; USA Hockey writes that clause as position rather than contact (Rule 613(d)). **And if you are the one taking the draw, contact becomes a penalty as well** — discretionary under **NHL and IIHF 76.7**, which print the same sentence, mandatory under **USA Hockey 613(e)**, **Hockey Canada 6.2(c)** and **CARHA 57(b)** — with the IIHF naming helmet-to-helmet contact specifically and its 2026/27 Situation Handbook ejecting the player responsible (Situation 76.43) where the 2025/26 one warned — and in Britain the IHUK In-House Rules 2026-27 replace the centre for every violation bar a post-icing draw, whichever edition is underneath. ⚠️ **And then assuming you are the one who steps in.** Under **Hockey Canada Rule 6.2(d)** the player who caused the violation *"is not eligible to take the face-off"* and *"[n]o change of players will be permitted"*, so the winger who just encroached is barred from replacing the centre he cost, which is why the plan needs two names rather than one.
- **Drifting into the middle** in the neutral zone. That's the centre's lane; you collapse your line's spacing.
- **Standing still on the half-wall.** A stationary winger is trivially covered.
- **Puck-watching on the backcheck** instead of picking up the trailing forward.
- **Arriving at the net front too early on a drive** — time it so you get there as the shot is taken. That is the drive in from the wall or your circle. **If the net front is your assignment it runs the other way**: you are planted there before the release, and [Offensive Zone Play](../systems/offensive_zone_play.md) treats getting there deliberately ahead of the shot as the whole of that job.
- **Treating the red crease line as the safe side of the boundary.** Keep your skates off the red line, not merely out of the paint — a net-front player standing on it can cost their team the goal. "Out of the blue paint" is not the line you are working to: every book that settles the question in its rule text or glossary counts the red line itself as part of the crease — the IIHF at **Rule 1.7**, USA Hockey in the Note to **Rule 625(b)** and its *Casebook*, Hockey Canada in its Glossary, and CARHA in its Glossary entry *Creases*. ⚠️ **The NHL's rule text leaves it unsaid and its Reference Table 14 answers the other way**, as the PWHL's copy of that table does — so a pair of reference tables is all that stands on the safe side of the line, and that is not a margin to stand on. [Rink Map](../foundation/rink_map.md) owns this split.
- **Standing in the goaltender's path back to the crease.** A screen from outside the paint without contact is legal — blocking the goaltender's *return* to it is not. Under **NHL and IIHF Rule 69.4** and the **PWHL's 71.4**, once a goaltender has played the puck outside the crease and is trying to get back to it, an attacker whose *"deliberate actions"* prevent that return *"may be penalized"* for goalkeeper interference — the clause itself turns on intent, not contact. See *Net-front and the crease* above.
- **Arriving into the goaltender rather than standing in front of them.** Take the ice in front of the net, never the goaltender, and arrive under control. A screen is a place you stand; a charge is a speed you arrive at, and at the net front those are one stride apart. Charging a goaltender reaches an ejection tier — a major plus a game misconduct — under the NHL, the PWHL, the IIHF, USA Hockey, Hockey Canada and CARHA alike, and two of the six make it mandatory: **Hockey Canada Rule 8.5(b)** (*"A Major penalty and Game Misconduct penalty will be assessed to any player who charges the goaltender"*) and **CARHA Rule 52(b)** (the same, *"while the goalkeeper is within the goal crease"*). Under **NHL Rule 42.1**, **PWHL Rule 42.1** — which prints the NHL's sentence in its own pronouns — **IIHF Rule 42.4** and **USA Hockey Rule 607(c)** it is the referee's to assess rather than automatic, but USA Hockey writes no bare minor for it and reaches beyond the crease into the *privileged area*, the ice between the two end-zone faceoff spots and the end boards.
- **Playing the net front with your head down.** Know where the near post is before the shot, take a defender's shove braced — never the goaltender's — with a wide base and your knees giving, keep your head up and your chin off your chest, and never duck. Your eyes are on the puck by design, so the defender moving you sideways is one you are feeling rather than watching — which is how a player meets a goal post head first without ever seeing it coming. USA Hockey's *Heads Up Hockey* guide names the goal post beside the boards in the same sentence — a head *"slightly flexed (head down) while making head-on contact with the boards or goal post"* — and records that the injury *"can occur at walking speed"*; its *Introduction To Body Contact* puts it as an instruction: *"Hit the boards or goal posts with an arm, a leg, or anything but your head first."* **This is not scoped to checking leagues** — a legal box-out puts you in the same place as an illegal shove. [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md) covers the battle from both sides.
- **Turning your back to the wall when contact is coming — and ducking.** These are two prohibitions against two different injuries, and you need both. Turning your back feels protective and does the opposite: it converts the contact into a hit from behind, which you can neither see nor brace for, and USA Hockey's warning to the player delivering one is that *"Even a light hit from behind could inflict a severe head or neck injury."* **A light one** — so do not read a slow closing speed as a safe one. Ducking tucks your chin to your chest, which straightens the cervical spine; USA Hockey's *Heads Up Hockey* guide puts *"almost all on-ice cervical spine injuries"* down to a head-down, head-first collision with the boards or a goal post, and USA Hockey records that the resulting injury can occur at walking speed. Both prohibitions are absolute, and neither of the two USA Hockey safety publications this document draws on ranks them against each other, so this document does not either. Skates parallel to the boards, forearm and hip into the contact, head up and chin off your chest — and not on the point of your shoulder, and not the whole shoulder if you can help it. USA Hockey writes *never* for the shoulder tip specifically, and its checklist for taking a check asks for *"No shoulder contact on the boards, if possible"* for the whole shoulder. Squaring your chest to the wall is the fallback for when you cannot turn; your back is never an option. [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md) covers this.
- **Blocking a shot from in front of your own goalie's eyes.** A puck deflected off a body your goalie could not see is one they cannot save — either block from close and on your feet, square to the shooter, head out of the lane and hands behind your body with the backs of the gloves out, or get out of the lane and let them see it. **Tucked in behind your shin pads is the going-down form, not a standing one** — standing, it folds you forward and drops your head into the lane. If you do go down, [Defender](defender.md) has that technique; it and [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md) own the rest of it, and the list of when *not* to block.

## Key Takeaways

1. Your lane is your responsibility, and width creates space for everyone else. That is the job inside a containment structure — a 1-2-2, a 1-2-2 trap or a 1-3-1 — not a law of hockey; under an aggressive neutral-zone press the instruction reverses, two forwards go hunting the puck and the wingers leave their lanes. Find out which one your team plays.
2. **"Stay high" in your own end means level with the opposing defenceman while they have the puck, and it expires the moment your team wins it** — then you come down the wall to the outlet. That is the low zone collapse job; under man-on-man you follow your check into the corner, and under a hybrid of the two only once he crosses the man/zone line — which teams draw at the dots, the hash marks or the tops of the circles, so learn your team's version.
3. Find out which defensive-zone system your team plays before your first shift — zone, man-on-man, or a hybrid of the two, which marks man-on-man low and plays zone high, with teams putting that dividing line in different places. It changes almost everything about your job.
4. One skate on the line keeps you onside, so drag a trail skate rather than coasting. **Head up**, because your body, hands and stick cross the line before your skate does, into the ice a defenceman standing up at their own line is defending. And keep the blade **touching**: an airborne trail skate is onside under the NHL, IIHF and PWHL books but offside under **USA Hockey Rule 630(a) and Hockey Canada Rule 6.11**, so find out which is yours. Tagging up is a five-man act, and nobody re-enters until every teammate has cleared (NHL Rule 83.3(i)) — four books word that clearing *"at the same instant"*, the PWHL's 85.3(i) among them, while Hockey Canada Rule 6.11(b)(ii) (the tag-up clause) and CARHA Rule 72(e)(2) state it as a result — *"completely clear of attacking players"* — which comes to the same thing, so tag as one and you are legal everywhere. Under **USA Hockey Rule 630(d)** tag-up exists only at Youth 15-Only and above, Girls 16U and above, High School and Adult — below those the whistle is immediate, and no other book here sets such a limit.
5. On the forecheck, F1/F2/F3 are roles set by who arrives first — if that's you, you're F1. Read the angle F1 took and seal the escape route. Angles beat speed.
6. Puck protection is your defining physical skill — a coaching ranking rather than a measured one. Puck on the far side of your body, wide base, feet moving, and know your out before you arrive. **Two things override all of it, and neither is a rule for fast checkers only.** Near the boards, never turn your back to the wall and never duck. USA Hockey's warning on a hit from behind is that *"Even a light hit from behind could inflict a severe head or neck injury"*, and in the head-down collision that ducking produces your neck can break at walking speed. Skates parallel to the wall, take the contact on your forearm and hip, head up and chin off your chest — and not on the point of your shoulder, and not the whole shoulder if you can help it: USA Hockey writes *never* for the tip and asks for no shoulder contact on the boards at all where that is possible ([Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md), which covers this).
7. A dump-in is a read, not the safe default. Put it in when nobody is arriving with you or your support is late. Tracked NHL evidence, owned by [Zone Entries](../systems/zone_entries.md), has NHL players giving the puck up at the blue line too easily. The chase angle matters more than the dump.
8. **Stand in the goaltender's eyes, never in their way home.** A no-contact screen set from *outside* the crease is one of the most valuable things you can do at the net front, and the NHL, the PWHL and the IIHF each print the answer to that exact play in a reference table: an attacker planted on the crease line or outside it, obstructing the goalie's vision, with a goal scored — *"Goal is allowed."* **Keep your body out of the blue paint and off the red line around it**, which every book that settles the question counts as crease; under USA Hockey keep your stick out of it too, because that book alone counts your stick as you. **Two plays cost you, and habit avoids both** — standing in a goaltender's route back to the net after they have played the puck outside it, and staying in the paint once a defender has shoved you in. Leave them their path; and if you get pushed in, get out and let the officials sort out who put you there. What each of those costs differs by book, and the net-front section above works through them. The one tariff worth carrying onto the ice is that **charging the goaltender reaches an ejection tier in all six, and two of them make it mandatory.** ⚠️ **The physical half outranks every word of it: know where the near post is before the shot, and take a defender's shove braced — never the goaltender's — head up, and never duck.**
9. Crashing the net for rebounds is one of the most effective ways a winger scores — coaching consensus, not a ranked measurement, and the ice in front of the net does convert several times better than the perimeter, on figures whose own source names no dataset ([Shooting](../technique/shooting.md) owns them and that qualification). **Time the arrival with the shot when you are driving in from the wall or your circle; when the net front is your assignment, you are planted there before the release instead.** **And the net front is where you take contact you never see coming.** Your eyes are on the puck by design, and the path a defender walks you along has a steel goal frame at one edge and a set goaltender at the other — so know where the near post is before the shot, and take a defender's shove braced — never the goaltender's — with a wide base and your knees giving. Keep your head up and your chin off your chest, and never duck. USA Hockey names the goal post beside the boards in its sentence on how cervical spine injuries happen, and records that the injury can occur at walking speed. **That applies in every league, checking or not** — a legal box-out puts you in the same place as an illegal shove.
10. Backcheck to the outside lanes and pick up the trailing forward. That is the standard assignment — a coaching choice, not a law; some teams give the middle to the first man back.
11. A faceoff is a place a winger gives the puck away without ever touching it. Crossing your hash marks throws your **centre** out of the dot, not you — and so does putting a hand or a shoulder on the opponent beside you, which is a violation in its own right in four books of the five (**Hockey Canada 6.2(a)**, **CARHA Rule 57(a)**, **NHL Rule 76.7(iii)**, **IIHF Rule 76.7(III)**). ⚠️ **And you cannot step in for the centre you just cost, under Hockey Canada at least: Rule 6.2(d)** requires the encroacher to be *"identified clearly as the player causing the violation"* and makes that player *"not eligible to take the face-off"*, permitting no change of players — so the back-up plan needs a second name. **When you *are* the one who steps in, contact becomes a penalty as well as a violation** — the referee's discretion under **NHL and IIHF 76.7**, mandatory under **USA Hockey 613(e)**, **Hockey Canada 6.2(c)** and **CARHA 57(b)** — and the IIHF names helmet-to-helmet contact between the two players taking the draw, with its 2026/27 Situation Handbook ejecting the one responsible where the 2025/26 one warned (Situation 76.43). Get your depth from your knees — and know that the officials do police the space over the dot, so this is not purely your own problem. **IIHF Situation Handbook Situation 76.42**, in both editions, takes two centres who lean in *"such that their heads are side by side, almost ear to ear"* and answers: *"This should not be allowed. The Linesperson should instruct both players to move back."* One who will not comply is warned under the 2025/26 handbook and *"removed from the face-off"* under the 2026/27 one, and the handbook grounds it in Rule 76.4's first paragraph. **What no book speaks to is the few inches two safe stances still leave** — that part is yours. **Move back the moment you are told to.**
