## Key focus

**Crash the net every time a shot goes toward it.** Rebounds and tips are where a winger's goals come from. Driving in from the wall, arrive as the shot goes; on the net-front job, be planted before the release, in the goalie's eyes. Stick down, skates off the crease's red line, head up — and never into the goalie.

**Shoot when the look is yours, instead of hunting for a better one.** Wingers lose goals by passing up good shots, not by taking bad ones. With a teammate at the net front, the shot from your circle beats the extra pass that lets the defence close. A teammate with a clear look at an open side is still worth the pass.

**Hold your lane, and stay wide.** Your lane is the outside third of the ice on your side. Stay in it and one defender cannot cover two of you, and the middle opens for your centre. Under an aggressive press the weak-side winger leaves it to hunt the puck, so ask which your team plays.

**In your own end, stay high — then come down the wall the instant your team wins the puck.** High means level with the opposing defenceman on your side. Once the puck is yours, get down the wall, between the hash marks and the goal line, as the first outlet. Under man-on-man you follow your check instead, even into the corner, so ask which your team plays.

**Win the puck on the wall, and move it early.** Puck on the far side of your body, side-on, hip toward the checker, feet moving; where checking is barred, hold your position and never bump them, and in a CARHA league avoid the contact. Look up ice first; if nothing is on, rim it or reverse it rather than force a pass through the middle. Head up, never turn your back to anyone coming at you, and never duck.

**Get back through your lane, and pick up the late forward, not the puck.** That is their third forward, arriving behind the play. Stay between them and the puck, stick in the passing lane. Never hit them: it can be called interference wherever you play, and where checking is barred an illegal check as well. Teams assign the backcheck differently, so ask how yours does.

## Overview

```facts
Action: Crash the net on every shot, stick down, just outside the crease and never into the goalie. Driving in from the wall or circle, arrive with the shot; on the net-front job, planted before the release
Key: Rebounds and tips are where a winger's goals come from — coaching craft, and the ice in front of the net converts several times better than the perimeter on figures whose source names no dataset
Rule: Keep your skates off the red line around the crease, not merely out of the blue paint — IIHF Rule 1.7, USA Hockey's Note to 625(b) and the Hockey Canada and CARHA glossaries all count the line as crease
Convention: Your lane is the outside third of the ice on your side, and holding it is a containment structure's spacing job, not a law — drift into the middle and one defender covers two of you. Under an aggressive press the weak-side winger does leave it, so find out which your team plays
Position: "Stay high" in your own end means level with the opposing defenceman on your side, not halfway to the corner — and it expires the moment your team wins the puck
Convention: When your team wins the puck, come down the side boards to receive, between the hash marks and the goal line, at or just below the faceoff dot — that is the conventional swing breakout; a flat/spread structure holds you higher and a stretch sends a winger up ice, so find out which you're in
Convention: Under a low zone collapse — a coaching choice not a rule, assumed throughout — you never go into the corner; under man-on-man you follow your man anywhere, corner included; under a hybrid, only below the line your team draws between man and zone — so find out where it is
Technique: Puck far side, side-on to the checker, feet moving — never bump where checking is barred; in CARHA avoid contact. Head up, chin off your chest, never turn your back to anyone coming at you, never duck
Action: On the backcheck, take your lane and pick up the opponent's late forward, not the puck — stay between them and the puck all the way back, stick in the lane, not on them, never finish a check into them
```

**Two patches of ice decide what a winger is worth: the front of the opposing net, and your own lane.** The net front scores. A goalie handles a clean shot from the point; a tip, a screen or a rebound beats them, and all three need a body in front of the net as the shot is taken.

Timing matters more than effort, and it runs opposite ways for the two jobs. **Driving in from the perimeter**, arrive too early and a defender ties you up; too late and the rebound is covered. **The net-front player** is planted *before* the release. The defending team recovers most rebounds, so you win one by deciding to go before the shot — [Offensive Zone Play](../systems/offensive_zone_play.md) owns that figure. Go every time, including on shots you are sure will be saved: into the ice in front of the goalie, never into them.

**And when the shot from the half-wall is yours, take it.** "Shoot first" is a bias rather than a rule. What you are breaking is the habit of *always* looking to pass, which lets a defence sit in the seam and wait.

**Holding your lane is a spacing job before it is a scoring one.** In a containment structure — a coaching choice, not a law — a flat line of defenders cannot cover three wide forwards without spreading thin; drift inside and one of them covers two of you. Coming up the ice, your width opens the seam your centre uses. Under an aggressive press the weak-side winger leaves the lane, so find out which your team plays. In the offensive zone the job changes, and that section says so.

**In your own end, staying high pays twice.** It denies the opposing defenceman time to shoot, and it leaves you one short trip from your breakout spot. Drift into the corner and you lose both: the point opens up, and nobody is on the wall to exit to. The read is when to go. Leave before your team has the puck and you have abandoned the point; leave late and the pass to you at the blue line is long, flat and easily picked off. Turn your feet as the battle is being won, and go as the puck becomes yours.

**Winning the puck on the wall is a winger's defining physical skill** — a coaching ranking, not a measured one. Puck on the far side of your body from the checker, side-on with your hip and backside toward them, a wide low base, feet that never stop, and a decision about where the puck goes made before you get there. Where checking is barred, hold the position you have and never bump or drive into them; in a CARHA adult league, avoid the contact altogether. Rim it when the checker is sealing you against the boards; reverse it when they have over-committed and skated past you.

**Two things override all of it, at any speed: never turn your back to anyone coming at you, and never duck.** A turned back makes the contact a hit from behind, which you can neither see nor brace for; a tucked chin straightens your neck and puts your head into the boards or a post first. Skates parallel to the wall, forearm and hip to it, head up and chin off your chest. [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md) covers the technique and what your free hand may legally do.

**The last job is the one nobody watches you do.** When the puck turns over, the job under the systems this document assumes — forwards take lanes and men, the defence takes the puck — is not to chase the puck but to find the opponent arriving behind it. Sprint into your own lane, pick up their third forward, and get to your spot before the puck does. Coaching material names an unmarked trailer as one of the cheapest goals off a rush. Then settle into the defensive-zone job: cover your point, and be ready to break out again.

**Other teams coach the reverse on purpose, and it has a name — *hound the puck*:** the first forward back pressures the carrier all the way in while the defence holds inside. It is a legitimate system, so find out which your team plays. What is wrong under *both* is abandoning your man mid-rush to double the puck, because nobody else on the ice expects it. **Pressuring the carrier never means finishing into the back of a player skating at their own end boards** — that is checking from behind, and [Defending the Rush](../systems/defending_the_rush.md#what-hounding-never-means) sets out what it costs.

**Every line and named area of the ice is defined in [Rink Map](../foundation/rink_map.md), and the working vocabulary — your lane, high and low, strong side and weak side, the point, the half-wall, rim and reverse — in [Language and Glossary](../foundation/language_and_glossary.md).** Read those two once and everything below gets easier.

Two of those words matter most here. **High** means toward the blue line you are facing, **low** toward the goal line. **Strong side** is the side the puck is on, **weak side** the other. The strong-side winger takes the puck up the wall; the weak-side winger holds the weak-side point and, under the low zone collapse this document assumes, fills the high slot while the centre is low.

**A note on the headings below.** Each zone is broken into the same four situations. *Without the Puck* means the puck is not on your stick **and** you are not the player pressuring whoever has it.

## Defensive Zone

Your job is to help your team exit the zone cleanly and to limit what the opposing defenceman can do on your side.

---

### Defensive Zone: Carrying the Puck — Free Space

```facts
Position: On the boards on your side, between the hash marks and the goal line, in the conventional swing breakout — Breakouts is the authority on this landmark
Action: Come down from your point coverage as the breakout starts; don't stand there while the other team has the puck
Convention: The hash-mark spot belongs to the conventional swing breakout — flat/spread holds you higher, stretch sends a winger up ice, so find out which you're in
Action: If you receive the puck, look to move immediately — carry out along the boards, or pass to the centre
Goal: Exit the defensive zone cleanly and transition to offence
Never: Hold the puck in your own zone longer than you need to — your first look is always up ice
```

**Where to be:** On the boards on your side, **between the hash marks and the goal line** — roughly level with the faceoff dot or just below it. You get there by coming *down* from your point coverage as the breakout starts; you do not stand there while the other team has the puck. [Breakouts](../systems/breakouts.md) is the authority on this landmark.

> **Wall position is system-dependent, and the authority says so.** The hash-mark spot belongs to the **conventional swing breakout** — the structure assumed throughout this document. Teams running a **flat/spread** structure ask wingers to hold higher toward the blue line; teams running an aggressive **stretch or flying-winger** structure send one winger up ice entirely. And under a **centre fly** the centre leaves early up the middle, so the **weak-side winger** drops in to take the low support job underneath the puck instead. **Find out which one you're in** — the failure mode of a winger standing at the hash marks in a stretch system is exactly as bad as the reverse. See [Breakouts](../systems/breakouts.md).

![](diagram:winger-wall-depth-by-system)

**What to do:** You are one of the primary outlets when your team starts a breakout (the controlled exit from your defensive zone). The defenceman may pass to you, the centre, or their D-partner depending on who is open. If you receive the puck, immediately look to move — either carry it out along the boards, or pass to the centre if they are in a better position to exit.

**Goal:** Exit the defensive zone cleanly and transition to offence.

**Key considerations:** Don't hold the puck in your defensive zone too long. Get it moving quickly. Once you have control on the boards, your first look is always up ice.

### Defensive Zone: Carrying the Puck — Under Pressure

**In short:** Puck on your far side, feet moving, and know your out before it arrives; a rim or reverse beats forcing a pass through traffic.

```facts
Position: Against the boards, with the puck on the far side of your body from the checker
Technique: Side-on, backside toward them, feet wide, low and moving — a stationary player on the wall gets pinned. Chest to the boards is the last resort for when you are already facing the wall, not the target
Never: Turn your back to anyone coming at you — USA Hockey: "Even a light hit from behind could inflict a severe head or neck injury". Skates parallel, forearm and hip, head up, not the shoulder tip
Never: Duck to avoid a check — in a head-down collision with the boards or a goal post, your neck can break at walking speed; no speed is slow enough to make that safe. Head up, chin off your chest instead
Never: Turn into pressure the instant you receive it
Read: Rim to beat a checker sealing you against the boards; reverse to beat one who has over-committed and skated past you; in CARHA keep the backswing under fifteen inches or it is a slap-shot penalty
Rule: The free hand may fend off an opponent or their stick, but may not hold their stick or body — NHL and IIHF 56.1, PWHL 57.1; Hockey Canada 8.1 writes it as blocking an opponent's stick with the hands, arms or body, and the arm in a strength move where you have body position, but never holding
Rule: USA Hockey writes the fend-off permission in its Casebook only, and conditions it — control the puck (Standard of Play Situation 6) or be reaching for it, engaged in a corner or in front of the goal (Situation 7), hold a position you "legally established", and never push off to create space
Rule: Fingers closing on a jersey or a stick is a holding minor, and not the ceiling — a hold that injures is a mandatory major and ejection in Hockey Canada 8.1(b) and CARHA 63(b), and CARHA 63(a) lets the referee make any hold a major, which ejects and bans you at least a game (30(a), 32(d))
Rule: Where checking is barred, hold position and never drive your hip in: a minor in Hockey Canada's non-checking divisions (7.3(a)) and USA Hockey's (604(c)). CARHA 49(a) also prices not averting body contact, and a major there ejects (30(a))
Action: Know your out before you get there, and don't force a pass through traffic in your own zone
Goal: Don't lose the puck in a dangerous position. Get it to safety
```

**Where to be:** Against the boards, protecting the puck with your body.

**What to do:** Shield the puck from the pressuring opponent. Look for your centre swinging low or your defenceman as a safe outlet. If no pass is available, **rim** the puck (a hard shot along the boards that follows the curve of the rink onward to a teammate further around) away from danger, or **reverse** it (send it back the way it came, against the direction the pressure is flowing) to a teammate arriving behind you. In a CARHA league keep the rim's backswing under fifteen inches: past that it is a slap shot, a minor, and if it injures an opponent a major that ends your game and sits you out at least the next one.

**Rim and reverse are opposite plays and you pick by where the pressure is.** A rim beats a checker who is sealing you against the boards, because it goes *past* them. A reverse beats a checker who has over-committed and skated past you, because it goes back into the space they just left.

**Make that pick before the puck is on your stick, not after.** You are receiving into a wall reception, which is exactly the case where a late look costs the most — so take a glance over your inside shoulder while the pass is still travelling, and let the last look land back on the puck just before it arrives. [Scanning and Anticipation](../hockey-iq/scanning_and_anticipation.md) covers the habit and the research behind it in full; it is not restated here.

![](diagram:winger-dz-rim)

![](diagram:winger-dz-reverse)

**Puck protection on the wall — the technique.** "Shield it with your body" is not instruction. Full detail in [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md); the essentials:

- **Puck on the far side of your body from the checker.** Your body is the wall between them and the puck. If the puck is on the same side as the pressure, you've already lost it.
- **Get side-on, your hip and backside toward them, and your feet wide.** A wide, low base is almost impossible to move. Standing tall and narrow is why players get knocked off pucks. Where checking is barred, hold the position you already have and never drive your hip into them: that is a minor in Hockey Canada's non-checking divisions (7.3(a)) and in USA Hockey's (604(c)), whose legal contact includes two players after the puck *"simply maintaining established body position"*, or closing a gap you have already angled. In a CARHA adult league, avoid the contact altogether — its Rule 49(a) penalises a player who *"does not avert body contact with an opponent"*, at a minor or a major, and a major ends your game and sits you out at least the next one (30(a), 32(d)).
- **Do not turn into pressure the instant you receive it.** Take it, absorb the contact, then spin off. Aim for **skates parallel to the boards, forearm and hip to the wall, head up and chin off your chest** — your shoulder faces the checker, and it is the boards it should not meet. Never the point of your shoulder, and no part of it if you can help it. Facing the boards chest-first is the *last resort*, for when you are already facing the wall and cannot turn: then get your stick and gloves up and spread on the glass, with no head contact at all.
- **Never turn your back to anyone coming at you, and never duck** — two prohibitions against two different injuries. A turned back makes the contact a hit from behind, and USA Hockey warns that *"Even a light hit from behind could inflict a severe head or neck injury"*. Ducking straightens your neck, and USA Hockey records that the resulting injury can occur at walking speed.
- **Keep your feet moving.** A stationary player on the wall gets pinned; a player with momentum can escape along the boards or spin out toward the middle. Spin only off a checker already leaning on you, never off one still coming at you, whatever their speed: mid-spin your back is to them.
- **Your free hand: fend off, never hold.** A forearm or open hand to keep a checker off you; never fingers closing on a jersey or a stick. Each book sets its own conditions even on the fend-off, so the habit that is legal everywhere is keeping the free hand on your own shaft and winning the wall with your feet and hips.
- **Know your out before you get there.** Decide where the puck is going while you're still skating to it, not after you're pinned.

**Goal:** Don't lose the puck in a dangerous position. Get it to safety.

**Key considerations:** Rimming the puck around the boards is a completely valid play. It moves the puck past the pressure — to a teammate further around, or at worst out of danger — and gives your team time to regroup. Don't try to force a pass through traffic in your own zone.

**What it costs.**

- **The free hand.** NHL and IIHF Rule 56.1 let the free hand *"fend off"* an opponent or their stick but *"not… to hold an opponent's stick or body"*, and PWHL 57.1 says the same; Hockey Canada 8.1 lets you *"block an opponent's stick with their hands, arms, or body"* but not hold it. USA Hockey grants it only in its Casebook (Standard of Play Situations 6 and 7): while you control the puck, or reach for it in a corner or net-front battle, from a position you *"legally established"* — and never to *"grab the stick, sweater or arm"* or *"push off and create space"*. CARHA writes no permission. An arm in a strength move to block needs body position under NHL and IIHF 54.2, PWHL 55.2 and Hockey Canada 8.1; USA Hockey 622 calls a free arm that restrains an opponent, or pins them on the boards, holding.
- **Holding is a minor everywhere, and not the ceiling.** A hold that injures is a mandatory major and game misconduct under Hockey Canada 8.1(b) and CARHA 63(b), and CARHA 63(a) lets the referee make any hold a major, which ends your game and at least your next one (30(a), 32(d)).

### Defensive Zone: Pressuring an Opponent with the Puck

**In short:** Under a low zone collapse, stay high between their point man and the slot rather than chasing into the corner; learn your team's system.

```facts
Position: High in the defensive zone under a low zone collapse, covering the opposing defenceman at the point on your side, between them and the slot
Convention: Staying high out of the corner is the low zone collapse job, not a law — under man-on-man you follow your check in, under a hybrid only below the man/zone line, which teams draw at the dots, the hash marks or the tops of the circles, so learn your team's version
Action: Find out which defensive-zone system your team plays before your first shift — on the coaching account a breakdown here is two players on different assumptions rather than an effort problem
Goal: Deny shots from the point and stop the opposition setting up a clean cycle
Read: If your man walks down off the point, follow a few feet, stay connected, and call the switch
Read: If the puck squirts up the strong-side boards to you, engage it — that is your breakout, not a corner battle
Read: Under low zone collapse, the centre gone into the strong-side corner — the weak-side winger collapses to the high slot until the puck leaves; under man-on-man they stay with their check
Risk: Under a low zone collapse, chase into the corner and the opposing defenceman walks in free, with nobody positioned for the breakout
```

**Where to be:** High in the defensive zone, covering the opposing defenceman at the point on your side. (The "point" is the area just inside the blue line where defencemen normally stand — an area, not the line itself. Covering the point means denying that defenceman the space to operate.)

**What to do:** Deny the opposing defenceman time and space at the point. Position yourself between them and the slot/net — this blocks the most dangerous shooting and passing lanes at once. Stay on your side of the ice.

> **Whether you stay high or go to the corner depends on your team's system.** This is a coaching choice, not a law of hockey — see [Defensive Zone Coverage](../systems/defensive_zone_coverage.md).
>
> - **Low zone collapse / zone coverage** — the system assumed throughout, and the one coaching material most often describes as taught first in North American minor, junior and rec hockey — consensus, not a survey. You stay high on the point. The low battle belongs to the strong-side defenceman and the centre. **Do not go into the corner.**
> - **Man-on-man** — you follow your assigned check wherever they go, including into the corner. The points get covered by whoever's man is there.
> - **Hybrid** — man-on-man below the dots, zone above. You take your man down only once they cross that line — and **where the line sits is a team decision**, some saying the dots, some the hash marks, some the tops of the circles, so learn your team's version.
>
> The rest of this section assumes low zone collapse. **Find out which one your team plays before your first shift** — on the coaching account a defensive-zone breakdown is two players on different assumptions rather than an effort problem ([Defensive Zone Coverage](../systems/defensive_zone_coverage.md) owns that point).

**Goal:** Deny shots from the point and prevent the opposing team from setting up a clean cycle (a play where their forwards rotate around the offensive zone passing to each other to maintain possession and create chances).

**Key considerations:** It is tempting to chase the puck into the corner when you see your team battling there. In a collapse system, resist it. If you go to the corner, the opposing defenceman is free to walk in for a shot, and your team has no one positioned for the breakout. Staying high is your job, not just your option.

**When their defenceman winds up, get into the lane.** Your stick and body belong between the shooter and the net, so a point shot has to go through you or wide — on your feet with your head out of the lane, blocking the way *Penalty Kill* below sets out. If you cannot get there in time, take away the pass to the weak side instead and let your goalie see the shot. Do not stand halfway, where you neither block it nor stay out of your goalie's eyes.

![](diagram:winger-leaves-the-point)

**Exception — when your man walks down:** If the opposing defenceman you are covering moves down off the point to join the puck battle, follow them down a few feet to stay tight on them. Don't chase all the way into the corner. But stay connected and call out the switch so the centre or defender knows you've moved with your man.

**Exception — when the puck comes up the wall to you (strong-side winger):** If a corner battle squirts the puck up the strong-side boards toward you, engage it. That's not a corner battle — it's your breakout opportunity. Grab the puck, get your feet moving, and start the exit.

**Exception — when the centre is in the corner (weak-side winger only):** If the puck is in the strong-side corner and the centre has gone in to help your defender, the **weak-side winger** collapses from the point to the high slot to cover the area the centre has vacated. Anyone with a clear view should call the rotation — you, the centre, or the weak-side defender. The strong-side winger holds their own point coverage. As soon as the puck moves out of the corner, the weak-side winger rotates back up to their point.

### Defensive Zone: Without the Puck — Ready to Break Out

**In short:** In a swing breakout, stay high until your team wins the puck, then come down the wall as the first outlet, calling for it.

![](diagram:winger-high-then-down-the-wall)

```facts
Position: High and level with your man while they have the puck; down the wall between the hash marks and the goal line the moment your team wins it, in the conventional swing breakout
Action: Watch the battle rather than the puck and read who is coming out of it with possession
Action: Turn your feet before the puck is won, but don't leave the point until it actually is
Technique: Present yourself as an outlet — stick on the ice, feet already moving, calling for it
Convention: The trip down the wall to the outlet belongs to the conventional swing breakout — flat/spread holds you higher, stretch sends a winger up ice, so find out which you're in
Key: Anticipating while still onside and able to get back is coached; abandoning coverage on a hope is not — the test is whether you can recover
Goal: Be the first line of the breakout and get into the neutral zone with speed
```

*The section above is you denying an opponent who has the puck. This one is the moment either side of that: the puck is loose or your team is winning it, and your job flips from coverage to outlet.*

**Where to be:** This is the one job on the ice that is defined by a *move* rather than a spot, so hold both ends of it:

- **While they still have the puck** — high on your side, roughly level with the opposing defenceman you are covering. Unchanged from the section above.
- **As your team wins it** — down the wall on your side, to **between the hash marks and the goal line**. That is the outlet position, **a little under 44 to 64 feet below** where you started. The blue line is 64 ft out from the goal line and **the point sits just inside it rather than on it**; the outlet band runs from level with the faceoff dot, 20 ft out, down to the goal line ([Rink Map](../foundation/rink_map.md) covers both figures). **In the conventional swing breakout**, that is — under a **flat/spread** structure you hold higher toward the blue line, and under a **stretch or flying-winger** structure one winger leaves up ice altogether. Find out which you're in (see the note under *Defensive Zone: Carrying the Puck — Free Space* above, and [Breakouts](../systems/breakouts.md)).

Staying high is what makes that trip short and makes you the first outlet available; it is not where you receive the pass. A winger who never leaves the point is as wrong as one who left it early — the pass to a winger standing at the blue line is long, flat and intercepted, and it arrives in your back. Come down as the puck becomes yours.

**What to do:** Watch the battle rather than the puck, and read who is going to come out of it with possession. The instant it is yours, start down the wall and present yourself as an outlet — stick on the ice, feet already moving, calling for it. Do not leave the point before your team actually has it; do turn your feet so that when they do, the first stride is already going.

**Goal:** Be the first line of the breakout. Get into the neutral zone with speed the moment your team has possession.

**Key considerations:** One distinction separates a good winger from a cheating one: **turning your feet in anticipation while you are still onside and still able to get back is coached and valuable; abandoning your coverage on a hope is not.** The test is whether you can recover if you read it wrong. Full treatment in [Breakouts](../systems/breakouts.md), which is the authority for breakout positioning.

## Neutral Zone

The transition zone. Your job is to carry the puck through your lane and support the breakout.

---

### Neutral Zone: Carrying the Puck — Free Space

```facts
Position: In your lane along the boards, on your own side of the ice
Action: Skate hard through the neutral zone — carry into the offensive zone if the path is open, or set up an entry play with the centre
Goal: Move the puck through the neutral zone efficiently and enter the offensive zone with speed
Never: Drift into the centre of the ice — that's the centre's lane, and staying wide is what keeps the ice open for your line
Read: On a 2-on-1 with the puck, drive at the net so the defender commits, stopping short of the goalie; shoot if they take the pass, pass if they take you. Without it, take the far post, stick on the ice
Read: On a 3-on-2 with no lane to the net, pull up at the half-wall and let the late man arrive rather than forcing it into two defenders
```

**Where to be:** In your lane along the boards, carrying through the neutral zone.

![](diagram:neutral-zone-lanes)

**What to do:** Skate hard through the neutral zone in your lane. Look to carry into the offensive zone if the path is open, or set up for a zone entry play with the centre. Stay on your side of the ice.

**Goal:** Move the puck through the neutral zone efficiently and enter the offensive zone with speed.

**Key considerations:** Don't drift to the centre of the ice — that's the centre's lane. Staying wide keeps the ice open and creates proper spacing for your line.

**When the rush is an odd-man rush, the read is the defender, not the goalie.** On a **2-on-1 with the puck on your stick**, drive toward the net from your lane so the lone defender has to choose: if they slide across to take the pass, shoot — a shot low to the far pad makes a rebound for your teammate — and if they step to you, the pass is on. If you are the one **without** the puck, drive to the far post with your stick on the ice, because that is where the pass and the rebound both arrive. On a **3-on-2**, take your defender wide with speed; if no lane to the net opens, pull up along the half-wall and let the late man arrive into the space behind you rather than forcing it into two defenders. That is the common coaching approach; some coaches want the 2-on-1 carrier to pass early and make the goalie move instead, so ask your bench which your team plays. [Zone Entries](../systems/zone_entries.md) covers the entries that follow.

### Neutral Zone: Carrying the Puck — Under Pressure

**In short:** Don't stickhandle through two players: dump when your support is late, pick the right kind of dump, and chase it on an angle.

```facts
Position: Along the boards in your lane
Options: Hard rim, soft area dump, chip past the defenceman, cross-corner dump, or a flip over a trap
Read: Chip past a defenceman who is stepping up; soft-dump when you'll win the race; flip when a trap has closed every route along the ice
Technique: Angle your chase to take away their easier escape rather than skating straight at the puck; if the goalie comes out for it, pull up
Never: Land a flip from your own side of the centre red line past the goal line. Land it behind their defence but short of the line instead, or you have iced it
Never: Fight through neutral-zone pressure with the puck — coaching material puts the making of odd-man rushes here; nothing published classifies them by the play that created them
Read: Dump when nobody is arriving with you, or when your support is late — a read, not the default when in doubt
Key: NHL tracking at 5-on-5 put a carry-in at about twice the value of a dump-in; the one-third break-even is modelled from one team's games and an assumed shooting percentage — Zone Entries covers both
```

**Where to be:** Along the boards in your lane.

**What to do:** If pressured, make a quick decision: dump the puck deep into the offensive zone and chase it, or pass back to a defenceman to reset. Don't try to fight through pressure in the neutral zone with the puck.

**Dump-ins are not one play. Pick the right kind.** You are your team's most frequent dump-in taker, so this is a core skill, not a last resort. See [Zone Entries](../systems/zone_entries.md) for the full treatment.

- **Hard rim** — around the boards behind the net to the far corner. Beats a defence that collapses to the middle, and arrives where your first forechecker is heading. Commonly the default of the five — an impression rather than a counted one, and [Zone Entries](../systems/zone_entries.md), which owns them, ranks none of them by frequency.
- **Soft area dump** — a gentle chip into a specific empty area, aimed to *stop* rather than carom. Used when you'll win the race; a hard dump just gives it to their defenceman.
- **Chip past the defenceman** — flick it off the boards past a defender who is stepping up or standing up at the line, and skate onto it yourself. This is a *possession* play, not a surrender, and it's the best option against a defenceman who is stepping up.
- **Cross-corner / north-south dump** — into the far corner away from where their D is retreating, forcing the longest possible retrieval skate.
- **Flip / area dump over a trap** — lift the puck high so it travels *over* a congested neutral zone and lands in open space behind their defence. This is the answer when a trap has closed every route along the ice. From your own side of the centre red line, land it past the defence but **before the goal line**, or you have iced it — past the red line it is not icing however far it travels, unless your league splits the ice at the blue lines, as some CARHA leagues do (see *Icing* below).

![](diagram:dump-soft-area)

![](diagram:dump-chip-past)

![](diagram:dump-cross-corner)

![](diagram:dump-flip-over-trap)

**Chasing it properly matters more than the dump itself.** Don't skate straight at the puck — that lets the retriever pick either side. Angle your route to take away their easier escape (usually the strong-side wall) so they're forced to the side your support is arriving on. If the goalie comes out for it, pull up. A dump-in with nobody chasing on an angle is just giving the puck away politely.

**Goal:** Keep possession or dump the puck safely into the offensive zone.

**Key considerations:** A turnover in the neutral zone is expensive, and it is where coaching material puts the making of odd-man rushes — where the attackers outnumber the defenders coming back, e.g. 2-on-1 or 3-on-2, a high-danger scoring chance against your team. **That is coaching consensus rather than a tracked figure: nothing published classifies odd-man rushes by the play that created them**, and not every one starts here — [Defending the Rush](../systems/defending_the_rush.md) owns how they are made and lists five causes, one of which is in the offensive zone. So do not try to stickhandle through two players at the line.

**But that is not an argument for dumping it in by default.** The tracked evidence runs the other way: at 5-on-5 a carry-in was worth about twice a dump-in, a failed carry cost far less than it feels like it does, and the study's own conclusion was that players give the puck up at the blue line too easily. That was hand-tracked NHL play, not your league, and it says nothing about amateur hockey — so take the direction rather than the decimals. The dump is a **read**: put it in when nobody is arriving with you, or when your support is late and the alternative is forcing it into two defenders. [Zone Entries](../systems/zone_entries.md) covers the figures, including the roughly one-third confidence in a carry that is the break-even — a threshold modelled from one team's tracked games and an assumed shooting percentage, so take its shape rather than its number.

### Neutral Zone: Pressuring an Opponent with the Puck

```facts
Position: Under a containment structure, in your lane, blocking the passing lanes through your side
Action: Cut off the passing options and force the puck carrier wide toward the boards
Convention: Lane-blocking belongs to a containment structure — a 1-2-2, a 1-2-2 trap or a 1-3-1 — and is not a law of hockey
Options: Under an aggressive press the lane-blocking instruction reverses: forwards hunt the puck and the weak-side winger does leave the lane
Action: Find out which neutral-zone system your team plays and what the call is for switching — most run two or three depending on the score and the shift
Risk: Leave your lane under containment and you open a seam the single defenceman behind their three-player line cannot cover
Goal: Prevent a clean entry — force a difficult pass, force them wide, or force a dump-in
```

**Where to be:** In your lane, blocking passing lanes through your side.

**What to do:** When the opposing team is moving the puck through the neutral zone, stay in your lane and cut off the passing options. Force the puck carrier wide toward the boards. In a containment structure, don't leave your lane to chase. Block the lane instead and let the pressure develop naturally.

> **Which of those two things you do is your team's neutral-zone system, not a law of hockey.** The lane-blocking job described here belongs to a **containment structure** — a **1-2-2**, a **1-2-2 trap** or a **1-3-1** — where the whole design is to deny the middle and wait for a mistake, and where one forward leaving the wall to chase opens the seam instantly. The realistic alternative is an **aggressive neutral-zone press**, the middle-third shape behind a 2-1-2 forecheck: there the instruction reverses, forwards hunt the puck, and the weak-side winger *does* leave the lane to pressure, because the point is to take the puck back rather than wait for it. **Find out which one your team plays — and what the call is for switching**, because most teams run two or three depending on the score and the shift. [Neutral Zone Systems](../systems/neutral_zone_systems.md) is the authority and sets out all of them.

![](diagram:nz-1-2-2-containment)

![](diagram:nz-1-3-1)

![](diagram:nz-pressure)

**Goal:** Prevent a clean entry into your defensive zone. Force the opponent to make a difficult pass, go wide, or dump the puck in.

**Key considerations:** Under a containment structure, blocking lanes is more effective than chasing: if you leave your lane to pressure the puck, you create a gap that the opponent will exploit, and the single defenceman behind their three-player line cannot cover two lanes. Stay disciplined. Under a press that trade is reversed deliberately — you accept the gap in exchange for winning the puck back sooner — so the discipline there is to pressure *together* rather than one player freelancing.

### Neutral Zone: Without the Puck — Filling Your Lane on the Rush

**In short:** Arrive at the blue line in stride and at full speed, head up, with a trail skate dragging on the line to stay onside.

```facts
Position: In your lane, moving toward the offensive zone
Action: Time your skating so you arrive at the blue line in stride, and be a moving target for the centre or defenceman
Technique: Drag a trail skate along the line while your body, stick and momentum get ahead of it — and keep the blade touching
Never: Drag the trail skate with your head down — your body, hands and stick are in the zone before your skate is, and that is where a defender standing up at the line will be
Rule: A dragged, touching skate keeps you onside wherever you play; a lifted trail skate is onside in the NHL, IIHF and PWHL but offside under USA Hockey Rule 630(a) and Hockey Canada Rule 6.11
Rule: Tagging up is a five-player act — the arm stays up until every teammate has cleared, in every book here; the NHL (83.3(i)), the IIHF, USA Hockey and the PWHL (85.3(i)) word it "at the same instant", Hockey Canada and CARHA as a result
Rule: Hockey Canada 6.11(b)(ii) and CARHA 72(e)(2) word the tag-up requirement as skate contact from every attacker and as a result — the zone "completely clear of attacking players", CARHA's in the Note under the clause — rather than as an instant, which comes to the same thing
Rule: ⚠️ **First check you have a tag-up at all** — USA Hockey Rule 630(d) grants the delayed offside only at Youth 15-Only and above, Girls 16U and above, High School and Adult; below those the whistle is immediate
Action: Tag, then look before you go — as the winger usually first to the line, you are the one who re-enters too early
Never: Coast at the blue line to be safe — arriving late and slow costs your line more than a whistle would
Read: On your off wing the lane is the same, but the puck sits on your inside hand — it helps entering the zone and costs you on a breakout pass up your own wall
```

**Where to be:** In your lane, moving toward the offensive zone.

**What to do:** Skate hard through the neutral zone in your lane. Be a target for a pass from the centre or defenceman. Time your skating so you arrive at the offensive zone blue line in stride — ready to attack.

**Goal:** Be a moving target and create a passing option through the neutral zone.

**Key considerations: arrive at the line at full speed, and let a dragged skate keep you legal — it is a technique worth drilling.** You attack the blue line widest and fastest, so the rule rewards you most: **drag a trail skate along the line while your body, stick and momentum get ahead of it.** Done properly you are a full stride into the zone, at speed, and still onside. The alternative — coasting at the line to be safe — is the classic way a winger arrives at a battle with no speed and loses it. Arriving late and slow costs your line more than a whistle would.

**One thing in the drag is not free, and it is your head.** Your body, hands and stick cross the line before your skate does, into the ice a defenceman standing up at their own blue line is defending. **A head up is the condition of the technique, not an extra** — you cannot brace for a hit you cannot see. [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md) owns that rule.

**Keep the blade in contact with the ice or the paint.** You are offside only when **both** skates have completely crossed the blue line before the puck has, so a dragged, touching skate keeps you onside wherever you play. A *lifted* trail skate is onside under the NHL, the IIHF and the PWHL, but **offside under USA Hockey and Hockey Canada**, which count only a skate in contact with the ice — so know which book is yours.

**A delayed offside is recoverable, but tagging up is a five-player act.** One foot on the line does your part; nobody re-enters until the last teammate has also cleared the zone. Tag, then **look** before you go — re-entering while a teammate is still deep gets the play whistled off, and as the winger usually first to the line, you are the one who does it. ⚠️ **And check that you have a tag-up at all:** under USA Hockey the delayed offside exists only at Youth 15-Only and above, Girls 16U and above, High School and Adult; below those the whistle is immediate. **The rule in full is in [Rules Primer](../foundation/rules_primer.md) and [Zone Entries](../systems/zone_entries.md).**

![](diagram:winger-arriving-at-the-line)

**If you are playing your off wing** — a left shot on the right side, or the reverse — your lane is the same but the puck now sits on your inside hand. That helps you entering the zone and costs you on a breakout pass up your own wall. [Switching Positions → Playing your off wing](switching_positions.md) covers the trade and the fix.

## Offensive Zone

Your natural home as a forward. Your job is to create scoring chances, win battles in the corners, and put pressure on the opposing defence.

---

### Offensive Zone: Carrying the Puck — Free Space

```facts
Position: Along the half-wall, or driving toward the net from the wing
Targets: Shoot, feed the centre in the slot, or find the defenceman at the point
Action: If you have space to drive the net, use your speed and get to the front of it — under control, and never into the goalie
Convention: "Shoot first" is a bias, not a rule — a teammate with a clear look at an open side is always worth the extra pass
Never: Stand still — movement is what creates the opportunities
Goal: Create a shot on net, or force the defence to react and open space for teammates
```

**Where to be:** Along the half-wall (the boards area roughly level with the faceoff dot — between the corner and the point), or driving toward the net from the wing.

**What to do:** If open on the half-wall, look to shoot, pass to the centre in the slot, or find the defenceman at the point. If you have space to drive the net, do it — use your speed and get to the front of the net — under control, and never into the goalie.

**Goal:** Create a shot on net, or force the defence to react and open space for teammates.

**Key considerations:** Don't stand still. Movement creates opportunities. If you're open, look to shoot first — most wingers pass up good shots rather than take bad ones. That said, "shoot first" is a bias, not a rule: a teammate with a clear look at an open side is always worth the extra pass. What you're avoiding is the habit of *always* looking to pass, which lets a defence sit back and take away the seam.

---

### Offensive Zone: Carrying the Puck — Under Pressure

```facts
Position: In the corner or along the boards
Technique: Use your body to protect the puck and fight to maintain possession — forearm and hip to the wall, not the point of your shoulder, and not the whole shoulder if you can help it
Never: Turn your back to anyone coming at you — USA Hockey: "Even a light hit from behind could inflict a severe head or neck injury". Skates parallel, forearm and hip, head up, chin off your chest
Never: Duck to avoid a check — in a head-down collision with the boards or a goal post, your neck can break at walking speed; no speed is slow enough to make that safe. Head up, chin off your chest instead
Options: Pass back to the defenceman at the point, or hold possession until the centre can support you
Read: Look up before passing to the point — the strong-side defender may have stepped down to support the cycle
Never: Force the puck out of the corner through heavy traffic — a rushed pass turns over possession
Goal: Maintain possession in the offensive zone and keep the pressure going
```

**Where to be:** In the corner or along the boards.

**What to do:** Use your body to protect the puck. Fight to maintain possession — but the same override applies here as in your own end: never turn your back to anyone coming at you, and never duck. Skates parallel to the boards, forearm and hip to the wall, head up and chin off your chest. See [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md), which covers it. Look for a pass back to the defenceman at the point, or hold possession until the centre can support you. Don't try to force the puck out of the corner through heavy traffic.

![](diagram:winger-corner-and-the-empty-point)

**Goal:** Maintain possession in the offensive zone and keep the pressure going.

**Key considerations:** The corners are a battle area. Protecting the puck and waiting for support is better than a rushed pass that turns over possession. Your defenceman is usually an option at the point — but the strong-side defender may have stepped down (moved from the blue line toward the top of the faceoff circle) to support the cycle, so look up before passing blindly to the point.

**When nothing is open, cycle it rather than give it away.** Protect the puck down the wall and leave it on the boards behind you for the teammate coming in low — usually your centre — then skate up and away from the wall to get open for the return pass or a shot. The defender who followed you is now out of position, and the one who stayed is facing two of you. If the wall is sealed, reverse it behind the net to the far side instead. That low cycle is the common approach; some teams prefer to go low-to-high to the point early and get bodies to the net, so ask your bench which yours runs.

### Offensive Zone: Pressuring an Opponent with the Puck

**In short:** As F2, seal the carrier's next escape and pin the puck, not the player; as F3, stay higher and cover the middle.

```facts
Position: As F2, in behind F1 on the strong side; as F3, higher in the zone covering the middle and the weak side in the 2-1-2 default, or puck-side in the stacked variant
Key: F1, F2 and F3 are roles set by order of arrival — if you dumped the puck in and got there first, you are F1
Read: Take the next-most-likely escape route from wherever F1 has pushed the carrier — typically the strong-side boards or behind the net
Technique: Pin the puck against the boards, not the player
Rule: IIHF women's Rule 101.1 prohibits using the boards to eliminate an opponent from the play, push her into the boards, or pin her along the boards
Rule: Do not hear the boards-pinning prohibition as a women's-hockey rule only — USA Hockey's holding rule reaches the same act for every player, naming "wrapping one or both arms around the opponent along the boards in a manner that pins them against the boards" (Rule 622 Note)
Rule: At a club in England or Wales, outside the Elite League, a game misconduct for checking from behind or a head check, or a boarding major, is at least a one-game ban, in every league or age group you are registered in (IHUK DOPS C.1, s.11)
Convention: A 2-1-2 sends two forwards deep and a 1-2-2 sends one, and F3 sits weak-side by default or puck-side in the stacked variant — find out which your team runs
Goal: Force a turnover or a bad outlet pass by trapping the carrier with no escape route
Key: Forechecking is about angles, not speed. Stay in your role and trust your linemates to be in theirs
```

**Forechecking** means putting pressure on the opposing team in their own defensive zone (your offensive zone) — trying to win the puck back rather than retreat.

> **F1, F2 and F3 are roles, not positions — and not people.** They are defined purely by **order of arrival**: whoever gets to the puck first is F1, the second support is F2, the highest is F3. If you dumped the puck in and you're first on it, **you are F1**, even though you're a winger and the centre is on the ice. Don't wait for the centre to arrive. Take the role your position on the ice gives you and let your linemates fill in around you.
>
> How many forwards go deep also depends on the system your team runs — a 2-1-2 sends two in, a 1-2-2 sends one. See [Forechecking Systems](../systems/forechecking_systems.md). The description below assumes a **2-1-2** with the centre arriving first, which is the standard forecheck throughout but not a law of hockey.

Your role depends on which side of the ice the puck is on, and on who got there first.

**Where to be:**
- **Strong-side winger (F2):** Coming in behind F1 on the strong side to seal the puck. Read where F1 has pushed the carrier and take the next-most-likely escape route — typically the strong-side boards (corner) or behind the net.
- **Weak-side winger (F3):** Higher in the zone, covering any opposing forward leaking up the middle or a cross-ice pass to the weak side. **Which side F3 sits on is a genuine coaching split, not a law of hockey.** The weak-side default described here is the common one; in the **strong-side (stacked) variant** F3 shades to the puck side instead, so all three forwards overload one corner and the far-side breakout is conceded deliberately. Find out which your team runs — [Forechecking Systems](../systems/forechecking_systems.md) sets out both.

![](diagram:forecheck-212)

![](diagram:forecheck-212-stacked)

**What to do:**
- **As F2:** Be F1's second support on the strong side. Seal the puck wherever F1 has angled it. **Pin the *puck* against the boards, not the player.** ⚠️ **Wrapping a player up against the boards is holding.** USA Hockey's holding rule names *"[w]rapping one or both arms around the opponent along the boards in a manner that pins them against the boards and prevents them from playing the puck or skating"*, for every player (Rule 622, Note), and the IIHF's women's Rule 101.1 bars pinning her along the boards in a battle for the puck. See [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md).
- **As F3:** Stay higher and read the play. Cover the weak-side breakout option and be ready to transition the moment the puck is won.

**Goal:** Force a turnover or a bad outlet pass by trapping the puck carrier with no escape route.

**Key considerations:** Effective forechecking is about angles, not just speed. Read where F1 has pushed the puck and seal the next-most-likely escape. Stay in your F2 or F3 role and trust your teammates to be in theirs — if everyone chases the puck, the opponent will pass around you.

### Offensive Zone: Without the Puck — Getting Open and Crashing the Net

**In short:** Get open on your side for a shot or one-timer, and crash the net on every shot toward it, around the goalie, never through them.

```facts
Position: On the half-wall, in your side's faceoff circle, or at the side of the net and the goalmouth
Never: Crowd the high slot, between the dots and the top of the circles, when another player is already there. Which player that is depends on the system, so find out rather than assuming it is the centre
Technique: For a one-timer, open your stance toward the passer with your weight back, start the backswing as the pass is released, and hit the ice just behind the puck
Read: If the pass is bad, don't force the one-timer. Take the extra touch, because a miss ends up in the neutral zone going the other way
Action: Crash the net on every shot toward it, never into the goalie. Driving in from the wall or your circle, arrive with the shot, not well before; on the net-front job you are planted before the release
Technique: At the net front a defender you feel, not watch, moves you, so know where the near post is before the shot and take the shove braced — wide base, knees and hips giving — never against the goalie
Never: Meet a goal post head first, and never duck. Head up, chin off your chest instead. A head-down, head-first hit on a goal post is the same injury as one on the boards, and can happen at walking speed
Rule: Under USA Hockey a game misconduct for going into the goalie, or any other, also suspends you for your team's next game (404(b)), and a second major in one game at least two (403(b))
Goal: Be a threat in your half of the zone, particularly from the side of the net for rebounds
```

**Where to be:** On the half-wall, in your side's faceoff circle, or at the side of the net and the goalmouth. **Stay out of the *high* slot — between the dots and the top of the circles — unless your team's structure sends you there. The goalmouth, meaning the immediate net front, is a different patch of ice and it is very often yours** (see *Net-front and the crease* below). [Offensive Zone Play](../systems/offensive_zone_play.md), which owns the zone, gives that upper layer to no position: it has one forward supporting in the high slot to form a triangle with the point, a defenceman who has activated down to the top of the circle receiving there, and the late-arriving trailer using it. Which of those your team asks for is a coaching choice, so find out. Two layers, two jobs; running them together is how two forwards end up standing on the same square metre.

![](diagram:winger-offensive-zone-patches)

**What to do:** Get open for a pass. From the half-wall, be ready to shoot or feed the centre in the slot. From your faceoff circle, be ready for a one-timer off a cross-ice pass. From the side of the net, look for rebounds and tip-ins. Crash the net on every shot toward it, timed by which of the two jobs you have — the drive in from the wall or your circle, or the net-front assignment.

**The net front is the one place you take contact you never see coming — learn this before the rules that govern it.** Your eyes are on the puck by design, so a defender working under your arms to walk you sideways is a body you *feel* rather than watch. You can be moved several feet without looking up — along a path with a steel goal frame at one edge and a set goalie at the other.

Two habits cover it. **Know where the near post is before the shot is taken** — USA Hockey's *Heads Up Hockey Program Guide* lists *"[a]voiding goal posts while driving to the net"* among the answers it expects on what playing safe hockey means. And **take the shove braced**: wide base, knees bent, weight low, knees and hips giving rather than a locked frame.

**Head up, chin off your chest, and never duck.** USA Hockey names the goal post alongside the boards in how these injuries happen — a head *"slightly flexed (head down) while making head-on contact with the boards or goal post"* — and that injury *"can occur at walking speed"*, so a slow, crowded net front is not a safe one. Its *Introduction To Body Contact* says it plainly: *"Hit the boards or goal posts with an arm, a leg, or anything but your head first."* **None of this is scoped to checking leagues** — a box-out or a shove, legal or not in your book, puts the same body in the same place.

**And there is one body at the net front you must not take braced — the goalie's.** Give ground the moment a goalie leans into you, then re-set the screen. [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md) covers the net-front battle from both sides.

**The one-timer — the basics of actually hitting one.** Full detail in [Shooting](../technique/shooting.md).

- **Set your feet before the pass arrives.** Open your stance toward the passer with your weight already on your back foot. Everything after this is just falling forward.
- **Start your backswing as the pass is released**, not when it arrives. The miss to guard against is being late.
- **Hit the ice just behind the puck**, the same as a slap shot — let the flex of the stick do the work rather than trying to smash it.
- **Aim low far side or for a tip.** A one-timer's value is that the goalie has to move across; you don't need to pick a corner.
- **If the pass is bad, don't force it.** A missed one-timer usually ends up in the neutral zone going the other way. Take the extra touch.

**Goal:** Be a threat in your half of the zone — particularly from the side of the net for rebounds.

**Key considerations:** Crashing the net for rebounds is one of the most effective ways for a winger to score — **coaching consensus rather than a ranked measurement**, and the ice in front of the net does convert several times better than the perimeter, on figures whose own source names no dataset ([Shooting](../technique/shooting.md) owns them and that qualification). **Time your arrival by which of the two jobs you have.** Driving in from the wall or your own circle, arrive as the shot is taken rather than well before it — go early and the defender has time to establish position on you and turn you away from the puck. **If the net front is your assignment it runs the other way**: you are planted there before the release, because a screen you are still walking into is not a screen. That split is coaching craft rather than a measured finding.

#### Net-front and the crease

```facts
Position: At the net front, work just outside the blue paint, off the red line, get in the goalie's eyes, keep your stick free for tips, keep your body out of the crease — head up, and know where the post is
Rule: A no-contact screen "on the crease line or outside the goal crease" keeps the goal in NHL and IIHF Situation 5 E and the PWHL's table; contact, or stopping the goalie moving in the crease, loses it (69.1, PWHL 71.1). Every rulebook or glossary settling it counts that line as crease, so stay off it
Rule: The screen test is NHL and IIHF Rule 69 and PWHL Rule 71 only — USA Hockey, Hockey Canada and CARHA reach no screen set from outside the crease; USA Hockey and CARHA write no goalie-vision provision, and Hockey Canada's two references to the goalie's view reach only a player in the crease
Rule: Hockey Canada 8.5 is not flat — a teammate's goal from outside the crease stands if you stay passive in it, and is disallowed once you play the puck, interfere with the play or obstruct the goalie's view or movements
Rule: Deliberate contact with a goalie, in or out of the crease, goal or not, is a minor or major in NHL and IIHF 69.2, PWHL 71.2, CARHA 66(b) and Hockey Canada 8.5, and charging in USA Hockey, no bare minor (607(d) Note 1); needless contact is penalised too — all six books say a goalie is not "fair game"
Never: Stay in the crease after a defender shoves you in. Get out, avoiding the goalie; under NHL and IIHF 69.1, PWHL 71.1, the goal dies if you impair the goalie from in there, whoever started the contact
Rule: USA Hockey does not run 69.1's premise — 625(b) bars a goal with an attacker in the crease whoever scored, unless the puck preceded them in, the goalie is out of it, or a defender put them there; and its Casebook Situation 10 disallows the goal for a stick in the paint, goalie in the crease
Rule: Two of USA Hockey's crease reliefs are narrower than they read — Casebook Rule 625, Situation 9 disallows the goal and adds an interference minor where you stopped a returning goalie making a play; and a defender pushing you in relieves your position only, not what you do next
Rule: NHL and IIHF 69.4 and PWHL 71.4 protect a goalie who has played the puck outside the crease and is then "prevented from returning" by "the deliberate actions of an attacking player" — a separate foul from 69.1's crease-position rule, turning on intent rather than on contact
Rule: Fouled into the goalie, make every attempt to avoid them — the IIHF's table, Hockey Canada 8.5(a) Interpretation 2 and CARHA 66(b) penalise you if you don't, the NHL's and PWHL's Table 14 price it, and USA Hockey's Casebook (607, Situation 4) spares only a player who clearly made every attempt
Rule: CARHA and Hockey Canada do not cap goalie contact at a minor: a major is discretionary (66(b), 8.5(b)), mandatory if it injures (66(e), 8.5(b)), and ejects (30(a), 8.5(e)), in CARHA with at least a one-game ban (32(d)); Hockey Canada makes attempted or deliberate injury a match (8.5(c))
Rule: Charging a goalie ejects in Hockey Canada 8.5(b) anywhere, and in CARHA 52(b) in the crease or on injury, plus at least a one-game ban (32(d)); NHL, PWHL and IIHF 42 and USA Hockey 607(c) leave it to the referee, but a major injuring face or head ejects (NHL and PWHL 42.5, EIHL Casebook 42)
Rule: All but the IIHF write a match above a goalie charge (NHL and PWHL 42.4, Hockey Canada 7.4(c), CARHA 48(a), USA Hockey 607(e)); if reckless in USA Hockey, plan on one and a likely multi-game ban (602(a)); from behind, IIHF 43.2 has no minor — plan on the major and ejection
Rule: A cross-check is a mandatory major and game misconduct above the shoulders or on injury (CARHA 54(b), 54(c), Hockey Canada 7.6(b), 9.2(b)), on a crease goalie (CARHA) and carelessly to head or neck (USA Hockey 620(b)); if reckless in USA Hockey, plan on a match and a likely multi-game ban (602(a))
```

You are the net-front player more often than anyone, so the working rule: **work just outside the blue paint and off its red edge, get in the goalie's eyes, keep your stick free for tips, and keep your body out of the crease.** A no-contact screen from outside the crease is legal and one of the most valuable things you do. ⚠️ **Take the goalie's eyes, never their movement:** under the NHL, the IIHF and the PWHL a position that stops the goalie moving in the crease can cost the goal even from outside it.

**Keep off the red line itself.** Every book that settles it in its rules or glossary counts the line as crease, though three reference tables allow a goal to a screener planted on it. Off the line is the reading that holds under all of them.

**Leave a goalie their route home.** A goalie who has played the puck outside the crease must be able to get back. In the NHL, IIHF and PWHL, deliberately blocking that path can be a penalty with no contact at all; under USA Hockey, interfering with a goalie trying to get back costs the goal and an interference penalty. Take their eyes and leave them their path.

**Go around the goalie, never through them** — in or out of the crease, goal or no goal. A screen is a place you stand; a charge is a speed you arrive at. Arrive at the net under control, and give ground the moment a goalie leans into you. ⚠️ **Charging a goalie ends your game outright under Hockey Canada, under CARHA in the crease or if it injures, and under USA Hockey if reckless, and can in every other book; under CARHA and USA Hockey, an ejection for it also costs at least your next game.**

**Shoved into the crease, get out — around the goalie, not through them.** Being pushed in is not a licence to stay, and it does not excuse contact. ⚠️ **Make the effort to avoid the goalie, whatever put you there** — every book penalises you for failing to. Make it early: under USA Hockey, a player who tries only once contact is unavoidable is still penalised.

**Under USA Hockey, keep your stick out of the crease as well as your skates.** It is the one book of the six that counts your stick as you, so the tip you are reaching for can cost the goal with both skates outside the line. It also bars a goal with an attacker standing in the crease unless the puck got there first or the goalie is out of it — ⚠️ **and that second exception ends the moment you are what stops the goalie getting back.**

**Make room with your feet, never with your stick across someone.** A cross-check at the top of the crease can be a penalty in every book, whoever you put it on. ⚠️ **Once it rides up toward the head it ends your game in several books, with no intent needed.**

**Never put your stick or your body into anyone's back at the net front.** The skater boxing you out usually has their back to you within a stick-length of the post, and a goalie's back is no different. ⚠️ **Hold your ground, or work the skater from the side.**

**Where body checking is barred, hold the spot you already have and make them move you.** The body shove is out there too, not only the stick: holding your own established position is legal, changing your lane, speed or position to force someone off the puck is not. ⚠️ **In a CARHA adult league, and under Hockey Canada at every level, hold your spot without leaning on anyone** — Hockey Canada makes impeding a player without the puck interference (8.3), checking game or not. In the Women's NIHL, plan no hit at all; in any other British women's competition, ask your league.

**What it costs.**

- **The goal, under the NHL, IIHF and PWHL.** Their reference tables (NHL Table 14 and IIHF Table 14 (16 in 2025/26), Situation 5 E; the PWHL's Table 14) allow the goal where an attacker *"plants himself on the crease line or outside the goal crease, as to obstruct the goalkeeper's vision and impair his ability to defend his goal, and a goal is scored"*. Rule 69.1 (PWHL 71.1) disallows a goal where an attacker's position or contact *"impairs the goalkeeper's ability to move freely within his crease or defend his goal"*, or where the attacker starts deliberate contact, in or out of the crease. 69.1's "defend his goal" names no location, but the tables allow a screen from outside the crease that impairs it, so how far 69.1 reaches outside the crease is a reading, not something either book prints. So a no-contact screen outside the crease keeps the goal; a position that stops the goalkeeper moving freely in the crease can cost it.
  - Pushed in by a defender, you are excused the contact only if you made a reasonable effort to avoid it, and the goal still dies if your position impairs the goalie. The IIHF can stop play for a neutral-zone draw when an attacker in the crease *"does not leave immediately"*. Under 69.4 (PWHL 71.4), when a goalie has played the puck outside the crease, a player whose *"deliberate actions"* keep them from getting back can be penalised, with no contact needed.
- **The crease line.** IIHF Rule 1.7, the Note to USA Hockey 625(b), and the Hockey Canada and CARHA glossaries count the line as crease; the NHL's rule text leaves it unsaid, and the IIHF answers both ways, since its own table allows the goal on the line. No book says which of its layers wins, which is why you stay off it.
- **The goal, under USA Hockey, Hockey Canada and CARHA.** None of the three writes a goalie-vision rule for a screen from outside the crease, but all three take the goal for contact.
  - **USA Hockey 625(b)** bars a goal with an attacker in the crease unless the puck preceded them or the goalie is out of it, and standing there can stop play. Its Casebook disallows the goal for a stick or skates in the crease while the goalie is in it (625, Situation 10), and, where the goalie was out, disallows it and adds an interference penalty if you stop them returning to make a play (Situation 9). Its push-in relief carries no reasonable-effort proviso but covers your position only, not what you do next (617(c)(4)).
  - **Hockey Canada 8.5** lets a teammate's goal from outside the crease stand if you stay passive in it — no playing the puck, interfering, or blocking the goalie's view or movements. Its Interpretation 1 disallows any goal where an attacker starts contact with the goalie, in or out of the crease, unless a foul left them *"unable to avoid"* it.
  - **CARHA 66(b)** disallows a goal where an attacker *"physically interfered with the goalkeeper, prior to or during the scoring of the goal"*.
- **Contact with a goalie.** No book treats a goalie outside the crease as *"fair game"*. NHL and IIHF 69.2 and PWHL 71.2 give a minor or major for deliberate contact *"whether or not the goalkeeper is inside or outside the goal crease, and whether or not a goal is scored"*, and 42.1 and 69.4 call for a penalty for unnecessary contact *"in every case"*. CARHA 66(b) gives a minor for deliberate contact *"whether in or out of the goal crease"*, or a major at the referee's discretion, and its 52(b) Note penalises unnecessary contact as interference or charging. Hockey Canada 8.5 penalises unnecessary contact *"anywhere on the ice"*: a minor (8.5(a)), or a major and game misconduct on the degree of violence (8.5(b)). ⚠️ **USA Hockey splits it by intent** (607(d) Note 1): contact you could not avoid is an interference minor (625(a)(8)); contact you meant is charging, which has no bare minor.
- **Fouled into the goalie.** Hockey Canada (8.5(a) Interpretation 2) and CARHA (66(b)) penalise an attacker who is tripped or fouled and makes no effort to avoid the goalie, in or out of the crease — CARHA for both possession states, and where your team has the puck *"if a goal is scored it shall be disallowed"*. The NHL's, IIHF's and PWHL's tables penalise more-than-incidental contact you made no reasonable effort to avoid. ⚠️ **Under Hockey Canada, being fouled first relieves neither the minor nor, unless you were *unable* to avoid the goalie, the goal.** USA Hockey's Casebook (607, Situation 4) spares only a player who *"clearly made every attempt to avoid the contact with the goalkeeper"*. One *"going hard to the goal"* who tries only once contact is unavoidable takes *"a minor plus misconduct"*; one who accelerates through it, extends the arms or drops the shoulder is where *"the major plus game misconduct, or match penalty option would be the proper call."*
- **Charging a goalie.** ⚠️ **Arrive at the net under control — a charge on a goalie can end your game in every book.** The major and game misconduct is mandatory for the charge itself under Hockey Canada (8.5(b)), and under CARHA in the crease or if it injures (52(b)), with at least a one-game suspension on top (32(d)). Under USA Hockey it is mandatory once the charge is reckless (607(b)), and 602(a) says a match *"shall be assessed"* for reckless endangerment — ⚠️ **so plan on one, and on a likely multi-game suspension.** Five of the six books write a match above the ejection; the IIHF's in-game ceiling is the major and game misconduct. If the charge reaches the goalie's head under Hockey Canada, plan on the harsher of 8.5(b) and head contact (7.6, or 7.7 in Junior and Senior). [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md#what-it-costs-to-touch-the-goalie) sets out every book's ladder.
- **In a CARHA adult league, almost any major ends your game and at least your next one.** Rule 30(a) puts a major off the ice for the rest of the game and Rule 32(d) adds an automatic suspension of at least one game; the exception is an accidental high stick, which still ejects you if it injures (62(b) Note) but falls outside 32(d)'s automatic ban, which excludes *"the Accidental High Stick Rule"*. Rule 66(e) makes injuring an opponent by interference a mandatory major, and 54(c) a mandatory major and game misconduct for cross-checking a goalie in the crease *"or who injures an opponent as a result of a cross-check"*. ⚠️ **So a net-front foul that hurts someone ends your night**, whether it is called as charging, cross-checking or interference. Under Hockey Canada, a major for goaltender interference carries a game misconduct too (8.5(e)).
- **Cross-checking.** All six books write a minor (USA Hockey 609(a), Hockey Canada 9.2(a), CARHA 54(a), NHL and IIHF 59.2, PWHL 60.2) and a major and game misconduct above it, mandatory under USA Hockey 609(b) once it is reckless. ⚠️ **The mandatory tiers need no intent.** CARHA 54(b) sets one for a cross-check above the shoulders *"whether or not injury results"*, and USA Hockey 620(b) for one that *"intentionally or carelessly contacts an opponent in the head, face or neck"*. Hockey Canada sets one for a cross-check that injures (9.2(b)), and for one above the shoulders, injury or not (9.2(b) Interpretation 2; 7.6(b), and 7.7(b) in Junior and Senior); in its minor and female hockey, though, minimal, accidental stick contact with the head drops to at least a minor (7.6).
  - **A match sits above that in five books** — for attempting to injure under NHL 59.4, PWHL 60.4, Hockey Canada 9.2(c) and CARHA 54(d), and for reckless endangerment, which asks no intent, under USA Hockey 609(c), where 602(a) says plan on it, with a likely multi-game suspension. A cross-check to the head of a player who cannot protect themselves is a match under Hockey Canada 7.6(c), or 7.7(c) in Junior and Senior. The IIHF's in-game ceiling is a major and game misconduct, with supplementary discipline under 59.6.
- **From behind.** ⚠️ **Never put your stick or your body into a turned back at the net, a goalie's included — hold your ground or work them from the side.** No book prices one that stays off the head at two minutes alone as checking from behind, and in most it ends your game; into the goal frame on a player who cannot protect themselves, Hockey Canada and CARHA must make it a match, and under USA Hockey, head first into it is a mandatory major and game misconduct (608(b)). *Backchecking* below sets out each book.
  - Under Hockey Canada, ⚠️ **a hit from behind on a goalie takes the harsher of 7.5 and 8.5** — on the head, the harsher of head contact (7.6; 7.7 in Junior and Senior) and 8.5. In the NHL, PWHL and IIHF, Rule 43 writes no minor (43.2), and on the head the harsher of Rules 43 and 48 applies: the NHL and PWHL make it a major and game misconduct (43.3, 43.5), and under the IIHF plan on the same. *(That IIHF Rule 43 reaches a goalie is a reading: Rule 5.1 makes "Player" cover goalies, but its glossary defines a vulnerable position for "[a] skater".)* [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md#checking-from-behind-and-what-the-onus-does-and-does-not-mean) sets out the full ladder.
- **Where body checking is barred.** USA Hockey bars it at 12 & under and below, in all girls' and women's classifications and in non-check adult (604(a)). Its Casebook (604, Situation 2) names the net-front shove: a player who *"makes no attempt to play the puck and instead physically forces the opponent off the puck by changing their skating lane, speed or legally established body position"*. In every USA Hockey game, checking a player *"physically engaged for possession of the puck with one or more other players"* is a minor (640(e)), a major and game misconduct where it recklessly endangers (640(g)), and plan on a match above that, and a likely multi-game suspension (640(h); 602(a)).
- **CARHA and women's hockey.** CARHA 49(a) reaches a player who *"intentionally bodies, pushes, shoves, stands in front of an opponent for the purpose of making contact, and/or does not avert body contact with an opponent"*: a minor or a major, the major mandatory where intentional contact injures, and ending your game and at least your next one (30(a), 32(d)). The Women's NIHL is *"Full ice, non-checking"* at U16 and senior under IHUK's 2026-27 Rules of Competition. Elsewhere, IIHF Rule 101.1 allows body checking in women's hockey only *"when there is a clear intention of playing the puck or attempting to “gain possession” of the puck"*, lets players *"hold their ground"* once they have established their position, and prices stepping or gliding into an opponent at *"at least a minor penalty"*.

Full rules in [Rules Primer](../foundation/rules_primer.md) and [Offensive Zone Play](../systems/offensive_zone_play.md); the same situation from the goalie's side is in [Goaltender](goaltender.md).

## Other Situations

A quick reference for situations outside the zone-by-zone framework above.

### Faceoffs

**In short:** Read the draw and react instantly, and stay still behind your hash marks and off the line, hands to yourself, until the puck lands.

```facts
Position: At the hash marks — one winger on the inside hash facing the slot, one on the outside hash facing the boards
Convention: The winger whose natural side matches the faceoff location usually takes the outside hash, but assignments vary by team, so ask
Action: Read the result of the draw and react instantly — pounce on a loose puck, support the centre, or get to your defensive position
Never: Creep inside the circle or over your hash marks, or lean on an opponent before the drop — that usually costs your centre the dot, and in a non-checking game the lean can cost you a penalty too
Rule: Your stick counts as well as your skates under USA Hockey (Casebook, Rule 613 Situation 8), Hockey Canada 6.2(a) and CARHA 57(a); NHL 76.7(ii), PWHL 78.7(ii) and IIHF 76.7(II) let a blade cross the hash marks if it touches neither your opponent nor their stick
Rule: Physical contact with an opponent before the drop is a faceoff violation in its own right in five books — Hockey Canada 6.2(a) and CARHA 57(a) name it for a winger and eject your centre, NHL 76.7(iii), PWHL 78.7(iii) and IIHF 76.7(III) list it unscoped; USA Hockey 613(d) asks about position
Rule: A second violation on one draw is a bench minor under NHL and IIHF 76.7 and PWHL 78.7; USA Hockey 613(d) puts the minor on the offender, and Hockey Canada 6.2(e) and CARHA 57(d) leave it to the referee — but Hockey Canada makes it automatic if that player then contacts a linesperson clearing the dot
Rule: On a winger's encroachment the IIHF 2025/26 book warned the team and ejected nobody, but its 2026/27 book ejects the draw-taker as the NHL does (IIHF Rule 76.6, both editions)
Rule: In IHUK and BUIHA games the In-House Rules, not the IIHF edition, settle the faceoff-violation question — "the linesperson shall immediately replace the centre for all violations", faceoffs following icings excepted (In-House Rules 2026-27, Section 10, Rule 76)
Rule: The Elite League sits outside the In-House Rules, which never name it, and the EIHL Casebook writes no Rule 76 at all — so an Elite League draw runs IIHF 2026/27 Rule 76.6, which ejects the centre too, and IIHF 76.4, which warns instead on the first violation after an icing
Action: Know in advance whether you are the backup draw taker, and aim for a clean tie-up — but under Hockey Canada 6.2(d) the player who encroached may not step in, so it cannot be you if it was you
Rule: Step in for an ejected centre and you are a "Player taking the face-off", so pre-drop contact is a minor as well — discretionary under NHL and IIHF 76.7 and PWHL 78.7, mandatory under USA Hockey 613(e), Hockey Canada 6.2(c) and CARHA 57(b); helmet-to-helmet contact is named at IIHF 76.7(III)
```

The centre takes the draw; you line up at the hash marks — one winger on the **inside** hash (facing the slot), one on the **outside** hash (facing the boards). The usual default is that the winger whose natural side matches the faceoff location takes the outside hash, but assignments vary by team and situation, so ask. **The nine locations, the full five-player alignments and the set plays off each are in [Faceoffs](../systems/faceoffs.md).** Your job in every one of them is the same: read the result of the draw and react instantly — pounce on a loose puck, support the centre, or get to your defensive position.

**Start with three things about faceoffs that are specifically your responsibility:**

- **Don't encroach.** Creep inside the circle or over your hash marks before the drop and **your centre gets thrown out of the dot** — not you. Your team takes the draw with its second-best option. Stay off the circle line too: Hockey Canada and CARHA count a skate on it. Hold your position — and, in three of the six books, your blade — until the puck hits the ice. ⚠️ **Give the official room to get out of the circle.**
- **Keep your hands, shoulder and head to yourself, and your stick off your opponent's, until the puck lands.** A glove or a shoulder on the winger opposite costs your centre the dot in five books of six, exactly as a skate over the line does, and in a non-checking game the lean can put you in the box. Get your depth from your knees and hips, not by pushing your head out over the dot.
- **Be ready to take the draw.** Centres get tossed several times a game. Know in advance whether you're the backup taker, and aim for a clean tie-up of their stick with yours — never a hand on it. ⚠️ **But if you caused the violation, Hockey Canada bars you from stepping in**, and neither it nor CARHA allows a change unless a penalty makes a team shorthanded, so a teammate already on the ice has to take it. **So ask your centre for a second name, and step aside if the first one is you.**

**What it costs.**

- **Encroaching.** A second violation by one team on the same draw is a bench minor under NHL and IIHF 76.7 and PWHL 78.7; under USA Hockey 613(d) it is a minor on the individual, and under Hockey Canada 6.2(e) and CARHA 57(d) it is at the referee's discretion. ⚠️ **Hockey Canada's discretion ends if the offender then contacts the linesperson** before they have had reasonable time to leave the spot — that minor *"will be assessed"* (6.2(e); 10.1(a)). The IIHF's 2026/27 book ejects the centre for a teammate's encroachment, where 2025/26 warned the team (76.6). Your stick counts as well as your skates under USA Hockey's Casebook (*"This includes all sticks and all skates"*, 613, Situation 8), Hockey Canada 6.2(a) and CARHA 57(a); the NHL (76.7(ii)), the PWHL (78.7(ii)) and the IIHF (76.7(II)) let your blade over the hash marks provided it touches neither your opponent nor their stick. Hockey Canada 6.2(d) makes the player who encroached *"not eligible to take the face-off"*, and CARHA 57(c) allows no substitution.
- **In Britain, plan on losing your centre for it on every draw but the one after an icing.** The IHUK In-House Rules 2026-27 amend Rule 76 so that *"[w]ith the exception of face-offs following icings, the linesperson shall immediately replace the centre for all violations"*, in England, Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland. They never name the Elite League, and the *Motorpoint EIHL Casebook 26-27* writes no Rule 76, so an Elite League draw runs IIHF 2026/27 Rule 76.6, which ejects your centre too, and 76.4, under which the first violation after an icing is a warning. *(Only IIHF Rules 76.3, 76.4, 76.6 and 76.7 have been read in the 2026/27 edition for this document; [Faceoffs](../systems/faceoffs.md) carries the full comparison and the scope of both readings.)*

#### Contact before the drop — including helmet to helmet

**Crossing a line is not the only way to give a faceoff away.** Touching an opponent before the drop is a violation in its own right, wherever your skates are — ⚠️ **and where body contact is barred, it is a penalty too.** Stand still and keep your hands to yourself until the puck lands.

**And the moment you step in for an ejected centre, the rules around you change.** You are now a *"Player taking the face-off"*, and ⚠️ **contact before the drop becomes a penalty as well as a violation.** Get low with your knees and hips, keep your head back from the dot and your hands off your opponent until the puck lands, and **move back the first time you are told** — then neither the penalty nor the edition question below reaches you. [Centre](center.md) owns the stance cue in full.

**And take one point past the penalty sheet.** Two players who both get low the safe way still finish with their heads a few inches apart on every draw, and **no rulebook or handbook read for this document speaks to that** — Situation 76.42 reaches two centres leaning into each other's space, which is a different thing. The 2026/27 changes raise what helmet-to-helmet contact costs you. ⚠️ **They do not make the space over the dot safe**, and nothing here should be read as saying they do.

**What it costs.**

- **Contact as a winger.** Hockey Canada and CARHA name it as yours even though the punishment lands on your centre: where a player other than the draw-taker *"moves off-side, makes physical contact with an opponent or encroaches on the face-off circle prior to the dropping of the puck"*, the draw-taker is ejected (Hockey Canada 6.2(a); CARHA 57(a)).
  - The NHL (76.7(iii)), the PWHL (78.7(iii)) and the IIHF (76.7(III)) list *"[a]ny physical contact with an opponent prior to the puck being dropped"* without scoping it to the centre. USA Hockey is built differently: 613(d) reaches a player who *"fails to maintain their proper position"* — where you stand, not what you touch — and its contact rule (613(e)) reaches only *"any player facing-off"*. Under CARHA, contact also feeds the second-violation minor at 57(d), though not the mandatory minor at 57(b), which reaches only the two in the dot. ⚠️ **Where body contact itself is barred, the lean can put you in the box:** every CARHA game (49(a), *"pushes, shoves"*), USA Hockey's Competitive Contact categories (604(c)), and Hockey Canada's U13-and-below, female and Member-approved divisions (7.3(a), *"bumps, shoves, or pushes"*).
- **Contact once you take the draw.** The minor is at the referee's discretion under the NHL (76.7), the IIHF (76.7, both editions) and the PWHL (78.7), and mandatory under USA Hockey (613(e)), Hockey Canada (6.2(c)) and CARHA (57(b)). **The IIHF is the only book whose faceoff rule names your head:** 76.7(III) makes helmet-to-helmet contact by either draw-taker a violation, its 2026/27 edition has the linesperson *"order the center of the offending team replaced"* where 2025/26 warned first, and 76.7's discretionary minor still reaches it. Its 2026/27 Situation Handbook has the linesperson *"immediately eject the center that is responsible for the helmet to helmet contact"*, or *"eject both centers"* if they cannot tell (76.43), and removes from the faceoff a player who will not move back when told (76.42). *(The handbook writes "center"; on those shifts that is you.)*
  - **Which edition a British linesperson uses is only half written down.** The *EIHL Casebook* defaults to the *"2026 - 2027 IIHF Rulebook and IIHF Situation Handbook"*, so in the Elite League refusing to move back costs you the draw. The In-House Rules name the 2025/26 Rule Book and never mention the Situation Handbook, so outside the Elite League nothing read for this document settles it. But don't read the older warning as the British answer, because the In-House Rules replace the centre for every violation bar a post-icing draw, and helmet-to-helmet is a violation under 76.7(III) in both editions.
- **Under Hockey Canada, head contact on the draw is priced by its Head Contact rule, which *"supersedes any other rules"*.** In minor and female hockey: a minor if accidental, a double minor if intentional, up to a major and game misconduct on violence, mandatory if it injures (7.6). In Junior and Senior: a minimal one is still a minor, called as roughing or another penalty, moderate contact a minor plus a misconduct, and high violence a major and game misconduct (7.7). In either, a match if they could not protect themselves or it was meant to injure. Push your head in on purpose and Rule 7.1 counts it as head-butting — a double minor, and a match with force or if it injures.

### Power Play (your team has the man advantage)

**In short:** From the half-wall, move the puck quickly to whichever option the kill leaves; at the net front, screen without touching the goalie.

```facts
Position: Almost always the half-wall or the net front — the formations are in Special Teams
Targets: From the half-wall you have four options: the point, the bumper, the net front, and the far half-wall through the seam
Technique: Walk down toward the goal line to drag a killer with you, then use the space you just made
Action: From the net front, screen, tip and hunt rebounds — get in the goalie's eyes and keep your body out of the paint and your skates off the red crease line
Rule: Don't initiate contact and don't crowd the goalie. Keep your body out of the crease (NHL and IIHF Rule 69, PWHL Rule 71) — and off the red line around it, which every book settling it in rule text or glossary counts as crease, while the NHL's, PWHL's and IIHF's own tables allow a goal on it
Rule: The don't-crowd-the-goalie test is NHL, PWHL and IIHF only — neither USA Hockey nor Hockey Canada reaches a screen set from outside the crease, and inside it those two differ from each other; Net-front and the crease sets out both
Never: Let a defender's shove carry you into the goalie without trying to avoid them — Hockey Canada 8.5(a) and CARHA 66(b) make that a minor on you, crease or no crease; get out of the paint as well
Convention: The bumper is usually the centre, but plenty of units put a winger there — find out whether that spot is yours
Key: Quick puck movement is what beats a kill — it can cover any single option, but not three in two seconds
```

**The formations are set out in [Special Teams](../systems/special_teams.md).** Wherever they put you, it is almost always one of two spots: the **half-wall** or the **net front**.

Move the puck quickly, look for one-timer setups, and crash the net for rebounds — around the goalie, never into them. Quick puck movement is what beats a penalty kill; a kill can cover any single option, but not three in two seconds.

**From the half-wall you are the primary distributor on your side.** Your four options are the point, the bumper — the player in the slot between the point and the net front — the net front, and the far half-wall through the seam. A kill can take away any one of them but not all four, which is why the half-wall is the best job on a power play. Walk down toward the goal line to drag a killer with you, then use the space you just made.

![](diagram:pp-131)

**From the net front your job is screening, tips and rebounds.** Get in the goalie's eyes; keep your body out of the crease and your skates off its red line, don't initiate contact, and don't crowd them. Contact with a goalie is not priced by the scoreboard: ⚠️ **a deliberate bump costs you the power play you are on, goal or no goal.** If you play under USA Hockey, Hockey Canada or CARHA, read *Net-front and the crease* above, because Rule 69's screen test is not theirs.

One thing worth asking rather than assuming: **the bumper is *usually* the centre, but plenty of units put a winger there**, especially a left-shot winger who can one-time a feed from the right half-wall. Find out whether that spot is yours.

**What it costs.** NHL and IIHF 69.2 and PWHL 71.2 give a minor or a major for deliberate contact with a goalie *"whether or not the goalkeeper is inside or outside the goal crease, and whether or not a goal is scored"*, and the same books' 42.1 and 69.4 (71.4 in the PWHL) call for a penalty for unnecessary contact *"in every case"*. ⚠️ **Under USA Hockey that contact is charging** (607(d) Note 1), which writes no bare minor — a minor plus a misconduct at the least, a mandatory major plus a game misconduct if the charge is reckless (607(b)), and a match and a likely multi-game suspension to plan on (602(a)). ⚠️ **The red line around the crease counts as crease** under IIHF Rule 1.7, the Note to USA Hockey 625(b) and the Hockey Canada and CARHA glossaries, while the NHL's and IIHF's reference tables allow a goal to a player planted on it. Off the line holds either way.

### Penalty Kill (your team is shorthanded)

**In short:** Hold your spot in the shape rather than chase the perimeter, block from close on your feet, and clear it whenever you get it.

```facts
Position: In a box you are one of the two high players covering a point; in a diamond you take a middle spot on a half-wall; in a wedge+1 you are often the "+1"
Action: As the +1, pressure whoever has the puck; when the puck moves the nearest wedge player takes over as the new +1, so you never follow it round
Options: Your route out as the +1 is a coaching choice — straight out from the goal, sweeping across the top of the zone, or roaming to the wall and point; ask which one your team runs
Action: Stay tight, block shots, and force the opposing point into low-percentage shots
Technique: If you block, do it from close and on your feet — square to the shooter, head out of the shot lane, hands behind your body, backs of the gloves out; behind the shin pads is the going-down form
Never: Block from in front of your own goalie's eyes — Defender and Body Contact and Battles cover the technique and the full list of when not to block
Action: Clear the puck whenever you get it — you don't need to be fancy
Rule: A shorthanded team may ice the puck freely under NHL, PWHL and IIHF rules, but the exemption is conditional; USA Hockey Rule 624(b)(1) grants it only from Youth 15-Only, Girls 16U, High School and Adult up
Rule: Under NHL 81.6 and PWHL 83.6 the shorthanded icing exemption survives a running major — the clawback bites only once it expires with nobody on the penalty box to come out, and only until the next stoppage — but it runs on the on-ice count, so a penalty to them taking you to 4-on-4 ends it
Rule: Shorthanded icing is an on-ice count, not a clock — expiry counts back only the player whose penalty ended, so a 5-on-3 kill is still exempt, and a clear released before expiry draws no icing however late it lands (NHL and IIHF 81.6, PWHL 83.6; Hockey Canada 6.7(b) takes the count at the shot)
Never: Chase the puck around the perimeter and pull the shape apart — that is exactly what the power play wants
```

A kill is four skaters, normally two forwards and two defencemen. **The shapes are described in [Special Teams](../systems/special_teams.md);** your job inside each one:

- **Box** — the standard. You are **one of the two high players**, alongside your centre, each responsible for one point.
- **Diamond** — the centre usually takes the apex and you take one of the two middle positions, covering a half-wall.
- **Wedge+1** — you are often the "+1", the single player working outside the triangle. That job is **pressure, not pursuit**: get at whoever has the puck and make every possession uncomfortable. When the puck moves you stop, and the nearest wedge player steps out as the new +1 while you drop into the triangle. Following the puck around the perimeter is how this system is beaten, not how it is played. **The route you take to the puck is your coach's choice, not a law** — published descriptions differ, some sending the +1 straight out from the goal towards the carrier, some sweeping them laterally across the top of the zone above the circles, some letting them roam to the wall and the point. Ask which one you are running.

![](diagram:pk-box)

![](diagram:pk-diamond)

Stay tight and force the opposing point into low-percentage shots. Block **from close, on your feet, square to the shooter, head out of the lane and hands behind your body with the backs of the gloves out** — and not from in front of your own goalie's eyes. **Keep your stick flat on the ice beside you, never out in front:** a puck off an outstretched blade tends to ride up toward your face, which is coaching craft rather than a measured finding. ⚠️ **Tucked in behind your shin pads is the going-down form, not a standing one:** on your feet it folds you forward and puts your head down into the low shot lane. If you do go down, [Defender](defender.md) has that technique; it and [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md) own the rest, and the list of when *not* to block.

**Clear the puck whenever you get it** — you don't need to be fancy. Don't chase the puck around the perimeter and pull the shape apart; that's exactly what the power play is trying to make you do.

**A shorthanded clear is usually exempt from icing, but not everywhere.** The NHL, the IIHF and the PWHL exempt it, conditionally. USA Hockey grants the exemption only *"For all Youth 15-Only and above, Girls 16U and above, High School and Adult classifications only"* (Rule 624(b)(1)), so outside those your clear can still be iced. Every book read here gives the exemption to the side *below* its opponent's on-ice numbers. NHL and IIHF 81.6 and PWHL 83.6 take that count at the instant a penalty expires, so the player whose own penalty ended counts back on even at the box door: on a 5-on-3, the first expiry leaves you four against five, still exempt. A penalty to *them* levels the sides, so you can be iced while a major of yours is still running. A puck *"released from the stick of the player shooting the puck down the ice prior to the penalty expiring"* is not icing however late it lands.

**Two more wrinkles.** NHL 81.6 and PWHL 83.6 take the exemption from a team killing a *major* only at expiry, not during the penalty, and only where the team has left nobody on the penalty box to come out — and only until the next stoppage — so clear it freely through the five minutes. Hockey Canada takes the count *"at the instant the puck is shot"* (6.7(b)), and a team stops being shorthanded once the penalty time expires, *"irrespective of whether or not the penalized player has left the Penalty Bench."* [Special Teams](../systems/special_teams.md) covers it, and [Faceoffs](../systems/faceoffs.md) sets the books side by side, the rec-league one included.

### Icing

**In short:** Dump from past the centre red line, and where your book runs an icing race, race for the puck, not the dot.

```facts
Rule: The dot is a decision deadline, not a finish line — NHL and IIHF 81.1 and PWHL 83.1 judge who would first touch the puck, by the time a skate reaches the dots; USA Hockey 624 runs no race outside sled, CARHA 65 none, Hockey Canada 6.7(e) only in U18AAA, Junior and, at the Member's option, Senior
Action: Carry the extra stride so the puck is past the red line on your stick before you dump it — legal however far it travels, unless your league splits the ice at the blue lines
Options: Or beat the icing by winning the race to the puck yourself — where your book runs a race at all; under automatic icing only a dump that stops short of the goal line beats it
Action: Sprint the first half of the chase — that is where the call is decided
Read: If you are clearly beaten to the dot, ease up; you gain nothing and the end boards are unforgiving. If the goalie comes out for it, pull up
Risk: An icing keeps your line on for a draw in your end — no timeout, no change bar injury and a few others — under the NHL, IIHF and PWHL; USA Hockey bars neither, but its window shuts at each stoppage
Rule: The post-icing no-change bar has exceptions — NHL and IIHF 81.4 and PWHL 83.4 allow a substitution for an injured player, for a pulled goalie going back in, and on a penalty that changes on-ice strength; the IIHF adds a broken skate that its own 82.1 omits
Rule: The USA Hockey change after an icing is ordinary, not free — Rule 204(a) gives each team five seconds, then "no additional player substitutions … until the face-off has been completed legally and play has resumed, except when a penalty … affect[s] the on-ice strength of either or both teams"
Rule: Undue delay in placing your lineup or changing players draws a warning first, and a minor for delay of game on any subsequent delay by the same team (USA Hockey Rule 204(a))
Rule: Hockey Canada is a third answer on the change — 6.7(d) bars it only in U18AAA and Junior, and in Senior at the Member's option, and where it applies 6.7(d)(iii) expressly permits either team's time-out, barring only the icing team from substituting during it
Key: The rule in full — hybrid icing, the wave-off list and "gaining the line" — is in Rules Primer
```

**The rule itself — hybrid icing, the full wave-off list, "gaining the line", and how USA Hockey and rec leagues differ — is in [Rules Primer](../foundation/rules_primer.md).** Icing touches a winger from both ends, which is why it is worth your attention more than most players'.

**You take more dump-ins than anyone, and dumping from behind the red line is the classic accidental icing.** Two fixes: carry the extra stride so the **puck is past the centre red line on your stick** before you let it go, which makes the dump legal however far it travels; or make the dump soft enough that you win the race yourself. ⚠️ **That second one needs a race to exist, and under three of the books here most hockey has none** — USA Hockey outside sled hockey, Hockey Canada outside three categories, and CARHA. A dump that stops short of the goal line works under every one of them, and so does the first fix — unless you play in a CARHA league that divides the ice at the blue lines instead of the red (Rule 65(b), (c)), so ask your league where its line is.

![](diagram:icing-gaining-the-line)

**You are also the one chasing, and under hybrid icing that makes you the reason it gets waved off.** The dot is a **decision deadline, not a finish line**: the linesperson judges who would reach **the puck** first, and must decide *"no later than the instant the first player reaches the end zone face-off dots"* (Rule 81.1). Beating the defender to the dot does not by itself wave anything off — being clearly on course to reach the puck first does. Sprint the first half of the race, because that is where it is decided. ⚠️ **If you are clearly beaten to the dot, ease up:** you gain nothing and the end boards are unforgiving. If the goalie comes out for it, pull up.

**Check that your book runs a race at all.** Hybrid icing is the NHL's, the IIHF's and the PWHL's (83.1). USA Hockey runs none outside sled hockey — its Rule 624 Note completes icing *"the instant the puck has completely crossed the goal line"*. Hockey Canada runs it only in U18AAA, Junior and, at the Member's option, Senior (6.7(e)); everywhere else, minor and female hockey included, icing is automatic (6.7(a)). CARHA's Rule 65 is automatic too. [Rules Primer](../foundation/rules_primer.md) owns the comparison, and [Rink Map](../foundation/rink_map.md) the definition.

**What it costs.**

- **Under the NHL, IIHF and PWHL, when icing is called, your line is stuck on the ice for a defensive-zone draw** — exactly the shift you don't want after a full-speed chase. There is no change and no timeout (NHL and IIHF 81.4 and 87.1; PWHL 83.4 and 89.1), so a British or European league punishes the chase just as hard. ⚠️ **The substitution bar has exceptions, and the one that matters is injury:** 81.4 permits one *"to replace a goalkeeper who had been substituted for an extra attacker, to replace an injured player, or when a penalty has been assessed which affects the on-ice strength of either team."* The IIHF adds a broken skate that its own Rule 82.1 omits, so treat that one as unsettled. None of the exceptions is a tired winger.
- **Other books let your line off, so find out which your league runs.** ⚠️ **USA Hockey bars neither the change nor the time-out, but the window is short:** the visitors get five seconds, then the home team five, then no changes until the faceoff unless a penalty changes on-ice strength (204(a)). Undue delay is a warning and then a delay-of-game minor, and a time-out must be asked for before the change procedure ends (636(f)). Hockey Canada bars the change only in U18AAA, Junior and, at the Member's option, Senior (6.7(d)), and there either team may still take its time-out, though the icing team cannot substitute during it (6.7(d)(iii)). CARHA writes no bar — Rule 19(d) allows each team one change at any stoppage. [Defender](defender.md) and [Rules Primer](../foundation/rules_primer.md) carry the rule.

### Line Changes

```facts
Convention: Wingers change as a group led by the centre — coaching craft rather than a measured finding; Centre sets out what is and is not established
Action: When the centre calls the change, go with them rather than staying out for one more look
Risk: A line that dribbles off one player at a time plays a stretch of every shift a man short in the middle of the ice
Never: Change while the puck is still in your defensive zone and not under your team's control — under a low zone collapse you are the point coverage
Key: Shift length, the safe and unsafe windows to change, and why a bad change concedes goals are in Game Management
```

**Wingers change as a group, and the group is led by the centre.** The centre's route covers more of the ice than yours and they fade first — **coaching craft rather than a measured finding**; [Centre](center.md) sets out what is and is not established here — so when the centre calls it you go with them rather than staying out for one more look. A line that dribbles off one player at a time plays a stretch of every shift a man short in the middle of the ice, which is exactly the cost a change is supposed to avoid.

The one that costs wingers goals: **changing while the puck is still in your defensive zone and not under your team's control.** Under a low zone collapse you are the point coverage. If you leave, the opposing defenceman is unmarked at the blue line with the puck coming to them.

Shift length, the safe and unsafe windows to change, and why a bad change concedes goals are in [Game Management](../systems/game_management.md).

### Backchecking

**In short:** Sprint back, pick up the trailing forward with your stick in the passing lane, and never finish a check on a turned back.

```facts
Action: Sprint back the moment your team loses the puck — often faster than the opposing forwards — and once set, take your defensive-zone role: cover the point, be ready to break out
Convention: Wingers take the outside lanes and the centre the middle under the standard assignment — a coaching choice, not a law
Options: Other backcheck schemes give the middle to the first man back, or pick up man-on-man from the puck outward — find out which, and note somebody always owns the middle
Read: Inside your lane the man to find is the opposing F3, the late-arriving forward — stay between them and the puck
Rule: NHL and IIHF 56.1, PWHL 57.1: beside or ahead of a puckless trailer, same direction, your body may lengthen their path; from behind it may not restrain them, nor may stick or free hand, and position is no licence for illegal checks. USA Hockey: never change lane or speed to play the body (625(a)(4))
Rule: Hockey Canada and CARHA write no right to lengthen a trailer's path — both make impeding a player without the puck interference (8.3; 66(a)), CARHA's Note 2 lets a defender "shadow" them, and its 49(a) penalises failing to avert body contact, with a major mandatory where intentional contact injures
Rule: Checking from behind is a rule of its own, and five of the six books never ask who has the puck; the IIHF may call a hit on a carrier boarding, so plan on its major and game misconduct anyway. The NHL, PWHL and IIHF write no minor for it (43.2), and CARHA 53(a) adds a game misconduct to its minor
Rule: Hockey Canada from behind: 7.5 off the head or head first into boards, from minor and game misconduct; on the head, major and game misconduct: any high cross-check, 7.6 in minor/female if hurt, 7.7 in Jr/Sr if highly violent or head hurt off glass/ice/boards; match if defenceless or meant to injure
Rule: USA Hockey 608(a) starts at minor plus misconduct, but a hit from behind sending a player head first into boards or frame (608(b)) or hard on the boards (Casebook 608 Sit. 1) is a mandatory major and game misconduct or match; reckless anywhere, plan on a match and a likely multi-game ban (602(a))
Rule: A check from behind that drives an opponent into the boards or goal frame unable to protect themselves is a mandatory match — Hockey Canada 7.5(c) and CARHA 53(b), whose Note tells referees to substitute nothing cheaper; Hockey Canada has no such Note
Rule: A check from behind that recklessly endangers a vulnerable or defenseless opponent with excessive force costs a match penalty and a likely multi-game ban even away from the boards — USA Hockey 608(c) names no place at all and assesses it “in all instances”, which is no discretion
Rule: A hit from behind meant to injure is a mandatory match, no place named — Hockey Canada 7.5(c) para. 2, CARHA 48(a), NHL and PWHL 21.1, USA Hockey 602(a), with a likely multi-game ban; CARHA 53(a)’s “could also be assessed” adds a discretionary one. The IIHF has none
Never: Puck-watch. Watch your assignment instead, and arrive at your covering position before the puck does
Never: Finish a backcheck into anyone’s back, carrier or trailer — take the puck, take the ice or angle them off it instead; under Hockey Canada or CARHA, only shadow a trailer
```

When your team loses the puck in the offensive or neutral zone, your job as a winger is to sprint back to your defensive zone — often faster than the opposing forwards. Backchecking well is one of the most important defensive responsibilities of a winger, and one of the most commonly missed — **coaching emphasis rather than a counted ranking**.

- **Take the outside lanes — under the standard assignment.** The assignment assumed throughout gives the centre the middle lane and the two wingers the outside lanes. **That is a coaching choice, not a law, and nobody has counted which scheme is commonest.** Other teams use **first man back takes the middle**, whoever that is — so as a winger you can end up in the middle. Others pick up **man-on-man from the puck outward**. Find out which your team plays; [Defending the Rush](../systems/defending_the_rush.md) sets them out, along with the four named team backchecking systems that decide which assignment applies. Under every variant, somebody owns the middle and nobody assumes it is somebody else.
- **Pick up the trailing forward.** Inside your lane, the player to find is the opposing F3 — the late-arriving forward. **Stay between them and the puck with your feet and your body, and keep your stick in the passing lane rather than on them.** Once you are beside or ahead of them, skating their way, you may lengthen their path with your body — never with your stick or your free hand. From behind, skate to get there rather than restraining them, and remember that the position you win is not a licence for the contact that follows it. ⚠️ **Under Hockey Canada or in a CARHA adult league, only shadow them** and hold the lane you already have. Coaching material names a trailer sneaking in unmarked while three defenders watch the carrier as one of the cheapest goals off a rush; [Defending the Rush](../systems/defending_the_rush.md) and [Neutral Zone Systems](../systems/neutral_zone_systems.md) state it the same way.
- **Watch your assignment, not the puck**, and arrive at your covering position before the puck does. **And when you get there, never finish the backcheck into the back of a carrier skating at their own end boards** — take the puck, take the ice, or angle them off it, whichever book you are under. Once you're set, transition into your normal defensive-zone role: cover the point, be ready to break out.

**Never finish a check on a turned back — if you can see their numbers, pull up and play the puck.** ⚠️ **No book puts a possession condition on checking from behind, and a backchecker arrives from behind by definition.**

**What it costs.**

- **The trailer.** NHL and IIHF 56.1 and PWHL 57.1 let a player beside or ahead of a puckless opponent, skating the same way, lengthen that opponent's path with their body, but bar a player behind the opponent from restraining them with stick, body or free hand, and position is no licence for an otherwise illegal check. USA Hockey's Declaration of Player Safety grants the body-position route *"while maintaining their skating lane"*; changing your lane or speed to take the opponent's body instead of their ice is a restraining foul (625(a)(4)).
  - **Under Hockey Canada and CARHA, only shadow.** Neither book writes a right to lengthen the trailer's path: both make impeding an opponent *"not in possession of the puck"* interference (8.3; 66(a)). CARHA's Note 2 lets defenders *"stand their ground"* or *"shadow"* the attackers, and its 49(a) reaches a player who *"does not avert body contact with an opponent"* — a minor or a major, the major mandatory where intentional contact injures, and ending your game under 30(a). A stick laid across a puckless opponent is interference where you have body position and hooking where you do not. [Playing Without the Puck → Backchecking](../hockey-iq/playing_without_the_puck.md#backchecking) quotes the interference rule in full.
- **Checking from behind.** ⚠️ **If you can see their numbers, pull up — a check from behind can end your game in every book, anywhere on the ice.** The NHL, PWHL and IIHF write no minor for it (43.2); Hockey Canada starts at a minor plus a game misconduct (7.5(a)), CARHA puts a game misconduct on its minor too (53(a)), and USA Hockey makes a head-first drive into the boards or goal frame a mandatory major and game misconduct (608(b)), and its Casebook makes that or a match mandatory for forcefully checking a player standing along the boards with their back to the middle (608, Situation 1). Into the boards or goal frame unable to protect themselves, it is a mandatory match under Hockey Canada 7.5(c) and CARHA 53(b).
  - The boards are not the only route to a match: ⚠️ **USA Hockey 608(c) assesses one *"in all instances"* of reckless, excessive force from behind on a vulnerable or defenceless opponent, anywhere**, with a likely multi-game suspension, and a hit meant to injure is a mandatory match in every book but the IIHF, which has no match penalty. If the hit reaches the head under Hockey Canada, head contact prices it (7.6; 7.7 in Junior and Senior), and driven head first into the boards or goal frame, the harsher of that and 7.5. The major and game misconduct is a must for a cross-check above the shoulders, hurt or not (in minor and female hockey, a deliberate one), for an injury in minor and female hockey, and in Junior and Senior for high violence or a head injury off the glass, ice or boards; the match is a must where they cannot protect themselves or you meant to injure them (7.6(b), (c); 7.7(b), (c)).
  - At a club in England or Wales, outside the Elite League, a game misconduct for checking from behind or a check to the head, or a major for boarding, is at least a one-game ban, in every league or age group you are registered in ([UK and England Rules](../foundation/uk_rules.md#a-first-major-can-end-your-game-and-adults-are-not-exempt)). [Defending the Rush](../systems/defending_the_rush.md#what-hounding-never-means) and [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md#checking-from-behind-and-what-the-onus-does-and-does-not-mean) set out every tier.

## Common Mistakes

- **Drifting into the corner in your own zone** when your team plays a collapse system. Stay level with the opposing defenceman on your side until your team wins the puck, then come down the wall. Drift low and the point opens up, and nobody is there for the breakout.
- **Cheating for offence** — leaving the zone before your team actually has the puck. Hold your coverage until it does, and leave early only on a read you could still skate back from if you are wrong.
- **Forcing a pass through traffic in your own zone.** Know your out before the puck reaches you, and look up ice first. If nothing is on, rim it past a checker sealing you against the boards, or reverse it behind one who has skated past you. A pass picked off in your own zone hurts most.
- **Coasting at the offensive blue line** to avoid offside. Drag a trail skate and enter with speed instead.
- **Dragging the trail skate with your head down.** Keep your head up and your chin off your chest as you cross. Your body, hands and stick are over the line before your skate, into the ice a defenceman standing up at their own line is defending.
- **Encroaching on a faceoff — or leaning on the opponent beside you.** **Stay behind your hash mark and off the line, keep your hands and shoulder off the man beside you and your stick off theirs, get your depth from your knees rather than your head — and have a second name ready for the draw.** A skate over the line usually costs your team its centre, and so does a hand or shoulder on the man beside you in every book but USA Hockey; in a non-checking game it can cost you a minor too, and if you step in, contact before the drop is a penalty as well. ⚠️ **Under Hockey Canada the winger who caused the violation cannot replace the centre**, which is why you need the second name. *Faceoffs* above sets out each book.
- **Drifting into the middle** in the neutral zone. That is the centre's lane, and it collapses your line's spacing. Hold the outside third on your side — unless your team runs an aggressive press, which sends the weak-side winger hunting the puck.
- **Standing still on the half-wall.** A stationary winger is easy to cover. Keep moving, be ready to shoot or feed the centre in the slot, and when you have the space, drive the net — under control, and never into the goalie.
- **Puck-watching on the backcheck**, then arriving into the trailer rather than the ice in front of them. Pick them up early, take the ice in front of them and keep your stick in the passing lane. ⚠️ **If you can see their numbers, pull up** — a hit to their back is checking from behind whether or not they have the puck, and in most books it ends your game. Never hit the trailer: it can be called interference wherever you play, and where checking is barred an illegal check as well. Under Hockey Canada or CARHA, hold only the lane you already have and avoid any contact. *Backchecking* above sets out each book.
- **Arriving at the net front too early on a drive.** **Driving in from the wall or your circle, get there as the shot is taken, under control and never into the goalie** — arrive early and a defender has time to tie you up. **If the net front is your assignment it runs the other way**: be planted there before the release.
- **Treating the red crease line as the safe side of the boundary.** Keep your skates off the red line, not merely out of the paint — standing on it can cost your team the goal. Every book that settles it in rule text or glossary counts the line as crease; only three reference tables say otherwise. ⚠️ **A set of reference tables is not a margin to stand on.** [Rink Map](../foundation/rink_map.md) owns this split.
- **Standing in the goalie's path back to the crease.** Leave a goalie who has played the puck outside the crease their route home, then re-set the screen. Blocking that return can be goaltender interference with no contact at all — under USA Hockey a mandatory penalty, and the goal comes off. See *Net-front and the crease* above.
- **Arriving into the goalie rather than standing in front of them.** Take the ice in front of the net, under control: a screen is a place you stand, a charge is a speed you arrive at. **Deliberate or unnecessary contact with a goalie is a penalty in every book, in or out of the crease, goal or no goal.** Charging one can reach a major plus a game misconduct in every book — mandatory under Hockey Canada, under CARHA in the crease or on an injury (with at least a one-game ban), and under USA Hockey when reckless, with a match and a likely multi-game suspension to plan on there (602(a)). Under Hockey Canada and CARHA any interference that injures a goalie is a mandatory major and ejection, and a hit on a goalie's back is checking from behind: never a minor in the NHL or the PWHL, plan on the major and game misconduct under the IIHF, and under Hockey Canada the harsher of 7.5 and 8.5. *Net-front and the crease* above sets out every book.
- **Cross-checking your way into net-front position.** Hold the spot you have with your feet and your base, never with your stick across someone. The shove becomes a cross-check the moment it goes through your arms — a minor in every book, and ⚠️ **a mandatory major and game misconduct in several once it rides above the shoulders, injures or is reckless, with no intent needed — and under USA Hockey, plan on a match and a likely multi-game suspension for a reckless one.** *Net-front and the crease* above sets out each book.
- **Putting a player who has their back to you into the goal frame.** Never put your stick or your body into their back; hold your ground or work them from the side — and where body checking is barred, or in a CARHA league, work their stick, not their body. ⚠️ **A check from behind is never a minor in the NHL or the PWHL; under the IIHF, plan on the major and game misconduct. Driven into the frame unable to protect themselves, it is a mandatory match penalty under Hockey Canada and CARHA** — and under USA Hockey, if they go in head first, a mandatory major and game misconduct at the least.
- **Shoving for net-front position where body checking is barred.** Hold the spot you already have and make them move you. ⚠️ **There the body shove is out too, not only the stick.** In a CARHA adult league, and under Hockey Canada at every level, hold your spot without leaning on anyone, since Hockey Canada calls impeding a player without the puck interference (8.3); under USA Hockey, never body check a player already battling someone else for the puck, in any game; and in the Women's NIHL, plan no hit at all.
- **Playing the net front with your head down.** Know where the near post is before the shot, take a defender's shove braced — never the goalie's — with a wide base, head up and chin off your chest, and never duck. You feel the defender moving you rather than see them, which is how a player meets a post head first; USA Hockey records that the neck injury *"can occur at walking speed"*. **This applies in non-checking leagues too.**
- **Turning your back to anyone coming at you — and ducking.** Side-on is the target: skates parallel to the boards, forearm and hip to the wall, head up — not on the point of your shoulder. Facing the wall square is the last resort, only when you cannot turn; then get your stick and gloves up and spread on the glass. A turned back makes the contact a hit from behind, and USA Hockey warns that *"Even a light hit from behind could inflict a severe head or neck injury."* Ducking straightens your neck; USA Hockey's *Heads Up Hockey* guide puts *"almost all on-ice cervical spine injuries"* down to a head-down, head-first collision with the boards or a goal post. [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md) covers this.
- **Blocking a shot from in front of your own goalie's eyes.** Block from close, on your feet, square to the shooter, head out of the lane and hands behind your body — or get out of the lane and let your goalie see it. **Tucked in behind your shin pads is the going-down form**; standing, it drops your head into the lane. [Defender](defender.md) and [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md) cover going down, and when not to block.

## Key Takeaways

1. **In the neutral zone, hold your lane.** With the puck, stay wide so the middle opens for your centre; without it, block the passing lane on your side rather than chasing. Under an aggressive press the weak-side winger hunts the puck instead, so find out which your team plays.
2. **In your own end, while they have the puck, stay high — level with the opposing defenceman on your side.** Turn your feet as the battle is being won. The moment your team has it, come down the wall to between the hash marks and the goal line as the first outlet in a swing breakout.
3. **Before your first shift, find out your team's defensive-zone system.** Under a low zone collapse you stay high; under man-on-man you follow your check anywhere, corner included; under a hybrid, only once your check crosses the line your team draws. Either way, a puck squirting up the wall to you is your breakout.
4. **At the offensive blue line, drag a trail skate instead of coasting**, blade touching the ice — a lifted skate is offside under USA Hockey and Hockey Canada. ⚠️ **Keep your head up as you cross.** On a delayed offside everyone tags up before anyone re-enters — if your level has a tag-up at all.
5. **On the forecheck, take the role your arrival gives you.** Whoever gets there first is F1, so if you won the race, don't wait for the centre. As F2, seal the next escape and pin the puck — never wrap the player up against the boards, which is holding. As F3 in a 2-1-2, stay high and cover the middle.
6. **On the wall, decide where the puck goes before you get there.** Puck on the far side, side-on, feet moving — never bump where checking is barred; in CARHA, avoid contact. If nothing is on, rim or reverse it. ⚠️ **Never turn your back to anyone coming at you and never duck** — even a slow hit can injure your head or neck.
7. **Under neutral-zone pressure, dump it on a read, not by default.** Carry it in when support arrives. If not, chip it past a defender stepping up, soft-dump it when you'll win the race, or rim it when their defence collapses. Chase on an angle that cuts off the retriever's escape, and pull up if the goalie comes out for it.
8. **At the net front, take the goalie's eyes, never their path.** Screen from outside the crease; shoved in, get out around them. ⚠️ **Never go into the goalie, in or out of the crease.** A charge can end your game in every book, and must under Hockey Canada, under CARHA in the crease or if it injures, and under USA Hockey if reckless.
9. **Crash the net on every shot your job sends you to.** From the wall, arrive with the shot; at the net front, be planted before it. Know where the near post is; take a defender's shove braced, never the goalie's. ⚠️ **Head up, never duck** — a head-down hit on a post can injure your neck, even at walking speed.
10. **On a turnover, sprint back through your lane and pick up the late forward, not the puck.** Stay between them and the puck, stick in the lane; under Hockey Canada or CARHA, only shadow them. ⚠️ **If you see their numbers, pull up** — a hit to their back is checking from behind and ends your game in most books.
11. **On a draw, stay behind your hash mark and off the line, hands and stick to yourself, until the puck lands.** A skate over the line, or in five of six books a hand on your opponent, usually costs your **centre** the dot. ⚠️ **Under Hockey Canada, if you caused it you cannot take the draw**, so have another taker ready.
12. **On the power play you are usually at the half-wall or the net front.** From the half-wall, walk down to drag a killer with you, then use the space: point, bumper, net front or far seam. From the net front, screen, tip and hunt rebounds, skates off the crease line. Move it quickly, and ask whether the bumper is yours.
13. **On the kill, shrink the ice rather than chase it.** As the +1 in a wedge, pressure the puck, then drop back when it moves. Block from close, on your feet, head out of the lane, never in front of your goalie's eyes. Clear it whenever you get it — though not every level exempts that clear from icing.
14. **In the offensive zone, shoot when the look is yours, and never stand still.** With a teammate at the net front, shoot rather than make the extra pass — unless a teammate has a clear look at an open side. In the corner under pressure, protect the puck and wait for support; look up before passing to the point.
15. **Before you dump it, carry the puck past the centre red line on your stick** — then it cannot be iced, unless your league divides the ice at the blue lines. Chasing, sprint the first half; ease up if clearly beaten, and pull up if the goalie comes out for it. Ask whether your league runs an icing race.
16. **Change with your centre when they call it, and never while the puck is in your own zone and not under your team's control** — under a low zone collapse you are the point coverage, and if you leave, their defenceman is unmarked at the blue line.
