## Key focus

**Watch for the habit that served you in your old position — it is the one that betrays you in the new one.** A winger's lane discipline becomes a centre's passivity; a defenceman's caution becomes a forward's reluctance to attack. Name the one you are fighting before the shift, then do the thing that feels wrong.

**Moving to centre, go to the puck and get underneath it.** Support low in the corner when the puck is there, and come back to the middle when it leaves. On the breakout, swing low inside the dots so your defenceman has a pass through the middle. That is the conventional breakout — ask which one your team runs.

**Moving to the wing, hold the outside third of the ice and let the middle go.** Drifting to the middle collapses your line's spacing. In your own end, stand between the defenceman on your side and the slot — or, if your team plays man-on-man, follow your check.

**Moving to defence, be goal side of your attacker, and never move the puck across the front of your own net.** Take the middle away and stay inside the carrier. Send the puck to your partner behind the net — or high above the circles, if that is your team's route.

**Moving up to forward, attack space instead of denying it.** Get to open ice late and at speed, before the play is obviously safe — by then the space has closed. If you are first to the puck on the forecheck, you are F1: go.

**Whatever the switch, ask the system before your first shift — and when you are lost, go to the safe spot.** Goal side of the puck as a defenceman, the high slot as a centre in your own end, your lane as a winger (in your own end, between their point man and the slot). Read the play from there rather than chasing.

## Overview

Almost every player who sticks with hockey gets moved at some point. This document is about what you do differently when it happens.

**More comes with you than you expect.** Skating, scanning, puck protection, reading pressure and the rules all transfer. What you lose is the map: where to look first, where to drift, and who you pick up. For a few weeks you will be thinking about things you used to do without thinking, and thinking is slow. It passes.

**The hard part is what you have to stop doing.** Your old instincts are automatic, so they surface under pressure — and several of them are exactly wrong in the new job. [The habits that actively work against you](#the-habits-that-actively-work-against-you) sets them out. The fix coaches point to is narrow: attention on the one reflex you are fighting, not general effort.

**Between wing and centre, the change is where you stand without the puck.** A centre follows the puck low and swings low on the breakout; a winger holds a lane and one opponent. This document assumes a **low zone collapse** in your own end and the conventional swing breakout, and says what changes under other systems. Those are your coach's choice, not laws of hockey, so ask which ones your team plays.

**Between forward and defence, the change is intent.** A defenceman weighs a decision by what it costs when it goes wrong — hence *goal side* and *never across the front of your own net*. A forward weighs it by the chance that never happens if you wait. The one part that is not intent is backward skating: a defenceman who cannot skate backwards fast enough has skating failures scored as bad reads, and that takes longer to fix.

**The off wing and the net are different again.** The off wing puts the puck on your inside hand — good entering the offensive zone, harder on your own wall, where you take the pass open to the ice. Goaltending is a different sport that shares a rink.

Two things do not bend with any system. Meeting the boards, never turn your back to anyone coming at you and never duck. At the faceoff dot, never push your head out over it. Almost nothing else here has been measured — there is no published evidence base for how players switch positions, so read it as what experienced coaches do.

## What transfers, whatever the switch

Start here, because it is more than new movers expect and it is the reason a switch is rarely a disaster.

- **Skating.** Every stride, edge, crossover, stop and tight turn you own comes with you. The single exception is backward skating and pivoting, which is a genuine technical barrier and gets its own treatment under *Forward → defence* below. Everything else is portable. [Skating](../technique/skating.md).
- **Scanning.** Turning your head to take a picture of the ice before the puck arrives is the same skill in every position — only the things worth looking for change. This is one of the few areas of hockey with real measurement behind it: Berg et al. (2025) coded 2,545 puck receptions by 88 SHL and SDHL professionals, and [Scanning and Anticipation](../hockey-iq/scanning_and_anticipation.md) covers those figures and their qualifications. Do not restate them from memory. Read them there.
- **Puck protection.** Body between the checker and the puck, wide base, feet moving. A defenceman shielding a puck behind their own net and a winger shielding one on the **half-wall** — the boards level with the faceoff dot, between the corner and the point — are doing the same thing. [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md).
- **Reading pressure.** Knowing whether a checker has sealed you against the boards (rim) or over-committed and skated past you (reverse) is a read, not a position. All three skater documents teach the same distinction because it is the same distinction.
- **Rules knowledge.** Offside, icing, faceoff violations, the trapezoid, goaltender interference. Your new position exposes you to a different subset of them, not to different rules.
- **The framework itself.** [Centre](center.md), [Winger](winger.md) and [Defender](defender.md) are built on one grid: three zones, and inside each, four situations — carrying in free space, carrying under pressure, pressuring an opponent, and playing without the puck. **You already know the questions. You are learning twelve new answers, not a new sport.** Reading your new position document with that grid in mind is the fastest orientation available.

### What does not transfer: the map in your head

```facts
Key: What you lose is a set of location reflexes — where to look first, where to drift, who you pick up when the puck goes to a particular place
Key: Location reflexes are position-specific and took a season to build
Risk: For the first few weeks you will be thinking about things you used to do without thinking, and thinking is slow
Mindset: The slowness of those first few weeks is normal, and it passes
```

What you actually lose is a set of location reflexes — where to look first, where to drift when nothing is happening, who you are responsible for when the puck goes to a particular place. Those took a season to build and they are position-specific. For the first few weeks in a new spot you will be **thinking** about things you used to do without thinking, and thinking is slow. That is normal and it passes.

### When you are lost: go to the safe spot

```facts
Action: When you do not know where to be, go to your position's safe spot first and read the play from there
Position: Defenceman — between the puck and your net, at the goalmouth if your partner is already on the puck
Position: Centre in your own end — the high slot, down into the corner only to support your defenceman when the puck goes in there
Position: Winger — your lane, and in your own end level with their defenceman on your side, between them and the slot
Convention: Those are the low-zone-collapse and three-lane versions; under man-on-man the safe spot is goal side of your check, so ask which your team plays
```

For the first few weeks you will lose the play — the puck goes somewhere your old map never sent you, and the instinct is to freeze or to chase it. **The fix is a default: when you do not know where to be, go to the spot that costs your team least, and read the play from there.** As a defenceman that is between the puck and your net, at the goalmouth if your partner is already on the puck. As a centre in your own end it is the high slot, the middle of the ice in front of your net, from the level of the faceoff dots up to the tops of the circles, going down into the corner only to support your defenceman when the puck goes in there. As a winger it is your lane, and in your own end level with the opposing defenceman on your side — their point — between them and the slot. Those are the defaults under a low zone collapse with three forwards in three lanes; under man-on-man the safe spot is goal side of your check, wherever they go. **Ask your bench which you are playing.** Arriving at the safe spot a beat late costs your team little; chasing the puck into somebody else's space is how an opponent ends up alone.

## Wing → centre

**In short:** Ask which coverage and breakout your team plays, learn faceoffs, and stop standing on the wall where a winger stands.

```facts
Position: Under a low zone collapse your defensive-zone responsibility moves with the puck — low to the corner when it is low and strong-side, back to the high slot when it is not
Convention: Zone, man-on-man and hybrid are coaching choices, not laws. Ask your coach which one your team plays before your first shift
Action: Swing low on the breakout — inside the faceoff dots and below the hash marks, so your defenceman has a middle-lane option
Never: Stand where a winger stands, out on the wall, where one opponent can cover two of you
Convention: Swinging low belongs to the conventional swing breakout specifically — a centre fly or a flat/spread breakout wants you somewhere else, so find out which you are in
Action: Start faceoff work with stance, grip and the defensive-zone draw — the NHL win-rate spread among regular centres is too wide to be luck, so it responds to work; Faceoffs has the figure and its limits
Action: Call the line change, and call it earlier than you used to
Key: A centre works more of the ice — three zones, full width — not more distance; Centre covers that claim and the measurement behind it
```

The most common switch in hockey, and the biggest step up in responsibility short of changing to defence.

**What is genuinely new**

- **You are responsible in all three zones instead of one lane.** As a winger your defensive-zone job had a home — high on your side, on the opposing point. The centre's job has no home; **under a low zone collapse, the coverage assumed throughout, your responsibility moves with the puck**: down to help in the corner when the puck is low and strong-side, back to the high slot when it is not. Under **man-on-man** you track an assigned opponent instead, and under a **hybrid** it is man-on-man below the dots and zone above. These are coaching choices, not laws of hockey, and the job is materially different between them. **Ask your coach which one your team plays before your first shift** — that single question is the highest-value thing a new centre can do. [Defensive Zone Coverage](../systems/defensive_zone_coverage.md) is the authority; [Centre](center.md) describes the job inside each.
- **Faceoffs, which are a whole new skill.** You have almost certainly never taken one seriously. The good news is that it is learnable, and there is evidence for that rather than just encouragement: **among the 109 centres who took at least 500 draws in the NHL in 2025-26, win rates ran from 62.1% down to 39.7% — a 22-point spread on a repeatable, isolated, closed-skill action.** That figure is **computed from the NHL's official skater statistics**, and [Faceoffs](../systems/faceoffs.md) sets it out with its limits. Nothing that wide is luck, which means it responds to work. Start with stance, grip and the defensive-zone draw; the technique, the nine locations and the five-player alignments are all in [Faceoffs](../systems/faceoffs.md). **And one rule at the dot is about your head rather than about winning the draw; the subsection at the end of this section sets it out.**
- **Low support in your own end.** This is the habit inversion that catches wingers hardest, and it is set out below.
- **The conditioning step-up.** Coaches will tell you the centre covers the most ice and fades first. **Half of that has been measured, and it goes the other way.** Lignell and colleagues tracked 36 NHL players through a game and found that *"defensemen (D) covered **29% more** (p ≤ 0.05) skating in total than forwards (F) and were on the ice **47% longer**. However, F performed **54% more** (p ≤ 0.05) high-intensity skating per minute than defensemen."* Centre against winger is not measured anywhere — the research splits players only into forwards and defence — so that half is coaching craft. [Centre](center.md) states the defensible version: a centre works more *of* the ice, not more distance. Shift length, and the Lignell citation, are in [Conditioning and Recovery](../off-the-ice/conditioning_and_recovery.md). In practice: as a centre you call the line change, so call it earlier than you used to.

**First thing to work on:** the swing low. In the conventional swing breakout the centre gets **inside the faceoff dots and below the hash marks** — the short lines outside each end-zone circle, roughly level with the dot — so the defenceman has a middle-lane option. The dot is 20 ft out from the goal line and the blue line 64 ft, so "below the hash marks" is lower than it sounds. The commonest new-centre error is standing out on the wall where a winger stands, where one opponent can cover two of you.

**That is the conventional swing breakout specifically.** Under a **centre fly** you leave early up the middle and the weak-side winger drops in to take low support. Under a **flat or spread breakout** the wingers hold higher and the exit uses longer passes. Find out which you are in — [Breakouts](../systems/breakouts.md) is the authority and [Centre](center.md) sets out what getting it wrong costs.

![](diagram:centre-swing-low)

**What to stop worrying about:** scoring. Your value as a new centre is almost entirely in being where you are supposed to be, taking the draw, and being available under the puck. The offence comes later and it comes from the middle of the ice, which you are now standing in.

### Helmet-to-helmet contact before the drop

**In short:** Get low with your knees and hips, not your head, and move back at once when the linesperson says so.

```facts
Never: Push your head out over the dot to get low — helmet-to-helmet contact before the drop is a violation (IIHF 76.7(III)); its 2026/27 handbook ejects the offending centre from the draw (Situation 76.43)
Rule: The 2026/27 IIHF edition deleted the first warning the 2025/26 edition gave that contact, and the 2026/27 Situation Handbook has the linesperson "immediately eject the center that is responsible" where the 2025/26 one said warn
Rule: Helmet-to-helmet contact before the drop is physical contact as well, so IIHF 76.7's own discretionary minor is available in both editions — and where the 2025/26 rule had the centre warned, the 2026/27 rule has the linesperson "order the center of the offending team replaced"
Rule: Outside the Elite League, Britain's IHUK In-House Rules 2026-27 amend Rule 76 so the linesperson replaces the centre for every faceoff violation bar the draw after an icing, though their Introduction names the 2025/26 IIHF edition
Rule: The officials police the space over the dot before any contact happens — IIHF Situation 76.42, in both editions, has the linesperson break up two centres whose heads are "almost ear to ear" and instruct both to move back
Key: Two safe stances still finish with heads inches apart, and that residue — as against leaning in over the dot — is what no rulebook or handbook read for this document speaks to
```

Getting low is right — you react faster and you cannot be pushed off the dot — but the depth comes from your knees and hips, not from your head. [Centre](center.md) has the stance cue. ⚠️ **If the linesperson tells you to move back, move back at once** — the officials police the space over the dot before anything touches.

**What it costs.** **IIHF Rule 76.7(III)** makes helmet-to-helmet contact before the drop a faceoff violation, in both the 2025/26 and 2026/27 editions. The 2026/27 edition deleted the first warning the older one gave and has the linesperson *"order the center of the offending team replaced"*. **The referee can add a minor:** 76.7 lets the referee *"at their discretion impose a minor penalty or penalties on the Player(s) whose action(s) caused the 'physical contact'"*, in both editions and in the NHL's words. Do not plan on the contact being free. The **IIHF Situation Handbook** moved the same way: at **Situation 76.43** the 2026/27 handbook has the linesperson *"immediately eject the center that is responsible for the helmet to helmet contact"*, or *"eject both centers"* if they cannot tell, where the 2025/26 handbook said *"warn"*.

**And the handbook reaches the stance before any contact happens.** At **Situation 76.42**, in both editions, two centres who *"lean in over the face-off dot such that their heads are side by side, almost ear to ear"* are told: *"This should not be allowed. The Linesperson should instruct both players to move back."* ⚠️ **One who will not is warned under the 2025/26 handbook and *"removed from the face-off"* under the 2026/27 one.**

🇬🇧 **In Britain, do not take the older edition's warning as your answer**, even though the IHUK In-House Rules' Introduction names that edition. The **IHUK In-House Rules 2026-27** amend Rule 76: *"[w]ith the exception of face-offs following icings, the linesperson shall immediately replace the centre for all violations"* — and helmet-to-helmet contact is a faceoff violation in both editions. The Elite League, which those rules do not reach, plays the 2026/27 edition, which replaces the centre too. [Faceoffs](../systems/faceoffs.md) carries every book and the British position.

**And take one point past the penalty sheet.** Two players who both get low the safe way still finish with their heads a few inches apart on every draw, and ⚠️ **no rulebook or handbook read for this document speaks to that residue** — Situation 76.42 reaches two centres leaning into each other's space, not the inches a safe stance cannot avoid. What the rules charge for contact does not make the space over the dot safe.

## Centre → wing

**In short:** Hold the outside lane, let go of organising everyone, and stand still on the hash marks until the puck lands.

```facts
Position: Your lane is the outside third of the ice on your side, running the length of the rink
Never: Drift into the middle because that is where the decisions are — it collapses your line's spacing every time
Convention: In your own end, between the opposing defenceman on your side and the slot, denying the shot and the seam at once — that is the low zone collapse version, and under man-on-man you follow your check wherever they go, corner included
Convention: Under the conventional swing breakout, leave the point the moment your team wins the puck and come down the wall to the outlet spot between the hash marks and the goal line
Convention: A flat or spread structure holds the winger higher toward the blue line instead, and a stretch or flying-winger structure sends one winger up ice entirely, so find out which one your team plays
Never: Direct traffic from the wall — the rotations are no longer yours to manage, and chasing them takes you out of your lane
Action: Stand still on the hash marks and keep your hands to yourself until the puck lands — the habit is the same in every book
Rule: On the hash marks you can give a draw away without touching the puck: encroach and your centre is ejected from the dot, not you — NHL 76.6, PWHL 78.6, IIHF 76.6 (2026/27), Hockey Canada 6.2(a), CARHA 57(a), USA Hockey 613(d)
Rule: Under the IHUK In-House Rules 2026-27, which do not reach the Elite League, Rule 76 is amended so the linesperson replaces the centre for every faceoff violation except the draw after an icing — IIHF 2025/26, which they name, gave only a warning at its 76.6
Rule: Touching an opponent before the drop also costs your team the draw in five rulebooks of six — Hockey Canada 6.2(a) and CARHA 57(a) name it for a player off the dot, NHL 76.7(iii), PWHL 78.7(iii) and IIHF 76.7(III) list it unscoped; USA Hockey 613(d) asks about position
Rule: In all six rulebooks a hand or stick that impedes the player opposite you before the drop can still be called as interference — NHL and IIHF 56.2, PWHL 57.2, USA Hockey 625 and Casebook Standard of Play Situation 12, Hockey Canada 8.3, CARHA 66(a)
Rule: Line up onside, or your centre is replaced for the draw — NHL 76.3(v), PWHL 78.3(v), IIHF 76.3(V) 2026/27; Hockey Canada 6.2(a) wants every other player "on-side, relative to the face-off spot" and ejects the centre if one moves offside, as CARHA 57(a) does
Key: You will touch the puck less, though across a whole game the difference is smaller than it feels — the measured share, with its qualifications, is in Playing Without the Puck
```

Usually experienced as a demotion. Usually isn't — coaches move centres to the wing to get a defensively reliable player onto a line that needs one, to put a good shot in a shooting position, to reduce a young player's load, or simply because they have four centres and three of them have to play somewhere.

**What is genuinely new**

- **Lane discipline, which is a spacing instruction and not a personality one.** Your lane is the outside third of the ice on your side, running the length of the rink. Three forwards in three lanes stretch a defence across the whole width of the rink; three forwards in one lane can be covered by two defenders. A former centre's reflex is to drift toward the middle because that is where the decisions are — and that reflex collapses the line's spacing every time. [Winger](winger.md).
- **The point-coverage job.** In your own end you cover the opposing defenceman on your side, between them and the slot, denying the shot and the seam at once. **That is the low zone collapse job; under man-on-man you follow your check wherever they go, corner included.** Watch an overloaded phrase: **"stay high" means two different things about twenty feet apart** — covering the opposing point while they have the puck, and holding near the blue line in a flat or spread breakout. The first sense **expires**. In the conventional swing breakout, the moment your team wins the puck you come down the wall to the outlet spot between the hash marks and the goal line; a winger who never leaves the point is as wrong as one who leaves early. **That trip is a system, not a law** — a **flat or spread** structure holds you higher, and a **stretch or flying-winger** structure sends one winger up ice. Ask which yours plays: [Breakouts](../systems/breakouts.md) is the authority, and [Winger](winger.md) sets out both ends of the move.
- **Much less puck time, and much more time deciding without it.** You had the puck more as a centre because the structure fed it to you. Across the whole game the difference is smaller than it feels: NHL skaters hold the puck for roughly **3.3% to 4.2% of their ice time at 5-on-5**, from a 2025 University of Waterloo study of 1,194 games. That is **5-on-5 only, normalised per 20 minutes of ice time**, and [Playing Without the Puck](../hockey-iq/playing_without_the_puck.md) owns it with all its qualifications. What changes for you is not the quantity so much as the shape: fewer touches, arriving in a narrower band of ice, more of them at speed on the wall.
- **A faceoff job you have never had: standing still on the hash marks.** **Stand still on your own side of the spot, outside the circle, and keep your hands to yourself until the puck lands.** You have spent your hockey life in the dot, which is always onside. Off it, you can lose a draw before it starts without touching the puck, and the cost lands on your centre. ⚠️ **Three things can do it: lining up offside, touching the player opposite, and moving into the circle early.** What each book charges is at the end of this section, and [Winger](winger.md) is the authority.

That point-coverage job — high on your side while they have the puck, and, in the conventional swing breakout, down the wall the moment you win it — is drawn in [Winger](winger.md).

![](diagram:winger-high-then-down-the-wall)

**What to stop worrying about:** organising everybody. You have spent your hockey life being the connective player, and the temptation is to keep directing traffic from the wall. Communication is still everyone's job — call what you can see — but the rotations are no longer yours to manage, and chasing them is how a winger ends up out of their lane.

**What it costs at the dot.** **Moving into the circle early gets your centre ejected from the draw** under the NHL (**76.6**), the PWHL (**78.6**), the 2026/27 IIHF book (**76.6**), Hockey Canada (**6.2(a)**) and CARHA (**57(a)**); the 2025/26 IIHF edition gave only a warning. USA Hockey's clause for a player off the dot asks instead whether the player *"fails to maintain their proper position"* (**613(d)**).

**Lining up offside does it too.** Hockey Canada wants every other player *"on-side, relative to the face-off spot"* and ejects the centre if one moves offside (**6.2(a)**), as CARHA does (**57(a)**). The NHL (**76.3(v)**), the PWHL (**78.3(v)**) and the 2026/27 IIHF book (**76.3(V)**) have the offending centre replaced; the 2025/26 IIHF edition gives a warning. Arrive late and end up offside, and those three books warn once, then give a bench minor, without ejecting your centre (**NHL 76.7**, **PWHL 78.7**, **IIHF 76.7**).

**Touching an opponent before the drop** costs the draw under Hockey Canada and CARHA, which name it for a player off the dot, and the NHL (**76.7(iii)**), PWHL (**78.7(iii)**) and IIHF (**76.7(III)**) list it as a faceoff violation without limiting it to the centres. ⚠️ **USA Hockey's contact rule reaches only the two players in the dot (613(e)), but that does not free your hands:** a hand or stick that impedes the player opposite can be called as interference in every book (USA Hockey **625**, the PWHL's **57.2**).

🇬🇧 **Under Britain's IHUK In-House Rules 2026-27, plan on your centre being replaced for every faceoff violation bar the draw after an icing** — stricter than either IIHF edition, including the 2025/26 one their Introduction names. They do not reach the Elite League, which plays the 2026/27 IIHF Rule 76.

### Wall play, and the one thing that overrides it

```facts
Key: Winning a 50-50 puck against the boards is the defining physical skill of a winger — a coaching ranking rather than a measured one — and it is not something a centre gets much practice at
Technique: On the wall, puck on the far side of your body from the checker, side-on, backside into them, feet wide, and know where it is going before you arrive
Never: Turn your back to anyone coming at you. USA Hockey warns: "Even a light hit from behind could inflict a severe head or neck injury". Skates parallel to the wall
Never: Duck. Ducking puts your head into the boards first, and USA Hockey warns that the neck injury from that head-down collision can occur at walking speed. Head up, chin off your chest
```

**First thing to work on:** wall play. Winning a 50-50 puck against the boards is the defining physical skill of a winger — **a coaching ranking rather than a measured one** — and a centre rarely practises it. Puck on the far side of your body from the checker, side-on with your hip and backside into them, feet wide, and know where the puck is going before you arrive.

**One thing overrides all of it: never turn your back to anyone coming at you, never duck, and never take the wall on the point of your shoulder.** Those guard against three different injuries, at any speed. A turned back makes the contact a hit from behind, and USA Hockey warns that *"Even a light hit from behind could inflict a severe head or neck injury"*. A chin tucked to your chest puts your head into the boards first, and USA Hockey records that the neck injury from that can happen at walking speed: *"[a] player doesn't have to be going at full speed for this to happen"*. Skates parallel to the wall, forearm and hip to it, head up and chin off your chest — not the point of your shoulder, and not the whole shoulder if you can help it. Already facing the wall and cannot turn? Spread the load with your stick and gloves up on the glass — damage limitation, not a position to aim for. [Body Contact and Battles → Receiving a Hit Safely](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md) covers it.

## Forward → defence

The largest change available in hockey without putting on pads. Two of the three things that change are attitudinal and can be fixed in a fortnight. The third is physical and cannot.

### The technical barrier is real: backward skating and pivots

```facts
Key: Gap control is entirely a skating problem — you cannot hold a tight gap unless you travel backwards nearly as fast as they travel forwards
Risk: Too big a gap and you get walked into the zone; pivot early and you are beaten by the first change of direction
Key: A gap that is too big and a pivot that is too early are both skating failures being scored as decision failures
Technique: Backward C-cut — one foot at a time to full extension, full weight onto the pushing foot, recovery back underneath you
Never: Push weakly with both feet at once, which is the slow way most beginners default to
Action: Drill backward crossovers, where backward speed actually comes from, and pivots in both directions
Convention: Pivoting toward the boards is a coaching default, not a law — some coaches want a middle pivot on specific reads, so ask yours and be competent both ways
Convention: Skating timeframes are coaching-provider estimates rather than measurements — weeks for edge feel, months for stopping both ways, a season for a rebuilt stride
```

Be honest with yourself about this, because it is the part most conversion stories skip. **Gap control — the distance you hold between yourself and an oncoming puck carrier — is entirely a skating problem.** You cannot hold a tight gap against a forward with speed unless you can travel backwards nearly as fast as they travel forwards. A defenceman who can't will do one of two things: leave a huge gap and get walked into the zone, or pivot early to skate forwards and get beaten by the first change of direction. As [Skating](../technique/skating.md) puts it: **both of those are skating failures being scored as decision failures.**

So the first thing a converting forward needs is not tactical. It is:

- **The backward C-cut stride** — one foot at a time to full extension, full weight transferred onto the pushing foot, recovery back underneath you. Pushing weakly with both feet at once is the slow way most beginners default to.
- **Backward crossovers**, which are where backward speed actually comes from.
- **Pivots in both directions**, drilled deliberately. The standard coaching instruction is to **pivot toward the boards**, so that when the pivot goes wrong you are beaten toward the wall rather than into your own slot. But [Skating → Which way to pivot](../technique/skating.md#which-way-to-pivot--a-coaching-default-not-a-law) is explicit that this is a **coaching default, not a law**, and some coaches want a middle pivot on specific reads. Ask yours, and be competent both ways, because the correct pivot is decided by where the attacker is and not by which way you happen to be good.

**Timeframes for skating changes are coaching-provider estimates rather than measurements**, and [Skating](../technique/skating.md) says so where it gives them: weeks for noticeably better knee bend and edge feel, a few months for competent stopping and turning both ways, a season for a genuinely rebuilt stride or a weak side brought up to match the strong. Read them as experienced guesses.

### "Force them outside" and "getting beaten wide" are not the same thing

**In short:** Shut the inside lane and stay between the attacker and the net; steer with the route you skate, not your body.

```facts
Technique: Angle your body so the inside lane is shut and the only route runs toward the boards, where the wall becomes a second defender — take the angle, not the man
Rule: Closing an angled gap is legal competitive contact (USA Hockey 604(c) Note). Riding a carrier into the wall is a body check, barred at 12U and below, all Girls'/Women's and non-check Adult classes (604(a)), and at U13 and below, female hockey and any Member-approved division (Hockey Canada 7.3(a))
Rule: Hockey Canada's Rule 7.3 preamble makes it body checking to leave "their skating lane to make contact" or to extend "an arm, shoulder, or hip after angling an opponent"; steering with your route stays legal only while neither player bumps, pushes or shoves (Interpretation 1 to 7.3(a))
Rule: Never angle into contact: CARHA 49(a) prices standing in front of an opponent to make contact, or failing to "avert body contact", at a minor or an ejecting major (30(a)), mandatory if intentional contact injures; into the boards a minor doubles, two of three penalties that eject (49(b), 32(a))
Rule: In the Women's NIHL plan no hit: it plays "non-checking", so read it as at least as tight as IIHF 101.1, which bars using the boards to push, pin or eliminate an opponent and makes any "step or glide into" her at least a minor; elsewhere, ask your league before you hit anyone
Rule: Where checking IS legal the ride has a limit, and the onus is on the hitter: USA Hockey 603 asks no defencelessness question in its base sentence; NHL, IIHF, PWHL and Hockey Canada name unnecessary contact in an icing or offside race as boarding, USA Hockey 624(d) penalises it in an icing race
Rule: USA Hockey boarding is never a bare minor — 603(a) adds a misconduct to it, 603(b) makes a hit that recklessly endangers an opponent a major plus a game misconduct, and plan on a match (602(a)); the game misconduct also costs your team's next game, at least two after a second major (404(b))
Rule: Their back to you? Take the puck: diagonal is from behind (USA Hockey 608); no book has a bare minor off the head; into the boards defenceless, mandatory match in CARHA 53(b), Hockey Canada 7.5(c) (7.6(c)/7.7(c) if head contact) and if reckless USA Hockey 608(c); IIHF 43.3: major and game misconduct
Rule: USA Hockey writes no bare minor for a check from behind (608(a)); a forceful one on a player along the boards, or one head first into boards or goal frame, is a major plus game misconduct or a match (608(b); Casebook Situation 1) — and plan on the match for a reckless one (602(a), 608(c))
Rule: What the wall does for you depends on sheet width — wider ice leaves a carrier forced outside more room, so gap control matters more than steering — and do not assume yours is wide: IHUK In-House Rules 2026-27, Rule 1 authorise British rinks below the IIHF Rule 1.2 minimum, bar two capped at U16
Position: Still between the attacker and the net — that is what makes it forcing outside rather than getting beaten wide
Risk: Beaten wide means the attacker is past your outside shoulder with open ice behind you, and there is nothing left
Never: Read "force them outside" as "never let anyone get to the outside". Stay on the defensive side of the puck instead, because anything that puts an attacker between you and your net has gone wrong
Key: Gap distances themselves belong to Defending the Rush, which covers them — take the numbers from there
```

New defencemen routinely hear both instructions and conclude the position contradicts itself. It doesn't.

- **Forcing them outside** means angling your body so the inside lane is shut and the only route runs toward the boards, where the wall becomes a second defender and the shooting angle is poor. **You are still between the attacker and the net.** This is what [Defending the Rush](../systems/defending_the_rush.md) and [Defender](defender.md) mean by taking away the middle. ⚠️ **The angle is yours to take. The contact is not** — the half of the instruction most converting forwards are never given. Steer with the route you skate, not with your body or your stick, and take the puck. What contact costs is set out at the end of this section.

- **Getting beaten wide** means the attacker gets past your outside shoulder with open ice behind you. You are no longer between them and the net, and there is nothing left. ⚠️ **Chasing them from behind, your stick goes on the puck and nothing else** — a hook, trip, hold or slash that denies the chance is a **penalty shot**, and can be an **awarded goal** if your net is empty. The detail is at the end of this section.
- **How much the wall does for you depends on the width of the sheet you are standing on.** [Rink Map](../foundation/rink_map.md) puts it this way: *"On narrow ice, the wall is your second defender. On wide ice, an attacker forced outside still has room to keep skating, so gap control and stick position matter more than steering."* **Do not read "IIHF" as "wide ice".** **IIHF Rule 1.2** allows a rink *"26m to 30m wide"*, and 26 m is 85.3 ft — fractionally wider than a full NHL sheet's 85 ft (**NHL Rule 1.2**). 🇬🇧 **In Britain, assume narrow.** The **IHUK In-House Rules 2026-27, Rule 1** authorise rinks *"with a non-standard playing surface (below 56 m × 26 m)"* *"for all levels of hockey, except"* **Sutton in England and Coatbridge in Scotland, each *"permitted up to U16 only"***, and **England Ice Hockey's Rules & Regulations 22.2** says the same. On a narrow sheet the wall does more of the work for you. Measure the building you actually play in.

The two diagrams above are the same rush. The carrier stands on the same square foot of ice in both **and skates the same lane in both**; the only player who moves is the defenceman, eighteen feet up the ice. **What separates the two is not where the carrier goes, it is where you are standing.** Being beaten wide does not mean the carrier ends up somewhere different from where you were sending them — it means there is nobody inside them when they decide where to go next.

The instinct to build is therefore *stay on the defensive side of the puck* — anything that puts an attacker between you and your net has already gone wrong — not *never let anyone get to the outside*. Gap distances themselves belong to [Defending the Rush](../systems/defending_the_rush.md), which covers them: 2–3 stick lengths in the neutral zone, closing to about a stick and a half at the red line and one stick length at your own blue line. That document calls them a guide rather than a law; they move with your backward speed and the attacker's skill. The published coaching pages disagree with each other and with that ladder: the only page found that anchors a distance to the centre red line prints two stick lengths there rather than a stick and a half.

**What it costs.** **Steering a carrier with the route you skate is legal, and two books say so in terms.** Hockey Canada's **Interpretation 1 to Rule 7.3(a)** lets a player *"steer or direct an opposing player into the boards, without actually touching or body-checking them"*. It holds only *"as long as either player does not use their body to intentionally body-check, **bump**, push, or shove an opponent"*, and a bump is far less than riding them along the wall. The Note to **USA Hockey 604(c)** makes closing an angled gap legal *"competitive contact"* even where checking is banned. **The NHL and IIHF books write no angling permission at all**, so there the claim rests on the absence of a prohibition, which is an inference.

Under CARHA's adult book, ⚠️ **the presumption runs the other way.** Its **Rule 49(a)** gives a minor or a major to a player who *"intentionally bodies, pushes, shoves, stands in front of an opponent for the purpose of making contact, and/or does not avert body contact with an opponent."* A major there ends your game and brings at least a one-game suspension (**30(a)**, **32(d)**), and it is mandatory where intentional contact injures. Put the carrier into the boards and the minor becomes a double minor (**49(b)**), which counts as two of the three penalties that eject you (**32(a)**). **Steering with your stick is no better:** USA Hockey's Casebook makes it a major plus a game misconduct where a hooked player is steered *"dangerously into the boards or goal frame"* (**Rule 623, Situation 3**).

**Riding them into the wall with your body is a different act, and in a great many classifications it is itself a penalty.** **USA Hockey 604(a)** bars body checking *"in the 12 & under youth age classifications and below, all Girls'/Women's age classifications and all non-check Adult classifications"*: a minor (**604(c)**), or a major plus a game misconduct for a check that *"recklessly endangers an opponent"* (**604(d)**) — and plan on a match penalty for that one (**602(a)**). ⚠️ **That list is a floor, not a ceiling.** A local governing body *"may prohibit body checking in any classification"*, and under **604(b)** a game between a Competitive Contact team and a Body Checking team is not a checking game.

**Hockey Canada 7.3(a)** gives a minor to a player who *"intentionally body-checks, bumps, shoves, or pushes any opposing player, in divisions of U13 and below, female hockey, and any other divisions approved by a Member of Hockey Canada"*. Its **Rule 7.3 preamble** draws the line inside the angle itself: it is body checking where a player *"leaves their skating lane to make contact"* or *"extends an arm, shoulder, or hip after angling an opponent."* **Nor is it only a minor:** the referee may give a major plus a game misconduct on the violence of the hit alone, and must if the player is injured (**7.3(b)**); a deliberate attempt to injure is a match penalty (**7.3(c)**).

🇬🇧 **In the Women's NIHL plan no hit, and in any other British women's competition ask your league first and do not hit anyone until you have the answer.** The WNIHL is played *"Full ice, non-checking"* under IHUK's 2026-27 Rules of Competition — an undefined label, so read it as at least as tight as **IIHF Rule 101.1**. That rule allows women's body checking only *"when there is a clear intention of playing the puck or attempting to 'gain possession' of the puck"*, a permission not to test until your league has answered. It also bars using the boards to *"eliminate her from the play, push her into the boards, or pin her along the boards."* ⚠️ **You may hold your ground, but only by standing in it:** *"[a]ny move by a Player to step or glide into an opposing Player will be assessed at least a minor penalty (2') for an 'illegal hit'"*, up to a *"[m]ajor penalty (5') and automatic game misconduct penalty"*. Under the **IHUK In-House Rules 2026-27, Rule 101**, any major in women's hockey also ends your game — and boarding is a discretionary major under IIHF 41.3.

**In a league that does allow checking, the ride still has a limit** — ⚠️ **and it does not lift because the carrier saw you coming.** Every book here penalises a check that sends an opponent violently or dangerously into the boards. The NHL, IIHF, PWHL and Hockey Canada write it of *"a defenseless opponent"*, but **USA Hockey 603** asks no defencelessness question in its base sentence: boarding is where a player *"pushes, trips or body checks an opponent causing them to go dangerously into the boards."* And the NHL, IIHF, PWHL and Hockey Canada name a case where nobody is defenceless: *"Any unnecessary contact with a player playing the puck on an obvious 'icing' or 'off-side' play which results in that player hitting or impacting the boards is 'boarding' and must be penalized as such."* (USA Hockey reaches the icing half at **624(d)**.)

**In every one of these books, the onus is on the hitter.** ⚠️ **USA Hockey 603(a) writes no bare minor** — its floor is a minor plus a misconduct — **and 603(b) makes a hit that recklessly endangers the opponent a major plus a game misconduct, with no choice; plan on a match penalty (602(a)).** Under USA Hockey, a game misconduct also costs you your team's next game, and a second major in the same game at least two (404(b)). [Rules Primer](../foundation/rules_primer.md) sets out each book's tiers.

**USA Hockey alone writes anything resembling a permission for riding a carrier along the wall, and** ⚠️ **it is narrower than it sounds.** Its **603(a)** says *"'Rolling' an opponent along the boards where they are attempting to go through too small an opening is not considered boarding."* That excuses a *boarding* call only, and only where body checking is legal at all — rolling is the torso action **604** prohibits.

**And a carrier you have steered to the wall is very often shielding the puck with their back half-turned when you arrive — if their numbers are toward you, take the puck and not the man.** **USA Hockey 608** covers a check *"delivered to a player directly from behind, or diagonally from behind"*, and **608(a)** writes no bare minor. **608(b)** requires a major plus a game misconduct for a check that *"recklessly endangers an opponent, or causes them to go head first into the boards or goal frame, as a result of checking from behind"*. **608(c)** requires a match penalty for one *"with excessive force while the opponent is in a vulnerable or defenseless position"*, and under **602(a)** a match *"shall be assessed"* for any reckless hit, so plan on it, and on a likely multi-game suspension. ⚠️ **At the wall USA Hockey does not wait for recklessness:** its Casebook says the major plus a game misconduct, or a match, *"must be called"* *"[i]n every instance where a player forcefully checks an opponent who is standing along the boards (back toward the middle of the ice)"* (**Rule 608, Situation 1**).

**The NHL, IIHF and PWHL books provide no minor for a hit from behind at all** (43.2 in each), and the IIHF's only tier ends your game: *"a major penalty and an automatic game misconduct penalty"* for one that recklessly endangers the player hit (**43.3**). If it reaches their head, the NHL, IIHF and PWHL take the harsher of Rule 43 and Rule 48; under the IIHF, plan on the major and game misconduct, never on a minor. **Hockey Canada settles fault in advance:** *"[i]n all circumstances the responsibility is on the player delivering the check to avoid contact to an opposing player's back"* (**Rule 7.5** preamble). It gives *"[a] Minor penalty and a Game Misconduct penalty"* for a hit from behind (**7.5(a)**), and a major plus a game misconduct on a violent one, mandatory if they are hurt (**7.5(b)**). It gives *"[a] Match penalty"* where they are hit *"into the boards or goal frame, in such a way that the player is unable to protect or defend themselves"* (**7.5(c)**).

Once the hit reaches their head, ⚠️ **Hockey Canada prices it as head contact**, because **Rule 7.6** *"supersedes any other rules, with the exception of Rule 7.10 – Fighting, and any foul that results in contact to the head must be penalized as Head Contact."* In minor and female hockey that is a minor if accidental and a double minor if intentional, with a third in one game an ejection (**7.6(a)**). Under 7.6 the referee may give a major plus a game misconduct on the violence alone, and **must if the player hit is injured or it was a cross-check above their shoulders (7.6(b))**; and a match penalty follows if they could not protect or defend themselves or you tried to injure them (**7.6(c)**). In Junior and Senior (**Rule 7.7**), moderate violence is a minor plus a ten-minute misconduct (**7.7(a)**), but **high violence, a cross-check above the shoulders, or an illegal hit that puts their head into the glass, ice or boards and injures it is a major plus a game misconduct (7.7(b))**, with the same match penalty (**7.7(c)**). **Drive them head first into the boards and it is still checking from behind as well — you take the harsher of the two prices.**

**CARHA's adult book is no softer:** its **Rule 53(a)** gives *"A Minor penalty plus a Game Misconduct or a Major penalty plus a Game Misconduct"*, the major mandatory if the player is injured. Either major brings at least a one-game suspension (**32(d)**). **53(b)** makes it a match penalty where a player is propelled from behind *"into the boards or goal net, in such a way that the player is unable to protect or defend themselves"*. A match keeps you out of later games until the Chairman has dealt with your case (**34(a)**). **If their numbers are toward you, the hit is not available. Take the puck instead.** [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md) covers it.

**Fouling a breakaway from behind.** A hook, trip, hold or slash from behind that denies a scoring chance is a **penalty shot** in every book read here, not just a two-minute penalty — the NHL and IIHF, the PWHL (**58.3**), Hockey Canada (**4.11(a)**) and CARHA (**86(c)**). With your net empty, **NHL Rule 25.1** awards a goal where an attacker with possession and control *"without a defending player between himself and the opposing goal … is prevented from scoring as a result of an infraction committed by the defending team"*, with no from-behind test. **USA Hockey 616(a)** awards *"a penalty shot/optional minor penalty"*: for a foul that would normally be a minor, the team you fouled may elect the minor instead (**406(a)**), and a major or match penalty is imposed on top of the shot. Its **616(b)** awards the empty-net goal only where *"an obvious and imminent goal has been prevented by the fouled from behind provisions"*. **Hockey Canada 4.12(a)** routes its awarded goal through the breakaway criteria of **4.11(a)(i)**. [Defending the Rush](../systems/defending_the_rush.md) sets out each book's conditions.

### What else is genuinely new

**In short:** Never carry or pass D-to-D through your own slot, and work as a pair: one on the puck, one at the goalmouth.

```facts
Mindset: As a defenceman there is one layer behind you now, not two — that changes how you weigh risk, not how hard you compete
Never: Carry or pass D-to-D through your own slot. Go behind the net, where a lost puck is a battle rather than a shot — or high above the circles, if that is your team's route
Position: One of you on the puck, one at the goalmouth, swapping every time the puck crosses the middle — and talk constantly
Convention: On a 2-on-1, take away the pass and give up the shot — sit in the passing lane with your stick flat; a timed slide to force the shot — into the lane, never into their skates — is the realistic alternative, so ask, and make sure your goalie agrees
Convention: Under a low zone collapse the goalmouth is yours and the high slot is the centre's; under man-on-man the net front belongs to whoever's man is standing there
Rule: Shooting the puck over the glass and out of play from inside your own defensive zone is a two-minute delay-of-game minor (NHL 63.2(iii), PWHL 65.2(iii)) — off the glass and along it is fine
Rule: A deliberate flip out of play is a minor from anywhere on the ice, not only from your own end — NHL and IIHF 63.2(ii), USA Hockey 610(c), Hockey Canada 10.1(ii), PWHL 65.2(ii) and CARHA 75(b) — so never put one out on purpose to stop play
Rule: The IIHF binds a British defenceman to the defending-zone over-the-glass minor, but not in the same words — its carve-out reads "no protective glass" for "no glass", and its bench clause reaches glass "surrounding" the bench where the NHL's says "behind" (IIHF 63.2(III), 2025/26 and 2026/27 alike)
Rule: USA Hockey 610(c) penalises only a deliberate shoot-out, and 631(d) makes an accidental one a last-play faceoff; Hockey Canada 10.1(ii) is deliberate-only too and reaches a goalie as well as a skater; its direct limb 10.1(v) names the goalie alone
Rule: Hockey Canada charges the accidental one in two divisions — in Junior and Senior it is no penalty "but the defending team may not make a line change" (Hockey Canada Rule 6.4(a))
Rule: Under the NHL and IIHF your icing brings a defensive-zone draw (81.2), no line change (81.4) bar injury and no time-out (87.1); USA Hockey imposes neither restriction; Hockey Canada bars the change in U18AAA and Junior, and in Senior at the Member's option, where 6.7(d)(iii) still allows a time-out
Options: Three offensive-zone depths — just inside the blue line by default, the top of the circle activated, below the circles only if a forward has taken your spot at the line
Convention: Only one defenceman pinches at a time — a near-universal coaching convention rather than a rule, with real exceptions coaches do call
```

- **Your mistakes are visible and close to final.** As a forward, a bad read had two layers behind it. As a defenceman it has one — your goalie — and that changes how you should weigh risk, not how hard you should compete. (The position with no layers behind it at all is described honestly in [Goaltender](goaltender.md), and it is worth reading that section before you decide your new job is the exposed one.) The practical consequence is a standing prohibition: **never carry or pass D-to-D through your own slot.** Go behind the net instead, where a lost puck is a battle behind the goal line rather than a shot from the slot. Some teams exchange high above the tops of the circles rather than behind the net, so ask which route yours uses.
- **You play as half of a pair, not as one of three.** One defenceman is on the puck, one is at the goalmouth, and the two of you swap every time the puck crosses the middle. Under low zone collapse the goalmouth is yours and the high slot is the centre's; that split is the whole of net-front defending, and under **man-on-man** the net front belongs instead to whoever's man is standing there. Talk constantly. [Defender](defender.md).
- **Your first odd-man rush.** On a 2-on-1 the common instruction is to take away the pass and give up the shot: sit in the passing lane with your stick flat on the passing side, and let your goalie take the shooter. The realistic alternative is a timed slide across the lane to force the shot — **into the lane, never into their skates.** Under the NHL, PWHL, IIHF and USA Hockey books a slide that takes out the carrier is still tripping even if you touch the puck first (only Hockey Canada and CARHA excuse a slide that reaches the puck first). In every book but CARHA, a slide that catches them at or below the knees — anywhere below the hips under Hockey Canada — is clipping, which can end your game. Ask which your team plays and make sure your goalie agrees. Either way, do not commit to the carrier early: a cross-ice pass to an open forward is a chance your goalie cannot save. [Defender](defender.md) sets it out.
- **Clearing your own zone without a penalty.** Two penalties you have probably never taken. **Never shoot the puck over the glass from inside your own defensive zone** — off the glass and along it is fine; over it is a two-minute delay-of-game minor (**NHL 63.2(iii)**, **PWHL 65.2(iii)**), and new defencemen take it constantly. 🇬🇧 The IIHF writes the same limb at **63.2(III)**, so in Britain it is your rule. For a skater, USA Hockey (**610(c)**) and Hockey Canada (**10.1(ii)**) penalise only a *deliberate* clear. ⚠️ **But a deliberate flip out of play is a minor from anywhere on the ice in every book, so never put one out on purpose to stop play.** Hockey Canada also charges a skater's accidental one in Junior and Senior: no penalty, *"but the defending team may not make a line change"* (**Rule 6.4(a)**). [Risk Management](../hockey-iq/risk_management.md) carries every book in full.
- **Icing costs your pair more than it cost you.** You will ice the puck far more often than you used to. Under NHL and IIHF rules that brings a defensive-zone draw with the attacking team choosing the dot (**81.2**), no line change (**81.4**) and no time-out (**87.1**) — though an injury is an exception, so your own icing never obliges you to keep a hurt partner on the ice. **USA Hockey imposes neither restriction**, but under **204(a)** each team has five seconds to change, and undue delay brings a warning and then a delay-of-game minor. **Hockey Canada bars the change only in U18AAA and Junior, and in Senior at the Member's option** (**6.1(f)**, **6.7(d)**), keeps the injury exception (**6.7(d)(i)**), and still lets either team take its time-out, though the icing team may not change during it (**6.7(d)(iii)**). [Defender](defender.md) and [Rules Primer](../foundation/rules_primer.md) cover both.
- **Restraint in the offensive zone.** Three depths: just inside the blue line (default), the top of the circle (activated, still recoverable), and below the circles (a commitment you cannot recover from, and only correct if a forward has already taken your spot at the line). **Only one defenceman pinches at a time** — a near-universal coaching convention rather than a rule, with real exceptions coaches do call.

**First thing to work on:** backward skating, and it is not close. Book the power-skating session. Everything tactical in the position is downstream of it.

**What to stop worrying about:** offence. A converting forward's shot and vision are an asset that will surface on its own once the defensive habits are automatic. Activating into the rush before you can hold a gap just means arriving at your own blue line out of position and out of breath.

## Defence → forward

**In short:** Attack space: find open ice, arrive in it late, and stop waiting for the play to be safe.

```facts
Mindset: Attack space instead of denying it. Find the space nobody is defending, arrive in it late, and force somebody to make a decision
Never: Wait until the play is obviously safe before moving — by then the space has closed
Key: F1, F2 and F3 are roles defined by order of arrival, not positions and not people — if you are first on the puck you are F1
Convention: How many forwards go deep is your team's system — a 2-1-2 sends two, a 1-2-2 sends one — and which side F3 sits on is a genuine coaching split, so ask
Rule: Keep the blade down — offside needs both skates completely over the blue line before the puck, and a raised skate keeps you onside under NHL and IIHF 83.1 and PWHL 85.1 but not under USA Hockey 630(a) or Hockey Canada 6.11, which require ice contact; CARHA 72(c) settles it neither way
Convention: Centre in the middle on the backcheck and wingers in the outside lanes is a coaching choice, not a law — under every scheme somebody owns the middle
Action: Work your first three steps and your stop-and-start — a forward's shift is a sequence of accelerations
Mindset: Stop being above the puck — somebody is above it, and on most shifts it is not you
```

The overlooked direction, and often the harder mental adjustment of the two, because you are being asked to unlearn caution that has been rewarded for years.

**What is genuinely new**

- **You read forechecks from the other side of them.** As a defenceman you spent every retrieval reading a forecheck coming at you — how many are in, which side F1 angled you to, whether the pressure has sealed you or over-committed. That knowledge is worth a great deal now, because you are the one applying it. **F1, F2 and F3 are roles defined by order of arrival, not positions and not people** — if you dumped the puck in and you are first on it, you are F1, whatever your jersey says. How many forwards go deep is your team's system: a **2-1-2** sends two in, a **1-2-2** sends one, and **which side F3 sits on is a genuine coaching split** — weak-side is the common default, but the strong-side (stacked) variant deliberately overloads one corner and concedes the far-side breakout. [Forechecking Systems](../systems/forechecking_systems.md) is the authority. Ask which one you are running.
- **You attack space instead of denying it.** This is the real change and it is a change of intent. A defenceman's whole training is to close ice, occupy the dangerous seam, and take options away. A forward's job is the mirror: find the space nobody is defending, arrive in it late, and force somebody to make a decision. The specific reflex that has to go is **waiting until the play is safe before moving** — as a forward, the moment the play is obviously safe is the moment the space has closed.
- **Timing the offensive blue line.** You have rarely been the player arriving at it at speed. You are offside only when **both** skates have completely crossed the line before the puck, so a trailing skate on the line keeps you onside. **Keep that blade down, because the books split on a raised one.** A skate held in the air still keeps you onside under **NHL and IIHF Rule 83.1** and **PWHL Rule 85.1**, which treat the line as a vertical *plane*. It does not under **USA Hockey 630(a)** or **Hockey Canada 6.11**, which require the skate to be *"in physical contact with the ice surface"*, in Hockey Canada's words. Hockey Canada's Interpretation 3 to Rule 6.11(a) puts one skate in the air and one over the line offside. **CARHA's Rule 72(c)** settles the raised skate neither way. **With the blade down you are legal in every book named here.** This document has no count of which leagues run which book, so find out yours. [Rules Primer](../foundation/rules_primer.md) and [Zone Entries](../systems/zone_entries.md) have the detail.
- **The backcheck is now a lane assignment.** Under the standard positional scheme, the centre takes the middle and the wingers the outside lanes. **That is a coaching choice, not a law** — other teams use "first man back takes the middle", others pick up man-on-man from the puck outward. [Defending the Rush](../systems/defending_the_rush.md) sets them out, along with the four named team backchecking systems that decide which assignment applies. Under every one of them somebody owns the middle and nobody assumes it is somebody else.

**First thing to work on:** your first three steps and your stop-and-start. A forward's shift is a sequence of accelerations — forecheck, reverse, backcheck, reverse again — and a defenceman's skating diet is heavier on gliding, reading and backward work. Straight-line speed matters less than how fast you get to it.

**What to stop worrying about:** being above the puck. It was the correct instinct and it is no longer your instinct to have. Somebody is above the puck; on most shifts it is not you.

## Anyone → goaltender

**In short:** Treat goaltending as a different sport, and ask about house gear and a goalie coach before you buy anything.

```facts
Key: Goaltending is a different sport that shares a rink — not a position you drift into for a few weeks to see how it goes
Technique: Short lateral pushes — shuffles and T-pushes, in and out along the angle, post-to-post, recovery to your feet — almost all inside-edge work in a deep stance
Action: Ask your club whether they have house goalie gear and a goalie coach before you buy anything
Rule: Five of six rulebooks bar a skater from wearing goalie gear — NHL and PWHL 5.3 lift it only if both goalies are incapacitated, USA Hockey 203(d) only if all are; Hockey Canada 2.2(g) and CARHA 16(f) allow only a goalie or their replacement; IIHF 5.3 denies a stand-in privileges
Rule: Going the other way needs the scoresheet changed — once designated a goalie you “may not participate as a skater unless added to the scoresheet as a skater and the team is assessed the bench minor penalty” (USA Hockey Casebook, Rule 203, Situation 6)
Rule: Across the Home Countries — England, Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland — a neck laceration protector is mandatory for every player at every age, adults included (IHUK In-House Rules 2026-27 and IIHF, both Rule 9.12)
Rule: Under the In-House Rules a missing neck guard is an immediate 10-minute misconduct with no warning (Rule 9.12), but the Elite League's own Rule 9.12 warns first, then gives a minor, and its Rule 9.5 ladder runs to a misconduct, then a game misconduct, if you go back out without it (EIHL Casebook)
Rule: In England and Wales the neck-protector requirement applies at practice too: "all on ice activities" (England Ice Hockey Rules & Regulations 24.3); whether that reaches Scotland and Northern Ireland is unsettled, so wear one at practice there too
Rule: A HECC-certified neck laceration protector is required in all age classifications except Adults (USA Hockey Rule 304(g))
Rule: A BNQ-certified throat protector is required for minor and female hockey, and a dangler on the mask does not satisfy it on its own (Hockey Canada Rule 3.6(c))
Action: Go to a goalie-specific session rather than a team practice, and give it a block of weeks — the first night is unrepresentative in both directions
Action: Read "What a Goalie Needs From Their Skaters" in Goaltender whether or not you ever play the position
```

**Goaltending is a different sport that shares a rink.** [Goaltender](goaltender.md) opens by explaining why it cannot even use the same document structure as the other three — you spend the whole game in one zone, you carry the puck essentially never, and you pressure nobody. It is not a position you drift into for a few weeks to see how it goes.

Four things make this switch different in kind from the others.

- **The skating is a separate discipline.** Short lateral pushes — shuffles and T-pushes — movement in and out along the angle, post-to-post, and recovery to your feet: almost all inside-edge work over very short distances in a deep stance. [Skating](../technique/skating.md) is explicit that ordinary skating work builds leg strength and edge feel but that the position-specific movement should be trained with a goalie coach.
- **The equipment is a barrier, and partly a legal one.** Goalie gear is sized from a seated floor-to-knee measurement, regulated by dimension, and costs several times a skater's kit. [Equipment](../off-the-ice/equipment.md) covers it, and the point that matters most: **many clubs own house goalie gear, so ask before you buy.** ⚠️ **And you cannot simply borrow pads mid-game.** Five of the six rulebooks read here bar it, including the NHL's. **NHL Rule 5.3**, which the PWHL prints word for word: *"Except when both goalkeepers are incapacitated, no skater on the playing roster in the game shall be permitted to wear the equipment of the goalkeeper."* **USA Hockey Rule 203(d)** says the same of *"all goalkeepers"*. **Hockey Canada Rule 2.2(g)** and **CARHA Rule 16(f)** write no incapacity exception — Hockey Canada's reads *"No player, other than a goaltender or their replacement, will be permitted to wear the equipment of a goaltender."*

  **The IIHF book does not write the bar — and its silence is not a permission.** Its Rule 5.3, read in both editions, says instead: *“The goalkeeper may be removed and substituted by another skater. Such substitute shall not be permitted the privileges of the goalkeeper.”* ⚠️ **A skater who picks up the pads on their own is that substitute: they take shots from the one position the rules protect, with none of that protection.** The privileges come only with being dressed and listed as a goalie — the IIHF Situation Handbook's **Situation 8.9** lets a team with no goalie left *"dress any one of their eligible registered skaters as a goalkeeper"*, who is *"given such privileges for the balance of the game"*. USA Hockey instead requires the team to *"immediately appoint a temporary goalkeeper (see Glossary) or place an additional skater on the ice who shall not have goalkeeper’s privileges"* (**203(d)**) — a term the IIHF books never use. **In every book, who goes in net is settled between your bench and the officials, on the scoresheet — never by you picking up a spare set of pads.**
- **Neck protection — check the rule for *your* classification, because it is not the same everywhere.** 🇬🇧 **Anywhere in the Home Countries — England, Wales, Scotland or Northern Ireland — there is no age exemption.** The IHUK In-House Rules' Rule 9.12 reads: *"A neck laceration protector is mandatory for all players in all IIHF categories and IHUK/EIH/SIH competitions."* There is no warning — *"an immediate 10-minute Misconduct Penalty will be issued"* — and a second offence is a game misconduct. **University hockey is not a gap.** The BUIHA adopts the In-House Rules, and Rule 9.12 covers *"all players in all IIHF categories"*, so expect the same 10-minute misconduct. Wear it. In England and Wales, England Ice Hockey's *Rules & Regulations* 24.3 makes it mandatory for *"all players at all levels"* and at *"all on ice activities"* — practice included. Whether that reaches Scotland and Northern Ireland is unsettled, so wear it at practice there too.

  Elsewhere, neither North American book applies universally. **USA Hockey Rule 304(g)** requires a HECC-certified neck laceration protector for all players, goalies included, *"in all age classifications except Adults"* — adults are strongly recommended, not required — and a note lets BNQ-certified ones satisfy it through the 2026-27 season. **Hockey Canada Rule 3.6(c)** requires a BNQ-certified throat protector *"for players registered in minor and female hockey"*, and adds the detail that catches new goalies out: goalies with a throat attachment on the mask *"must still wear a BNQ-certified throat protector"*. **A dangler hanging off the mask does not satisfy it on its own.** [Equipment](../off-the-ice/equipment.md) covers the standards.
- **Goaltending needs its own coaching from the start.** Grooving a compensation is the most expensive mistake in skill acquisition — [Practice and Development](../off-the-ice/practice_and_development.md) makes the general case — and goaltending is dense with technique that is nearly impossible to self-correct, because you cannot see yourself and the feedback (the puck went in) is far too coarse to tell you why.

### Goaltender → skater

**And going back the other way is paperwork, not just a decision.** Listed on the scoresheet as a goalie, you cannot simply play out. USA Hockey's Casebook answers a team that wants to dress its substitute goalie as a forward: *"Yes, as long as the team has not exceeded the maximum number of skaters or game participants"*. But *"once a player has been designated as a goalkeeper, they may not participate as a skater unless added to the scoresheet as a skater and the team is assessed the bench minor penalty under this rule"* (**USA Hockey Official Rules and Casebook 2025-29, Casebook — Teams, Rule 203, Situation 6**, rule reference 203(a)). The IIHF Situation Handbook answers the mirror-image question just as administratively (**Situation 8.9**, above). **Neither book says a word about how to play the position.**

### If you want to try it

**How to try it properly, if you want to.** Ask your club whether they have house gear and a goalie coach. Go to a goalie-specific session rather than a team practice, where you will simply be shot at. Give it a block of weeks rather than one night, because the first night is unrepresentative in both directions. And read [Goaltender](goaltender.md) — particularly *Stance and Depth*, *Angles and Positioning* and the honest section on the mental demand — before you decide.

**One thing every skater should do regardless of whether they ever play the position:** read the *What a Goalie Needs From Their Skaters* section of [Goaltender](goaltender.md). Most net-front goals a team concedes are not goalie errors; they are skater habits, and the switch is not necessary to fix them.

## Playing your off wing

**In short:** On your off wing the puck sits on your inside; take wall passes open to the ice, a step off the boards.

```facts
Position: Off wing means the puck sits on the inside of your body, stick toward the middle of the ice — more options for moving it and a better forehand angle from prime mid-ice
Convention: The off-wing half-wall is standard on the power play because it keeps the cross-seam one-timer live — Special Teams covers that
Risk: A breakout pass up the wall now arrives on your backhand, which costs a beat and can weaken the exit — an open blade curve makes those receptions easier, and Equipment covers the trade-off
Technique: Receive it open to the ice — hips open, a step or two off the boards, so you keep the skate, the pass to the centre, the pass back and the middle; Breakouts covers that reception
Never: Turn your back to anyone coming at you as you receive it, or duck — two injuries: a hit from behind, and a head-first collision. Skates parallel to the wall, head up and chin off your chest
Technique: CARHA's back-to-the-boards reception means boards behind you, open to the ice — not your back to anyone coming at you. If contact is coming, take it with forearm and hip to the wall
Key: On CARHA's account, citing nhlspecialteams.com not tracking of its own, most professionals play their strong side at even strength and stronger puck-handlers typically elect the off wing
Key: A defenceman's off side puts retrievals behind your own net and D-to-D passes on your backhand — common and not a disaster, and Rink Map covers the reasoning
```

Your **off wing** means a left-shot playing right wing, or a right-shot playing left wing, so the puck sits on the inside of your body — between you and the middle of the ice — rather than toward the boards. It is not a demotion, it is not a promotion, and it is much more common than new players expect.

**Why coaches do it.** Entering the offensive zone with your stick toward the centre of the ice opens more options for moving the puck, and gives a better angle for a forehand shot from a prime mid-ice location. A winger coming in on their strong side has the stick along the boards and needs more work and more time to get into a shooting position. On the power play the **off-wing half-wall is standard**, precisely because it puts the puck on your inside hand and makes the cross-seam one-timer live at all times — [Special Teams](../systems/special_teams.md) covers that. The CARHA Hockey material, **citing nhlspecialteams.com rather than any tracking of its own**, notes that at even strength the majority of professionals still play their strong side, and that it is typically players with stronger puck-handling who elect the off wing.

**What actually gets harder, and the fix.** The cost is on the boards in your own end: a breakout pass up the wall now arrives on your backhand, which costs a beat and can weaken the exit. **Receive it open to the ice.** [Breakouts](../systems/breakouts.md) teaches it for exactly this puck: **open your hips to the ice** so you see the rink and the puck at once, and **get a step or two off the boards**. That separation leaves you able to skate, pass to the centre, pass back to your defenceman *or* attack the middle; flush against the wall you can do one of those. If the puck arrives hard, angle a skate to kill it onto your stick rather than reaching and missing.

**When contact is coming, never turn your back to anyone coming at you, and never duck.** Keep your head up and your chin off your chest. A turned back makes the contact a hit from behind, which you cannot see or brace for — USA Hockey: *"Even a light hit from behind could inflict a severe head or neck injury."* A tucked chin puts your head into the boards first, and USA Hockey says the neck injury from that *"can occur at walking speed."* No reception is worth either trade. [Body Contact and Battles → Receiving a Hit Safely](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md) covers how to take the hit.

![](diagram:off-wing-open-to-the-ice)

**The source for this section teaches the same reception.** The CARHA material has you **put your back to the boards** to take the backhand puck. That means boards behind you, facing the ice and open to it — not your back to anyone coming at you. [Breakouts](../systems/breakouts.md) adds only the step off the wall, so you keep every option. If contact is coming and you have time to turn, take it with forearm and hip to the wall.

Receiving on the backhand is a trainable skill and [Passing and Receiving](../technique/passing_and_receiving.md) covers it directly; it is also one of the arguments for an **open blade curve**, which makes backhand receptions easier ([Equipment](../off-the-ice/equipment.md)).

**For defencemen this trade is weighted differently.** Handedness normally determines a defenceman's side, because retrievals behind your own net and D-to-D passes come to your forehand on your natural side. Playing your off side is common and not a disaster, but those two touches come to your backhand — [Rink Map](../foundation/rink_map.md) covers the reasoning and notes that analysis of NHL pairings has generally found natural-side pairs perform better.

## The habits that actively work against you

```facts
Key: Your existing instincts are not neutral — several of them are precisely wrong in the new job
Risk: Because old positional instincts are automatic, they surface exactly when you are under pressure and have no time to think
Convention: That your old habits work against you is coaching craft throughout — but the most consistently described pattern in this whole topic
Action: Name the habit out loud before the shift — "I am going to feel wrong going into that corner and I am going in anyway"
Key: Unlearning is slower than learning
Never: Rely on general effort — the only reliable lever you have is deliberate attention on the specific reflex
```

This is the section that distinguishes a position switch from simply learning a new position. Your existing instincts are not neutral. Several of them are precisely wrong in the new job, and because they are *automatic* they surface exactly when you are under pressure and have no time to think. **Coaching craft throughout — but it is the most consistently described pattern in this whole topic.**

| Switch | The habit that served you | What it becomes |
|---|---|---|
| **Wing to centre** | Lane discipline: hold your side, don't chase | **Passivity.** You stay on the wall or in the high slot when the puck is in the corner, and your strong-side defenceman is alone in a battle they will lose |
| **Wing to centre** | "Stay high" on the opposing point | **Standing too high on the breakout.** Your defenceman has no middle-lane option and eats the forecheck |
| **Centre to wing** | Go where the decision is | **Drifting into the middle.** You collapse your own line's spacing and let two defenders cover three forwards |
| **Centre to wing** | Manage the rotations | **Talking instead of covering.** The opposing point man is unmarked while you organise |
| **Forward to defence** | Attack the puck, win it back | **Chasing.** Both defencemen drawn to the puck, goalmouth empty |
| **Forward to defence** | Skate forwards at the problem | **Pivoting early**, and getting beaten by the first change of direction |
| **Defence to forward** | Stay above the puck, never gamble | **Reluctance to attack.** You arrive late to space that has already closed, and your line plays 2-on-5 in the offensive zone |
| **Defence to forward** | Contain, deny, wait for the mistake | **Not initiating the forecheck.** F1 is whoever gets there first, and if you hesitate nobody is F1 |
| **Any position to off wing** | Receive on your forehand | **Turning to find the forehand** on a wall pass, and losing the puck while you do |

**On chasing specifically:** coaches at every level name both defencemen being drawn to the puck, leaving the goalmouth empty, as one of the commonest causes of goals against. That is coaching consensus rather than a statistic, and [Defensive Zone Coverage](../systems/defensive_zone_coverage.md) covers both the claim and the caveat.

The general fix is the same in every row: **name the habit out loud before the shift.** "I am going to feel wrong going into that corner and I am going in anyway." Unlearning is slower than learning — [Practice and Development](../off-the-ice/practice_and_development.md) treats this seriously under *The unlearning problem* — and the only reliable lever an individual player has is deliberate attention on the specific reflex, not general effort.

## The honest timeline

```facts
Key: There is no published measurement of how long a position switch takes — the timeframes below are coaching craft, with enormous individual variation
Convention: A few shifts to stop being lost — you will know where to stand long before you know when to move
Convention: A handful of games before the routine reads are automatic, and a season before the position feels like yours
Convention: Anything skating-dependent takes longer, and possibly much longer — forward to defence is a technical rebuild, not a set of decisions
Risk: Expect a real performance dip, and expect it to feel like evidence you cannot play the position
Never: Abandon the change in the first few weeks, at the bottom of the dip — the classic error, and coaches see it happen between weeks two and five
Mindset: Improvement is a staircase, not a line — plateaus are normal and some of them are the useful kind
```

**There is no published measurement of how long a position switch takes** (see the note at the top of this document). What follows is craft, and it is consistent with the one adjacent thing this guide does have a view on: [Practice and Development](../off-the-ice/practice_and_development.md) states plainly that **every published adult-beginner timeframe comes from coaching providers and retailers rather than research**, and should be read as experienced guesses with enormous individual variation.

With that stated, the shape people describe:

- **A few shifts** to stop being lost. You will know where to stand long before you know when to move.
- **A handful of games** before the routine reads are automatic — who you pick up, where you go when the puck goes to a particular place.
- **A season** before the position feels like yours rather than like a job you are doing carefully.
- **Longer, and possibly much longer, for anything skating-dependent** — which in practice means the forward to defence switch, where your ceiling is set by backward skating and that is a technical rebuild rather than a set of decisions.

Two things worth knowing in advance, both of which [Practice and Development](../off-the-ice/practice_and_development.md) covers:

- **New techniques feel worse before they feel better.** A switch is a large-scale version of that. Expect a real performance dip, expect it to feel like evidence you cannot play the position, and know that abandoning a change in the first few weeks — right at the bottom of the dip — is the classic error. Coaches see it happen somewhere between week two and week five; that window is coaching observation, not measurement.
- **Improvement is a staircase, not a line.** Plateaus are normal and some of them are the useful kind.

## Asking for a switch — and being moved without being asked

### If you want to move

```facts
Action: Ask a day or two later, in private — not on the bench, not immediately after a game, not in front of the group
Action: Ask a specific question — "What would I need to show you to get a look at centre?" beats "Can I play centre?", which gets a shrug
Action: Ask to try it in practice first — far easier for a coach to say yes to than a change on a game sheet, and it costs them nothing
Action: Then do the work where it is visible — if the answer is "I need to see you win draws", take draws at the end of every practice
Mindset: Accept that some answers are structural — four centres and no left wingers is a roster fact, not a judgement of you
```

The model here is the same one [Mental Game](../off-the-ice/mental_game.md) sets out for asking about ice time, and it works for the same reason.

- **Ask at the right time.** Not on the bench, not immediately after a game, not in front of the group. A day or two later, in private.
- **Ask a specific question, not a vague one.** "What would I need to show you to get a look at centre?" is hard to dodge and gives you something to work on. "Can I play centre?" gets a shrug.
- **Ask to try it in practice first.** This is much easier for a coach to say yes to than a change on a game sheet, and it costs them nothing.
- **Then do the work where it is visible.** If the answer is "I need to see you win draws", take draws at the end of every practice — which [Faceoffs](../systems/faceoffs.md) recommends anyway, for every player.
- **Accept that some answers are structural.** A team with four centres and no left wingers is going to keep you on the left, and that is a roster fact rather than a judgement of you.

### If you have been moved without being asked

```facts
Mindset: Being moved without being asked is usually not a punishment — roster arithmetic, an injury, a line that needs a defensively reliable player, or a spot that suits you better
Key: Moving a forward to defence is frequently what happens to the player a coach trusts most with the puck in their own end
Action: Find out the system before you find out your feelings about it — zone, man-on-man or a hybrid of the two in the D-zone, and which forecheck
Action: Ask what a good shift from you looks like in this spot — coaches rarely get asked, and the answer is more useful than you expect
Mindset: Separate the message from the delivery — being told bluntly does not make the decision wrong
Never: Audition for your old position by playing the new one badly — it reads as sulking and makes a temporary move permanent
Action: Tell your linemates you are new to the position — thirty seconds before the shift saves three games of the same overlap error
```

- **Being moved is usually not a punishment.** The commonest reasons are roster arithmetic, an injury, a line that needs a defensively reliable player, or a coach who thinks the new spot suits you better. Some of them are compliments in disguise: moving a forward to defence is frequently what happens to the player a coach trusts most with the puck in their own end.
- **Find out the system before you find out your feelings about it.** One question — zone, man-on-man or a hybrid of the two in the D-zone, and which forecheck — determines most of what your new job actually is. Ask it before your first shift, not after your first mistake.
- **Ask what specifically they want from you there.** "What does a good shift from me look like in this spot?" You will get a much more useful answer than you expect, because coaches rarely get asked.
- **Separate the message from the delivery.** Being told bluntly does not make the decision wrong, and being told kindly does not make it right. [Mental Game](../off-the-ice/mental_game.md).
- **Do not audition for your old position by playing the new one badly.** It reads as sulking even when it isn't, and it is the single most reliable way to make a temporary move permanent.
- **Say something to your linemates.** A line where nobody has acknowledged that the centre is new to the position will spend three games making the same overlap error. Thirty seconds of conversation before the shift fixes most of it — [Team Play and Culture](../off-the-ice/team_play_and_culture.md) makes the general case for talking before rather than after.

### If you are a parent

```facts
Convention: Many programmes deliberately rotate young players through positions so nobody specialises early
Key: USA Hockey's Parent Handbook 2025-26 recommends an 8U experience with "no full-time goalies", and says playing multiple sports is encouraged
Key: A coach rotating your eight-year-old through the net and through defence is following the model, not experimenting on your child
Mindset: Treat a move as information about the team, not a verdict on your child
Action: Let them ask the coach the specific question themselves — it is a more valuable skill than the position is
Key: Beyond USA Hockey's 8U recommendation the published guidance runs out; the registration, programme and age-classification background sits in Getting Started
```

Youth players get moved constantly, and at younger ages many programmes deliberately rotate players through positions so nobody specialises early. **That last part is not just custom — it is the governing body's own recommendation at the youngest age group.** USA Hockey's *Parent Handbook 2025-26* states that the 8U experience it recommends *"includes no full-time goalies, 7-9 players per team, station-based practices and cross-ice games to maximize fun, puck touches and skill development for every player"*, and on the same page that *"playing multiple sports is encouraged."* A coach rotating your eight-year-old through the net and through defence is following the model, not experimenting on your child.

Beyond that specific recommendation the published guidance runs out; *How claims in this document are labelled*, above, sets out which books were searched and what they do and do not contain. Treat a move as information about the team, not a verdict on your child, and let them ask the coach the specific question themselves; it is a more valuable skill than the position is. The registration, programme and age-classification background sits in [Getting Started](../getting-started/getting_started.md), which covers it.

## Common Mistakes

- **Assuming your new position works like your old one with different geography.** **Learn what the new job is responsible for first — who you pick up, and where you go when the puck moves.** The map follows.
- **Not asking which system your team plays.** Ask before your first shift: zone or man-on-man in your own end, which forecheck, which backcheck lane, which breakout. Each one changes your new job, and a new player has an unlimited licence to ask.
- **Treating a move to the wing as a demotion.** Play it as the job it is: hold your lane, cover the point on your side in your own end (or your check, under man-on-man), and win your pucks on the wall.
- **Getting low at the dot by pushing your head out over it.** **Get your depth from your knees and hips** — [Centre](center.md) has the stance cue. In the IIHF book, helmet-to-helmet contact before the drop is a faceoff violation that can cost you the draw and a minor.
- **Moving to the wing and forgetting you can now give a draw away without touching the puck.** **Stand still on your own side of the spot, outside the circle, blade behind your hash marks, and keep your hands off the player beside you until the puck lands.** ⚠️ **Move in early or line up offside and your centre is thrown out of the dot** — the NHL's book included — and in most books touching an opponent does the same. Impeding them can be called as interference in every book.
- **Moving the puck across the front of your own net as a new defenceman.** **Send it behind the net to your partner, or high above the circles if that is your team's route.** A puck lost behind the goal line is a battle; one lost in your slot is a point-blank shot.
- **Converting to defence without fixing your backward skating**, then deciding you cannot read the game. Book power skating for backward crossovers and pivots first — a gap you cannot hold is usually a skating failure, not a bad read.
- **Reading "force them outside" as a licence to ride somebody into the wall.** **Shut the inside lane with the route you skate, stay between the carrier and your net, and take the puck with your stick.** ⚠️ **The angle is yours to take; the contact is not** — leave your lane to make contact, or put a hip out after angling them, and it is a body check, a penalty wherever checking is barred. Under CARHA's adult book, failing to avoid contact is a penalty in itself. 🇬🇧 **In the Women's NIHL plan no hit at all, and in any other British women's game ask your league first.** ⚠️ **If their numbers are toward you, take the puck.** Called as checking from behind, it is never a bare minor and ends your game in every other book, and in USA Hockey's too if it is forceful along the boards, drives them head first, or is reckless (plan on a match). Into the boards or goal frame with them unable to protect themselves, it is a mandatory match in several. Under Hockey Canada one that reaches their head is priced as head contact, a match if they could not protect themselves; drive them head first into the boards or goal frame and you take the harsher of that and the checking-from-behind price. Where checking is legal, the onus is still on you as the hitter.
- **Waiting for the play to be safe after moving up to forward.** **Go to the open space before it is obviously open** — by then it has closed. First to the puck on the forecheck, you are F1: go.
- **Taking a puck on the wall flush against the boards, or turned to face them.** **Receive it open to the ice** — hips open, a step or two off the boards ([Breakouts](../systems/breakouts.md)). When contact is coming: skates parallel to the wall, head up and chin off your chest — never your back to anyone coming at you, never duck. A turned back makes even a light hit a hit from behind, and USA Hockey warns that a head-down collision with the boards can injure your neck at walking speed.
- **Trying goal casually.** **Get gear that fits you, a goalie coach and goalie skating from day one, and make the change on the scoresheet, not on the fly.** Most books bar a skater from even wearing goalie equipment, with narrow exceptions. ⚠️ **The IIHF book writes no equipment bar, and that is not a permission:** until the bench has dressed and listed you as a goalie, you take shots with none of a goalie's protection. **Coming back out is paperwork too:** under USA Hockey a designated goalie plays out only once added to the scoresheet as a skater, which costs the team a bench minor.
- **Going into net without a neck laceration protector.** **Wear one from your first time in net, whatever your age, then find out which rule is yours.** Across the Home Countries it is mandatory for every player at every age, and in England and Wales at practice too — wear it at practice in Scotland and Northern Ireland as well, where that reach is unsettled. The adult exemption people quote is American, and in Hockey Canada minor and female hockey a throat guard hanging from the mask does not count on its own.
- **Trying to prove yourself in week one.** Judge the switch over at least a couple of months — longer for forward to defence, longer still into goal ([Practice and Development](../off-the-ice/practice_and_development.md)). Being where you are supposed to be buys you time to be good later.
- **Abandoning the switch at the bottom of the dip.** Most people give up somewhere in weeks two to five — a coaching observation, not a measurement. Expect the dip and keep going.
- **Repeating "the centre covers the most ice" as though it were measured.** **Pass on positional claims as what coaches see, not as findings** — [Centre](center.md) shows that this one is half wrong once measured.

## Key Takeaways

1. **When you are moved, rebuild the map, not the skills.** Skating, scanning, puck protection and the rules come with you. Learn where to look, where to drift and who you pick up in your new spot. Almost none of this is measured, so read it as coaching craft.
2. **Before your first shift in a new position, ask your coach which system you are playing in** — zone or man-on-man in your own end, which forecheck, which backcheck lane. Each is a coaching choice, and each changes your job more than any technique.
3. **When you are lost, go to the spot that costs your team least and read the play from there** — goal side of the puck as a defenceman, the high slot as a centre, your lane as a winger. Under man-on-man, go goal side of your check. Chasing turns one mistake into two.
4. **Wing to centre is a coverage change first and a faceoff change second.** Under a low zone collapse your job moves with the puck. The hardest new habit is swinging low on the breakout, so your defenceman has a pass through the middle.
5. **Faceoffs are learnable, so take draws at the end of every practice.** ⚠️ **Get your depth from your knees and hips, never by pushing your head out over the dot** — in the IIHF book helmet-to-helmet contact before the drop can cost you the draw and a minor, but your head is the reason.
6. **Centre to wing is not a demotion.** Hold the outside third; in your own end stay between their defenceman on your side and the slot, or follow your check under man-on-man. At a faceoff, stand still, hands to yourself: moving in early, lining up offside or, in most books, touching an opponent costs your centre the dot.
7. **Moving to defence, book power skating first, play goal side, and never move the puck through your own slot:** go behind your net, or high above the circles if your team does. **Force a carrier outside with your route and take the puck with your stick:** ⚠️ **riding them into the wall is a body check, barred in many classifications — find out whether yours allows it.**
8. **If a carrier's numbers are toward you,** ⚠️ **do not hit them, whatever your league.** Called as checking from behind, it ends your game in every other book, and in USA Hockey's too if it is forceful along the boards, drives them head first, or is reckless (plan on a match and a likely multi-game suspension). Into the boards or goal frame with them unable to protect themselves, it is a mandatory match in several books.
9. **Moving up to forward, attack space before it is obviously safe** — by then it has closed. First to the puck on the forecheck, you are F1: go. Crossing the offensive blue line, keep your trailing skate on the ice; a raised one does not keep you onside under USA Hockey or Hockey Canada.
10. **If your goalie cannot continue, settle it on the scoresheet between your bench and the officials — never by picking up spare pads.** To try the net, get house gear, a goalie coach and a block of weeks. **Wear a neck laceration protector from your first time in net** — in the Home Countries it is mandatory at every age.
11. **On your off wing, take the breakout pass up the wall open to the ice:** hips open, a step or two off the boards. ⚠️ **Never turn your back to anyone coming at you, and never duck:** skates parallel, head up. Looking first and seeing nobody makes none of it safe: a head-down hit into the boards can injure your neck at walking speed.
12. **Your old good habits are the problem.** Name the one you are fighting out loud before the shift — under pressure, the automatic pattern is what surfaces.
13. **Judge a switch over a couple of months, not a couple of skates** — longer into defence, longer still into goal. Expect a dip; most people quit between weeks two and five, which is coaching observation, not measurement.
14. **Ask for a switch in private, with a specific question, and ask to try it in practice first.** "What would I need to show you to get a look at centre?" beats "Can I play centre?" Moved without asking? Ask what a good shift looks like in that spot, and tell your linemates you are new to it.

*Notes on the evidence: **(1)** There is no published evidence base for how players transition between hockey positions. **What was actually searched for that claim:** the four playing rulebooks (NHL 2025-2026, IIHF 2025/26, USA Hockey 2025-29, Hockey Canada 2026-2028) and two casebooks — the USA Hockey Official Rules and Casebook 2025-29 and the IIHF Situation Handbook 2025/26, the copy read here giving its own version as 1.1, August 2025. **The NHL and Hockey Canada casebooks are not held here and were not searched**, and no claim is made about them. Worth knowing about the gap: the IIHF handbook's Foreword says it "is based on the NHL Situation Handbook", which is some reason to expect the NHL book to answer similarly, but that book has not been read here and the inference is not evidence. **Of the four rulebooks and two casebooks that were searched, none is wholly silent.** Both casebooks, and the USA Hockey Playing Rules Glossary, treat the change between goalkeeper and skater; those passages are quoted in *Anyone → goaltender* rather than summarised away, and they are administration — who may be on the scoresheet as what, and what it costs to change it. What none of the six does, and what the scanning and zone-entry research, the body-contact and goaltending manuals and the USA Hockey ADM pages listed above do not do either, is say anything about how a switch is made, what carries over, or how long it takes. Searches covered the change/switch/interchange of position, position as a designation, goalkeeper and skater designation and substitution, and the developmental vocabulary (specialisation, long-term athlete development, learning a new position); the only playing-rulebook matches for "new position" were the NHL and IIHF faceoff-alignment clauses, which are a different subject. The one adjacent piece of governing-body developmental guidance is USA Hockey's 8U "no full-time goalies" recommendation, quoted in the text. Everything in this document not attached to a citation is therefore labelled coaching craft. **(2)** "The centre covers more ice than any other position" is repeated by essentially every coach. **A measured comparison of skating distance by position does exist, and it is already cited here** — Lignell et al. (2018), *J Strength Cond Res* 32(5):1303–1310, PMID 28557852, 36 NHL players, cited in [Centre](center.md) and quoted in [Conditioning and Recovery](../off-the-ice/conditioning_and_recovery.md) — and it contradicts the folklore: defencemen covered **29% more** skating in total and were on the ice **47% longer**, while forwards did **54% more** high-intensity skating per minute. What that study does *not* do, and what no published study does, is separate **centres from wingers**; the literature splits players only into forwards and defence. So the forwards-versus-defence half is measured and points the other way, and the centre-versus-winger half is untested craft. [Centre](center.md) covers the claim and states the defensible version — a centre works more *of* the ice, not more distance. A second, weaker figure points the same direction: **defencemen averaged 49.3 seconds per shift against 47.6 for forwards** in the 2025-26 NHL season, computed from the NHL's time-on-ice report and carried in full by [Defender](defender.md), not a published figure. Shift length is not distance skated, so treat that one as corroborating direction only. **(3)** The transition timelines in "The honest timeline" are craft with no source. They are given because a reader facing a switch needs some frame of reference, and they are labelled rather than omitted.*
