# Reading Ice Hockey Diagrams

> **Rule set:** **This document is not about rules at all** — it is about the symbols the
> diagrams in this guide are drawn with. Those symbols come from published coaching
> material rather than from any rulebook, and coaching material is not standardised the
> way a rulebook is. Two published keys are cited below and they disagree in places; where
> they do, it says so. Nothing here has been checked against how any particular club or
> coach actually draws things.
>

## Overview

Many of the documents here carry rink diagrams — a picture of the ice with players
and arrows on it. They use a notation borrowed from published coaching material, and
five minutes here will make every diagram in the rest of the guide readable at a
glance. You do not need to memorise it; come back when a symbol is unfamiliar.


These diagrams use a drill notation drawn from **two published symbol keys**. The
two disagree in places, so it is worth two minutes to know which is which.

![](diagram:notation-key)

**Where each symbol comes from.** Five of the eight line symbols — forward skating,
skating with the puck, passing, shooting and backward skating — are drawn the same
way in both keys, so those are uncontroversial. The other three are not:

- **A line ending in two bars** is called *stopping* here, following the Hockey
  Eastern Ontario sheet. The IIHF key draws that same glyph and calls it a **sudden
  stop**; its plain *stop* is a pair of slashes.
- **A line ending in one bar** is *checking pressure*, and comes from the Hockey
  Eastern Ontario sheet only. **The IIHF key has no such symbol** — and in it, one
  bar is half of a sudden stop.
- **Bars across a line** are *lateral crossovers*. The published glyph is a bare
  picket of bars with no line through it and no direction; the arrow and the line are added here, because a picket that does not say which way
  you are going is no use in a play diagram. The IIHF key's nearest equivalent is a different shape
  again.

**Players follow the IIHF key's axis.** A **circle is your own team; a triangle is
the opposition** — shape carries the team, which is IIHF *International Symbols*
§21.1's convention and *not* the Hockey Eastern Ontario sheet's, where shape carries
position instead. That matters more than it sounds: if you meet the HEO sheet
elsewhere, its triangles mean defencemen, not opponents. An **X is a pylon** — a
practice cone — and never a player, in both keys and here.

**Two departures on top of that**, so you are not caught out:

1. **The letters inside a glyph are roles, not positions.** The IIHF key puts a
   numeral inside — 1 goaltender, 2 right defence, 3 left defence, 4 right wing,
   5 centre, 6 left wing. They are not used here, because
   [F1, F2 and F3 are roles set by order of arrival and not people](../systems/forechecking_systems.md),
   and a numeral would say "F1 is the centre" where the text takes care to say it is
   not. So the glyph carries whatever label the surrounding section uses.
2. **Circle and triangle map to *your team* and *theirs*, not to *offensive* and
   *defensive*.** The IIHF key is written for a coach drawing both ends; these
   diagrams are drawn from your team's point of view, so you can tell at a glance
   which shape is you.

⚠️ **Do not call this notation "international" to anyone.** A key of that name exists
— it is one of the two above — and what is drawn here is not it. None of this has been
checked against how any particular club or coach actually draws things; if your
coach's whiteboard disagrees, their whiteboard wins.

**What a diagram is and is not.** A diagram shows one moment. A movement that only
makes sense over time needs two diagrams, so where you see a pair, read them as a
sequence or as two answers to the same instant — the captions say which. And a
picture is the most absolute-looking thing on a page: where a caption says a detail
is a coaching choice, believe the caption, not the picture.

## Common Mistakes

- **Reading a diagram as the way hockey is played.** A picture is the most
  absolute-looking thing on a page. Almost every diagram here shows one system among
  several, and the caption says so — believe the caption.
- **Taking a shape as a place to stand.** Positions in a diagram are drawn at one
  instant. Where the play moves, the shape moves with it.
- **Assuming your coach uses these symbols.** Nothing here has been checked against
  how any particular club draws things. If your coach's whiteboard disagrees, their
  whiteboard wins.
- **Expecting a diagram to show a whole play.** One frame shows one moment. Where a
  movement only makes sense over time you will find two diagrams, and the captions
  say whether they are a sequence or two answers to the same instant.

## Key Takeaways

1. **A circle is your own team; a triangle is the opposition.** Shape carries the
   team, so the diagrams work in either theme and without relying on colour.
2. **An X is a pylon — a practice cone — and never a player.**
3. **Solid arrow, forward skating. Long smooth wave, skating with the puck. Dashed
   arrow, a pass. Double line, a shot.** Those four cover most of what you will meet.
4. **The letters inside a glyph are roles, not positions.** F1 is whoever arrives
   first, not the centre.
5. **Where a caption says a detail is a coaching choice, it is.** Ask which version
   your team runs rather than taking the picture as the answer.
