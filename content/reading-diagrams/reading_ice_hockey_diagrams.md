# Reading Ice Hockey Diagrams

> **Rule set:** these symbols come from published coaching material, not a rulebook, and
> coaching keys are not standardised. The two keys cited below disagree in places; where
> they do, this page says so. Nothing here has been checked against how any particular
> club or coach actually draws things.
>
> **No symbol on this page tells you what your league permits.** The mark at the end of a
> route says where it stops, never whether you may make contact there. Whether you may
> body check at all depends on your age group, your league and your book. USA Hockey
> prohibits it in 12 & under, all girls' and women's classifications and all non-check
> adult classifications, and in any game against a team from one of those. Hockey Canada penalises it at U13 and below, in female
> hockey, *"and any other divisions approved by a Member of Hockey Canada"*. Read
> [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md) before acting on any
> route that finishes on a player.
>
> **Related:** [Rink Map](../foundation/rink_map.md) · [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md) · [Getting Started](../getting-started/getting_started.md) · [Forechecking Systems](../systems/forechecking_systems.md) · [Special Teams](../systems/special_teams.md)

## Key focus

**Read the caption first, and believe it over the picture.** A diagram freezes one moment of one system among several. The caption says what the picture is for, which details are a coaching choice, and what a shaded region means — so ask which version your team runs.

**Find yourself next.** Shape is the position — a circle is a forward, a triangle a defenceman, a bare G the goalie — and fill is the team: open is yours, solid is theirs. The letter inside is your role: if you reach the puck first on the forecheck, you are F1. Some coaches assign F1 by position, so check.

**Follow numbered routes in order, and read how each one ends.** A numbered tag gives the order things happen, never who does them. An arrowhead means the move carries on; two short bars mean you stop there, under control.

**Keep the relationship, not the spot.** A drawn position is where a player is relative to the puck and the other players at one instant. As the puck moves, keep that relationship rather than skating to the drawn mark and staying there.

**Treat a route that ends on an opponent as "arrive under control", never as permission to hit.** Whether you may body check at all depends on your age group, your league and your book. Find out first, and until you know, do not hit anyone.

**Check the key on anyone else's diagram — and on your own team, the coach's whiteboard wins.** The IIHF's own key uses the two shapes for the two *teams*, so a defending right wing there is a triangle. Where no key is printed, ask whoever drew it.

## Overview

A diagram is only useful if you can turn it into what you do on your next shift. It is
the most absolute-looking thing on a page, but it shows one moment of one system, and
the caption is where it says so — including whether a shaded box is ice to head for or
ice a pass must never cross. Where the caption and your eye disagree, the caption wins.

Shape and fill carry two separate facts, so you can find yourself in any picture, in
print or in greyscale; a winger stays a circle whether attacking or backchecking. The
letters are roles, so the route you play is set by what happens, not by the name on
your line-up card. The positions are relationships: the drawn spot is only where the
player was at one instant.

**A route that finishes on an opponent is not an instruction to hit.** Many youth,
women's and non-check adult leagues ban body checking outright, so find out what yours
allows before you play one.

Last, keys differ. Shape as position follows one published key, and reading fill as the
team is this guide's own choice; the IIHF key uses the same two shapes for the two
teams instead. Colour carries nothing except the red tint on a hazard region, so a
hazard's caption says it in words. You do not need to memorise the rest; come back when
a symbol is unfamiliar.

## Where each symbol comes from

**In short:** Most line symbols here follow the IIHF key; do not assume another diagram's key matches this one.

The notation draws on **two published symbol keys**, and one symbol is compared against a
third source: a coaching manual with its own house key, not a governing body's. The two
keys disagree in places, so it is worth knowing which is which.

![](diagram:notation-key)

Six line symbols are drawn the same way in both keys: forward skating, skate and
stickhandle, passing, shooting, the drop pass and the two-bar mark. **The keys often
name a symbol differently even where they draw it the same** — the wave is *skate and
stickhandle* on one and *skating with control of the puck* on the other. Where this
guide had to pick one name, the bullets below say which.

**Backward skating differs.** The Hockey Eastern Ontario sheet draws it as a tight wave;
the IIHF key draws a row of separate overlapping arches, flatter than they are wide.
These diagrams follow the IIHF key. **A sharp zigzag — straight lines and corners, not
curves — is backward crossovers**, also the IIHF key's.

**A bare hook, curling in low and leaving the other way, is a pivot**: turning from
forwards to backwards or back again. Both keys have it and draw it differently — the
Hockey Eastern Ontario sheet runs a wave into the hook — and these diagrams use the IIHF
key's hook. It is in the key above but in no play diagram, because this notation cannot
draw which way a player is facing. The rest:

- **A line ending in two bars** is a **sudden stop** — the IIHF key's name, and the one
  used here. The Hockey Eastern Ontario sheet draws the same mark and calls it
  *stopping*. The IIHF key's plain *stop*, a pair of slashes, is a different mark and is
  drawn here too.
- **A line ending in one bar is deliberately not used here.** It means *checking
  pressure* on the Hockey Eastern Ontario sheet and *screen, pick, or block* in a
  coaching manual (Johnston & Walter, *Hockey Plays and Strategies*, 2nd ed., 2019),
  **and the IIHF key has no such symbol** — one bar is *half* of its two-bar sudden
  stop. Draw one bar for pressure and an IIHF-trained reader sees a player stopping
  where you meant a player closing. It is the clearest reason not to assume another key
  matches this one.
- **A route that closes on an opponent ends in the two-bar mark, not an arrowhead** —
  the one place these diagrams knowingly draw one glyph for two things. An arrowhead
  reads as carrying on past the end of the line; the bars read as ending there. Where the
  end point is an opponent, read the bars as **arrive under control**, and let the
  caption carry the rest. The IIHF key does have a mark for a hit — a line ending in a
  small curled bracket, named *body check* — but that tells you what the drill's author
  had in mind, not what your league allows. Where body checking is banned, a hit is a
  penalty, and a hard one can get you thrown out of the game.
- **Bars across a line** are *lateral crossovers* — **the one line symbol here taken
  from the Hockey Eastern Ontario sheet**, because the IIHF key has no lateral-crossover
  symbol. (The player glyphs come from that sheet too; most line symbols are the IIHF
  key's.) It is kept because a barred line collides with nothing in the IIHF key. The
  published glyph is a bare picket of bars with no direction; the line and arrow are
  added here so it says which way you are going. The IIHF key's nearest equivalent is a
  different shape again.
- **Dropping the puck follows neither key.** Both draw a wave — the carrier skating with
  the puck — then a marker, then a straight line and an arrowhead. These diagrams leave
  out the wave, so the mark shows the drop and not the carry before it. It is this
  guide's own version.

## What a tinted area or a faint dashed line means

**In short:** A tint names a region, not paint on the ice or an instruction; read the caption for what to do there.

**Shaded regions and faint dashed lines are this guide's own; neither published key has
anything like them.** Many diagrams wash an area in a light tint with a thin dashed
edge, usually named inside — *the slot*, *the house*, *home plate*, *the box*, *the
passing lane*. The Hockey Eastern Ontario sheet's seventeen rows are players, lines and
a cluster of pucks, and the IIHF key's list runs from *players* to *body check* with no
region mark in it. Four things are worth knowing.

First, **the tint is not paint on the ice unless the caption says it is.** The slot, the
house and the high slot are names for regions, and [Rink Map](../foundation/rink_map.md)
defines their edges. The edge is drawn thin and broken so it cannot be taken for a line
on the rink. The trapezoid is the exception: it really is painted, and its caption says
so.

Second, **the shading does not say whether to go there or stay out.** The caption does:
one shaded box is the ice a centre heads for, another is ice a pass must never cross. A
hazard is tinted red rather than blue — the one thing in this notation carried by colour
alone, and nearly lost in greyscale or a photocopy — so read the caption.

Third, **the same dashed mark between two points, with no tint, is a line rather than a
region**, with a short name beside it. It marks something you need to see that is
neither on the ice nor a route — the **royal road**, the imaginary line from one net to
the other that a pass crosses to make a goalie push hard sideways
([Shooting](../technique/shooting.md#the-royal-road) owns it), or the shooting lane a
defenceman had a second ago. Nobody skates it; it shows what someone else is set up for.

Fourth, **where one region is tinted more heavily than another, the depth is a ranking.**
The turnover-cost map in [Risk Management](../hockey-iq/risk_management.md) marks five
regions, four red and one blue, and the four reds carry only **three** strengths.
⚠️ **Two are equal on purpose** — the middle of the neutral zone is shaded as heavily as
your own end, because a mistake in either costs about the same — **so do not expect four
distinguishable reds or try to rank them by eye.** Red is still the hazard tint and blue
still the ice you want; only the depth carries the order, and neither key has anything
like it.

So read the order from the caption, not the page. The tints are close by design,
greyscale and photocopying compress them further, and across red and blue the order
inverts outright, because a light blue prints darker than the faintest red. A graded
picture names its regions in order in words, and the words are the part to trust.

## Shape, fill and what goes inside a glyph

**In short:** Shape is the position and fill is the team: a circle is a forward, a triangle a defenceman, open yours, solid theirs.

**Shape is the position.** The *International Drill Symbols* sheet gives it in two
lines — *"● ○ Forward / Player"* and *"▲ △ Defender / Player"* — so a **circle is a
forward and a triangle is a defenceman**, in every picture. A **goalie is a bare G**,
with no shape round it, because a circle would say forward and a triangle defenceman.
Both keys offer that form; the IIHF key also draws a goalie as the numeral 1 inside a
team glyph, which this guide does not use.

**The IIHF key does this differently, and it will catch you out.** Its columns read
*TEAMS — OFFENSIVE / DEFENSIVE*, with circles in one and triangles in the other, and the
**position as a numeral inside** — 1 goalie, 2 right defence, through 6 left wing.
⚠️ **On that key a right wing on the defending team is a triangle.** These diagrams follow
the Hockey Eastern Ontario sheet.

**Fill is the team.** An **open** glyph is your own team and a **solid** one the
opposition. The key prints a solid and an open version of each player row without saying
what the pair means — *"● ○ Forward / Player"* could as easily read as solid-is-a-forward
and open-is-any-player — so reading it as the two teams is this guide's doing, not the
key's. Shape says what a player is; fill says whose side they are on. Neither depends on
colour, so neither is lost in greyscale, in a photocopy, or to a reader who does not see
red and green apart.

**Which side has the puck is not carried by the shapes** — the puck is drawn, and the
caption says the rest. It is **a small solid dot**, about a third the width of a player,
and it is this guide's own mark: neither key has a symbol for a single puck. Do not
confuse it with the marker on a drop-pass line, which sits *on* a route rather than
beside a player.

**Two departures on top of that**, so you are not caught out:

1. **The letters inside a glyph are roles, not positions.** The IIHF key's numerals — 1
   goalie, 2 right defence, 3 left defence, 4 right wing, 5 centre, 6 left wing — are
   not used, because
   [F1, F2 and F3 are roles set by order of arrival and not people](../systems/forechecking_systems.md),
   and a numeral would say "F1 is the centre". **One label collides:** a circled `C`
   means *coach* on both published keys and a centre here. No diagram in this guide draws
   a coach, but on someone else's diagram a circled `C` is the person on the bench.
2. **Reading fill as the team, and open as "you", are this guide's choices.** The key
   also has no opposition symbol of any kind.

**And one thing that is almost not a departure:** the player glyphs and line symbols are
monochrome, and none of the keys consulted separates the teams by colour either. So a
printed page, a photocopy and a reader who does not see red and green apart get the same
information as the screen — except for the red tint on a hazard region, where the
caption has to carry the warning.

## The word "international"

*Both* keys carry "international" in their titles — *International Drill Symbols* and
*International Symbols* — and what is drawn here is neither of them, ⚠️ **so do not call
this notation "international" to anyone.** The one-bar glyph shows why: pressure on one
key, a screen in a coaching manual, undefined on the IIHF key. Each meaning is right
inside its own system; they are not the same system. If your coach's whiteboard
disagrees with this page, their whiteboard wins.

## Numbered tags on a route

**Numbered tags say what happened first, and they count routes, not players.** A still
picture cannot say whether the pass beat the forechecker, so where the order matters each
route carries a small numbered tag a short way along it. A tag is never a player's name
and never sits inside a player's shape, where the letters go. Like the shading, it is
this guide's own and appears in neither key.

## What a diagram is and is not

A diagram shows one moment. A movement that only makes sense over time needs two, so
read a pair as a sequence or as two answers to the same instant — the captions say
which. Where a caption says a detail is a coaching choice, believe the caption, not the
picture.

## Common Mistakes

- **Reading a diagram as the way hockey is played.** Almost every diagram here shows one
  system among several. Believe the caption, then ask which version your team runs.
- **Skating to the drawn spot and staying there.** Positions are drawn at one instant.
  Keep your relationship to the puck and the other players as the play moves.
- **Assuming a key you meet elsewhere means the same thing.** A circle is a forward here
  and the attacking team on the IIHF's key; a single bar is pressure on one key, a screen
  in a coaching manual and undefined on the IIHF's. Check the key first, and where none
  is printed, ask whoever drew it.
- **Reading the letter inside a glyph as where you stand.** Letters are roles: when you
  are first to the puck on the forecheck, do F1's job whichever forward position you
  play. Some coaches assign it by position
  ([Forechecking Systems](../systems/forechecking_systems.md) has the fuller case). On
  someone else's key, a circled `C` is the coach.
- **Assuming your coach uses these symbols.** Nothing here has been checked against how
  any club draws things. Their whiteboard wins; ask about a new mark before the drill
  starts.
- **Reading a route that ends on an opponent as permission to hit.** The two bars say
  where you stop, under control — never whether you may make contact. Many youth,
  women's and non-check adult leagues ban body checking. Find out what yours allows, and
  until you know, do not hit anyone;
  [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md) sets out the books.
- **Looking for a shaded region painted on the ice.** The slot and the house are names
  for areas, not paint. Learn them from [Rink Map](../foundation/rink_map.md) and find
  them from the painted circles, dots and crease. Only the trapezoid is painted, and its
  caption says so.
- **Ranking regions by how dark they look.** Take the order from the caption's words. On
  the turnover-cost map in [Risk Management](../hockey-iq/risk_management.md) two reds
  are equal on purpose, and in greyscale or a photocopy red against blue inverts.
- **Expecting one diagram to show a whole play.** One frame is one moment. For a pair,
  read the caption first: it says whether they are a sequence or two answers to the same
  instant.

## Key Takeaways

1. **Read in this order: the caption, your own position, the routes in number order,
   then where each route ends.** An arrowhead carries on past that point, two short
   bars stop there under control, and a backward-skating route simply ends where it is
   drawn.
2. **A circle is a forward, a triangle a defenceman, a bare G the goalie; open is
   your team, solid the opposition.** A backchecking winger is still a circle. Fill as
   the team is this guide's convention, and ⚠️ **the IIHF's key uses the two shapes for
   the two teams instead**, so do not carry it onto IIHF diagrams.
3. **An X is a pylon — a practice cone — and never a player.**
4. **Solid arrow, forward skating. Long smooth wave, skate and stickhandle. Overlapping
   flattened arches, skating backwards. Sharp zigzag, backward crossovers. Dashed arrow,
   a pass. Double line, a shot. Two short bars, stop there — on an opponent, arrive
   under control, never a licence to hit.**
5. **A tinted area with a dashed edge is a named region; the same mark with no tint is a
   line that is not on the ice.** The caption, not the tint, says whether to go there or
   keep out. Where tints differ in strength, that is a ranking: take it from the
   caption's words, not your eye.
6. **A small numbered tag gives the order of routes**, never a player, and never sits
   inside a player's shape.
7. **The letters are roles: when you reach the puck first on the forecheck, the F1 route
   is yours.** Some coaches assign F1 by position, so check how yours does it.
8. **A drawn position is a relationship to the puck and the other players, not a spot
   to skate to.**
9. **Where a caption says a detail is a coaching choice, it is** — ask which version
   your team runs.
10. **On anyone else's diagram, check the key; on your team, the coach's whiteboard
   wins**, so ask about any new mark before the drill. No key says whether
   you may hit — your age group, league and book do; until you know, hit nobody. Where
   checking is banned, a hit is a penalty and a hard one can get you thrown out of the
   game.
