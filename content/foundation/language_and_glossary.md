## Overview

Hockey's working vocabulary is small, and almost all of it is **relative**. "High", "low", "strong side", "F1" and "the wall" do not name fixed places or fixed people. They name a position relative to the net that matters right now, the side the puck is on right now, or the order players happened to arrive in. The same patch of ice is high to one player and low to another, and it changes again when the puck moves.

That is why a beginner can do exactly what they were told and end up in the wrong half of the rink. The fix is not to memorise more words. It is to learn, for each word, **what it is relative to** — and, for the handful that mean genuinely different things to a centre, a winger, a defenceman and a goaltender, to learn which sense is yours.

This page collects those words, and it has two halves. The first works through them by theme — direction, the named areas, roles, pressure, moving the puck, and the goaltender's own language — because the terms that shift meaning depending on who is speaking only make sense set beside each other. The second is an alphabetical [Glossary](#glossary), from *1-3-1* to *zone entry*, for looking a word up when you have just heard it. **Bold** does two jobs on this page. Where it marks the *name of a thing*, that thing is defined either in the section discussing it or in that glossary. Where it marks a phrase that is not the name of anything, it is doing the ordinary work of emphasis, and there is nothing to look up.

[Rink Map](rink_map.md) is the other half of the vocabulary and the place to start: every line and marking and what it does, the three zones, and every named area of the ice, drawn to scale with the dimensions behind them. Read that once and everything here gets easier.

## Direction, and what it is relative to

### High and low

- **High** means closer to the blue line you are facing, away from the net.
- **Low** means closer to the goal line, toward the net.

This holds in all three zones, and it is always relative to the net that matters in that moment. In the **offensive** zone, "high" means back toward the line you came in over — so a forward rotating high is climbing away from the goal they are attacking. In your **own** end, "low" is toward your own goal line, because that is the only goal line in play there. Do not carry that narrower reading into the offensive zone, where "low" means toward the net you are shooting at.

For a **defenceman**, almost every instruction about your own positioning is a height instruction, in both zones: hold the line, stay above the puck, and do not go below the circles in the offensive zone without a forward taking your place at the line first.

For a **winger**, the height instruction you will hear most is "stay high", which means two different things to you and has its own section below.

For a **centre**, it is "swing low".

### Swing low

A **centre** hears this more than any other player, and it is more specific than it sounds: it means get **below the hash marks** — the short red lines level with the faceoff dot, roughly a third of the way from the goal line to the blue line — not merely below the puck. Getting underneath the puck by a stick length is not swinging low.

### Stay high

**For a winger this means two different jobs at almost the same place on the ice, so check which one is meant.**

- **In your own end** it means covering the opposing **point** — the area just inside your blue line where their defencemen stand — on your side while the other team has the puck, level with that defenceman and not halfway to the corner.
- **In a flat or spread breakout** it means holding near the blue line instead.

The first sense **expires**. The moment your team wins the puck, the winger covering the point comes down the wall to the **outlet** spot — where the first pass out of the zone is received — between the hash marks and the goal line. Nothing announces the switch; winning the puck is the switch.

That first sense is also the **low zone collapse** job — the zone defensive system, in which each player covers an area of ice rather than an opponent, assumed as the default throughout this guide. It is a coaching choice rather than a law of hockey. Under **man-on-man** you follow your check wherever they go, the corner included, and there is no height to hold. Find out which your team plays.

There is a third sense, and it is the plain one. Said of **F3** — the third forward to arrive on a forecheck — "stay high" carries neither winger meaning: it is the directional word doing its ordinary job, toward the blue line you are facing, with the third forward staying above the puck as the safety valve. See [Winger](../positions/winger.md) and [Switching Positions](../positions/switching_positions.md).

### Strong side and weak side

**Strong side** is the side of the ice the puck is currently on. **Weak side** is the other. They flip the instant the puck crosses the middle.

What changes between positions is not the definition. It is **how often the label moves, and what it obliges you to do.**

- **For a defence pair they are job titles rather than descriptions** — under a **low zone collapse**, the zone defensive system assumed as the default throughout this guide, and a coaching choice rather than a law of hockey. There the strong-side defenceman plays the puck and the weak-side defenceman holds the goalmouth, and the two swap every time the puck crosses the middle, which is why defence partners talk constantly. Under **man-on-man** neither of them holds a layer: each takes the player they are assigned and goes where that player goes. Find out which your team plays.
- **For a centre they switch just as often**, in the same system. A centre's defensive-zone job changes side every time the puck does, exactly as the pair's does.
- **For a winger the label usually holds for a whole sequence** rather than switching every few seconds — and the two jobs are genuinely different. The strong-side winger takes the puck up the wall; the weak-side winger collapses to cover the slot.

In [Neutral Zone Systems](../systems/neutral_zone_systems.md), **D1** and **D2** mean the strong-side and the weak-side defenceman for that sequence. They do not mean the left and the right defenceman.

### Your lane

The ice is divided lengthwise into three lanes: the **strong-side boards lane**, the **middle lane** and the **weak-side boards lane**. The usual default puts one player in each, which for a **winger** means the outside third on their side, for the centre the middle third, and for the other winger the far third.

"Stay in your lane" is a spacing instruction, not a personality one. Three forwards in three lanes stretch a defence across the whole width of the rink; three forwards in one lane can be covered by two defenders.

**One player per lane is a coaching choice rather than a law**, and the realistic alternative stacks a lane deliberately: a **1-3-1 regroup** puts two players in the middle lane to overload the seam and pull the opposing defence inward, accepting that one checker can see both. Some teams also send the weak-side winger out of their lane to press. **Find out whether your team spreads or stacks** — the two look identical for the first stride and then ask for opposite decisions. [Puck Support and Spacing](../hockey-iq/puck_support_and_spacing.md) sets out the lanes and the alternative.

## The named areas, and who owns them

The names below are terminology and they do not change. **Who covers each area is a coaching choice, and it does.** [Rink Map](rink_map.md) draws every one of them to scale.

### The slot, the high slot and the goalmouth

- **The slot** is the ice directly in front of the net, between the two faceoff circles, running from the tops of the circles down toward the goalmouth. **It is where most goals are scored from** — [Shooting](../technique/shooting.md) owns the shot-location figures and the qualifications on every one of them. You will hear "the most dangerous ice on the rink" said of the slot, and you will also hear it said of **home plate**, which is a wider shape, and of the **inner slot** nearest the crease, which is a narrower one. Treat it as the shape those figures agree on rather than as a measured ranking of one region against another.
- **The high slot** is its upper layer, from the level of the faceoff dots up to the top of the circles. This is the shooting layer: a player unmarked here gets a clean look with time to pick a corner.
- **The low slot** is the layer beneath it, from the dots down toward the goalmouth and outside the crease. Tips, rebounds and screen position live here, and it is the ice a net-front player fights for. [Rink Map](rink_map.md) owns the scoring evidence for it, and the limits on what has actually been counted.
- **The goalmouth** is the immediate net front, right at the edge of the crease. It is a much smaller area, and it is the layer *below* the slot rather than a part of it.

**Who covers each layer is not terminology.** Under a **low zone collapse** — the zone defensive system assumed as the default throughout this guide — the **high slot belongs to the centre** and the **goalmouth belongs to the weak-side defenceman**. Two layers, two owners.

Two things qualify that, and both matter more than the split itself.

- **The centre's layer passes to the weak-side winger collapsing down whenever the centre goes to help in the corner.** That is a handoff to agree before it happens rather than during it.
- **Under man-on-man nobody holds a layer at all.** Each belongs to whoever's check is standing in it, and you go with your check wherever they go. Under a **hybrid** — man-on-man below the dots, zone above — the net front belongs to whichever of the low three is matched to the net-front forward.

**Find out which system your team plays.** [Defensive Zone Coverage](../systems/defensive_zone_coverage.md) sets out each one.

Either way, keeping the two layers apart is most of defensive-zone centre play, and confusing them is how net-front goals happen — coaching observation rather than a counted ranking. **A defender who leaves the goalmouth to cover the slot has left a tap-in unmarked.** They are two different jobs one layer apart.

The high slot is also where the dangerous passes out of the neutral zone are aimed — at somebody arriving into it, rather than at somebody already standing there.

### The half-wall

The boards roughly level with the faceoff dot, between the corner and the point. There is one on each side of each end zone. You will also hear it called the **half-boards**; it is the same place under another name.

**Two positions are told different things about it. They are two halves of one spacing rule rather than a contradiction.**

- **For a winger the half-wall is home base** — in offensive-zone possession and on the power play. From there you can see the point, the slot, the corner and the net front.
- **For a centre at even strength it is not your ice.** It is where the wingers live, and drifting onto it collapses your own line's spacing — a common way for a centre to do that, as a coaching observation rather than a counted ranking.
- **On the power play that second instruction does not hold.** The two half-wall spots there usually go to forwards, and which forward is a question of shot handedness — ideally the off-wing, so the puck sits on the inside hand — rather than of position. A centre in an umbrella or an overload is often low or on the strong-side wall instead. [Special Teams](../systems/special_teams.md) sets out who plays where in each formation; ask where your unit puts you.

The half-wall is **not** the point. The point is up at the blue line; the half-wall is level with the dot, roughly twenty feet from the goal line, far below it.

### The point

The area **just inside the blue line**, toward each set of boards, where the defencemen set up in the offensive zone. **It is an area, not the line itself.**

- For a **defenceman** it is where you live in the offensive zone, and where the opposition's defencemen stand in yours.
- For a **winger**, **covering the point** — denying the opposing defenceman the time and space to shoot or walk in — is your defensive-zone job under a **low zone collapse**, the zone system assumed as the default throughout this guide. That is a coaching choice rather than a law of hockey. Under **man-on-man** the opposing defenceman is still yours, but the ice is not: you follow them wherever they go, into the corner if that is where they go, and there is no point to hold. Find out which your team plays.

Those are the same job seen from two ends of the rink. The winger covering the point is doing to an opposing defenceman exactly what an opposing winger is doing to yours at the other end.

### The top of the circle

The edge of a faceoff circle furthest from the goal line, roughly thirty-five feet out. It is a common landmark for where a shot becomes dangerous, and the standard depth marker for a defenceman supporting a **cycle** in the offensive zone. [Rink Map](rink_map.md) has the full entry.

## Roles, not people

### F1, F2 and F3

**F1, F2 and F3 are roles defined by order of arrival. They are not positions, and they are not people.** F1 is whoever reaches the puck first, F2 the second support, F3 the highest and the safety. They change the moment the order of arrival does.

F1 is *often* the centre, and much coaching material describes that common case. But if a winger gets there first, **the winger is F1 and the centre is F2 — immediately, and without swapping back** once the sequence is under way. Communicate, so the line moves as a unit and nobody is left guessing.

⚠️ **They belong to whichever team is forechecking, which is not always yours.** In [Forechecking Systems](../systems/forechecking_systems.md) the numbers describe you, because you are the team applying the pressure. In [Breakouts](../systems/breakouts.md) they describe **the opposition**, because a breakout is what you run against their forecheck. Read the numbering off whichever team is chasing the puck.

### D-partner

The other defenceman on your shift. Most of what [Defender](../positions/defender.md) teaches is really about the pair as a unit rather than about either defenceman alone.

### Checker, and "your check"

⚠️ **The word runs both ways in hockey, and the two senses point at opposite players.**

- In [Passing and Receiving](../technique/passing_and_receiving.md), **the checker** is the opponent currently pressuring *you*.
- Elsewhere here, and in most coaching language, **"your check"** is the opponent *you* are responsible for covering.

**Both usages are common.** Take the direction from the sentence around it.

## Pressure, and which end of it you are on

### Forecheck, backcheck, and the neutral zone forecheck

- **Forechecking** is pressuring the team that has the puck in **their** own end, trying to win it back rather than retreat.
- **Backchecking** is racing back toward your own end to defend after your team loses the puck. It is the opposite job, not a variant of the same one.
- A **neutral zone forecheck** is the structure your five players hold across the middle third while the opponent tries to break out. It is not a forecheck in the strict sense, because it is not in their end — but that is the name it goes by, so it is used that way here.

**The shape names are shared between the two, and the ice they describe is a hundred feet apart.** **2-1-2**, **1-2-2**, **1-3-1** and **1-4** each name an in-their-end forecheck *and* a neutral-zone one; the 1-3-1, the 1-4 and the retreated 1-2-2 are usually played as the neutral-zone version, and a 1-4 puts four skaters about 114 feet from the net they are supposedly forechecking. Ask which your coach means. [Forechecking Systems](../systems/forechecking_systems.md) sets out the in-their-end version and [Neutral Zone Systems](../systems/neutral_zone_systems.md) the middle-third one.

⚠️ **Which team is forechecking depends on which end of the play you are being taught.** [Forechecking Systems](../systems/forechecking_systems.md) describes your forecheck, in their end. [Breakouts](../systems/breakouts.md) describes the same pressure arriving in *your* defensive zone and trying to stop your exit. One play, two points of view.

### Breakout

Your team's structured exit from your own zone with possession. [Breakouts](../systems/breakouts.md) names nine — up, **wheel**, **reverse**, **D-to-D**, **rim**, over, quick-up, **stretch pass** and off the glass — each chosen by a read rather than ranked.

⚠️ Seen from the other side it is the thing you are trying to break: in [Forechecking Systems](../systems/forechecking_systems.md), "breakout" means the **opponents'** organised attempt to carry or pass the puck out of their own end.

### Angling, steering and sealing

- **Angling** is forcing an opponent to go in a direction you have chosen, normally toward the boards or the outside. It is the foundation of forechecking, and where body checking is barred it is the whole toolkit rather than a beginner's version of hitting. [Forechecking Systems](../systems/forechecking_systems.md) owns the skill, its sources and the rules that bound it in each rulebook.
- **Steering** is shadowing the carrier to push them into an area where you can take away their time and space.
- **Sealing** is arriving second and closing the escape route the first forechecker has forced them toward.

## Moving the puck

### Tape-to-tape

A pass that arrives flat on the receiver's stick blade, on the taped part, ready to be played immediately.

### The blade, its heel and its toe

The **blade** is the flat part of your stick that touches the ice. Its **heel** is the end nearest the shaft; its **toe** is the far end. Where the puck sits on the blade changes what a shot does and how quickly it leaves.

### Cupping

Tilting the top edge of the blade slightly forward over the puck, so the face is closed rather than open to the sky. On a pass that is what makes the puck arrive flat; on a shot it is what stops the puck sliding off the blade. [Puck Handling](../technique/puck_handling.md) covers the technique.

### Support

A teammate positioning themselves to receive a pass from you.

### Release

The moment the puck leaves your blade — and, by extension, how long your shooting motion takes from first movement to that moment.

### Flex

How much your stick shaft bends. A bent shaft stores energy and springs it back into the puck, like a drawn bow.

## The goaltender's vocabulary

Goaltending has its own precise vocabulary, and these terms recur throughout [Goaltender](../positions/goaltender.md). They are worth a skater's time as well, because several of them name the thing you are trying to beat. The words below are the ones about *choice*. The places, the equipment and the things done to a goaltender — **the paint**, the **five-hole**, the **post**, the **blocker**, the **back door**, the **screen**, **traffic** and the **butterfly** — are in the [Glossary](#glossary).

### Depth

How far out of the net a goaltender is, measured along the line from the puck to the middle of the goal.

### Square

Chest, hips and shoulders facing directly at the puck, with the middle of the body on the line from the puck to the centre of the net.

### Challenging, staying deep, playing back

**Challenging** is coming out toward the shooter to reduce how much net they can see. The opposite is **staying deep**, or **playing back**.

### Save selection

Choosing which save to make. The word "selection" is doing real work in that phrase: it names a decision rather than a reflex.

### Reading the situation

Recognising where every player and the puck are, and deciding from that where to be and which save to use. **A goaltender does not play a series of shots; they play situations, many of which never end in a shot at all.**

## Glossary

Alphabetical. Where an entry's name starts with the word *the*, it is filed under the word that follows — so *the paint* sits under P, and *the trap* under T. Most entries run to a sentence or two; a substantial minority carry a rule, or a difference between rulebooks, and run longer because the detail is the point. Where a **term** appears in bold inside a definition, it is defined elsewhere — in this list, in the sections above, or on [Rink Map](rink_map.md), which holds the lines, the markings, the three zones and the named areas of the ice. **Bold also marks plain emphasis here**, so a bolded phrase that is not the name of a thing is not a term to go and look up. Where a word already has a section of its own above, the entry points at it rather than defining it a second time.

**1-3-1** — three different formations share these digits, and all three appear in this guide. On the **power play** it is one point, two half-walls, a **bumper** in the slot and a net-front player. In the **neutral zone** it is a trap posture — one forechecker, three across the middle, one back. As a **forecheck** it is the modern trap-adjacent shape pressuring in the opponent's end. Different zones, different shapes, same three digits: context tells you which, and if it does not, ask. See [Special Teams](../systems/special_teams.md), [Neutral Zone Systems](../systems/neutral_zone_systems.md) and [Forechecking Systems](../systems/forechecking_systems.md).

**Activation** — a defenceman joining the attack as an extra forward. No rule assigns a skater to a position or a zone, so a defenceman going up ice is bound by **offside** and the rest of the book exactly as any skater is; the coaching constraint is that a forward back-fills so someone stays **above the puck**, and [Defender](../positions/defender.md) lists activating without that cover as a common mistake. Trailing late raises how much risk a team will *accept*, and it is not on its own a reason for you to go: what removes the cover requirement is a **bench call** — a full-ice press, a delayed penalty, 6-on-5 — and not your own read of the scoreboard. See also [Offensive Zone Play](../systems/offensive_zone_play.md) for the three depths.

**Attack triangle** — the shape three forwards make during an established offensive-zone possession when one is *below* the puck, one *level* with it and one *above* it, giving the attack width and depth at the same time. This is a coaching choice and not a law of hockey: an **overload** loading one side, a five-man rotation and a **1-3-1** spread are all real alternatives, so find out which your team plays. See [Offensive Zone Play](../systems/offensive_zone_play.md).

**Backcheck** — racing back toward your own end to defend after your team loses the puck. It is the opposite job to a **forecheck**, not a variant of it. Set out in [Forecheck, backcheck, and the neutral zone forecheck](#forecheck-backcheck-and-the-neutral-zone-forecheck) above.

**Backdoor** — the **weak-side** net-front area, usually the far post. A backdoor pass goes cross-ice to a player arriving there unmarked, behind the goaltender's line of sight. Goaltenders use the phrase for something related but not identical: when a goaltender challenges a shooter, the shooter is the *front door*, and the space opened up behind them — usually toward the far side of the net — is the *back door*. One sense is fixed by which side the puck is on, the other by where the goaltender is standing, so they cover the same ice most of the time but not by definition. Every positioning decision a goaltender makes trades the front door against the back door. The shouted call **"Back door!"** is narrower than either: an unmarked opponent at the far post, to be taken *now*. See [Goaltender](../positions/goaltender.md) and [On-Ice Communication](on_ice_communication.md).

**Blocker** — the rectangular pad on a goaltender's stick hand. The catching mitt on the other hand is the *glove* or *trapper*. A shot "blocker side" is to the goaltender's stick hand, not their glove. See [Goaltender](../positions/goaltender.md).

**Board battle** — a contest for a loose puck against the boards. Body position and leverage often beat strength — but not always, and a large enough strength gap wins anyway. That leverage beats strength is coaching consensus rather than a measured law: nothing found in the ice hockey biomechanics literature quantifies it. See [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md).

**Boards** (also **the wall**) — the perimeter wall around the rink, 40–48 inches high. Always in play. That is the sense of "the wall" in "on the wall", "off the wall", "along the wall", "the left wall" and "the strong-side wall", and "on the wall" means playing tight to them. **But "the wall" names a second thing you will also hear** — a line of skaters strung across the ice denying a lane, which is the sense in "behind the wall", "the flat wall", "the three-man line" and "a wall of four". The preposition usually tells you which one you are hearing: you play a puck *off* or *along* the boards, and you get *behind* a line of skaters.

**Breakout** — your team's structured exit from your own zone with possession. Seen from the other end of the play it is the thing the forecheck is trying to break. Set out in [its own section above](#breakout), which names the nine kinds and what the word means from that other side.

**Bumper** — the player in the **middle** of a **1-3-1** power play, sitting in the slot between the point and the net-front player. *Not the same as* the **net-front** player, who is at the goalmouth. A true **umbrella** has no bumper.

**Butterfly** — a goaltender dropping to their knees with both pads extended sideways and the **five-hole** closed. It covers the great majority of the lower net, which is the usual explanation given for its place as the base save of modern goaltending. It is a **save selection, not a style**: go down when going down is the answer. See [Goaltender](../positions/goaltender.md).

**Chip** — a short, deliberate flip of the puck past a defender into space, usually off the boards or the glass, for a teammate to skate onto. The same word covers chipping the puck *out* — past a forechecker and clear of your own zone — where there may be no teammate to receive it at all.

**Crease** — the light-blue ice in front of the net where a goaltender operates. Keep your skates out of it and live just outside it — **and under USA Hockey your stick as well, which its Rule 625(b) names beside your skates**. Its other name is **the paint**, and that entry — filed under P, per the rule above — carries the dimensions, that stick limb, and which of the four books count the marked red line around it as part of the crease.

**Cycle** — offensive-zone possession maintained by rotating forwards around the low zone — corner, **half-wall**, *behind* the net — passing short and low-to-low while protecting the puck, rather than shooting. Wears defenders out and pulls coverage out of shape. It is a set-up, not an outcome: a low-to-high with a screen is one of the things that *ends* a cycle. See [Offensive Zone Play](../systems/offensive_zone_play.md).

**Dangle** — a stickhandling move to beat a defender one-on-one. Broadly interchangeable with **deke**; "dangle" usually implies a longer sequence of moves.

**Deke** — a single fake with the puck, body or head to make a defender or goaltender commit the wrong way.

**Drop-in** — pay-at-the-door pick-up hockey. You turn up alone, are given a jersey colour and play; usually full equipment, usually no referees, and no commitment beyond the session. Usually distinguished from **stick-and-puck** and **shinny**, though all three words are conventions rather than defined categories and rinks use them inconsistently — **the rink's own posted session description is the authority**, and turning up to a session above your standard is the commonest beginner mistake. [Getting Started](../getting-started/getting_started.md) explains the practical differences and what to ask at the desk.

**D-to-D** — a pass between defence partners. Resets the forecheck and changes the angle of attack. The usual instruction is to go behind your own net rather than through the slot, but that is a coaching default rather than a rule: some teams allow the exchange high in the zone, above the tops of the circles, where an interception is not a slot chance. Ask where your coach draws the line. See [Risk Management](../hockey-iq/risk_management.md).

**Dump-and-chase** — deliberately putting the puck deep into the opponents' zone without possession and forechecking to recover it. The act of putting it in is a **dump-in**; the chase is the rest of the name. Chosen when a controlled **zone entry** isn't available. Effective in part because, *where the **trapezoid** applies*, the goaltender cannot come and collect it — neither the USA Hockey nor the Hockey Canada book has one, so a league running on either has none, and its status in England and Wales is unsettled; check before you plan around it. [Rink Map](rink_map.md) carries the trapezoid, its dimensions and that unsettled status.

**Empty net** — the net with the goaltender pulled for an extra attacker, usually late when trailing. A shot that goes *in* an empty net counts as a goal rather than an **icing**, whatever end it came from — **but the four books are not equally explicit about that, and the NHL's is not explicit at all**, so read **Icing** below before you rely on it. A shot that **misses** and crosses the goal line untouched is **icing**, and pulling your goaltender does not exempt you. NHL Rule 81.3 says so in terms: a goaltender who leaves *"to go to the players' bench to be substituted for an extra attacker"* does not nullify the call. Being trapped in your own end on a defensive-zone draw is the last thing a trailing team needs. See [Game Management](../systems/game_management.md).

**F1 / F2 / F3** — roles defined by order of arrival, not positions and not people: F1 is whoever reaches the puck first, F2 the second support, F3 the highest and the safety. They belong to whichever team is forechecking, which on a breakout is the opposition. Set out in [their own section above](#f1-f2-and-f3).

**Faceoff** — the restart, with one player from each team facing off at one of the nine dots. Stick-placement order is the reverse of what most players assume: at **eight of the nine spots** — both end zones *and* all four neutral-zone dots — the **defending** player places their stick first, followed immediately by the attacking player. Only at the **centre ice** dot does the **visiting** player place first (Rule 76.4). **The IIHF sets the same order in the same terms** — word for word in both its 2025/26 and 2026/27 editions. ⚠️ **But a British reader cannot use it as written**, because the IHUK In-House Rules 2026-27 switch that order off domestically: *"Either centre can now put their stick down first for a face-off."* That is a British departure from the IIHF book, not an IIHF change. ⚠️ **The rest of Rule 76.4 has itself changed between IIHF editions:** the NHL has the offending centre *"replaced for that face-off by any teammate then on the ice"*; the IIHF's **2025/26** book assessed a warning instead, and its **2026/27** book adopted the NHL's replacement in the same words. **Under USA Hockey Rule 613(a) it is reversed** — the *attacking* player places first at all eight, with centre ice again going to the visitor. **Hockey Canada is different again**: Interpretation 1 to Rule 6.2(a) puts the *visiting* player's stick down first *"regardless of whether they are the attacking or defending team"*, at every spot — though it adds that a home player may go first *"if it does not cause a problem."* Three answers in four books, and a fourth on top if you play in Britain, where the domestic amendment lets either centre go first — check yours before you build a habit on it. See [Faceoffs](../systems/faceoffs.md) and [UK and England Rules](uk_rules.md).

**Five-hole** — the gap between a goaltender's legs. The *six-hole* is the gap between their stick arm and their body, which opens when the paddle is too long or the shoulder rolls. They are two different targets with two different causes, and [Goaltender](../positions/goaltender.md) carries the second cause — the paddle length and stick-hand position that open it. [Shooting](../technique/shooting.md) is where shooting at the five-hole is taught.

**Forecheck** — pressuring the team that has the puck in **their** own end, trying to win it back rather than retreat. The word also reaches the **neutral zone forecheck**, the structure your five hold across the middle third. Set out in [Forecheck, backcheck, and the neutral zone forecheck](#forecheck-backcheck-and-the-neutral-zone-forecheck) above, which covers both zones, the shape names they share, and which team the numbering belongs to.

**Gap control** — the distance a defender keeps between themselves and an oncoming puck carrier. Aim for roughly a stick-and-a-half at the red line, closing to about a stick length at your blue line. Too big and they build speed; too small and one move beats you. **Those distances are a guide, not a law** — they move with your own backward speed and the attacker's skill. **They are also coaching convention rather than measurement:** of the ladder [Defending the Rush](../systems/defending_the_rush.md) sets out, the neutral-zone figure of two to three stick lengths is published, and so is the stick length at the blue line — but the published pages disagree with each other: the only page found that anchors a distance to the centre red line prints **two** stick lengths there rather than a stick and a half, and it prints a stick length at the blue line where a second page prints two.

**Give-and-go** — pass to a teammate and immediately skate into open space to receive it straight back. The simplest and most reliable way to beat a defender without stickhandling.

**Goalmouth** — the immediate net front, right at the edge of the **crease**. It is a much smaller area than the **slot**, and it is the layer *below* it rather than a part of it. Set out in [The slot, the high slot and the goalmouth](#the-slot-the-high-slot-and-the-goalmouth) above, with the layers in front of the net and who covers each of them.

**Half-wall** (also **half-boards**) — the boards roughly level with the faceoff dot, between the corner and the **point**, one on each side of each end zone. It is a long way below the point, not another name for it. Set out in [its own section above](#the-half-wall), which covers the different things a winger and a centre are told about it.

**Hash marks** — the short red lines outside each end-zone faceoff circle: two inches wide, 2 ft long and 5 ft 7 in apart in the NHL, at the outer edge of both sides of the circle and parallel to the goal line. **Four per circle** (Rule 1.9) — a pair on the boards side and a pair on the middle-of-the-ice side, both roughly level with the dot. They restrain players on a draw; [Rink Map](rink_map.md) owns the markings and what they do on a faceoff. **They are also the standard depth landmark in your own end, and the distance is the part people get wrong:** the faceoff dot there sits twenty feet out from the goal line (NHL Rule 1.9) — a long way above the crease, not beside it — and the blue line is sixty-four feet out (NHL Rule 1.5). So **"on the wall at the hash marks" means the band on the boards between the hash-mark line and the goal line, at or just *below* the dot line rather than above it** — roughly the bottom third of the distance from the goal line to the blue line, and the same spot meant by "between the hash marks and the goal line".

**Home plate** — also called *the house*; the analysts' high-danger scoring area, and a wider shape than the **slot**. It runs square across the top along the tops of the two faceoff circles, straight down the dot lines to the two faceoff dots, then angles in to the two goalposts — so drawn out it looks like a baseball home plate with its point at the net, widest where the slot is narrowest. It is the working definition of a high-danger chance, and it is an analytics convention rather than an official marking. **The exact boundaries vary a little between analytics providers, so treat it as a concept rather than a measurement.** [Rink Map](rink_map.md) owns the shape and its measurements, and [Shooting](../technique/shooting.md) owns the scoring figures and the qualifications on them. **On a breakout it is the ice you do not move the puck through.**

**House league** — also *local league*: the recreational tier of a local association. Hockey Canada describes it as one or two practices and a game a week, inexpensive registration, away games *"rarely hosted more than an hour's drive away"*, and players *"almost always given equal ice time regardless of skill or talent"*. So it does travel — just not far. *Not the same as* travel/rep hockey, which is entered by tryout and asks much more of you. Tier names vary by association and by year; [Getting Started](../getting-started/getting_started.md) explains how to work out where you belong.

**Hybrid** — the defensive-zone system that plays **man-on-man** below the dots and zone above them: the two defencemen and the low forward take bodies down low, while the two wingers cover the points as an area but go with their point man the moment he crosses the line and comes down. **Where that line sits is a team decision** — some say the hash marks, some the tops of the circles — so learn your team's version. A coaching choice rather than a law of hockey; the alternatives are a **low zone collapse** and **man-on-man**. See [Defensive Zone Coverage](../systems/defensive_zone_coverage.md).

**Icing** — shooting the puck from your own half across the opponent's goal line untouched. ⚠️ **Which version you are playing is a book-level question, and "most leagues use hybrid" is not the answer.** The **NHL** (from 2013-14) and the **IIHF** (from 2014) use **hybrid icing**: the race is to **the puck**, and the end-zone faceoff dot is the **decision deadline**, not the finish line. **NHL and IIHF Rule 81.1** say so in terms — *"the determining factor is which player would first touch the puck, not which player would first reach the end zone face-off dots"* — and the linesperson must make that judgment by the instant the first player's skate reaches the dot. **If it is too close to call at that moment, icing is called.** 🇬🇧 ⚠️ **But that deadline is for a race, and the IIHF writes a limb for the case where there is no race that the NHL book does not have** — one sentence at the end of its own 81.1, in all three editions on disk: *"If there is no 'race' for the puck, icing should not be called until a defending skater crosses its defending blue line and the puck crosses the goal line (not between the goal posts)."* **So with nobody chasing, your book's trigger is the blue line rather than the dot** — and that parenthesis is where the IIHF says a puck that goes *in* is a goal, not an icing. Waved off if the opposing goalie plays it or, under Rule 81.3, *"feigns playing the puck, attempts to play the puck, or skates in the direction of the puck… at any time"* — no departure from the crease is required (NHL — USA Hockey does *not* wave off for goalie movement), if it comes directly off a faceoff, if the team is **shorthanded** (conditionally — see **Shorthanded**, below), or if a linesman judges an opposing skater could have played it. ⚠️ **A puck that goes *in* is a goal rather than an icing — but check which book actually says so.** **USA Hockey 624(a)** says it flatly — *"if the puck has entered the goal as a result of a legal action by the team shooting the puck, the goal shall be allowed"* — and so does **Hockey Canada Rule 6.7**: *"If the puck enters the net on such a play, then it is not icing."* The **IIHF** says it only inside the no-race limb above, and the **NHL book does not write it anywhere in Rule 81**, which was read whole for it. ⚠️ **The other books run no race at all, so do not carry the hybrid habit into them.** **USA Hockey is automatic in every standing classification:** Rule 624's Note completes the icing *"the instant the puck has completely crossed the goal line"*, and the one place that book says "hybrid icing" is a **sled hockey** adaptation, not a general rule. **Hockey Canada is automatic by default**, and hybrid only *"[i]n categories of U18AAA, Junior, and at the option of the Member in Senior"* (Rule 6.7(e)). **CARHA is automatic** (Rule 65(a)) — and some CARHA leagues divide the ice at the **blue lines** rather than the red (65(b), 65(c)). **How much rec, beer-league and youth hockey each of those adds up to is a question about leagues rather than about books, and this document has no count for it.** After your own icing under the **NHL and IIHF** books: defensive-zone draw, **no line change** (Rule 81.4 in both) and **no timeout** (Rule 87.1 in both, in the same words). **The two 81.4s are not identical, though.** The NHL's excepts three things — replacing a goaltender who had gone off for an extra attacker, replacing an injured player, and a penalty that changes the strength on the ice. The IIHF's adds a fourth, *"a Player with a broken skate"*, in the 2025/26 and 2026/27 editions alike — but the same IIHF book's Rule 82.1 writes the identical ban with only the NHL's three, and neither rule says which copy governs. **And one Situation Handbook answer is adverse without naming a broken skate at all** — Situation 81.7 says a change is permitted *"only"* on a strength-changing penalty, which is over-broad (it drops the injured player and the pulled goalie too) but unhelpful to you. So ask your officials rather than plan a change around it. Send a replacement over on it anyway and **IIHF Rule 82.2(V)** has the referee send them back and warn your coach; that warning is one per team per game and covers all three of the acts 82.2(V) names, so once it is spent the next attempt is a bench minor for delay of game. **The NHL is harsher still** — its Rule 82.3 gives the visiting team a bench minor for any change after the referee's signal, with no warning — though its own 82.2(v) says warning first, and the book does not say which governs. **After its own icing a USA Hockey team may both change its line and take a time-out**: nothing in its Rule 624 touches substitution, and its time-out rule 636(f) attaches no icing condition. **Hockey Canada takes a third position, and only in the categories that run hybrid** — its no-change rule (6.7(d)) applies in *"U18AAA, Junior, and at the option of the Member in Senior"* and nowhere else, and inside those categories 6.7(d)(iii) allows the time-out but not the change: *"Either team is permitted to take their time-out… but during that time out, the team in violation of icing will not be permitted to make player substitutions."* See [Rules Primer](rules_primer.md).

**Limb** — one of the separately-stated parts a single rule breaks into. Nothing to do with arms and legs: it is the word for a branch of a written rule, and it is worth having because a rule and the exception that rescues you are usually two limbs of the same rule rather than two rules. Goaltender interference at Rule 69.7 is a clean example. One limb permits *"incidental contact"* with the goaltender in a rebound or loose-puck scramble, inside the crease or outside it, and allows the goal. A second disallows the goal where an attacking player pushes the goaltender into the net together with the puck after a stop. A third can give that goal back where a defender pushed or fouled the attacker and that is what caused it. A fourth stops anyone scoring by shoving a player who is lying on the puck into the goal. Four limbs, and a player who takes only the second one away has the rule backwards. **Count limbs, not paragraphs.** A limb is a piece of the rule's meaning; a paragraph is a piece of its printed layout, and the layout moves between books — the **NHL** prints those four limbs as three paragraphs and the **IIHF** prints them as four, in Rule 69.7 of each. Where a book gives a part its own printed name — Hockey Canada's **Interpretations**, USA Hockey's **Notes** and its Casebook's **Situations** — quote that name instead of counting, because a name still finds the right text after a new edition has renumbered everything around it. See [Rules Primer](rules_primer.md).

**Low zone collapse** — the zone defensive system assumed as the default throughout this guide: in your own end each player covers an *area* of ice rather than an opponent, sagging toward the middle rather than chasing to the boards, with the wingers holding the points and the centre the high slot. **It is a coaching choice rather than a law of hockey**, and the realistic alternatives are **man-on-man** and the **hybrid**. Find out which your team plays. See [Defensive Zone Coverage](../systems/defensive_zone_coverage.md).

**Man-on-man** — the defensive-zone system in which you take an opponent rather than an area and follow them wherever they go, the corner included, so there is no height and no layer to hold. The two wingers take the opposing defencemen and go down with them, which is the sharpest practical difference from a **low zone collapse**. **A coaching choice rather than a law of hockey** — find out which your team plays. See [Defensive Zone Coverage](../systems/defensive_zone_coverage.md).

**Net drive** — skating hard at the net *without* the puck. It pulls a defender with you, creates a rebound and deflection threat that changes the goaltender's depth, and bends the defence toward the net, which opens the **high slot** and the **point** behind you. A half-hearted glide does none of those things. The drive stops at the blue paint, though: keep both feet outside the crease — **and, under USA Hockey, your stick with them, because Rule 625(b) names it alongside your skates** — and never initiate contact with the goaltender, inside it or outside it — deliberate contact is penalised *"whether or not the goalkeeper is inside or outside the goal crease"* (NHL Rule 69.2), while the goal itself comes off the board for **where you stood** only once you have *"enter[ed] the goal crease"* (69.1) — **and under USA Hockey 625(b) your position in the crease voids the goal on its own, whoever scored — unless the puck preceded you in, the goalkeeper is out of the crease area, or a defender's interference is what put you there**. ⚠️ **That goalkeeper carve-out reverses while he is on his way back:** under USA Hockey's Casebook, Rule 625, Situation 9, interference that prevents a returning goalkeeper from making a play means *"the goal must be disallowed and an interference penalty shall be assessed"* — **and the NHL and the IIHF reach the same play from their own Rule 69.4** — **but both write a precondition the shorthand drops**: it reaches you only where the goalkeeper *"has played the puck outside of"* the crease and is then prevented from returning *"due to the deliberate actions of an attacking player"*. A narrower test, and *"may be penalized"* rather than must, but not an absence. Under Hockey Canada 8.5 a teammate's goal from outside the crease stands only if you stay passive in there **and clear of the goaltender's view and movements**. See [Playing Without the Puck](../hockey-iq/playing_without_the_puck.md).

**Odd-man rush** — an attack where the attackers outnumber the defenders back, e.g. 2-on-1 or 3-on-2. On a 2-on-1 the common teaching is that the defender takes away the pass and leaves the shot to the goalie, though a timed slide to force the shot is a legitimate alternative system — [Defending the Rush](../systems/defending_the_rush.md) sets out both.

**Offside** — an attacker entering the attacking zone before the puck. **Both skates** must have completely crossed the blue line before the puck completely crosses it; one skate on or behind the line keeps you onside, and a skate in the air over the neutral zone counts as onside **under NHL and IIHF rules only — USA Hockey Rule 630(a) and Hockey Canada Rule 6.11 both require skate *contact* — two books of the four**. ⚠️ **Three other things share this word and none of them is this rule.** Your **off-side** is your unnatural handedness side (a left shot on right defence). **Off-side** is sometimes used for the weak side of the ice — see **Strong side and weak side** above. And *lining up in an off-side position* at a faceoff is a separate violation with its own remedy (NHL and IIHF Rule 76.3). Arriving late to the dot draws a once-a-game warning before it is penalised (Rule 76.7 in both books). Rule 82.2 restates that escalation at (vii), and **82.2(viii) switches *that* copy of it off** in the last two minutes of regulation and at any time in overtime, giving both teams *"a reasonable amount of time to set up"*. ⚠️ 76.7 carries the same escalation and **no such carve-out**, so whether it stops late in a game is not something the books settle.

**One-timer** — a shot taken directly off an incoming pass without stopping the puck. Devastating when timed, because the goaltender cannot reset between the pass and the shot. *Not the same as* a **one-touch**, which is a *pass* played the same way. See [Shooting](../technique/shooting.md).

**One-touch** — a pass you redirect on contact, without controlling it first; also called a *first-time pass*, and the basis of a *tic-tac-toe* sequence of quick passes. *Not the same as* a **one-timer**, which is a *shot* played the same way. When it is on, it beats coverage, because every touch you take gives the defence time to re-set; when it is not on, it fails badly. See [Passing and Receiving](../technique/passing_and_receiving.md).

**Outlet** — the first pass out of your own zone, *and* the teammate available to receive it; both uses are standard. Shouted — **"Outlet!"** — it means *I am your exit*: move the puck and start the breakout. (The *term* is standard; its use as something a player actually shouts is in common use but is not documented by any published coaching source held here.) The **outlet spot** for a strong-side winger is on the wall between the hash marks and the goal line. See [Breakouts](../systems/breakouts.md) and [On-Ice Communication](on_ice_communication.md).

**Overload** — an offensive or power-play structure that loads four players onto one side of the ice, overwhelming that side and creating a weak-side option. Distinct from the **1-3-1** and the **umbrella**.

**The paint** — the light-blue **crease** in front of the net, where a goaltender operates: a semi-circle of six-foot radius struck from the centre of the goal line, with straight side lines starting one foot outside each post and running four feet six inches out (NHL Rule 1.7). The red line around it counts as crease under three of the four books — the IIHF (Rule 1.7), USA Hockey (the Note to 625(b)) and Hockey Canada (its Glossary) all put the marked line inside the crease, and only the NHL leaves it unsaid ([Rink Map](rink_map.md) sets out all four). For a skater the two instructions come as a pair: **"stay out of the paint" is the instruction, and "live just outside the paint" is the skill.** "Stay out of the paint" is about where your body is rather than whether you are legal: an attacker works *just outside* it, and stepping in is what puts the goal at risk. ⚠️ **Under USA Hockey it is about your stick too, and that is the limb a net-front player loses a goal to with both feet outside the line.** Rule 625(b) writes its stoppage for an attacking player who *"stands, **holds their stick**, or skates through the goal crease"*, and its Casebook at Rule 625, Situation 10 takes the goal off the board on the same reach: *"if a goal is scored while an attacking player has their stick and/or skates in the opponent's goal crease while the goalkeeper is in the crease, the Referee shall have no alternative but to disallow the goal."* **The other three books key their crease bar to your body** — the NHL's to an attacker who *"enters the goal crease"* (69.1), Hockey Canada's to one who stands in it (8.5) — **so the reach of your stick into the paint costs you a goal in one book of the four, and nothing on the ice tells you which book you are under.** Screening from outside the crease is normally legal: under the NHL and IIHF every clause of Rule 69 that voids a goal for **where you stood** names the goal crease, and 69.4, the rule for outside it, reaches contact only — **a reading of the rule's structure rather than something either book states, and Rule 69 is *"enforced exclusively in accordance with the on-ice judgement of the Referee(s)"***. ⚠️ **The crease line is the unresolved edge, so keep off it too.** **Neither USA Hockey nor Hockey Canada reaches a screen set from outside the crease at all, and inside it they differ from each other: USA Hockey bars the goal on your position alone whoever scored, unless the puck preceded you in, the goalkeeper is out of the crease area, or a defender's interference is what put you there (625(b)), while Hockey Canada lets a teammate's goal from outside the crease stand if you stay passive and clear of the goaltender's view and movements (8.5)**. ⚠️ **And that goalkeeper carve-out reverses while he is skating back:** USA Hockey's Casebook, at Rule 625, Situation 9, disallows the goal and adds an interference penalty where you prevent a returning goalkeeper from making a play — **and the NHL and the IIHF reach the same play from their own Rule 69.4** — **but both write a precondition the shorthand drops**: it reaches you only where the goalkeeper *"has played the puck outside of"* the crease and is then prevented from returning *"due to the deliberate actions of an attacking player"*: a narrower test, and *"may be penalized"* rather than must, but not an absence. See **Screen**.

**Penalty kill (PK)** — playing shorthanded while serving a penalty. Standard shapes: **box** (two forwards high, two defencemen low — *no single top*), **diamond** (a single apex, used against a **1-3-1** because a box leaves the **bumper** unmarked), and **wedge+1** (three in a tight triangle around the net, plus a fourth player — the **"+1"** — who pressures whoever has the puck rather than following it around the perimeter; when the puck moves, the nearest wedge player steps out as the new **"+1"** and the previous one drops into the triangle). **The route the +1 takes — straight out from the goal, sweeping across the top of the zone, or roaming to the wall and the point — is a coaching choice that published descriptions differ on, so ask which one your team runs.** See [Special Teams](../systems/special_teams.md).

**Pick** — a penalty, not a play. **Hockey is not basketball, and there is no legal pick on a skater.** NHL Rule 56.1 defines it as *"the action of a player who checks an opponent who is not in possession of the puck and is unaware of the impending check/hit"*, and a player delivering one as *"one who moves into an opponent's path without initially having body position"*. That is an interference minor, and [Playing Without the Puck](../hockey-iq/playing_without_the_puck.md) sets out the wording in each of the four books and how far up the penalty ladder it can go. What you may do instead is hold the ice you are already standing on — see **Traffic**.

**Pinch** — a defenceman stepping *down* from the **offensive** blue line into the zone to keep a puck in. *Not the same as* **step up**. Relatively safe, because your forwards are behind you to recover. **Under most systems only one defenceman pinches at a time**, the other holding the middle as the safety — but that is a near-universal coaching default, not part of the definition and not a rule: some teams give both defencemen a standing green light, and every team sends both on a delayed penalty, a full-ice press or 6-on-5. See [Forechecking Systems](../systems/forechecking_systems.md) and [Offensive Zone Play](../systems/offensive_zone_play.md).

**Point** — the area just inside the blue line, toward each set of boards, where the defencemen set up in the offensive zone. **It is an area, not the line itself**, and it is *not the same as* the **half-wall**. Set out in [its own section above](#the-point), which covers what covering it asks of a winger and how that changes with the system.

**Post** — the vertical pipes at the sides of the net. "Far post" and "near post" are read from the puck's position, so which is which changes as the puck moves. *Post integration* is the family of goaltending techniques for sealing against a post and getting off it cleanly. **When a goaltender goes to the post is a coaching choice, not a law of the game.** The conservative North American default is late on and early off, with post seals kept for pucks genuinely below the goal line or genuinely on top of you; other coaches, particularly in the Swedish tradition, teach an earlier and more systematic seal with a defined trigger line. Both are defensible, so ask what your trigger is — what nobody defends is dropping to a post out of habit, without a read. See [Goaltender](../positions/goaltender.md).

**Power play (PP)** — playing with a man advantage while the opponent serves a penalty. Common structures: **1-3-1** (exactly **one** player at the point, two half-walls, a **bumper** between them, one net front), **umbrella** (three high), and **overload** (four to one side). **The four-forward first unit has been counted; the shape has not.** Matt Cane, writing on Hockey Graphs in March 2017, measured four-forward units at *"roughly 56% of the 5-on-4 ice-time this season"* — **2016-17 data**, a share of **league-wide ice time, not a count of teams**, and not split between first and second units. Which *shape* teams run is not counted by anything found for it, so "most teams use a 1-3-1" is consensus rather than measurement. See [Special Teams](../systems/special_teams.md).

**Privileged area** (the goalkeeper's) — **a USA Hockey term with no counterpart in the NHL, IIHF or Hockey Canada books**, and much larger than the **crease**. Rule 607(d) Note 2 draws it *"by connecting the end zone face-off spots with an imaginary line and imaginary lines from each face-off spot running perpendicular to the end boards"*, and the *Casebook* at Rule 607, Situation 6 answers in terms that it *"extends to the closer end boards"*. **So it takes in the whole net front, the goal line and the ice behind the net — but not the corners.** ⚠️ **Being outside it is not a licence to hit the goalkeeper, and 607(d) writes that into the same clause as the definition:** *"A goalkeeper is NOT 'fair game' because they are outside the privileged area. A penalty for interference or charging should be called in every case where an opposing player makes unnecessary contact with a goalkeeper."* The *Casebook* at Rule 607, Situation 5 is written on that exact question: a goalkeeper *"can be legally checked when outside the privileged area"*, but a penalty *"should be assessed in every instance where unnecessary or avoidable contact is made"* out there, and any check *"delivered with the intent to intimidate or punish the goalkeeper should be penalized as charging"* — which is **607(a)**, a minor plus a misconduct at the least. Two separate rules key off it. **Rule 614(c)** stops a goalkeeper *freezing* the puck outside it; [Rink Map](rink_map.md) sets that limit out alongside the trapezoid and the goaltender's other limits on covering the puck. And **Rule 607(c)** prices contact on a goalkeeper inside it: *"A minor plus a misconduct or a major plus a game misconduct penalty shall be assessed to a player who body checks or charges a goalkeeper while the goalkeeper is within the goal crease or privileged area"* — ⚠️ **there is no bare minor anywhere in that rule**, and **607(e)** adds that *"A match penalty for reckless endangerment may also be assessed for charging."* ⚠️ **Which rule you are under is decided by 607(d) Note 1, so do not read every bump at the net front as an ejection:** *"any accidental or unavoidable contact that occurs with the goalkeeper shall be penalized under the Interference rule"* — a minor under **625(a)(8)** — while *"[a]ny deliberate body contact or check that is delivered to the goalkeeper shall be penalized as charging."* Inside charging the *Casebook* at Rule 607, Situation 4 runs three rungs: **no penalty** only *"[i]n the case where a defending player has forced the attacking player into their own goalkeeper"* and the attacker *"has clearly made every attempt to avoid the contact"*; **a minor plus misconduct** where a player going hard to the goal *"makes an honest attempt to avoid the goalkeeper once it is realized contact is unavoidable"*; and *"the major plus game misconduct, or match penalty option"* where they make no attempt and accelerate through the contact, extend the arms or drop the shoulder. ⚠️ **Read that top rung as the Casebook's shorthand rather than as a choice the official has**, because the rule it sits under is written as a command: **607(b)** — *"A major penalty plus game misconduct penalty **shall** be assessed to any player who recklessly endangers an opponent as a result of charging."* **The other books reach the same ice by other routes** — **NHL Rule 42.1** writes the range into the rule — *"A minor, major or a major and a game misconduct shall be imposed on a player who charges a goalkeeper while the goalkeeper is within his goal crease"* — with 42.2–42.5 setting the minor, major, match and game misconduct, while **IIHF Rule 42.1** writes only *"A penalty shall be imposed on a Player who charges a goalkeeper while the goalkeeper is within their goal crease"*, with 42.2–42.4 running minor, major and major plus game misconduct, and Hockey Canada's Rule 8.5(b) makes a major plus game misconduct mandatory *"to any player who charges the goaltender."* ⚠️ **And none of those three stops protecting the goalkeeper at the crease line either.** **NHL Rule 42.1** carries the counterweight in the same rule — *"A goalkeeper is not 'fair game' just because he is outside the goal crease area. The appropriate penalty should be assessed in every case where an opposing player makes unnecessary contact with a goalkeeper"* — **IIHF Rule 42.1** writes that sentence in its own pronouns, and **Hockey Canada Rule 8.5** is the widest of the four: a penalty *"will be called where an opposing player makes unnecessary contact with the goaltender anywhere on the ice"*, and its mandatory major plus game misconduct for charging the goaltender carries no location limit at all. **So what the crease and the privileged area decide is the tier, not whether you may hit him: a goaltender who has come out to the wall or the corner for a puck is not a hittable player in any of the four books.** See [Rules Primer](rules_primer.md), [Goaltender](../positions/goaltender.md) and [Special Teams](../systems/special_teams.md).

**Quarterback** — the player who runs a power play from the top, which in a **1-3-1** is the single point player. **Calling that player the lone defenceman describes the common set-up rather than a counted one** — see **Power play** above, which carries what has actually been measured about power-play personnel and what has not. Their job is distribution and walking the line, not just shooting.

**Regroup** — turning the puck back toward your own end when there is no good way forward, resetting, and coming again. **You are not giving anything up: you still have the puck.** Usually better than forcing a bad **zone entry** — at amateur level the error runs almost entirely one way, toward regrouping far too rarely — a coaching observation rather than a tracked figure. One professional regroup count does exist, on AHL power plays and under a wider definition of a regroup than this one; nothing found counts the amateur side, so the comparison itself is untracked. [Neutral Zone Systems](../systems/neutral_zone_systems.md) owns that study and its limits.

**Reverse** — sending the puck **back against the flow**, into the space a forechecker has just vacated, usually to a defence partner arriving behind you. *Not the same as* a **rim**. A reverse beats a forechecker who has over-committed.

**Rim** — a hard pass along the boards that follows the curve of the rink **onward** in the direction you are facing, typically corner-to-corner behind the net. *Not the same as* a **reverse**. A rim beats a forechecker who is sealing you against the wall.

**Royal road** (also **the slot line**) — an imaginary line running down the middle of the ice, lengthwise, from the middle of one net to the middle of the other. Nothing is painted for it. A pass that **crosses** it in the attacking zone immediately before a shot — a *royal road pass*, *slot-line pass*, *cross-seam pass* or *cross-slot pass* — forces the goaltender to break from small shuffles into a hard lateral push, so the shot arrives before they are set, and it is the **highest-value pass type measured**, roughly double the next one: volunteer-tracked data, 51,308 shots at five-on-five, counting all attempts rather than shots on goal. **Published descriptions disagree about how far the line runs** — most accounts take it net-centre to net-centre down the whole sheet, while the two sources closest to the goaltender who coined it stop it at the tops of the circles. [Shooting](../technique/shooting.md) sets out that disagreement; [Offensive Zone Play](../systems/offensive_zone_play.md) owns those figures with their limits and covers how to create one.

**Saucer pass** — a pass flipped so the puck flies flat, off the ice, and lands flat. Used to clear a defender's stick or a body in the passing lane.

**Screen** — standing in the goaltender's line of sight so they cannot see the shot. **Normally legal** as long as you stay outside the crease — stick included, under USA Hockey, whose Rule 625(b) names it beside your skates — and off the red line around it, and do not contact the goaltender. Under the NHL and IIHF every clause of Rule 69 that voids a goal for **where you stood** names the goal crease, and 69.4, the rule for outside it, reaches contact only; **neither USA Hockey nor Hockey Canada reaches a screen set from outside the crease at all, and inside it the two of them differ from each other** (see [Rules Primer](rules_primer.md)). Shouted — **"Screen!"** — it is the goaltender telling you to move, or to move the player in front of them; **that "Screen!" is also among the most ignored of the shouted calls is an observation rather than a count.** Act on it — **box them out with hips and back, and never with a two-handed shove on the shaft, which is cross-checking.** ⚠️ **The instruction holds in all four books; the penalty wording does not.** The NHL and the IIHF leave the minor to the referee — *"at the discretion of the Referee based on the severity of the contact"* (NHL 59.2), *"based on the degree of force and violence"* (IIHF 59.2) — while **USA Hockey 609(a)** and **Hockey Canada 9.2(a)** write it flat, a minor that *"shall"* and *"will"* be assessed. **What is the same in all four is the top of the ladder:** a game misconduct is automatic on any major for cross-checking (NHL 59.5, IIHF 59.3, USA Hockey 609(b), Hockey Canada 9.2(e)). ⚠️ **Hockey Canada writes down where the referee draws that line, and reading its sentence as permission for this play is the mistake.** Interpretation 1 to 9.2(a) tells the referee to *"consider whether the player is 'pushing' or 'striking' an opponent with the shaft of their stick. If a pushing motion is used, a penalty may not be necessary. However, a striking motion must be penalized."* **That is written about how an official grades a motion, and it is not a licence to put your stick on a player who is facing away from you.** The same book's Rule 9.2 defines the offence as *"when a player uses the shaft of the stick, between their two hands, to check an opponent"* — which is the two-handed shove exactly — and its Rules 7.6(b) and 7.7(b) both add that *"[a]ny player who strikes an opponent above the normal height of their shoulders with a cross-check will be penalized with a Major penalty and a Game Misconduct penalty, whether or not injury results"*, under a rule that opens *"There is no type of legal contact to the head, face, or neck."* ⚠️ **And the reason to keep your stick out of this is the injury rather than the whistle.** A screening player is by definition facing the net, so the shove lands on someone who has their back to you and cannot see it coming, in front of the posts. That is the book's own reading, not a gloss on it: USA Hockey's *Standard of Play and Rule Emphasis – Body Checking* gives this precise scenario as the first of its two cross-checking examples — *"When a player in front of the goal uses the shaft of the stick and extension of the arms to contact an opponent **in a dangerous manner** to create space"* — and **the word *dangerous* is USA Hockey's**, in a sentence written about nothing else. ⚠️ **And Hockey Canada — the same book again — puts a shove delivered from behind under a different rule entirely.** Its **Rule 7.5(a)** assesses *"A Minor penalty and a Game Misconduct penalty will be assessed to any player who intentionally pushes, body-checks, cross-checks while in motion, or otherwise hits an opposing player from behind, anywhere on the ice"* — **a game misconduct on a *minor*, which no cross-checking minor in any book carries** — and **the NHL (43.3) and the IIHF (43.1) name cross-checking inside their own checking-from-behind rules**, while USA Hockey's **608** reaches *"pushing an opponent from behind"*. [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md#checking-from-behind-and-what-the-onus-does-and-does-not-mean) owns that rule, its tiers and the carve-out for a player who turns into the check. **Hips and back, every time.** See [On-Ice Communication](on_ice_communication.md). *Not the same as* **traffic**: a screen is something done to a goaltender's sightline. Do it to a *skater* and it is interference — USA Hockey penalises that exact act by name, as *"providing a protective screen and limiting the opportunity for an opposing player to apply pressure to a teammate in possession or control of the puck"* (Rule 625(a)(1)), while the NHL and IIHF call it a **pick**. See [Goaltender](../positions/goaltender.md).

**Seam** — a gap between two defenders' coverage areas, and the passing lane that runs through it. "Finding the seam" means threading a pass through that gap into the slot.

**Shinny** — informal pick-up hockey, indoor or outdoor; in much of Canada it is the standard municipal-rink word for **drop-in**. House rules vary enormously — contact, lifting the puck and whether there is a goalie are all local. See [Getting Started](../getting-started/getting_started.md).

**Shorthanded** — playing with fewer skaters because of a penalty. A shorthanded team **may ice the puck freely** (Rule 81.6), which is the single most useful fact on the penalty kill — **but it is not unconditional.** USA Hockey grants the exemption only at Youth 15-Only and above, Girls 16U and above, High School and Adult (Rule 624(b)(1)), and the NHL withdraws it from a team shorthanded by a *major* that has left nobody on the penalty bench. 🇬🇧 **Under the IIHF book neither carve-out exists** — Rule 81.6 grants the exemption plainly, so in British hockey it is unconditional, one of the few places your rule is the simpler one. **Hockey Canada 6.7(b) is unconditional too**, and unlike its no-change and hybrid rules it carries no category limit: *"Icing will not be called and play will continue if the team shooting the puck is below the numerical strength of the opposing team at the instant the puck is shot."* [Special Teams](../systems/special_teams.md) covers how this shapes a kill; [Rules Primer](rules_primer.md) has the rulebook wording.

**Shoulder check** — the everyday name for a *scan*: a head turn to see the ice away from the puck. It is a head turn rather than an eye flick or a chin dip — your chin should pass over your shoulder — and it is a snapshot, not a stare. Check both shoulders, because the information you need is usually on the side you were *not* planning to use. See [Scanning and Anticipation](../hockey-iq/scanning_and_anticipation.md). **The same phrase names something else entirely** — an open-ice body check delivered with the shoulder. Many leagues ban body checking outright — under USA Hockey and Hockey Canada it is barred in younger age groups and throughout girls' and women's play, and USA Hockey bars it in *"all non-check Adult classifications"* (Rule 604(a)), one of the four adult classifications its Glossary names — **and the same rule lets a local governing body prohibit body checking in any classification, so the printed list is a floor rather than an answer.** **How much adult hockey is played in one is not something a rulebook records**, and Britain publishes no age at all, so find out what your league allows before you play. USA Hockey's *Introduction To Body Contact* calls the open-ice version a *"high risk check"* and permits it only when the opponent is close enough that a single stride reaches them. See [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md) for that sense.

**Slot** — the ice directly in front of the net, between the two faceoff circles, running from the tops of the circles down toward the **goalmouth**. **It is where most goals are scored from** — [Shooting](../technique/shooting.md) owns the shot-location figures and the qualifications on every one of them. Set out in [The slot, the high slot and the goalmouth](#the-slot-the-high-slot-and-the-goalmouth) above, with its **high slot** and **low slot** layers, the goalmouth below them, and who covers each.

**Stay high** — "high" means toward the blue line you are facing, away from the net — but for a **winger** the phrase names two different jobs at almost the same place on the ice, and the one that means covering the **point** **expires the instant your team wins the puck**. Said of **F3** it is the plain directional word. Set out in [its own section above](#stay-high), which covers all three senses.

**Step up** — moving *forward* to challenge an attacker early, in the neutral zone or at your own blue line. *Not the same as* a **pinch**. Riskier, because there is nothing behind you if you miss.

**Stick-and-puck** — open ice booked for individual practice rather than a game (a convention rather than a defined category — see **Drop-in**): many pucks, no teams, no referees, often no goalie, and frequently a lighter gear requirement than **drop-in**. See [Getting Started](../getting-started/getting_started.md).

**Stretch pass** — a long pass from your own end through the neutral zone to a forward who has left early, aiming to create a breakaway or odd-man rush. Legal since the two-line pass was removed in 2005-06. High reward, high turnover risk.

**Tag up** — on a delayed offside, the attacking team clearing the zone, after which they may immediately re-enter and attack with no whistle. **⚠️ Check your book before you rely on it.** Under **USA Hockey Rule 630(d)** the delayed offside and the tag-up exist only *"For all Youth 15-Only levels and above, all Girls 16U levels and above, High School and Adult classifications only"* — below those classifications there is no raised arm and nothing to recover, because the whistle goes immediately, and a player who clears and re-enters is rejoining a play that has already stopped. **The NHL, the IIHF and Hockey Canada (Rule 6.11(b)) set no such classification limit** — under all three the recovery is yours at every level. **🇬🇧 That includes Britain, and here it can be said plainly:** the IHUK In-House Rules 2026-27 amend Section 10 (Game Flow) only at Rules 76, 78, 84 and 85, and Rule 83 appears nowhere in the document at all, so **the In-House Rules carry no amendment to IIHF Rule 83.3** — the delayed off-side and the tag-up reach British players at every age band, and the In-House junior section (Rules 200 and 201) sets age categories and a penalty rule, not an offside floor. **Two limits on how far that statement reaches:** the In-House Rules say IHUK *"may also issue Rule Bulletins from time to time"* and no bulletin has been read here, and the Elite League's own casebook has not been obtained — so this is what the published In-House Rules say, not a statement about every British competition. Where you do have one: **it is a five-man act, not something you do alone.** Rule 83.3(i) requires that ***"all players* of the offending team clear the zone at the same instant (skate contact with the blue line)"** — the linesperson's arm stays up, and nobody is legally re-entered, until the *last* attacker in the zone has tagged. What is generous is the standard for each individual: **skate contact with the line** is enough, not both skates fully back over it, so you can drag a foot onto the line and go again without leaving the zone properly. What is not generous is the "all players" part — tagging alone and re-entering while a teammate is still inside gets the play whistled dead. ⚠️ **And tagging up rescues the possession, never the shot that was already on its way.** If the puck that created the delayed offside goes in, the goal comes off the board in every book, and clearing the zone first makes no difference: **NHL and IIHF Rule 83.4**, word for word in both, disallow it *"as the original shot was off-side"* and add that *"[t]he fact that the attacking team may have cleared the zone prior to the puck entering the goal has no bearing on this ruling"*, and **Hockey Canada 6.11(b)** carries a Note saying the same in its own words. ⚠️ **The books then split on the one way back in.** The NHL and the IIHF leave an opening — *"The only way an attacking team can score a goal on a delayed off-side situation is if the defending team shoots or puts the puck into their own net without action or contact by the offending team"* — and **USA Hockey closes it**, its *Casebook* at Rule 630, Situation 41 answering *"May a goal ever be allowed during the course of a delayed offside? No"* and adding that *"[e]ven if the defending team shoots, passes or deflects the puck into its own goal, a goal may not be allowed."* See [Zone Entries](../systems/zone_entries.md).

**Tip-in** — redirecting a shot — usually a point shot — with your stick blade as it passes, changing its direction late so the goaltender cannot adjust. There is a ceiling on how high you may tip: a puck contacting your stick **above the height of the crossbar** is no goal, and *"the determining factor is where the puck makes contact with the stick"* (NHL Rule 80.3). ⚠️ **That ceiling reaches this puck's path into the net and stops there.** Rule 80.3 takes the goal off the board only where your high contact sends it in *"either directly or deflected off any player or official"*, so a redirect off a body or a stick still counts against you — but a teammate who takes **possession** and shoots starts a new chain, and that goal stands (IIHF Situation Handbook, Situation 80.9(c), put in the same words in its 2025/26 v1.1 and 2026/27 editions). **That escape exists only below your own shoulders**, because above them Rule 80.1's whistle goes as soon as your own side gains possession and the teammate never gets the shot away. **So do not stop playing after a high touch.** ⚠️ **Under USA Hockey the ceiling for a goal is lower — the shoulder, not the crossbar**: Rule 621(d) says *"no goal can be scored as a result of an attacking player playing the puck above the shoulder with the stick and directly entering the goal."* ⚠️ **But note the word *directly*, because USA Hockey's ceiling is the narrower of the two in reach:** where NHL 80.3 takes the goal off the board *"either directly or deflected off any player or official"*, 621(d) bars only the puck that goes straight in — so a deflection on the way is inside the NHL's reach and outside USA Hockey's. Batting it above shoulder height is separately prohibited (NHL Rule 80.1) — though that whistle is conditional, not automatic: under the NHL play stops only if *your* team then gains possession. **USA Hockey Rule 621(d) is written the other way up** — play stops *unless* an opponent gains possession, or a defending player bats it into their own net, where the goal stands. ⚠️ **But its own Casebook applies the same test the other books print:** *"For play to be stopped for the high stick violation, an offending team player must be the first to gain possession of the puck"*, and *"[a] deflection off of a player from either team would not cause the play to be completed"* (Casebook Rule 621, Situation 15), and Situation 6 has play stopped only *"When the goalkeeper gains possession of the puck"* where a defender high-sticks it to their own goalkeeper. **So on a puck someone may still play, the whistle waits in all four** — USA Hockey is quicker only where the puck goes dead or nobody claims it — **but the ceiling on a goal does not wait for anybody: shoulder under USA Hockey, crossbar under the other three.** ⚠️ **And Situation 15 carries one exception to that waiting whistle, and it is the high tip that goes straight in:** *"The exception to this rule is when the puck played with the high stick goes directly in the goal of the non-offending team, which calls for an immediate stoppage of play and a face-off in the defending zone of the offending team."* Aim knee, waist or chest. See [Shooting](../technique/shooting.md).

**Traffic** — bodies between the goaltender and the puck, from either team. Distinct from a **screen**, which is specifically an opponent standing in the goaltender's sightline: your own defenceman can be traffic without being a screen. The word has a second sense away from the net — the bodies a skater has to navigate on the way somewhere, as in running a route through traffic. Use *traffic* or *route* for that, never "screen": no rulebook recognises a legal skater-on-skater screen, and USA Hockey Rule 625(a)(1) lists *"providing a protective screen and limiting the opportunity for an opposing player to apply pressure to a teammate in possession or control of the puck"* as interference outright. **The word is not what makes it legal.** You are entitled to the ice you are standing on, and you may block an opponent's path while you are in front of them and moving the same direction — but *"moving laterally and without establishing body position, then making contact with the non-puck carrier"* is interference (NHL and IIHF Rule 56.1), and moving into their path without body position is a *pick*. Never stop in a defender's path to take the contact. See [Playing Without the Puck](../hockey-iq/playing_without_the_puck.md) and [Time and Space](../hockey-iq/time_and_space.md).

**Trailer** — also the *late man*: a player arriving late into the offensive zone behind the puck carrier, into open space the defence has already collapsed away from. Often the most dangerous attacker on a rush. See [Defending the Rush](../systems/defending_the_rush.md).

**The trap** — a neutral-zone defensive system (usually a **1-2-2** or **1-3-1**) that concedes the opponent's own end and clogs the middle to force turnovers there. It is defined by *intent* as much as shape — a neutral-zone 1-2-2 is not automatically the trap. Beaten by patient regroups, quick puck movement, and — when the entry is not on — putting the puck deep past the red line and hunting it with three forwards. **Take those three as alternatives to choose between rather than as a ranking**, and the last one especially as a read on the picture in front of you rather than a general preference: the tracked work behind it watched NHL players and concluded they give the puck up at the line *too readily*, not too rarely. See [Neutral Zone Systems](../systems/neutral_zone_systems.md), which owns that study and its limits.

**Umbrella** — a power-play structure with **three players high** across the top and two low, shaped like an umbrella canopy. There is **no bumper** in a true umbrella.

**Walk the line** — a point player skating laterally along the blue line to change the shooting angle and pull the penalty kill or defensive box out of shape.

**The wall** — usually the **boards**: the perimeter wall around the rink, which is the sense in "on the wall", "off the wall" and "the strong-side wall". **But it also names a line of skaters strung across the ice denying a lane**, which is the sense in "behind the wall" and "a wall of four". The preposition usually tells you which — you play a puck *off* or *along* the boards, and you get *behind* a line of skaters. See **Boards**, which carries both senses in full.

**Wheel** — a defenceman skating the puck themselves from behind their own net around and up the ice to start the breakout, rather than passing. Good against a slow or one-man forecheck.

**Wrap-around** — carrying the puck from behind the net around the post and attempting to stuff it in at the far side before the goaltender can seal.

**Zone entry** — the act of getting the puck into the offensive zone. **Controlled** entries (carrying or passing it in with possession) produce far more shots and goals than **dump-ins**, which is why teams work so hard to enter with speed and width. See [Zone Entries](../systems/zone_entries.md).

## Common Mistakes

- **Hearing "high" as "up the ice".** It means toward the blue line you are facing, which in the offensive zone is back the way you came in. A forward who hears "rotate high" as "go to the net" has gone the wrong way.
- **Carrying "low means toward your own goal line" into the offensive zone.** In your own end that reading is fine, because there is only one goal line in play. At the other end, low is toward the net you are shooting at.
- **Taking "stay high" as one instruction.** For a winger it is two, and what separates them is not distance — both are up at the blue line — but *which team has the puck*: the defensive-zone one **expires** the moment your team wins it. A winger still glued to the point when their team has possession is not the outlet anybody is looking for.
- **Swinging low by a stick length.** "Swing low" means below the hash marks, not merely below the puck.
- **Reading F1, F2 and F3 as positions, or as people.** They are order of arrival. If a winger gets to the puck first, the winger is F1 and the centre is F2, from that moment.
- **Assuming F1 means somebody on your team.** On a breakout, F1 is the opponent bearing down on you. The numbers belong to whichever team is forechecking.
- **Treating a rim and a reverse as the same play.** A rim beats a forechecker sealing you against the wall; a reverse beats one who has over-committed past you. Sent the wrong way, each one finds an opponent instead of a teammate.
- **Confusing a pinch with stepping up.** A pinch is a defenceman coming down from the *offensive* blue line with forwards behind them. Stepping up is challenging forward in the neutral zone or at your own line, with nothing behind you if you miss.
- **Conflating the point and the half-wall.** The point is just inside the blue line; the half-wall is level with the faceoff dot, roughly twenty feet from the goal line. A defenceman who drifts down to the half-wall has abandoned the blue line.
- **Conflating the goalmouth and the slot.** They are two different coverage jobs one layer apart, and a defender who leaves the goalmouth to cover the slot has left a tap-in unmarked.
- **Hearing "the high slot is the centre's" as a rule of hockey.** It is what a low zone collapse asks for. Under man-on-man nobody holds a layer at all, and under a hybrid the low assignments are matched to players rather than to ice. Ask which your team plays.
- **Hearing "the checker" and "your check" as the same player.** One is the opponent pressuring you; the other is the opponent you are covering. The sentence around the word tells you which.
- **Reading "at the hash marks" as "above the dot line".** On the wall at the hash marks means the band between the hash-mark line and the goal line, at or just below the dot.
- **Confusing a one-touch with a one-timer.** A one-touch is a *pass* redirected on contact; a one-timer is a *shot* played the same way. Both fail badly on a bouncing puck or when you have not picked the target before it arrives, and a missed one usually goes the other way.
- **Calling your route past a defender a "screen".** A screen is something you do to the goaltender's sightline; between two skaters it is **traffic**, or a route. And the word does not make it legal — moving laterally into a defender's path, or stopping in it to take the contact, is interference whatever you call it.
- **Answering "Screen!" with a two-handed shove on the shaft.** That is cross-checking — a penalty in every book, and the reason to avoid it is the injury rather than the whistle: a screen faces the net, so the player you are shoving has their back to you and cannot see it coming, and USA Hockey names that exact scenario in its *Standard of Play and Rule Emphasis – Body Checking* as the first of its two cross-checking examples — *"When a player in front of the goal uses the shaft of the stick and extension of the arms to contact an opponent in a dangerous manner to create space."* Box them out with hips and back instead. All four books penalise it and only the wording differs — a minor at the referee's discretion under the NHL (59.2) and the IIHF (59.2), one that *"shall"* or *"will"* be assessed under USA Hockey 609(a) and Hockey Canada 9.2(a) — and **any major for cross-checking carries an automatic game misconduct in all four** (NHL 59.5, IIHF 59.3, USA Hockey 609(b), Hockey Canada 9.2(e)), so there is no cross-checking major you stay in the game after.
- **Keeping both feet outside the crease and reaching in with the stick.** Under USA Hockey that is the same offence as standing in there: Rule 625(b) writes its stoppage for an attacking player who *"stands, holds their stick, or skates through the goal crease"*, and its Casebook at Rule 625, Situation 10 disallows the goal scored *"while an attacking player has their stick and/or skates in the opponent's goal crease while the goalkeeper is in the crease"*. The NHL, the IIHF and Hockey Canada key their crease bar to your body instead. **One book of the four reaches your stick, and nothing on the ice tells you which one you are under.**
- **Hearing "back door" as one thing.** It is the weak-side space to a skater, the ice opened up behind a challenging goaltender to a goalie, and — shouted — an unmarked opponent at the far post who has to be taken right now.
- **Treating a goaltender outside the crease as a hittable player.** A goalkeeper is not *"fair game"* out there in any of the four books. The NHL, the IIHF and Hockey Canada say it of the crease in terms — Rules 42.1 and 69.4 in the first two, Rule 8.5 in the third, whose penalty for unnecessary contact with the goaltender reaches *"anywhere on the ice"*. **USA Hockey gets there in two steps rather than one**: 607(c) prices a body check or charge on a goalkeeper *"within the goal crease or privileged area"*, and 607(d) says they are not *"fair game"* even beyond that. A forechecker who finishes a check on a goalie who has come out to the wall or the corner to play a puck is charging, and Hockey Canada 8.5(b) makes that a major plus a game misconduct with no location limit and no discretion. **Play the puck, not the goaltender.**

## Key Takeaways

1. **Almost every location word in hockey is relative, and the thing it is relative to is the net that matters right now.** High is toward the blue line you are facing; low is toward the goal line. Learn what each word is measured from and the instructions stop contradicting each other.

2. **Strong side and weak side flip every time the puck crosses the middle — and how much that costs you depends on where you play.** Under a low zone collapse a defence pair swaps jobs on every crossing, a centre's job switches just as often, and a winger usually holds one side for a whole sequence. The labels still flip under man-on-man, but there are no side-based jobs to swap: you go with your check. Find out which your team plays.

3. **F1, F2 and F3 are order of arrival, not positions and not people — and they belong to whichever team is forechecking.** When you are breaking out, they are the opposition.

4. **The slot, the high slot and the goalmouth are fixed names for fixed ice. Who covers each one is a coaching choice.** Under a low zone collapse the high slot is the centre's and the goalmouth is the weak-side defenceman's; under man-on-man neither is anybody's until their check stands in it. Find out which your team plays.

5. **The half-wall is a winger's home base, and at even strength it is ice a centre stays off.** Those are two halves of one spacing rule given to two players, not two contradictory instructions. On the power play the second half does not hold — a half-wall spot there goes to a forward chosen for shot handedness rather than for position, and a centre may well have one. Ask where your unit puts you.

6. **The point is an area just inside the blue line, not the line itself — and it is not the half-wall.** Standing at it is a defenceman's offensive-zone job. Covering it is a winger's defensive-zone job **under a low zone collapse** — under man-on-man you take that defenceman and follow them off the point, into the corner if that is where they go. Find out which your team plays.

7. **A rim goes onward with the flow and a reverse goes back against it.** They beat opposite kinds of pressure, so the words are never interchangeable.

8. **A pinch comes down from the offensive blue line; stepping up goes forward in the neutral zone or at your own line.** Different ice, different risk, different cover behind you.

9. **"Stay high" and "your check" are two of the overloaded phrases most likely to catch you out.** Each has two live senses, and the sentence around it is what resolves them — ask if it does not.

10. **A goaltender's vocabulary is about position and choice rather than reaction.** Depth, square, front door and back door all name a trade-off being made on purpose, and a goaltender plays situations rather than a series of shots.

11. **A goaltender is never *"fair game"*, and whether you may body check anybody at all is a question about your classification rather than about hockey.** Unnecessary contact with a goaltender outside the crease is penalised in all four books. The NHL, the IIHF and Hockey Canada say it of the crease in terms — Rules 42.1 and 69.4 in the first two, Rule 8.5 in the third, whose penalty reaches *"anywhere on the ice"*. **USA Hockey gets there in two steps rather than one**: 607(c) prices a body check or charge on a goalkeeper *"within the goal crease or privileged area"*, and 607(d) says they are not *"fair game"* even beyond that. So finishing a check on a goalie who has come out to play a puck is charging rather than forechecking. And body checking itself is barred in the younger age groups and throughout girls' and women's play under USA Hockey and Hockey Canada — Hockey Canada's **Rule 7.3** *"applies only in divisions of U13 and below, in female hockey, and any other divisions approved by a Member of Hockey Canada"*, and where it applies **7.3(a)** is a minor for anyone who *"intentionally body-checks, bumps, shoves, or pushes any opposing player"*, which reaches a good deal less than a check — and in *"all non-check Adult classifications"* under USA Hockey Rule 604(a) — which also lets a local governing body prohibit it in any classification, so the printed list is a floor rather than an answer, and Britain publishes no age at all. **Find out what your league allows before you play.**

12. **Two things go wrong at the net front, and both of them are about your stick.** Under USA Hockey the crease bar reaches it — Rule 625(b) names an attacking player who *"stands, holds their stick, or skates through the goal crease"* — so a goal can come off the board with both your feet outside the line — **but only while the goalkeeper is in the crease, and not where the puck preceded you in or a defender's interference put you there** (Casebook, Rule 625, Situation 10, and the carve-outs written into 625(b) itself) — where the NHL, the IIHF and Hockey Canada key theirs to your body. And when a goaltender shouts *"Screen!"*, the player in front is moved with hips and back: a two-handed shove on the shaft is cross-checking in all four books and any major for it carries an automatic game misconduct — and **the reason is the injury rather than the whistle.** You are shoving a player who is facing the net with their back to you, which is the scenario USA Hockey's Standard of Play singles out as contact *"in a dangerous manner"*, and which can put the shove under the checking-from-behind rule instead, where none of the four books allows a bare minor: the NHL (43.2) and the IIHF (43.2) provide none at all, USA Hockey's 608(a) floor is a minor plus a misconduct, and Hockey Canada's 7.5(a) is a minor plus a game misconduct. **Box out with your body, and keep your stick out of the paint.**
