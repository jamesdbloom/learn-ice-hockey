# UK and England Rules

> **Rule set:** This document is *about* the rule set. The other documents here are written to NHL rules with IIHF, USA Hockey and Hockey Canada differences flagged inline; this one tells a reader in Britain which of those flags is theirs.
>

## Overview

If you play in Britain, **your rule book is the IIHF's.** Every level of the game here runs on it — the National Ice Hockey League, the recreational and junior game across England, Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland, university hockey and the GB national teams, and the Elite League too, though that last rests on the League's own public reporting rather than on its Casebook, which has not been read. Almost all hockey writing in English is written to NHL rules and so describes somebody else's game: wherever it flags an **IIHF** difference, that flag is yours; wherever it flags **USA Hockey** or **Hockey Canada**, it is not.

**Six things set British hockey apart** — five of them change what you do, and one is a question the rule books do not answer and a different document does. The first is the sharpest difference between Britain and North America:

**A neck laceration protector is mandatory for every player, adults included** — *"mandatory for all players in all IIHF categories and IHUK/EIH/SIH competitions"*, covering *"as much of the neck as possible"*. Under the In-House Rules, which apply across the Home Countries and in university hockey, failing to wear one is an immediate 10-minute misconduct with **no warning**, a second offence escalates to a game misconduct, and England Ice Hockey extends the requirement to *"all on ice activities"*, practices included. Assume no warning: the two British documents disagree on that point, nothing published resolves it, and the strict reading costs you nothing. USA Hockey exempts adults and Hockey Canada's rule reaches only minor and female hockey — neither exemption is yours. **And if it comes off during play you must leave the ice, and you may not retrieve it.**

**Whether body checking is legal at your age is not in the rule book — it is in your competition's Rules of Competition, and IHUK publishes one for every league it runs.** Neither the IIHF book nor the In-House Rules sets a checking age, and the North American lines are not in force and do not transfer. What IHUK's own regulations set for 2026-27 is this: junior **U10 and U12 are non-checking and U14, U16 and U19 are checking**; **NIHL Division 1 and 2 is checking**; **WNIHL is non-checking** at U16 and senior. ⚠️ **That covers the leagues IHUK runs and not the EIHL, BUIHA or Scottish domestic competitions outside the junior structure** — for those, ask your league before you hit anyone. [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md) §If you play in Britain quotes each one and sets out how a competition regulation sits alongside two silent rule books.

**There is no on-ice video review in EIH or SIHA competition, and no Coach's Challenge either.** The referee's call is the call. Video is still used after the final whistle for supplementary discipline, which can reach incidents nobody penalised at the time.

**Your centre is replaced on the draw for almost any faceoff violation, and either centre may put their stick down first.** Both are In-House Rule 76, new for 2026-27, and the stick-down order is a departure from the IIHF book rather than a copy of it. [Faceoffs](../systems/faceoffs.md) covers what that changes at the dot.

**Your rink may well be smaller than the rulebook's.** The In-House Rules authorise sheets below 56 m × 26 m for all levels, in all fixtures, naming two rinks permitted only up to U16 — and a sheet under that threshold is shorter than the only length the IIHF book recognises, 60 m, and narrower than its 26 m minimum width. No census of British rink dimensions has been published, so go and look at yours.

**A first major can end an adult's game.** IIHF Rule 20.4 attaches an automatic game misconduct to a major for any infraction on Reference Table 6 — twenty of them, including checking from behind, cross-checking, slashing, hooking, high-sticking, slew-footing, clipping and an illegal check to the head — and unlike the NHL it requires no injury for any of them. **That table number is the 2025/26 book's — the edition the In-House Rules' Introduction names — and the 2026/27 book has no Table 6 at all, having merged it into a single Table 5 that still carries all twenty.** This is the rule most likely to catch a player who learned the game watching the NHL, where the residual count is a **third** major and the IIHF's is a **second**.

On top of the IIHF book sits a short layer of local amendment:

| You play in | Your rules are |
|---|---|
| EIHL (Elite League) | IIHF Rule Book, plus the **EIHL Casebook** where the League's interpretation differs |
| NIHL, and the recreational and junior game across the Home Countries | IIHF Rule Book, plus the **In-House Rules** |
| BUIHA (university) | IIHF Rule Book, plus the same In-House Rules |
| GB national teams | IIHF Rule Book, unamended |

The In-House Rules say so in terms. Their Introduction reads: *"IHUK and the British Universities Ice Hockey Association (BUIHA) adopt the latest IIHF Official Rule Book (Version 1.1, published July 2025) as the foundation for the rules of the game to be applied in England, Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland (the Home Countries)."*

⚠️ **But the document contradicts itself about which IIHF edition that is, and nothing published settles it.** The In-House Rules' **cover** points to *"the Current IIHF Rule Book"*; its **Introduction** names *"Version 1.1, published July 2025"*, which is the **2025/26** book — yet also calls that book *"the latest"*, when the true latest, the 2026/27 edition, actually carries the *lower* version number, **Version 1.0, June 2026**. So the cover and the Introduction point at different books. **Ask your league which book its officials are working from, and where the two editions differ, plan around the stricter reading.**

**Which IIHF edition these documents quote.** Every IIHF rule below is the **2025/26** book's (Version 1.1, July 2025) unless flagged otherwise. ⚠️ **A 2026/27 edition exists and, where it has been checked, the differences are real** — its Rules 76.4, 76.6 and 76.7, for instance, turn a faceoff violation from a warning into the offending centre's replacement (see [Faceoffs](../systems/faceoffs.md)). Only a short list of rules has been checked against the 2026/27 book — Rules 20.4, 20.5, 28.1, 46, 60, 63.2, 64, 70, 76, 82.2, 87.1 and Appendix IV — the rest of that edition has not been read for this document. Treat every unflagged rule here as the 2025/26 position and check the current book before you rely on a detail.

⚠️ **The renumbering is the trap, and Rule 46 (fighting) is where it bites.** Every Rule 46 number in this document is the 2025/26 book's; the 2026/27 book reorders the whole rule, so the same number can mean something else entirely — most sharply **third player in**, which is **46.7** in the 2025/26 book and **46.14** in 2026/27, where **46.7 becomes fighting off the playing surface instead**. The rest of the moves: instigator 46.3→46.10, aggressor 46.4→46.2, sucker punch 46.5→46.13, altercation 46.2→46.3, clearing the area of a fight 46.8→46.4, fighting outside the periods 46.9→46.8, fighting off the playing surface 46.11→46.7, helmets 46.12→46.6, fighting prior to the drop of the puck 46.10→46.9, jerseys 46.13→46.11. The new order does not track the old one, so **look a fighting rule up by its heading, not its number, and find out which book your officials are carrying.**

**Appendix IV is renumbered too, and the 2026/27 book has no Table 6 at all.** The 2025/26 book's Tables 5, 6 and 7 are merged there into a single **Table 5**, with *"MAJOR ONLY"* and *"MAJOR + GMP"* columns covering the same twenty automatic and five discretionary entries, plus two fighting rows the old tables did not carry. Every entry named below is still on it. The merge also shifts every later table down by two — the 2025/26 book's Table 9, 16 and 18 are the 2026/27 book's Table 7, 14 and 16. **A table number only resolves in the book it came from — always name the edition.**

⚠️ **Five Rule 46 clauses changed in substance too, and the two that matter most both widen what you are exposed to.** **46.1 inverts the default.** The 2025/26 rule says a willing combatant *"may"* be ejected; the 2026/27 rule makes the major-plus-automatic-game-misconduct **"the standard penalty"** for anyone who fights. **Ejection stopped being something the referee may do and became the starting point.** The one discretion that widened runs the other way — a player judged to be *defending* against an aggressor or instigator can have the game misconduct waived. But the **third player in** waiver narrowed: the old rule waived it whenever a game misconduct was already being imposed in the original altercation; the new one waives it only where an *aggressor* penalty is being imposed **and** the referee judges the intervening player was stopping the fight, not starting a second one. **So stepping in to break up a fight costs you more under the new book, not less.**

Two smaller moves: the **persisting after being told to stop, or resisting a linesperson** limb shifts to its own clause (**46.5**) and is softened from a major-plus-misconduct that the 2025/26 book already left *"at the discretion of the Referee"* with a floor of *"at least"* that penalty, to *"a misconduct or game misconduct penalty"*; and **sucker-punching's** injury test narrows from a punch that *"could cause"* injury to one that *"causes"* it — though a sucker-punch is still a major plus an automatic game misconduct either way, so nothing here changes what it costs you. ⚠️ **And the 2025/26 book — Britain's — carries the test that decides whether you are a fighter or not, in Rule 46.6 *Defender – Unwilling Combatant*:** a player who *"defends themself with a 'few punches'"* is an unwilling combatant, while *"a Player who retaliates to being punched will be assessed at least a minor penalty ('Roughing') or a major penalty ('Fighting', without a game misconduct penalty), if the criteria for 'fighting back' with a few punches is not fulfilled."* **So punching back beyond a few defensive punches is what moves you up a tier.** ⚠️ **The 2026/27 book deletes the *Defender – Unwilling Combatant* heading — 46.6 is HELMETS there — and the protection that survives is weaker.** The sentence *"'unwilling combatant' and the automatic game misconduct penalty **may be waived at the discretion of the referee**"* appears in four separate clauses instead — but the **test is gone**: *"few punches"*, *"retaliates"* and *"fighting back"* return **zero** in that edition. So where 2025/26 said a defender **shall be considered** an unwilling combatant against *"an Aggressor, an Instigator / Initiator **or a fighter**"*, the new book leaves it **discretionary** and names only an aggressor or an instigator. **Read Rule 46 in the edition your competition actually plays.**

⚠️ **Rule 60 (high-sticking) also changed in substance, in two directions at once.** The 2025/26 book calls a high stick one *carried* above the opponent's shoulders; the 2026/27 book instead requires *contact* above the shoulders, provided the shoulders struck are at waist height or higher — narrower. But the double minor moved the other way: the 2025/26 book requires the stick to have been *carried* above the shoulders before contact causes injury — and both editions define that as *"in the manner of drawing blood **or otherwise**"*, so a bloodless facial injury still reaches the double minor; the 2026/27 book drops that carry requirement and reaches any contact above the struck player's waist. **So under the new book, a stick you never raised above your own shoulders can still be the four-minute double minor if it injures someone.** Plan around both halves at once: a stick carried high is a penalty before it touches anyone, and a stick that stays low can still be the double minor if it makes injuring contact. The IIHF's own published list of changes mentions only the narrower half, not the one that widens your exposure — so do not rely on that list either. [Shooting](../technique/shooting.md) sets out the full high-sticking ladder, in both editions and against the other three books.

⚠️ **And the height that decides it is measured on the player who was struck, not on you.** The 2026/27 Situation Handbook's Situation 60.13 rules that a stick barely above waist height is still a high-sticking penalty if it strikes an opponent bent over the puck in the face — the key, in the Handbook's own words, being that the player *"was hit in the neck/face/head with the opponent's stick, at or above the height of the waist of the player being struck."* **It is where the contact landed on them, not how high you carried your stick.** ⚠️ **And that ruling is 2026/27 only — the 2025/26 handbook stops at Situation 60.12 and publishes no interpretation of this case, so on Britain's own edition there is no published ruling either way.** **Lift a stick low, on the shaft, and not at a player bent over the puck.**

> **What to call them.** This document calls the domestic amendments the **IHUK
> In-House Rules** — the name IHUK itself uses throughout them, including in the
> file name England Ice Hockey publishes them under. Both the 2025-26 and 2026-27
> editions were read in the primary text (29 August 2026).
>
> ⚠️ **What they still do not state.** The In-House Rules carry **no "issued by", no publisher, no author and no copyright line anywhere**, and amendments to the document itself are written in the passive with no agent. England Ice Hockey's sister *Rules & Regulations* does name itself, so the silence reads as habit rather than accident — but it means the document's own authority is asserted nowhere inside it.
>
> **And nothing here rests on a superseded revision of the IIHF book.** Its two 2025/26 revisions were compared line by line on **30 July 2026**: **all 474 rule numbers are identical in v1.0 and v1.1**, none renumbered, and the Rule 60 clauses quoted **above** are word-for-word the same in both.

> **⚠️ The In-House Rules are not a rule book.** They are amendments, and they say it themselves: *"This document is to be read alongside the IIHF Rule Book and is not intended to serve as a standalone rule book."* You need both. They are also the **overriding** interpretation — where they differ from the IIHF book, they win domestically.

## Who governs what

> **⚠️ The playing rules have caught up with the merger. The governance document
> has not.** On **30 June 2026** England Ice Hockey, Scottish Ice Hockey and Ice
> Hockey UK merged into a single body covering all four home nations — see
> [Getting Started](../getting-started/getting_started.md) for the merger and its
> dates.
>
> **The 2026-27 In-House Rules answer what the merger left open.** Their
> Introduction states: *"The governing body for ice hockey in the United Kingdom
> is Ice Hockey UK Limited (IHUK)… References to EIH or SIH shall, where the
> context requires, be construed as references to IHUK."* They also widen their
> own reach from England and Scotland to *"England, Wales, Scotland and Northern
> Ireland (the Home Countries)"*. So where an older document says EIH or SIH, read
> IHUK.
>
> **The older EIH and SIH documents have not been repealed. They have been
> reassigned.** The same Introduction says: *"There may be references in these
> terms to policies of England Ice Hockey (EIH) and Scottish Ice Hockey (SIH)
> which are now policies of IHUK and will continue to apply to all Clubs and
> Teams. IHUK is now entitled to enforce these. In due course we will rebrand EIH
> and SIH policies and other documentation."* So an England Ice Hockey rule quoted
> below is still live, and it is now Ice Hockey UK that is entitled to enforce it.
>
> ⚠️ **What that sentence does not say is whether a policy keeps the boundary it
> was written with.** England Ice Hockey's *Rules & Regulations* scope themselves to
> England and Wales. Nothing published says whether a policy that becomes IHUK's
> thereby reaches Scotland and Northern Ireland, or carries its old boundary across
> with it. That question decides the trapezoid suspension below, and neither British
> document answers it.
>
> **The *Rules & Regulations* quoted in this section have not caught up.** They
> are the **2024-25 edition**, issued before the merger, and no post-merger
> replacement could be found on either governing body's site as of 30 July 2026.
> Their provisions still describe rules nobody has published anything to replace.
> But where this document leans on their **England-and-Wales scoping** — for the
> trapezoid suspension especially — that scoping now sits underneath a single
> UK-wide governing body, and whether it survives the next edition is not
> something any published document answers. Treat every body name below as the
> name used in the document it is quoted from.

**England Ice Hockey (EIH)** governs the game in **England and Wales** — its own *Rules & Regulations* say so at 1.1, 3.1 and 17.1. It was called the English Ice Hockey Association, and you will still see "EIHA" everywhere — on club sites, in league names, in older documents. Same body.

**The Scottish Ice Hockey Association (SIHA)** governs Scotland, and shares the same In-House Rules document with EIH. You will meet it under two names in the same document: **SIHA** in the rules themselves, and **Scottish Ice Hockey (SIH)** in their Introduction and in the phrase *"IHUK/EIH/SIH competitions"*. Same body.

**Ice Hockey UK (IHUK)** is the national governing body recognised by the IIHF, and runs the GB national teams. Since the merger it is also *"the governing body for ice hockey in the United Kingdom"* in the In-House Rules' own words, it is IHUK that adopts the IIHF book for domestic play, and games are described there as **IHUK-sanctioned** where the previous edition said EIH-sanctioned.

**The British Universities Ice Hockey Association (BUIHA)** runs university hockey and adopts the same In-House Rules. It is the one body named alongside IHUK in the 2026-27 Introduction and did not merge into it.

## The amendments that change what you do

These are the ones worth knowing before you play. Everything not listed here is enforced in line with the IIHF book.

### Neck protection is mandatory, for everyone, with no warning

This is the sharpest difference between Britain and North America, and it is the one most likely to catch out a reader who has learned the game from North American material.

Under the In-House Rules, *"a neck laceration protector is mandatory for all players in all IIHF categories and IHUK/EIH/SIH competitions"*, and it must *"cover as much of the neck as possible."* Enforcement is unusually hard:

- **No warning is given.** Failing to wear a neck laceration protector is an immediate **10-minute misconduct penalty**.
- If your neck protector comes off during play, **you must leave the ice** and may not retrieve it.
- A second neck-protector violation by the same player is another 10-minute misconduct, which **automatically escalates to a game misconduct**.
- **The neck-protector requirement is not only a game rule.** England Ice Hockey's *Rules & Regulations* 24.3 makes it *"mandatory that all players at all levels use an approved Ice Hockey Neck Guard/Protector whilst participating in all on ice activities"* — practices and stick-and-puck included.
- **Somebody is now required to check your neck protector before the warm-up.** In-House Rule 5.4 puts a pre-warm-up equipment check on the coach or manager, who *"must verify helmet/facemask fit, chin-strap tightness and neck laceration protection are worn per manufacturer instructions and these rules"*. The neck protector was added to that check for 2026-27; the check itself is not new.

The **mandate** itself is IIHF Rule 9.12, which requires a neck laceration protector *"for all Players in all categories of IIHF Competition"* — so it travels with you to any IIHF competition, not just British ones. What the In-House Rules change is the **enforcement** — and only in senior hockey. IIHF 9.12 gives *"a warning by the Referee"* and then a minor penalty, where the In-House Rules give no warning and a 10-minute misconduct. **In junior hockey the gap is only the warning:** 9.12 routes junior violations to Rule 202.7 (men's junior) and 102.7 (women's), where the ladder is a warning to the team through the coach and then *"a ten minute (10') misconduct penalty"* — the same penalty the In-House Rules impose. So against the IIHF book the domestic rule is stricter for adults and, for juniors, differs only in dropping the warning. Either way it is stricter, never more lenient.

⚠️ **University hockey is not named in the domestic list, and is not exempt.** In-House Rule 9.12 reaches *"all IIHF categories and IHUK/EIH/SIH competitions"* and does not mention the BUIHA. But the BUIHA adopts the IIHF Rule Book alongside IHUK, and the **mandate** is the IIHF's, so it binds in a university game whatever the domestic list says. The In-House Rules are also the *"overriding interpretation of how the IIHF Rule Book is to be applied domestically"*, and that is what the BUIHA adopted — so expect the domestic **enforcement** as well. Their BUIHA section's only equipment provision is the half-visor minimum, and nothing in it lifts the neck-guard requirement. Neither edition says in terms whether the 10-minute misconduct reaches a BUIHA fixture; wearing the guard settles the question either way.

⚠️ **The two British documents do not agree on the warning, and nothing published resolves it.** The In-House Rules 9.12 say *"No warning will be given for failing to wear a neck guard"*. But England Ice Hockey's *Rules & Regulations* 24.6 — three clauses below the equipment rules quoted above — sets out a warning scheme: *"This counts as a warning to the player and bench, for any further violation of rule either 24.3, 24.4 or 24.5 misconduct penalties will be assessed."* The In-House Rules are the current playing-rule amendments and the *Rules & Regulations* on disk are the 2024-25 edition, which is why the In-House Rules are followed throughout here. **Wear the neck guard and assume no warning** — that is the conservative reading and it costs nothing. This is the same pattern as the trapezoid: a second British document saying something different, and no published statement of which governs.

Compare North America, where the requirement is narrower: **USA Hockey Rule 304(g)** requires it in all classifications *except* adults, and **Hockey Canada Rule 3.6(c)** covers players registered in minor and female hockey. A British adult reading only those would reasonably conclude the requirement is not theirs. It is.

### There is no on-ice video review

In-House **Rule 37** reads: *"On-ice video review is not available in EIH or SIHA competition and may not be used by referees, official scorers, timekeepers, or any other game official."* The In-House Rules then repeat that bar in **nine** further provisions, spread across **eight** other rules: double minor penalties (18.4), the official scorer (33.2), the game timekeeper (34.7), the penalty timekeeper (35.8), high-sticking (60.3), goaltender interference (69.1), goals (78.5 **and** 78.6) and puck out of bounds (85.7). Eight of the nine repeat Rule 37's opening clause verbatim and add a cross-reference — *"On-ice video review is not available in EIH or SIHA competition (see Rule 37)"* — while **78.6** alone words it differently: *"Video review is not in use in EIH or SIHA competition (see Rule 37)"*.

**The Coach's Challenge goes with it.** In-House Rule 38: *"As on-ice video review is not available (see Rule 37), a Coach's Challenge is not permitted in EIH or SIHA competition."* There is nothing to challenge with, so there is no challenge.

Practically: the referee's call on the ice is the call. Where you read or hear about a decision being reviewed on video, that is the NHL and international tournament game, not yours.

**"On-ice" is doing work in that sentence, though.** Video is still used *after* the game. In-House **Rule 28** says a supplementary-discipline case may be opened either through an official match report or through *"the defined video review process"*. And a separate, unnumbered **Supplementary Discipline** section later in the same document — after Appendix 2, not part of Rule 28 — adds that the disciplinary body may investigate any incident after the final whistle *"regardless of whether such offences were penalised by the Referee at the time."* Nothing is reviewed during the game; that is not the same as nothing being reviewed.

### Rinks are often smaller than the book says

IIHF Rule 1.2 specifies a sheet *"60m long and 26m to 30m wide"* — note that 30 m is the top of a range, not the standard. The In-House Rules provide that *"rinks with a non-standard playing surface (below 56 m × 26 m) are authorised by the NGB for all levels of hockey"*, with two named rinks permitted only up to U16 — **Sutton** in England and **Coatbridge** in Scotland.

**Both the list of named rinks and the scope of that In-House authorisation changed for 2026-27.** The 2025-26 edition named a third rink, Limekilns Road in Scotland, and carried the scope line *"This rule applies to all EIH and SIHA fixtures."* The 2026-27 edition names only the two and reads *"This rule applies to all fixtures."* So the authorisation now reaches university hockey too, where before it did not. The Elite League is not mentioned anywhere in the In-House Rules, so nothing here establishes what applies there.

That threshold sits **outside IIHF Rule 1.2 altogether**: the book fixes the length at 60 m and makes 26 m its *minimum* width, so a rink below 56 m × 26 m is short of the book on both counts. [Rink Map](rink_map.md) covers the dimensions and the markings.

This matters more than it sounds. [Time and Space](../hockey-iq/time_and_space.md) explains what rink size does to the game: on a smaller sheet the distances shrink, the angles do not, and everything arrives sooner. Check your rink before you assume the geometry in any document applies to it.

### Facial protection and helmet fit

- **Women's hockey only:** the In-House Rules place *"Players may wear a half visor only from the start of the season following their 18th birthday"* under **Rule 102 – Women's Hockey Specific Equipment Rules**. Read it as a **relaxation, not a restriction**: IIHF Rule 102.1 requires *"full-face protection (full-visor, or cage facial protection)"* for every player in the women's Senior category, and this reads as permitting a half visor instead once the player is past that birthday. ⚠️ **That is the natural reading and nothing published states it.** The whole of In-House Rule 102 is that one sentence, identical in the 2025-26 and 2026-27 editions, and it never says it displaces 102.1 — which closes *"Players without the mentioned protection are not allowed to participate in a game."* **A full face satisfies both readings, so wear one if you want certainty.** Nothing here advises dropping to a half visor.
- All players in **SIHA Recreational or SIHA University** hockey must wear at minimum a half visor, *"regardless of their date of birth"* (Rule 9.7).
- **The women's half-visor clause and the SIHA half-visor minimum do not conflict: a woman under 18 wears full-face protection.** A half visor is a floor, and full-face protection clears it. **Scottish recreational hockey does not reach the question anyway** — no player under 18 may play recreational hockey in Scotland.
- **University hockey has its own clause too.** *"All players in BUIHA games are required to wear a half visor as a minimum."* So if you play BUIHA — English and Scottish university hockey — the half-visor minimum is yours regardless of which clause you reach first.
- **Face cages are mandatory in junior hockey in England and Wales only** — that's an England Ice Hockey rule, and outside its jurisdiction the floor is the looser IIHF one: a full-face visor **or** a cage at Under-18, just a visor at Under-20. The In-House Rules impose no cage mandate anywhere. So: a cage throughout junior hockey in England and Wales, full-face protection of **either** kind at Under-18 elsewhere in Britain.
- The gap between chin and chin strap **must not exceed 2.5 cm** — about one finger. First offence sends you to the bench to adjust; second is a 10-minute misconduct. The IIHF book beneath this only requires the strap be *"properly fastened,"* with no measurement — the figure is British. USA Hockey uses the same one-finger test, so it is not uniquely strict; don't assume the British number is the strict end if you play elsewhere.
- ⚠️ **If your helmet comes off during play, you must leave the ice immediately.** In-House **Rule 9.8**: *"If a player's helmet becomes detached during play, they must immediately leave the ice. Failure to comply will be penalised, with a misconduct penalty, under Rule 12 – Illegal Equipment."* That is the exact counterpart of the neck-guard rule above, and playing on bare-headed is not a choice the rule leaves you — it is a misconduct, and two misconducts in a game end it under IIHF Rule 20.4.
- A full cage must have the chin cup fitted and in contact with the chin, *"within the helmet's J-clips"*; loose or dangling cages and straps are not permitted. **Enforcement has its own ladder** under In-House **Rule 12**: the referee sends you off the ice to adjust; returning with the same violation is a *"Minor Penalty – Illegal Equipment"*; and subsequent violations by the same player are 10-minute misconducts, the second of which is a game misconduct.
- **A cage and a helmet that were not sold to go together are Dangerous Equipment.** In-House Rule 12: *"Helmet–facemask combinations must match manufacturer sizing. Mis-sized combinations are considered Dangerous Equipment, and the player will be removed until corrected."* This is the rule that catches second-hand kit, so check the manufacturer's sizing before you put a cage on a helmet you already own — see [Equipment](../off-the-ice/equipment.md).

### The trapezoid may not be enforced

The IIHF book has a goalkeeper's restricted area (Rule 1.8 for the geometry, 27.7 for the penalty), so on the face of it British hockey has a trapezoid. England Ice Hockey's own *Rules & Regulations* say otherwise:

> **22.3.** *"Goalkeepers' Restricted Area is not currently enforced to allow ice rinks the necessary time to make the changes to implement this rule."*

England Ice Hockey's Rule 22.3 is the governing body suspending an IIHF rule domestically — **and only in its own jurisdiction.** England Ice Hockey is the NGB *"in England and Wales"* (*Rules & Regulations* 1.1), so 22.3 does not reach **Scotland (SIHA)**, **Northern Ireland** or **BUIHA university hockey**. All three sit inside the In-House Rules' territory — since 2026-27 those apply *"in England, Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland (the Home Countries)"*, where the 2025-26 edition reached only England and Scotland — and the In-House Rules are silent on the restricted area, their Introduction saying *"Unless otherwise stated, all rules will be enforced in line with the IIHF Rule Book."* So for a Scottish, Northern Irish or university player IIHF 27.7 reads as live. **The Elite League is a separate question that cannot be answered here**: EIHL clubs play in England, and the League runs its own Casebook that nobody here has read. The suspension's stated reason — giving rinks time to repaint — is also why so many British rinks have no trapezoid painted on them; treat an unmarked sheet as a hint rather than a ruling, because 27.7 turns on *"the position of the puck"*, not on the markings.

**Play as though it binds, wherever you are.** For a goaltender that costs nothing and the alternative is two minutes. For an attacking team the safe assumption runs the other way — plan as though *their* goalie may come for a dumped puck.

**And note what 22.3 does not suspend.** It names the *"Goalkeepers' Restricted Area"* and nothing else. It leaves untouched the separate delay-of-game minor for a goalkeeper who *"holds, 'freezes' or plays the puck with their stick, skates or body in such a manner as to deliberately cause a stoppage of play"* — a rule that, in its own words, *"applies outside of their goal crease area"* (IIHF Rule 63.2(I), word-for-word identical in the 2025/26 and 2026/27 editions). Outside the crease means **inside** the designated area as well as beyond it. So a goaltender who is told the trapezoid is not enforced must not conclude that they are unrestricted behind the net. Where they may **play** the puck, and what they may **do with it** once they are there, are two different rules — and only the first of them is suspended. The trigger is the deliberate stoppage — a goaltender who comes out to cut down the angle and covers the puck after making the save is expressly legal (63.2(VI), Note).

**⚠️ Treat this as unsettled.** The non-enforcement is in the **2024-2025** edition of the *Rules & Regulations*. **Neither the 2025-26 nor the 2026-27 In-House Rules mention the restricted area at all** — both editions were searched for it — so the published playing-rule amendments do not establish whether the suspension still stands. Ask your league. It changes where your goaltender may retrieve a rim, which is a decision they make several times a game.

### Your centre is replaced for almost any faceoff violation — and either centre may put the stick down first

Two sentences, both new for 2026-27, both under In-House **Rule 76 – Face-Offs**. The 2025-26 edition had no Rule 76 at all.

**On a faceoff violation under In-House Rule 76, the centre goes.** *"With the exception of face-offs following icings, the linesperson shall immediately replace the centre for all violations."* So on any draw except the one following an icing, a violation costs your team its centre for that draw — including a violation your **winger** commits by stepping into the circle, because it is the centre the rule removes, not the player who moved.

**Either centre may put their stick down first.** *"Linespersons must ensure both centres are squared up correctly behind their marks with the whole of their stick blades (not toe or heel) in the white portion of the face-off circle. Either centre can now put their stick down first for a face-off."* ⚠️ **That is a British departure from the IIHF book, not an IIHF change.** IIHF Rule 76.4 gives the order to the **defending** player at eight of the nine spots and to the **visiting** player at centre ice — in identical words in the 2025/26 and 2026/27 editions, both checked. In Britain that order is switched off.

[Faceoffs](../systems/faceoffs.md) covers what both changes do at the dot, and how the British position now sits against the NHL, USA Hockey and Hockey Canada.

### Embellishment costs you twelve minutes, not two

In-House **Rule 64: Embellishment** is one line, and it is new for 2026-27: *"Adding a misconduct to a minor. The required call is now 2 plus 10."*

Under the IIHF book a dive is a **minor and nothing more**. Rule 64.1 penalises a player who *"blatantly dives", "embellishes" a fall or a reaction, or who "feigns an injury"* with a minor penalty, and 64.2 says the same of attempting to draw a penalty; the wording is identical in the 2025/26 and 2026/27 editions. The British amendment bolts a **10-minute misconduct** onto it.

**Read "2 plus 10" the way the penalty box does.** You sit for twelve minutes. Your **team** is short-handed for two, not twelve, because a substitute goes to the box and serves the minor while you serve the misconduct (IIHF Rule 22.3). But the misconduct still counts against you: **a second misconduct in the same game is an automatic game misconduct** under IIHF Rules 20.4 and 22.1. So an embellishment call plus one other misconduct — a missing neck guard, a chin-strap gap on the second offence — ends your night.

### A broken stick must be dropped, not carried

In-House **Rule 10.3** is new for 2026-27: *"Skating with a broken stick anywhere on the ice is not permitted under IHUK In-House Rules. A player whose stick is broken must drop their stick immediately. Failure to do so will incur a 2min penalty for illegal equipment"*.

The IIHF book already required a broken stick to be dropped — its Rule 10.3 lets a player *"participate in the game provided they drop the 'broken stick'"*, on a minor penalty for an infraction. What the domestic rule adds is **reach and a label**: skating with it *anywhere on the ice*, not merely playing with it, and the call announced as illegal equipment. Playing without a stick is fine. Get a replacement handed to you from your bench or by a teammate on the ice, or pick an unbroken one off the ice — but nobody may **throw** one to you, which is a penalty in itself under the same IIHF rule.

### Fighting

In NIHL (all leagues), SNL and BUIHA Varsity, fighting is a **five-minute major**. In NIHL, NIHL1 and NIHL2, a fighting major in the **last five minutes of regulation or in overtime also carries an automatic game misconduct**.

**New for 2026-27, the unwilling combatant is dealt with in terms.** Section 6 now adds that such a player *"may be awarded (at the referee's discretion) a minor OR a major penalty (2 OR a standalone 5 without a game misconduct)"*. So being dragged into a fight you did not want can still cost you five minutes, and which of the two you get is the referee's call — but on the domestic wording it does not end your game.

⚠️ **Two limits on that, and both matter more than the five minutes does.**

**It reaches those three competitions and no others.** In-House Section 6 scopes itself to *"NIHL (all leagues), SNL, and BUIHA Varsity"*, and the In-House introduction says *"Unless otherwise stated, all rules will be enforced in line with the IIHF Rule Book."* In any other fixture under the In-House Rules the IIHF book stands unamended, and there **46.1 is a Reference Table 6 entry** — so a fighting major carries an automatic game misconduct, and 46.1 itself reaches the plain willing combatant directly — ejection is the referee's discretion in the 2025/26 book and **the standard penalty** in the 2026/27 one. In junior hockey any major adds a game misconduct regardless.

**And In-House Section 6 reaches the plain willing fight and nothing else.** Section 6 names no rule number, so the rest of Rule 46 is untouched: **instigator (46.3)** and **aggressor (46.4)** each add a major and an automatic game misconduct on top of a minor; **sucker-punching (46.5)** and **fighting outside the periods (46.9)** are a major plus an automatic game misconduct; **fighting off the playing surface (46.11)** adds a game misconduct on top of any major; **third man in (46.7)** is a game misconduct at the referee's discretion, except where one is already being imposed in the original altercation; and **46.1** lets the referee impose *"at least"* a major plus an automatic game misconduct on a player who persists after being ordered to stop, or who resists a linesperson. Rule 28 supplementary discipline sits above all of it.

### Leaving the bench or the penalty box during an altercation

**This rule is scoped to no age and no competition, and the folklore about it is wrong in both directions.** [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md) sets the four rule books side by side and prices each of them. This is the British half: which book binds you, what the domestic layer does **not** change, and what moved in the 2026/27 edition.

**IIHF Rule 70.1 is one sentence with no exception written into it:** *"No Player may leave the Players' bench or penalty box at any time during an altercation or for the purpose of starting an altercation."* And **Rule 70 is one of the rules the renumbering left alone** — all ten of its sub-rules keep the same numbers under the same headings in both editions, so unlike Rule 46 there is no number here to get wrong.

**Nothing domestic amends it, and that negative was looked for rather than assumed.** In-House **Section 6** is the fighting amendment and it reaches fighting only — the five-minute major, the unwilling combatant, and the last-five-minutes game misconduct in NIHL, NIHL1 and NIHL2 (§Fighting). Neither edition of the In-House Rules mentions Rule 70, and neither uses the word *altercation* anywhere; the one provision they head for benches and penalty boxes, Rule 3, is about how the home venue must build them. So the Introduction's default governs — *"Unless otherwise stated, all rules will be enforced in line with the IIHF Rule Book"* — and **the domestic softening of the plain willing fight does not reach this rule.** Playing NIHL changes what a fight costs you. It changes nothing about leaving the bench.

⚠️ **"Altercation" is a far lower bar than a fight, and your own book defines it.** IIHF Rule 46.2 — renumbered **46.3** in the 2026/27 book, one of the moves set out in §Overview — reads *"An 'altercation' is a situation involving at least two (2) Players with at least one Player (1) to be penalized"*, and the 2026/27 wording is the same but for dropping *"at least"* before *"two"*. **One penalty, not one punch.** A post-whistle scrum in which somebody takes a roughing minor is an altercation, and from that moment the bench door and the box are shut.

⚠️ **The penalty box is where this catches ordinary players rather than fighters.** The IIHF's own Situation Handbook rules on a player sitting on a hooking minor who leaves the box while two others fight, doing nothing but skating around the altercation: **an additional minor penalty plus a game misconduct.** **Skating around it is not staying out of it.**

⚠️ **And do not read "leave on expiry of your penalty" as your permission.** Rule 70.6 attaches a second condition: you may not leave until your penalty expires **and** your team is entitled to an extra skater on the ice. Coincident penalties are the everyday case where those two moments don't line up — your time is up, but your team isn't yet entitled to the extra skater, and the stoppage you were waiting for turns out to be a fight. **Stay in the box until an official waves you out.**

⚠️ **The 2026/27 book writes explicit supplementary-discipline exposure into these clauses, for both the player and the coach — but the in-game penalty is unchanged.** Leaving the bench or box during an altercation is still a minor plus a game misconduct either way; what's new is that several Rule 70 clauses now say in terms that a player who leaves, and that player's coach, can also face supplementary discipline under Rule 28. That authority already existed in Rule 28 itself, so nothing here changes what leaving costs you on the night — only that the route to further discipline is now written down.

**What to do is one sentence.** If an altercation starts while you are on the bench or in the penalty box, you stay where you are until an official releases you — not to help a teammate, not to change, and not because your penalty has just expired.

### Overtime is not universal

In-House **Rule 84** provides that overtime *"is only played in NIHL fixtures and in SNL fixtures where specifically provided for in competition regulations."* Many games simply end level.

Where NIHL National League games do go to overtime: **five minutes of 3-on-3**, then a game-winning-shots competition with **three shooters per team** taking alternate shots, and if still level it continues one shot each as sudden victory. And unlike IIHF 84.4, which requires five *different* shooters, the British procedure says in terms that *"the same player may be used for multiple attempts."*

**The Playoff Final Weekend carries two separate procedures, and they differ in kind — not in degree.** Appendix 1 sets them out as distinct provisions:

- **Semi-finals.** *"If the score in the Semi Finals is level after regulation"*, the game goes to **20 minutes of 5-on-5** overtime, and *"if still tied, a Game Winning Shots (GWS) competition will take place"* — the same three-shooter procedure as above.
- **The Final.** *"If the score in the Final is level after regulation, the game will proceed to successive 20:00 periods of 5-on-5 sudden-death overtime until a goal is scored ('golden goal')"* — and then, in terms: *"No Game Winning Shots will be used."*

So a semi-final can end in a shootout and the Final cannot. Do not carry either across to the other: the Final is played out until somebody scores, however many periods that takes.

**BUIHA has a designated exception, and it sits in tension with Rule 84's word "only".** Under *Rules Applying Specifically to BUIHA Games in England and Scotland*, Appendix 1 provides: *"Certain fixtures will be designated as VARSITY OT games on the schedule. These games will follow the NIHL rules for Overtime and Game Winning Shots."* Read literally, Rule 84 confines overtime to NIHL and SNL fixtures and so excludes BUIHA altogether. **That tension is in the In-House Rules themselves rather than introduced here**, and nothing published resolves it — but the Varsity OT clause is the more specific provision, written for a named case, so follow it where your fixture is marked **VARSITY OT** on the schedule. Where a BUIHA fixture is not so marked, Rule 84 is the general rule and there is no overtime.

### Age categories are British, and unisex

Junior categories are set by birth year, not by the North American banding — and **the bands move by a full year every season**, so the only table you can safely use is the one printed in the current season's In-House Rules. These are the **2026-27** bands, from In-House **Rule 200 – Junior Hockey Age Categories**:

| Category | Birth years — 2026-27 season |
|---|---|
| Under 19 | 2008–2010 |
| Under 16 | 2011–2012 |
| Under 14 | 2013–2014 |
| Under 12 | 2015–2016 |
| Under 10 | 2017 and younger |

⚠️ **Check the season on any birth-year table you are handed, including this one.** Every band shifted by a year between the 2025-26 and 2026-27 editions: under the 2025-26 In-House Rules Under 19 was 2007–2009, Under 16 was 2010–2011, Under 14 was 2012–2013, Under 12 was 2014–2015 and Under 10 was 2016 and younger. So a player born in **2010** was Under 16 last season and is **Under 19** this one. A table copied from a previous season puts a child in the wrong age group — in a collision sport that is not a filing error, and the gap between a fourteen-year-old and an eighteen-year-old is the whole point of having categories. Read the bands out of the current In-House Rules and nowhere else.

The In-House Rules add a note worth quoting: *"Junior age categories are unisex and are not classified as 'Mens' within the UK."*

**In women's hockey a major penalty ends your game at every age, adult included.** In-House Rule 101 says a major *"will, in all cases,"* also draw an ejection, and states no age scope — a separate sibling clause in the same rule adds the IIHF's own under-20 ejection rule for non-adults specifically, which would do nothing new if the main clause were already limited to juniors. **The Adult definition (14th birthday onward) is not an exemption from it.** ⚠️ No official British interpretation of how this is called on the ice has been obtained here, but the text supports the conservative reading: plan on a major ending your game at any age, and confirm with your league.

**Junior hockey reads the same way, U19 down to U10 — but this is this document's inference, not the rule's own words.** In-House Rule 201 applies "these rules" to all junior age groups without naming the specific IIHF ejection rule the way the women's rule does. It's the natural reading of a rule scoped to all junior age groups, and the conservative one: play as though a major ends a junior's game, and confirm with your league.

### A first major can end your game, and adults are not exempt

The section above covers junior hockey, and women's hockey at every age including adult. **This
one is about you if you are an adult in any competition**, and it is the rule most likely to catch a player who learned the game watching the
NHL — and unlike most of this section it is the IIHF's own text rather than a British amendment, so
it binds in every competition that plays the IIHF book.

Your book is the IIHF's, and **IIHF Rule 20.4** attaches an automatic game misconduct to any major penalty listed in the Reference Tables. No injury needed, and once the major is called the ejection is not the referee's to withhold.

⚠️ **Two majors end your night too, on a separate trigger — and so do two misconducts.** Rule 20.4 also ejects a player on a **second** major or a **second** misconduct in the same game, whatever they're for, and **Rule 22.1** repeats the misconduct half — *"An 'Automatic Game Misconduct' shall be applied to any Player who has been assessed a second (2nd) misconduct penalty in the same game."* ⚠️ **Both halves are stricter than the book most hockey writing is about.** The NHL's own Rule 20.4 counts only majors and counts them one higher — *"a third major penalty in the same game"* — and its Rule 22, the misconduct rule, has no counting limb at all: the whole of 22.1 to 22.5 was read, along with Rule 23 and the summary tables behind both, and nothing in the NHL book turns two misconducts in a game into an ejection. So a player calibrated on NHL hockey has one major and one misconduct more of margin than they actually have here. That matters because a missing neck guard is a 10-minute misconduct with no warning, and a second violation escalates to a game misconduct automatically — see [Rules Primer](rules_primer.md) for the repeat-offence rule.

**Reference Table 6** carries **twenty** entries, three of them fighting; the other seventeen include **checking from behind, cross-checking, slashing, hooking, high-sticking, slew-footing, clipping and an illegal check to the head**, plus a women's-only checking entry and a catch-all for any dangerous action a referee judges endangers another player — so not knowing the exact list is no defence. **Plan around all of them.** The only domestic softening is the **plain willing fight**, in NIHL, SNL and BUIHA Varsity (§Fighting) — every other Rule 46 fighting offence (instigating, being the aggressor, sucker-punching, fighting outside the periods, fighting off the ice) keeps its IIHF major-plus-ejection.

**Table 7 adds five more first-major ejection risks** — boarding, charging, elbowing, interference and tripping — each bundling the major and game misconduct into a single act the referee assesses at their discretion rather than as an automatic consequence of Rule 20.4. The book's own wording leaves genuine room to read Table 7 as outside 20.4's automatic reach, but nothing settles it either way. **Play as though the ejection follows the major regardless** — being wrong about *that* costs you nothing, because you simply avoided a hit you did not have to take; being wrong the other way costs you the game.

⚠️ **Do not read this as leniency by comparison with the NHL.** A player used to NHL rules should recalibrate, and the NHL's own summary table gives the size of the gap. Its Table 5 lists **thirteen** non-fighting infractions that can bring an automatic game misconduct with the major, and on **six** of them — boarding, charging, clipping, elbowing, hooking and interference — the automatic ejection arrives only where the opponent was injured, in three cases only on an injury *"to face/head"*. **None of the IIHF equivalents require an injury at all** — recklessness or endangerment alone is enough. And the IIHF book has **no match-penalty tier at all**, for anything: your in-game ceiling is the major plus the automatic misconduct, with Rule 28 supplementary discipline sitting above it uncapped. **On first-major ejection, yours is the stricter book.**

### Officials have been told to call the foul what it is

New for 2026-27, and addressed to referees rather than to you. In-House **Section 4 – Types of Penalties** opens: *"This season we are going to place stronger emphasis on our officials to correctly label penalties they are assessing."* It reports that across the British championships officials have in many cases defaulted to catch-all labels such as Interference, and asks for something more specific: *"we would like our officials to call the foul what it is."* Its worked example is a trip: if a player without the puck is tripped, the penalty is to be labelled Tripping, and the section says the same goes for other fouls. It then names a second case in terms: *"If one player cross checks another player in the head or neck area, we want the penalty to be called Cross Checking, not Checking to the Head."* The 2025-26 edition's Section 4 carried none of this and went straight to Rule 18.

**This changes the name of the call, not what is legal.** Nothing in it permits an act that was a penalty before, or forbids one that was not, and it attaches no age to anything. In particular it is not a body-checking rule — see §What this document does not cover.

⚠️ **And do not hear a relabelled call as the lesser one, because on the cross-check it is not.** Both names sit in the same place in your rule book. **Cross-checking 59.3** and **Illegal Check to the Head 48.3** are both entries on IIHF **Reference Table 6** — *"Summary of major penalties that result in an automatic game misconduct"* — so a cross-check to the head called as *cross-checking* carries exactly the ejection exposure it would have carried under the other label. The interference-to-tripping switch runs the same way one tier down: **Interference 56.5** and **Tripping 57.4** are both on **Table 7**, whose game misconduct rests on the referee's judgement. §A first major can end your game sets out what both tables do.

**Both table numbers are the 2025/26 book's**, which is the edition the In-House Rules' Introduction names. The 2026/27 IIHF book merges Tables 5, 6 and 7 into a single Table 5, and every entry named here is still on it.

### Team sheets and bench staffing

The official team list must reach the referee crew **no later than 20 minutes before the scheduled face-off**, and only listed players are eligible. Late submission is a bench minor for delay of game, *"to be served at the start of the game or at the first stoppage if identified later"* — so it is served even where the omission is only spotted after the game has begun.

For **IHUK-sanctioned games**, benches must carry a **Level 2 or higher non-playing coach aged 18+**, plus one more coach at minimum Level 1. The rule opens by scoping itself — *"For all IHUK-sanctioned games (except where competition regs state otherwise)"*, where the 2025-26 edition said EIH-sanctioned — and there are three documented carve-outs:

- **Adult recreational hockey in England:** *"If all players on both teams are 18 years of age or older on game day, there is no requirement for qualified EIH coaches to be present on the bench."* If any under-18 plays, the standard rules return: *"If any player under 18 is taking part, the standard IHUK Rules of Competition shall apply (see Rule 25)"* — a separate document not reproduced here, and one the 2025-26 edition called the **EIH** Rules of Competition.
- **BUIHA (university) hockey:** *"There is no requirement for coaches in BUIHA ice hockey, as players under 18 are not permitted to participate."*
- **SIHA-sanctioned games** run a different coaching-qualification ladder: Level 2 minimum for junior and senior, **Level 1 minimum for recreational.**

A team manager may sit on the bench but does not count toward the requirement. On medics the In-House Rules say *"While not mandatory, the presence of a qualified medic on the bench is strongly recommended"* — a recommendation worth taking on its own terms rather than reading as an exemption.

### What medical cover you can expect at the rink, and what to ask for

⚠️ **This is England-and-Wales only, with one UK-wide exception.** Everything below about what a venue must have is England Ice Hockey's, which governs only England and Wales. Neither edition of the In-House Rules — the document that reaches Scotland, Northern Ireland and university hockey — sets any first-aid, ambulance, emergency-plan or emergency-number requirement, and no Ice Hockey UK document was found that does either. So outside England and Wales, no document was found that establishes what your rink must have. **The one exception is a person, not a place:** the IHUK *Coaching Regulations* make a Basic First Aid certificate mandatory for every registered coach, UK-wide — but that tells you nothing about what the building itself has, and the accepted online courses don't confer full "qualified first aider" status.

**Five things are required, in England and Wales:** a first aider — and ⚠️ **the rule attaches no qualification to that person**: 33.5 says only *"First aider"*, while the NQF Level 3 bar in 33.7 belongs to the *recommended* professional practitioner, not the mandatory first aider; a first aid kit checked before each game; a risk assessment and an emergency action plan, both shared with the visiting team; and a telephone to summon the emergency services. A doctor or physio is only **recommended** — check the team sheet to see whether one is present. ⚠️ **And an ambulance is explicitly not required on-standby** — the rule leaves that to the club's own risk assessment. So a fixture with a first aider, a phone, and no ambulance and no doctor is exactly what the rule allows, which is why the phone is on the mandatory list.

**The numbers.** No British rulebook names one; England Ice Hockey has adopted the **UK Concussion Guidelines for Non-Elite (Grassroots) Sport**, which give **999** for red flags or any other significant injury, and the NHS line **111** for assessment within 24 hours otherwise. ⚠️ **Know what counts as a red flag before you need it:** severe neck pain, worsening consciousness, a severe or worsening headache, and increasing confusion all mean **999**, not 111. [Conditioning and Recovery](../off-the-ice/conditioning_and_recovery.md) carries CRT6's own ten red flags, worded slightly differently (*neck pain or tenderness* rather than *severe neck pain*) — **on the neck, treat CRT6's wording as the stricter one and call 999.**

**And the same adopted concussion guidance — the UK Concussion Guidelines for Non-Elite (Grassroots) Sport, November 2024 edition — sets a floor under when you play again.** It states that *"[a]ll concussions should be managed individually, but there should be no return to competition before 21 days from injury"*, and its graduated return programme carries the same bar at the final stage: return to sports competition, *"(NOT before day 21)"*. **Count the day of the injury as day 0** — the guidance says so in terms — so day 21 falls twenty-one days after the day you were hurt. ⚠️ **That is a bar, not a date and not a clearance.** It does not say you are ready on day 21, and it does not replace the medical assessment, which still has to happen first — it means a clearance arriving sooner does not put you in a non-elite British game sooner. **The guidance carves out one group and grassroots hockey is not it:** athletes in the *"Enhanced Care pathways"* of elite and professional sport, managed by healthcare professionals experienced in sports concussion, *"may be formally cleared for an earlier return to competition."* [Conditioning and Recovery](../off-the-ice/conditioning_and_recovery.md#concussion) has the red flags, the ordinary signs and what has to happen in the first hours.

**Two more instructions, from two different sources, and neither is contradicted anywhere found.** The UK guidance: *"If a neck injury is suspected, the player should only be moved by Healthcare Professionals with appropriate training."* And separately, from **CRT6** — the international concussion recognition tool, not any British document — *"Do not remove helmet (if present) or other equipment."* The UK guidance never mentions helmets at all, so its silence is not a contradiction of CRT6's instruction; a British document not repeating something is not a British document rejecting it. **Leave the helmet on, and don't move a player with a suspected neck injury yourself.** [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md) owns the full on-ice response.

**What to do with all of that is short, and it is done before you need it.** Find out who the first aider is, where the telephone is, and whether your team has been given the rink's emergency action plan — and ask the same of an away rink, since sharing both with visitors is part of the rule.

## What this document does not cover

- **The EIHL Casebook.** The Elite League publishes its own casebook recording where its interpretation differs from the IIHF book. It has not been read here, and nothing in this document should be taken as describing EIHL interpretation.
- **Competition regulations, except on body checking.** Each league sets its own regulations on rosters, imports, fixtures and discipline, separately from the playing rules, and they are not reproduced here. The In-House Rules note that IHUK *"may also issue Rule Bulletins from time to time"* — the 2025-26 edition attributed that to EIH and SIHA — and cross-refer, at Rule 25, to a standing **IHUK Rules of Competition** setting the coaching requirements. The 2025-26 edition called that same document the **EIH** Rules of Competition, so both names are in circulation. **The one thing this document does take from them is the checking format**, because the playing rules do not carry it: four of IHUK's 2026-27 *Rules of Competition* — junior, U10, NIHL 1 and 2, and WNIHL — are quoted in the Overview above, on that point and no other.
- **Northern Ireland — partly covered, and only on the playing rules.** The 2026-27 In-House Rules apply *"in England, Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland (the Home Countries)"*, where the 2025-26 edition reached only England and Scotland. So the playing-rule amendments in this document now cover Northern Irish hockey. ⚠️ **But several individual amendments still scope themselves more narrowly than the document does** — the video-review and Coach's Challenge bars are written as applying *"in EIH or SIHA competition"*, eleven times between them, and that wording was carried into the 2026-27 edition unchanged. What governs registration, discipline and competition regulations in Northern Ireland is not established here: England Ice Hockey's *Rules & Regulations* describe EIH as the governing body for **England and Wales** (Rules 1.1, 3.1, 17.1), and no Northern Irish equivalent has been read for this document.
- **What medical cover is required at a rink outside England and Wales.** **No Ice Hockey UK document was found that sets it** — a statement about what could be found rather than about what exists — see §What medical cover you can expect at the rink, and ask your club and league.
- **Whether body checking is legal at your age — outside the leagues IHUK runs itself.** **Neither British rule book sets a checking age**, and it is worth being explicit about why, because the negative is the reason the answer has to be looked for somewhere else. The *Rules & Regulations* contain no body-checking provision at all. The In-House Rules set no age rule for it either: the 2025-26 edition used the language of body contact nowhere, and the 2026-27 edition uses it in exactly one place — a Section 4 instruction to officials about **which penalty to call**, asking that a cross-check to an opponent's head or neck be called *"Cross Checking, not Checking to the Head."* §Officials have been told to call the foul what it is quotes that section in full. ⚠️ **That is about the name of the penalty, not about what is legal.** A cross-check to the head is a penalty under either label; nothing in it permits contact, restricts it, or attaches an age to it. The IIHF book restricts checking only in women's hockey, at Rule 101.1, and the North American age lines are **not** in force here. **What answers it is a competition regulation rather than a playing rule**, and IHUK publishes one for each of its own leagues — the junior, U10, NIHL 1 and 2 and WNIHL *Rules of Competition*, all quoted in the Overview above and in [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md) §If you play in Britain. ⚠️ **What is still not covered is everything outside those four documents:** the **EIHL**, **BUIHA** university hockey, **NIHL National**, and Scottish domestic competitions outside the IHUK junior structure. No regulation for any of those was read for this document, so for those, **ask your league before you hit anyone.**

## Common Mistakes

- **Reading the USA Hockey flags as though they were yours.** They are the commonest rule-set flags here, because the base text is written to NHL rules. In Britain, the IIHF flags are the ones that apply to you.
- **Assuming neck protection is optional because you are an adult.** That is the North American position, and it is not the British one: IIHF Rule 9.12 mandates it at every British level, adults included. Under the In-House Rules — which IHUK and the BUIHA adopt across England, Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland, university hockey included — it is a 10-minute misconduct with **no warning**, and in England and Wales England Ice Hockey extends the requirement to practice too, not just games.
- **Expecting a review, or a Coach's Challenge.** There is no on-ice video review in EIH or SIHA competition, and no challenge either. Video can still be used for supplementary discipline after the game.
- **Reading a junior birth-year table from last season.** ⚠️ **Every band moved a full year between the 2025-26 and 2026-27 In-House Rules.** A player born in 2010 was Under 16 and is now Under 19. Get the bands from the current season's Rule 200 and nowhere else — an out-of-date table puts a child in the wrong age group in a contact sport.
- **Assuming your centre only loses the draw the way the NHL does.** In-House Rule 76 replaces the centre for **all** violations except on a draw following an icing — and it lets **either** centre put the stick down first, which the IIHF book does not. Both are new for 2026-27.
- **Thinking a dive costs two minutes.** In-House Rule 64 makes it *"2 plus 10"*: a minor plus a 10-minute misconduct, where the IIHF book gives a minor alone. You sit twelve; your team is short two; and a second misconduct in the game ends it.
- **Looking for a checking age in the rule book, not finding one, and inferring the North American answer.** Neither British book sets one — the 2026-27 In-House Rules' only body-contact sentence is an instruction about **how officials label** a penalty, not about who may check. **The document that does set it is your competition's Rules of Competition**, and for IHUK's own leagues the junior line falls between **U12 and U14** — a year after USA Hockey's 12U and a year before Hockey Canada's U13, so neither guess would have landed.
- **Hearing "cross-checking" instead of "checking to the head" and thinking you got the lesser call.** You did not. Officials are now told to name fouls more precisely, but cross-checking and checking-to-the-head sit on the same **automatic-ejection table** — Table 6 of the 2025/26 IIHF book, and a table number only resolves in the edition it came from. ⚠️ **Interference and tripping sit on the discretionary table instead, where the game misconduct is the referee's judgement rather than automatic** — a different tier, and §A first major can end your game, and adults are not exempt says so. The name changed; the tier didn't. See §Officials have been told to call the foul what it is.
- **Playing on after your helmet comes off.** In-House Rule 9.8 requires you to leave the ice immediately, and failing to is a misconduct — the same tier as a missing neck guard, and two misconducts in one game end it. The same rule set treats a helmet and facemask that do not match manufacturer sizing as **Dangerous Equipment**, and removes you until it is corrected.
- **Bending down for a neck guard that has come off in play.** The rule is the same shape as the detached-helmet one above, and it is written just as flatly. In-House Rule 9.12: *"If a neck guard comes off during play, the player must leave the ice and cannot retrieve it."* Go to the bench and let somebody else collect it. The rule states no separate penalty for picking it up, but playing on without one is a 10-minute misconduct with no warning, and a second violation by the same player is another that *"automatically escalates to a Game Misconduct."*
- **Wearing the chin strap loose.** The gap must not exceed 2.5 cm, about one finger — first offence sends you to the bench, second is a 10-minute misconduct. A loose strap is also a strap a helmet can come off over, which puts you under the detached-helmet rule above: off the ice, immediately. ⚠️ **If you ever play under USA Hockey, the same loose strap is riskier there**, because under **USA Hockey 615(c)** a game misconduct goes to a player *"who doesn't wear their helmet/facemask as designed and the helmet/facemask is removed during an altercation"* — the loose strap is the condition that makes it bite — where the IIHF's book does not penalise either player for a helmet that simply comes off.
- **Playing English university hockey and reading past the half-visor minimum.** BUIHA has its own clause, separate from the SIHA one, and it binds every player in a BUIHA game.
- **Thinking a major in junior or women's hockey costs you five minutes.** In women's hockey it ends your game outright at every age, adult included, and junior hockey reads the same way U19 down to U10 — confirm either with your league, but plan for the ejection. **And don't read that as adults elsewhere being safe**: the IIHF book ejects any adult on a first major for seventeen non-fighting offences on its automatic-ejection table, with no injury required, plus five more on the discretionary table at the referee's judgement. See §A first major can end your game for the full list and what's domestically softened.
- **Leaving the penalty box because your time was up and the whistle went.** If the whistle went for an altercation, the box is shut regardless — see §Leaving the bench or the penalty box during an altercation.
- **Assuming your rink is 60 × 30.** Sheets below 56 × 26 are authorised here for all levels, so measure rather than assume. (No published census of British rink dimensions exists, so no distribution is claimed here — only that the authorisation is broad.)
- **Assuming the NIHL Playoff Final can go to a shootout.** The semi-finals can; the Final cannot. It plays successive 20-minute sudden-death periods to a golden goal, and Appendix 1 says in terms that no Game Winning Shots will be used.
- **Assuming a cage is mandatory for juniors everywhere in Britain.** The cage rule is England Ice Hockey's, and EIH governs England and Wales. In Scotland and university hockey the IIHF book applies, and its Under-18 rule permits a full-face visor as well as a cage.
- **Assuming an ambulance, or a doctor, is at the rink.** Neither is required, even in England and Wales — a first aider, a kit, a plan and a phone are the mandatory minimum, and outside England and Wales even that isn't written down. See §What medical cover you can expect at the rink.
- **Thinking a doctor's clearance is the last thing between you and your next game.** The concussion guidance England Ice Hockey has adopted sets a floor for the non-elite game: **no return to competition before 21 days from injury**. That is a bar underneath the medical decision, not a replacement for it — the assessment still has to happen, and the bar does not move because you feel fine. Elite and professional athletes on an Enhanced Care pathway are the one group it can be lifted for. See §What medical cover you can expect at the rink.
- **Treating the In-House Rules as the rule book.** They are a diff. Without the IIHF book beside them they do not describe the game.
- **Assuming "EIHA" is defunct because the website says England Ice Hockey.** Same organisation, renamed.

## Key Takeaways

1. **If you play in Britain, your rule book is the IIHF's** — at every level, recreational through the GB national side (the Elite League too, on public reporting rather than its own unread Casebook). Wherever a document flags an **IIHF** difference, that is your game; **USA Hockey** or **Hockey Canada** flags are not. On top sit the **In-House Rules** — now covering England, Wales, Scotland, Northern Ireland and university hockey — which are amendments, not a rule book, and win domestically where they differ. New for 2026-27: **no on-ice video review or Coach's Challenge** in EIH/SIHA competition; your **centre is replaced for any faceoff violation** except after an icing, and **either** centre may drop their stick first; **embellishment is a minor plus a 10-minute misconduct**.

2. **A neck laceration protector is mandatory for all players at every British level** (IIHF Rule 9.12 itself, not a domestic add-on) — no age or competition is exempt. The In-House Rules strip out the warning: **no warning**, straight to a 10-minute misconduct. **If it comes off during play, leave the ice — you may not retrieve it.** Wear it regardless of age.

3. **Your rink may well be smaller than the book's** — sheets below 56 m × 26 m are authorised for all levels and, from 2026-27, in all fixtures, with two rinks named as U16-only exceptions. That is short of the IIHF's fixed 60 m length and its 26 m minimum width. Measure yours.

4. **Facial and head protection has several separate floors, and they don't conflict.** Women may drop to a half visor only from the season after their 18th birthday; below that, full face. **Face cages are mandatory throughout junior hockey in England and Wales only** — elsewhere the IIHF's Under-18 rule permits a full-face visor instead. Three rules travel with the helmet itself: leave the ice immediately if it comes off, keep the chin-strap gap under 2.5 cm (about one finger), and never pair a helmet and cage that weren't sized for each other.

5. **In women's hockey, any major penalty is an automatic ejection at every age, adult included** — the rule states no age scope, and the Adult definition does not exempt you. **Junior hockey reads the same way, U19 down to U10**, though that extension is this document's inference rather than the rule's own words; both readings are the conservative ones, and confirming with your league costs nothing. Junior categories are British, unisex and set by **birth year**, and ⚠️ **every band shifted by a full year for 2026-27** — take them from the current season's rules, never a table from last season.

6. **Adults are not safe either: a first major can end your game too.** IIHF 20.4 ejects on a first major for any of the twenty infractions on its automatic-ejection table, with no injury required — stricter than the NHL, which requires an injury on six of its thirteen. A second, discretionary table adds five more (boarding, charging, elbowing, interference, tripping) at the referee's judgement; play as though the ejection follows the major there too. **A relabelled penalty is not a lesser one** — new-for-2026-27 instructions telling officials to call fouls by more specific names (cross-checking instead of checking-to-head, tripping instead of interference) change the name, not the tier. On first-major ejection, yours is the stricter book.

7. **Two majors end your game, and so do two misconducts, on their own separate count** (20.4 and Rule 22.1) — the NHL's equivalent trigger is a third major, with no misconduct-count limb at all. The only domestic softening is the **plain willing fight**, in NIHL, SNL and BUIHA Varsity; every other Rule 46 fighting offence — instigating, being the aggressor, sucker-punching, fighting outside the periods — still carries the IIHF's automatic ejection. Look up a fighting rule by its name, not its number: the 2026/27 book renumbers the whole of Rule 46. **The IIHF also writes no match penalty at all, for anything** — that tier does not exist in the book, so a first major is your ceiling and Rule 28 supplementary discipline sits above it.

8. **If an altercation starts while you are on the bench or in the penalty box, stay put until an official releases you.** IIHF Rule 70.1 bars leaving for any reason during an altercation, scoped to no age and no competition — the domestic fighting rules don't touch it. An "altercation" needs only one penalty, not one punch, and the IIHF's own guidance penalises a player who leaves the box and merely skates around one without joining in — **skating around it is not staying out of it.** **Your penalty expiring is not a release** — your team must also be entitled to an extra skater, which coincident penalties often mean it isn't. [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md) has the four rulebooks side by side.

9. **Overtime is not universal.** In-House Rule 84 confines it to NIHL fixtures, SNL fixtures whose regulations provide for it, and BUIHA fixtures designated **VARSITY OT**. Where a shootout is held it is **three** shooters, and the same player may shoot more than once — the IIHF requires five different shooters. The **Playoff Final Weekend** runs two procedures: semi-finals can end in a shootout; the Final cannot and plays sudden-death periods to a golden goal instead.

10. **Know what medical cover your rink has before you need it — the rule is narrower than you'd expect.** In England and Wales, clubs must provide a first aider, a checked first aid kit, a shared risk assessment and emergency plan, and a phone — but **not** an ambulance or a doctor, both of which are only recommended. The In-House Rules impose none of this, so elsewhere in Britain no document was found that sets what a rink must have. On numbers: call **999** for red flags (which include severe neck pain) or another significant injury, **111** otherwise. A suspected neck injury should only be moved by a trained professional; nothing found contradicts that. **Leave the helmet on** — that instruction comes from CRT6, the international concussion tool, not from any British document, and nothing found in the British documents contradicts it. **And on coming back, that same adopted concussion guidance sets a floor: no return to competition before 21 days from injury** — a bar underneath the medical decision rather than a substitute for it, and lifted only for elite and professional athletes on an Enhanced Care pathway. [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md) has the full response.

11. **Body checking is answered by a different kind of document, and that is true of a lot else here: your competition's own regulations are the last word.** Neither the IIHF book nor the In-House Rules sets a British checking age. Your competition's **Rules of Competition** does, and for the leagues IHUK runs itself the 2026-27 editions make junior **U10 and U12 non-checking and U14 upward checking**, **NIHL Division 1 and 2 checking**, and **WNIHL non-checking** at U16 and senior — so the British junior line sits between U12 and U14, matching neither North American line. For the **EIHL**, **BUIHA**, **NIHL National** or Scottish domestic hockey outside that junior structure, nothing was read and you should ask. See [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md) §If you play in Britain. **Where your league's regulations disagree with anything here, your league's regulations are right.** Check them.
