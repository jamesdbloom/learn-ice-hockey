## Key focus

**Play the part of the shift you spend without the puck.** NHL forwards at five-on-five hold it for about forty seconds in every twenty minutes. The rest is your game: be somewhere the carrier can pass to, and when your team loses it, get back above the puck.

**Look before the puck reaches you.** A glance while the pass is travelling is free; one taken after it arrives costs you possession. Goaltenders are the exception: they track a live puck all the way in.

**Defending, stay goal side.** Keep your body between your attacker and your net, with your stick in the passing lane. Once they are past you, your goaltender is doing your job.

**When the puck goes to the net, go with it** — unless your team has made you the safety on that shot. Stop at the front, skates and stick blade just outside the crease, stick on the ice: the rebound goes to whoever is already there. Take the ice, never the goaltender.

**Where you are standing decides what a mistake costs.** Play simple in your own end and creative in theirs. Take the stride that changes the ice before you choose the play.

**Never pass the puck — or skate it — across the front of your own goal.** Go behind the net, up the wall, or through a high exchange above the circles; ask which your team uses. A puck lost in your own slot is a shot against.

**Give the carrier two places to put it, at different angles.** Two teammates in one line from the puck are one option.

**Find out what system your team plays, and play it.** Most "always do X" in hockey is a system, not a law. When your bench and this page disagree, your bench wins.

**Find out whether your league allows body checking before your first game.** Your age group, league and country decide it, and nothing on the ice tells you — ⚠️ **so ask your coach or your league which category you are in.**

**Decide how you will meet the boards on the way in:** go in on an angle, skates parallel to the wall, head up, chin off your chest. ⚠️ **Never turn your back to the checker, and never duck.** This holds in non-checking hockey too, because a wall can hurt you without an opponent.

## Overview

These ideas hold whatever system, position or league you play in. Most are about the time without the puck, because that is most of your shift.

Defending, everything turns on the front of your own net. A mistake there is a shot against rather than a lost possession, which is why goal side, getting back above the puck and playing simple there matter more than anything clever.

Attacking, your value is to the player with the puck. Looking early means the decision is made before the puck arrives. A second option at a new angle beats a crowded one. And the rebound goes to whoever is already at the net.

Three things are settled before the play reaches you, and they cost the most if you get them wrong. Your team's system decides most of the "always do X" you will hear. Your league's checking category is the difference between a legal play and a penalty — or, for a reckless hit, being thrown out of the game. And how you meet the boards decides whether a race to the wall is nothing or an injury.

Learn these as principles rather than instructions: instructions expire with a team's system, but the reasoning transfers. Each section says what its evidence is — published research, the governing documents, or coaching reasoning with no measurement behind it — and where to read more.

## 1. Most of what you are told is your team's system, not a law of hockey

**The principle.** Almost every "always do X" in hockey really means "in our system, do X". Who takes the first forechecker's job, who covers the front of the net, whether your partner follows you to a puck, how high a winger holds in your own end: a coach decides these, and a different coach decides differently and is also right.

Take "stay high" for a winger in your own end. While the other team has the puck, it means level with the opposing defenceman on your side. It expires the moment your team wins the puck, when you come down the wall to the outlet instead.

**Why it matters.** Treat a coaching choice as a law and you do the wrong job when you change teams — and you stop asking, because a law does not need asking about. The same instruction can be right on one bench and a serious mistake on the next.

**What to notice.** An instruction about *where a teammate goes* is probably a system. One about *what the referee will do* is a rule. Rules are written in a book; systems are written on a whiteboard.

**What to do.** Ask which system your team plays rather than guessing. [Defensive Zone Coverage](../systems/defensive_zone_coverage.md) calls coverage the most system-dependent part of hockey and gives the fastest question to ask a new coach.

**Worked example.** Ask: *when the puck is in our corner, what does the weak-side winger do?* "Stays up" or "comes down" is not enough, because in a low zone collapse the winger does come down. The tell is what they come down *to*. Dropping into the high slot and climbing straight back is a collapse: the job is a piece of ice. Following a player who leaves the blue line, into the corner if need be, is man or hybrid: the job is a player. [Defensive Zone Coverage](../systems/defensive_zone_coverage.md) has the other tells.

**The evidence, and its limits.** This is a convention, and these pages follow one too. Where they must assume a system, they assume a **2-1-2 forecheck** and a **low zone collapse** in the defensive zone, and say so. [Breakouts](../systems/breakouts.md) calls that choice *"a documentation convention, not a claim about what most teams play"*. No published count of what teams actually run was found, so read any claim that one forecheck is "the most common" as an impression.

**A realistic alternative.** A **1-2-2** forecheck is the normal alternative. [Forechecking Systems](../systems/forechecking_systems.md) recommends it for adult recreational hockey, where a 2-1-2 is hard to sustain for a whole game — as a recommendation for that level, not a claim about what most teams play. In your own end, the alternative to a zone collapse is man-marking or a hybrid.

**Where to read more.** New to this: [Getting Started, section 10](../getting-started/getting_started.md#10-how-to-find-out-what-applies-to-you), which is about finding out what applies to you. Deeper: [Forechecking Systems](../systems/forechecking_systems.md) and [Defensive Zone Coverage](../systems/defensive_zone_coverage.md).

## 2. Which book you are under, and where you sit inside it, decides what is legal

**The principle.** Hockey has several rule books and they disagree. Your age group, your league and your country decide which one is yours, and the same act can be normal play in one book and a penalty in another.

**Why it matters.** This is the difference between a mistake that costs you a shift and one that gets you ejected or hurts somebody. And you cannot work it out on the ice; you have to be told.

**What to do.** ⚠️ **Ask your coach or your league secretary which competition you play in and whether it is a checking category, and do it before your first game rather than after your first hit.** In Britain, ask for your competition by name: the answer is in its own Rules of Competition, not in the rule book.

**What to notice.** Nothing, and that is the point. A checking and a non-checking league look identical from the bench. The answer is on paper: your competition, your age band and that competition's regulations. ⚠️ **If you cannot name your competition, you do not yet know what is legal for you.**

**Worked example: whether anybody may body check you.**

**Under USA Hockey**, Rule 604(a) prohibits body checking *"in the 12 & under youth age classifications and below, all Girls'/Women's age classifications and all non-check Adult classifications"*. The same rule adds that *"[a] local governing body may prohibit body checking in any classification"*, so your association can take checking away from an age group the list leaves in.

**Under Hockey Canada**, the rule runs the other way round: Rule 7.3 *"applies only in divisions of U13 and below, in female hockey, and any other divisions approved by a Member of Hockey Canada"*. In those divisions a deliberate body check is itself the penalty.

**In Britain**, neither the IIHF rule book nor the domestic In-House Rules states a checking age; your competition's regulations do. **Four IHUK *Rules of Competition* were read for this page** — junior, U10, NIHL Division 1 and 2, and the Women's NIHL. The **Junior** rules for 2026-27 (unisex, by birth year) set **U10 and U12 as non-checking and U14, U16 and U19 as checking**, and the U10 document says outright: *"Bodychecking is NOT allowed in U10 matches."* ⚠️ **Do not read an adult answer off that junior table.** NIHL Divisions 1 and 2 are *"Full ice, **checking**, stop clock"*, but the **Women's NIHL is a separate competition and is non-checking at both its levels**, senior and U16, each printed as *"Full ice, **non-checking**, stop clock"*.

**Those four documents settle only the competitions they name.** No regulations were read for the EIHL, NIHL National, the PNIHL, BUIHA university hockey or Scottish domestic competition outside the IHUK junior structure. **"Ice Hockey UK runs my league" is not the test** — IHUK's In-House Rules reach NIHL National, the SNL and BUIHA Varsity, and none of the four covers them. Ice Hockey UK also revises these regulations in place at unchanged addresses, so read your own competition's current edition. ⚠️ **Ask your league before you hit anybody.**

**A second example, which catches out adults: whether you may stand your ground.** Most of the books here say you may. The NHL and the IIHF both write it at Rule 56.1 — in the IIHF's words, a player *"is allowed the ice they are standing on (body position) and are not required to move in order to let an opponent proceed"*; the NHL's sentence says *"he"* for *"they"* — and the PWHL writes the same at Rule 57.1. USA Hockey's Declaration of Player Safety lists as legal contact that *"[a] skater is entitled to stand their ground and is not required to move if an opponent wishes to skate through that area of the ice."* **Hockey Canada writes no equivalent anywhere in its book**; its only other body-position language, Rule 8.1 (Holding), permits arm-blocking and says nothing about standing your ground against an oncoming skater.

**CARHA, which governs affiliated adult recreational leagues, writes both sides.** A Note to its interference rule says defenders *"are entitled to 'stand their ground' or 'shadow' the attacking players"* (Rule 66(a), Note 2). Its body-contact rule, 49(a), penalises a player who *"intentionally bodies, pushes, shoves, stands in front of an opponent for the purpose of making contact, and/or does not avert body contact with an opponent"*. **The two govern different infractions and the book nowhere reconciles them**, so [Rink Map](rink_map.md) reads them together: you may hold your ground to take away a lane, but a seal you deliberately ride through is inside 49(a)'s words. ⚠️ **Find out whether your league is CARHA-affiliated before you take "hold your ground" from anywhere, this page included.**

**What it costs under CARHA.** A Rule 49(a) penalty is a minor or a major at the referee's discretion, and a major whenever intentional body contact causes injury. Under Rule 30(a) a major for body contact also carries a game misconduct, so you are ejected, and Rule 32(d) adds at least a one-game suspension. Rule 49(c) keeps genuine accidents out — *"Where, in the opinion of the Referee, accidental contact has taken place, no penalty shall be assessed"* — except that accidental contact with the head, face or neck is still a minor (Rule 50(a)) and a player driven from behind into the boards or net is a match penalty (Rule 53(b)). **That 49(a)'s last limb amounts to a duty to get out of the way is this guide's reading of the rule's grammar, which CARHA does not gloss.**

**The evidence, and its limits.** Every quotation above is the governing document's own wording, named by rule number or by document, so you can check it. No rulebook records how many leagues sit in each category, so this page can tell you categories exist but not which one you are in. That question has a person attached to it, not a document.

**A realistic alternative.** None. This is the one place where "it depends what your coach says" is the wrong answer: a coach cannot make a body check legal in a non-checking category.

Deeper: [Body Contact and Battles, section 1](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md#1-which-contact-rules-are-you-actually-playing-under), which works through which contact rules you are actually playing under.

## 3. You almost never have the puck

**The principle.** Your time with the puck is a rounding error in your shift, and it comes in bursts of about a second. What you do in the rest of the time decides what those bursts are worth.

**Why it matters.** Most players practise the part of hockey they spend the least time doing. The three to four percent of your shift with the puck feels like hockey; the other ninety-six percent is where games are decided.

**What to notice.** Watch your own shift back and count the seconds you were touching the puck. It will be fewer than you remember.

**What to do.** Off the puck you have four jobs, and what is right in one is close to the opposite in another. Most players have a plan for only one of them.

- **Your team has the puck:** get open where the carrier can see you. When the puck goes to the net, go with it and stop at the front for the rebound — skates and stick blade just outside the crease, never on the goaltender — unless your job on that shot is the safety at the point or the top of the circle.
- **They have the puck:** these pages assume the closest player pressures it, with the stick and an angle; a 1-2-2 forecheck or a trap sends fewer, so find out what your team runs. If you are not the one pressing, stay goal side of your attacker with your stick in the passing lane.
- **The puck is loose:** one player goes hard, one supports and nobody else joins. Which of you is which is your team's call.
- **Possession has just changed:** act before the other side does. If your team won it, go; if it lost it, get back above the puck, between it and your net.

**Worked example.** A winger who "had a quiet game" usually touched the puck rarely. But their two most valuable plays — arriving so the defenceman had somewhere to put a breakout pass, and getting back above the puck so a turnover did not become a rush — both happened without the puck, and nobody remembers either.

**The evidence, and its limits.** This one is measured. Researchers at Waterloo used the NHL's own puck-and-player tracking across **1,194 games of the 2023-24 season, covering 671 skaters**. At **five-on-five** a forward holds the puck for about **forty seconds in every twenty minutes of ice time** and a defenceman about forty-nine seconds, in possessions averaging roughly **1.3 seconds** — about 3.3% to 4.2% of ice time. **Carry the scope with the number:** the league calls this tracking data *unofficial*, the paper says it *"may differ from other datasets that track possession information"*, and it covers NHL professionals at five-on-five only, measuring possession rather than involvement.

**A realistic alternative.** None — this is an observation, not a coaching choice. What your team asks you to do with the time *is* a coaching choice, and that is principle 1.

**What it costs at the net.** Stay out of the crease and give way to the goaltender: it is a rule as well as a habit.

- **Standing in the crease can stop play.** Under USA Hockey, standing in the crease, skating through it or holding your stick in it — its line included — while your team has the puck in the zone and the goaltender is in contact with the crease stops play for a neutral-zone faceoff, unless a defender put you there (Rule 625(b)). The IIHF lets the referee do the same if you do not leave it immediately (Rule 69).
- **Holding your spot is a penalty.** If you are in the crease and the goaltender makes contact to establish their position, give ground at once. Holding your spot is goaltender interference, goal or no goal, under the NHL, PWHL and IIHF books — a minor in the first two (NHL 69.3, PWHL 71.3, IIHF 69.3).
- **It can cost your team the goal.** Under USA Hockey and CARHA, a goal scored while an attacker stands in the crease does not count unless the puck got into the crease first (USA Hockey Rules 617(c)(4) and 625(b), CARHA Rule 66(b)). Hockey Canada also disallows it, but lets a teammate's goal from outside the crease stand if you did not play the puck, interfere with the play or block the goaltender's view or movement (the *Goal Crease Area* interpretation under its Rule 8.5). The NHL, PWHL and IIHF say your position alone does not decide it: the goal comes off for contact with the goaltender, or for a position that stops them moving freely in their crease, and planting yourself **in the crease** to block their view and hamper the save is enough (NHL and IIHF Rules 69.1 and 69.3, PWHL 71.1 and 71.3). In every book, a screen from just outside the crease that never touches the goaltender keeps the goal.
- **Outside the crease the goaltender is still not fair game.** Deliberate or unnecessary contact with a goaltender anywhere on the ice is a penalty in the NHL, PWHL and IIHF books (NHL and IIHF 69.2 and 69.4, PWHL 71.2 and 71.4), under Hockey Canada (Rule 8.5), CARHA (Rule 66(b)) and USA Hockey (Rule 607(d)). USA Hockey's casebook allows a check on a goaltender outside the privileged area (the box from the end-zone faceoff dots back to the end boards) only to win a puck the goaltender has, and only where body checking is allowed (Rule 604). Take the ice in front of the net, never the goaltender.

## 4. Look before the puck reaches you

**The principle.** A glance over your shoulder while the pass is travelling is free, because your eyes have nothing else to do. A glance after the puck arrives costs possession time, and the picture has changed while you looked.

**This reverses in goal.** A goaltender facing a shot has nothing to gain by looking away from a live puck. [Goaltender](../positions/goaltender.md) has them "[t]rack the puck all the way into the pad, the glove, the chest" instead, because losing sight of it is what costs the goal.

**Why it matters.** Players who seem to see the whole ice have almost always simply already looked. It is the cheapest improvement in hockey: it costs no fitness, no ice time and no talent.

**What to notice.** Two players receiving the same pass look completely different from the bench, and it is not their hands. One watched the puck the whole way and discovered the forechecker on arrival. The other knew about the forechecker before the pass was made.

**What to do.** Take a look while the puck is being retrieved, another as the passer's head comes up, and a last one just before it reaches you. Then receive the puck already turning the way you decided to go.

**Worked example.** You are on the half-wall in your own end and your defenceman has the puck behind the net. If your first look comes when the puck touches your stick, your decision takes about as long as a forechecker needs to reach you. If you looked twice while the puck was travelling, the decision is made and the reception and the pass are one movement.

**The evidence, and its limits.** This one is measured in hockey too. Berg and colleagues, in the *Journal of Sports Sciences* in 2025, hand-coded **2,545 puck receptions by 88 professional players** in the Swedish men's and women's top leagues. The action after the reception succeeded **78.8% of the time when the player had scanned first against 70.1% when they had not** — 8.7 percentage points. **Two limits travel with that.** "Success" is a criterion the authors drew up with the Swedish Ice Hockey Association, not a count of goals. And because they recorded scanning as yes-or-no, **the authors report that their data cannot say whether scanning more often is better than scanning once.** No look to one look is the finding; two looks to four is not.

**A realistic alternative.** None. Coaching material that tells you to scan on a fixed clock — every one or two seconds — goes further than the measurement supports, and the professionals in that study averaged a good deal less than that.

## 5. Where you are standing decides what a mistake costs

**The principle.** The same play is not worth the same everywhere. A cross-ice pass is brilliant in the corner of their end and catastrophic in front of your own net; nothing about the pass changed, only the ice under it.

**Why it matters.** Most bad hockey decisions are good decisions made in the wrong place. Thinking about location first turns a long list of things you must not do into one question you can answer under pressure.

**What to notice.** The question is: *if I lose the puck here, how many seconds and how many bodies stand between the turnover and a shot on my net?* In front of your own net the answer is none and none. Deep in their corner it is four or five seconds and the whole team.

**What to do.** Change where you are standing before you decide what to do. One stride out of the most expensive ice improves every option, and it is almost always available. **And never pass the puck — or skate it — across the front of your own goal.** Go behind the net or up the wall instead. Teams differ on the route out — some exchange behind the net, some high above the circles — so ask which yours uses; your own slot is never the route.

**Worked example.** You are behind your own net with the puck and a forechecker arriving. The cross-ice pass to the far winger is on, but it is the one option whose failure is a goal: if it is read, the turnover and the shot are the same event. The rim up the wall gains less if it works and costs almost nothing if it does not. That makes it the better bet from there, and the worse bet from the other end of the ice.

**The evidence, and its limits.** **The ranking of areas by what a turnover costs is coaching reasoning, not a measured result, and [Risk Management](../hockey-iq/risk_management.md) says so.** It is reasoned from where dangerous shots come from and how long a recovery takes. One of the five areas — the offensive blue line — has a tracked turnover price, from a single Swiss-league dataset at five-on-five; [Risk Management](../hockey-iq/risk_management.md) sets out what was searched for the other four and which candidate could not be read. Take the order of the areas rather than the labels on them.

**A realistic alternative.** Not an alternative so much as the other half: play simple in your own end and inventive in theirs, because a missed chance in their end costs a chance and a turnover in yours costs a goal. **And the ranking moves while the game runs:** [Risk Management](../hockey-iq/risk_management.md) shows how the score, the clock and a man advantage shift it. Leading by one in the third, reach for the safe options earlier; trailing late, the arithmetic inverts.

Deeper: [Risk Management](../hockey-iq/risk_management.md), which has the full risk map, and [Game Management](../systems/game_management.md) for how the scoreboard changes the answer.

## 6. Give the player with the puck two places to put it

**The principle.** A player with the puck and two options beats a player with the puck and none, whatever the difference in skill. Being one of those options, on purpose, is most of what a teammate is for.

**Why it matters.** Almost every bad pass is bad because there was nothing to pass to. Fixing your own position is faster than fixing somebody else's hands.

**What to notice.** Two options only count as two if they are at different angles from the puck. Two teammates in one line from the carrier are one option, because one defender's stick takes both away.

**What to do.** Move so that you, the carrier and a third player make a triangle rather than a line, and check that the lane between you is genuinely empty, not merely short. The test is time rather than distance: would the pass beat the opponent closing on your teammate?

**Worked example.** Your defenceman has the puck on the half-wall under pressure and you skate straight toward them to help. Now one forechecker's stick covers you both, and you have taken the ice your teammate was going to use. Moving *away* from a teammate pinned in a battle is usually the help: they need somewhere to put the puck, not another body in the pile.

**The evidence, and its limits.** **This is reasoning rather than a measured result, and [Puck Support and Spacing](../hockey-iq/puck_support_and_spacing.md) labels it as such.** No governing body has been found to issue a support distance as a standard — read that as "none found", not "none exists". The closest published figure is a coach's rule of thumb quoted in a USA Hockey article, which is why [Puck Support and Spacing](../hockey-iq/puck_support_and_spacing.md) gives a working range and then tells you to judge it by time instead.

**A realistic alternative.** Defending, the instinct reverses: stay behind the first defender rather than joining them on the same puck. It reverses again when you are the one applying pressure. An angling forechecker or a penalty kill denies the carrier a second option on purpose — [Forechecking Systems](../systems/forechecking_systems.md) aims to leave the carrier *"one bad pass available"*, and [Special Teams](../systems/special_teams.md) positions the kill's forwards so they *"never both drift to the same side"*.

[Defending the Rush](../systems/defending_the_rush.md) turns this into a full situation. On a 2-on-1 your goalie can save the shot but not the pass, so a defender commonly takes the pass away and gives up the shot on purpose; the realistic alternative is a timed slide across the passing lane, and that document says to ask your coach and your goalie which your team plays. And "usually" carries weight both ways: a deliberate double-team behind your own net is a common coaching instruction, and if your team marks players rather than areas you may have to follow yours out of the supporting position entirely. Ask which your team plays.

## 7. Choose how you meet the boards before you have to

**The principle.** Races to a loose puck along the wall end in head-on contact with the boards, and the posture you arrive in decides whether that is nothing or a spinal injury. Decide the posture on the way, because there is no time to decide on arrival.

**Why it matters.** This one is not about winning the puck. It applies in non-checking hockey exactly as in checking hockey, because a wall does not need an opponent to hurt you, and it applies at every speed.

**What to do.** **When you cannot avoid the wall, get your skates parallel to the boards, feet apart and knees bent, your forearm and hip to the wall, with your head up and your chin off your chest — not the point of your shoulder, and not the whole shoulder if you can help it.** Go in on an angle rather than straight at the wall. The whole-shoulder part is USA Hockey's own hedge: its checklist asks for *"No shoulder contact on the boards, if possible"*. ⚠️ **Never turn your back to the checker, however protective it feels, and never duck.**

**If the wall arrives with you already facing it and no time to turn, spread the load:** stick and gloves up on the glass, arms out, taking the impact *"over as wide a surface as possible"*. That is damage limitation rather than the posture to aim for — USA Hockey's wording is to turn your skates parallel *"[i]f at all possible"* — but it is the answer when the choice has already gone. USA Hockey names the opposite as a common error: *"Not getting your stick and gloves up and spread out on the glass"*.

**What to notice, and the two prohibitions guard against two different injuries.** A back turned to the checker makes the contact a hit from behind, and USA Hockey warns that *"Even a light hit from behind could inflict a severe head or neck injury."* A chin tucked to the chest is a separate hazard: it straightens the cervical spine, and USA Hockey says the injury from that head-down, head-first collision with the boards or a goal post *"can occur at walking speed."* ⚠️ **So there is no speed slow enough to make a head-down collision with the wall safe.**

**Worked example.** The puck is dumped into the corner and you and an opponent both go. You are half a stride behind, and the temptation is to put your head down and dig — exactly the head-down posture that straightens the cervical spine. Instead, take the angle wide so you arrive alongside the wall rather than into it, with your head up. It costs a fraction of a second and nothing else.

**The evidence, and its limits.** This rests on USA Hockey's *Heads Up Hockey* materials. They set out the mechanism — with the head flexed forward *"this normal curve is removed, and the cervical spine becomes straight"*, so the head stops on impact while the body keeps moving — and restate research by Charles Tator and colleagues in the *Canadian Journal of Neurological Sciences*. The walking-speed finding belongs to the head-down collision, and the light-hit warning to the turned back; they are two statements about two things. The postures come from a second USA Hockey manual, *Introduction To Body Contact*: the angle, the parallel skates, the forearm and hip, the shoulder prohibition and the spread-the-load fallback are all its teaching points or its own named common errors.

**A realistic alternative.** None. This is the one principle on this page where "ask your coach" is not part of the answer: it is not a tactic or a coaching preference, and no system changes it.

## Common Mistakes

- **Watching the puck instead of the attacker you are covering.** Stay goal side, keep them in view, and take short looks at the puck. Otherwise the player you should be covering walks in behind you.
- **Joining the chase when you are not the one pressing.** Unless your team has sent you to pressure the puck, stay goal side with your stick in the lane. Two players on one carrier leave somebody free.
- **Skating toward a teammate who is under pressure.** Move away instead, to somewhere they can pass to at a different angle. Going to them lets one defender's stick cover you both.
- **Stopping short of the net, or peeling away once the shot has gone.** Unless you are the safety, go all the way and stop at the front: skates and stick blade just outside the crease, stick on the ice, never on the goaltender.
- **Moving the puck through your own slot under pressure.** Go behind the net, up the wall or through a high exchange — whichever your team uses. A pass across your own goal that gets read is a shot against.
- **Using a principle to argue with a coach.** Your bench wins on the ice; ask about it afterwards. A principle is for the situations nobody has given you an instruction for.
- **Treating a principle as though it were a rule.** Most of this page is judgement, and the play in front of you can overrule it. The exceptions are principle 2, which is about finding out which rules are yours; the crease rule in principle 3; and principle 7 — never turn your back to the checker and never duck, in any situation.
- **Assuming what is measured in professional hockey is true of your league.** Take the direction from the measurements and leave the exact numbers with the players measured: NHL tracking for puck possession, the Swedish men's and women's top leagues for scanning.
- **Reading a note that no source was found as "this is made up".** It says how much measurement exists, not that the advice is wrong. The linked document says what was searched for.
- **Skipping principle 2 because you already know how to play.** Your checking category cannot be learned from experience, and an adult moving between leagues is more exposed, not less. Ask again every time you change team, league or age group.
- **Taking "you can stand your ground" as true in every adult recreational league.** Find out whether your league is CARHA-affiliated first. There, a player who *"does not avert body contact with an opponent"* can be penalised — up to a major that throws you out of the game with at least a one-game suspension — even though the same book lets a defender stand their ground. Genuine accidents are excluded, except contact to the head, face or neck (a minor) and driving a player from behind into the boards or net (a match penalty). Reading 49(a) as a duty to move is this guide's reading, not CARHA's.
- **Waiting until the boards arrive to decide how to meet them.** The angle and the parallel skates have to be chosen on the way in. ⚠️ **What is still available late is head up and chin off your chest — and, if you are already facing the wall, stick and gloves up and spread out on the glass.** That is damage limitation, not a reason to leave the choice that late.

## Key Takeaways

1. **You almost never have the puck, so play the rest of the shift.** With it, get open where the carrier can see you; against it, stay goal side; when it is loose, one goes and one supports; when it changes hands, act first.
2. **Look before the puck reaches you**, while the pass is still travelling, and receive it already turning the way you chose. Goaltenders are the exception: they track a live puck all the way in.
3. **Defending, stay goal side of the attacker you are responsible for** — your body between them and your net, your stick in the passing lane. Unless your team sends you to pressure the puck, hold that position rather than chasing.
4. **When the puck goes to the net, go with it** unless you are the safety, and stop at the front with skates and stick blade just outside the crease, stick on the ice. Take the ice in front of the net, never the goaltender.
5. **Play simple in your own end and creative in theirs**, and take the stride that changes the ice before you choose the play. Leading late, reach for the safe option earlier; trailing late, take more risk.
6. **Never pass the puck — or skate it — across the front of your own goal.** Go behind the net, up the wall, or through a high exchange, whichever your team uses.
7. **Give the carrier two options at different angles**, because two teammates in one line are one option. When a teammate is pinned in a battle, move away to give them an outlet.
8. **Find out which system your team plays.** Most of what you are told is your team's system, not a law of hockey.
9. **Find out which checking category you are in before you play a shift** — ⚠️ **ask your coach or your league, because you cannot work it out on the ice.**
10. **In adult recreational hockey,** ⚠️ **find out whether your league is CARHA-affiliated before you rely on "hold your ground."** There, failing to *"avert body contact with an opponent"* can be penalised, up to an ejection with a suspension, even though the same book lets you stand your ground.
11. **Go in on an angle, and when you cannot avoid the wall, meet it with skates parallel, knees bent, forearm and hip to the boards, head up and chin off your chest.** ⚠️ **Never turn your back to the checker and never duck: a head-down collision can injure your spine at walking speed.** If the wall arrives with you already facing it and no time to turn, spread the load: stick and gloves up on the glass, arms out.
