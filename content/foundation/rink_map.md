## Key focus

**Zone names are relative to your team, not the building, and they swap every period.** "Defensive zone" means wherever your own net sits right now; the ice never changes, only who is attacking through it. Say "our end" or "their end" if there is any doubt.

**If you play in Britain, assume your rink is smaller than both the NHL and IIHF standard until you have measured it or asked.** The IIHF book fixes a 60 m length and a 26–30 m width range, but the In-House Rules authorise British rinks below even that — under 56 m × 26 m — for all levels, in all fixtures. No distance in any diagram here is safe to assume on an unfamiliar sheet.

**Playing the puck behind the goal line outside the trapezoid, where one applies, is a two-minute minor** — the rule's only exception is a goaltender keeping skate contact with the crease. The NHL and IIHF play one; USA Hockey and Hockey Canada do not; and in England and Wales whether it is enforced is genuinely unsettled. Play as though it binds, and plan as though an opponent's goalie may come out for a dumped puck regardless.

**Keep your skates off the red crease line itself, not merely out of the blue paint.** Every rulebook that settles the question in its rule text or glossary counts that line as part of the crease — the IIHF, USA Hockey, Hockey Canada, and the CARHA book your adult league may run on. **The NHL and the PWHL are the exceptions: their rule text leaves the line unsaid, and the one place those books address it answers the other way.** So off the line is the only ice that is safe whichever book you are under, and a net-front player standing on it can cost their team the goal.

## Overview

Hockey's vocabulary is spatial: almost every instruction you will ever be given on the ice is a location plus a reason. Learn the locations and the instructions start to make sense on their own.

**The hard part is that most of those location words are relative, not absolute.** "High", "low", "strong-side" and "defensive zone" all change meaning depending on which team you are on and where the puck is at that moment. The same patch of ice is high to one player and low to another; the end you are defending swaps every period; and the strong side — wherever the puck happens to be — flips several times in a minute, which is why defence partners have to keep talking. Mistaking a relative word for a fixed place on the map is how a player ends up in the wrong half of the rink while doing exactly what they were told, and it is the single biggest source of beginner confusion.

**Job titles behave the same way.** F1, F2 and F3 are roles defined by the order players arrive, not names for particular people — whoever gets there first is F1, and it will be somebody else the next time down the ice.

**Do not assume the rink you are standing in matches the diagrams.** The dimensions below come from rulebooks, and rulebooks describe rinks built to them; most recreational, community and older sheets were not. **That last point is general observation rather than a sourced figure.** In British hockey the smaller sheet is the governing body's own position rather than a local quirk — the In-House Rules authorise rinks below 56 m × 26 m for all levels and, from 2026-27, in all fixtures, which is shorter than the only length the international (IIHF) book gives, 60 m, and narrower than its 26 m minimum width. Distances shift with the building: how far a pass has to travel, how much room there is behind the goal, whether a long pass down the ice is available at all. **The first time you play somewhere unfamiliar, look rather than assume.**

The rest is the map. Read it once end to end, then keep it to hand and look things up. It covers the dimensions, every line and marking and what each one does, the three zones and every named area of the ice — all of that drawn to scale — and then the directional vocabulary and the six positions that go with them. What it does not cover is the working vocabulary layered on top: the words that shift meaning depending on who is speaking, and the alphabetical glossary of terms. Those are in [Language and Glossary](language_and_glossary.md). Neither page is exhaustive: a term that carries a special sense somewhere is defined at the point it is used.

## 1. Rink dimensions, and why they matter

### NHL / North American

Every NHL rink is the same size, and it is the size most North American rinks are built to. **The first half of that is Rule 1.2. The second half is general observation rather than a sourced figure** — no measured distribution of rink sizes stands behind it — and the section on rec rinks below sets out how far real sheets vary from it.

| Feature | NHL measurement | Metric |
|---|---|---|
| Overall | 200 ft × 85 ft | 60.96 m × 25.91 m |
| Corner radius | 28 ft | 8.53 m |
| Goal line to end boards | 11 ft | 3.35 m |
| Blue line to goal line | 64 ft | 19.51 m |
| Blue line to blue line (neutral zone) | 50 ft | 15.24 m |
| Boards height | 40–48 inches (ideal 42 inches) | 1.02–1.22 m |
| Glass above boards | 8 ft at the ends, ≥6 ft along the sides | 2.44 m / 1.83 m |
| Goal opening | 6 ft wide × 4 ft high (Rule 2.1), 40 inches deep (rink diagram, not Rule 2.1) | 1.83 m × 1.22 m |

*(NHL Official Rules 2025-2026, Rules 1.2, 1.3, 1.5, 2.1 and the official rink diagram. The **50 ft neutral zone is a derivation, not a figure the rule text states**: the 200 ft sheet of Rule 1.2 less two 11 ft end strips and two 64 ft goal-line-to-blue-line distances, both from Rule 1.5.)*

### IIHF / international ("Olympic ice") — and the British base

**If you play anywhere in Britain, this is your specification, not the NHL one above.** Every level of the game here — the Elite League, the NIHL, recreational and junior hockey across England, Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland, university hockey and the GB national teams — runs on the IIHF Rule Book, amended locally. See [UK and England Rules](uk_rules.md), and read the England Ice Hockey amendment below before you take any IIHF figure as a description of your rink.

The IIHF book does not specify one size; it specifies a range. Rule 1.2: *"The official size of the rink shall be 60m long and 26m to 30m wide. The corners shall be rounded in the arc of a circle with a radius of 7.0m to 8.50m. Any deviations from these dimensions for any IIHF competition require IIHF approval."* Note two things: the **length is fixed at 60 m** — only the width is a range — and a deviation is not simply a smaller rink, it is something that needs **approval**.

**"60 × 30" is not the IIHF standard rink size**, although it is often quoted as one: 30 m is the top of the width range, not the specification.

| Feature | IIHF | Rule |
|---|---|---|
| Overall | 60 m × 26–30 m (~196.9 ft × 85.3–98.4 ft) | 1.2 |
| Corner radius | 7.0–8.50 m (23.0–27.9 ft) | 1.2 |
| Goal line to end boards | 4.0 m (13.1 ft) | 1.5 |
| Blue line to goal line | 18.5 m (60.7 ft) | Appendix VI (2250 cm from the end boards) |
| Neutral zone (¼ of rink length) | 15.0 m (49.2 ft) | Appendix VI (¼ L = 1500 cm) |
| Blue line width | 30 cm (~12 inches) | 1.5 |
| Boards height | ideal 1.07 m (~42 inches) | 1.3 |
| Glass above boards | 2.4 m behind each goal (extending at least 4.0 m from the goal line), 1.8 m along the sides *"except in front of the Players' benches"* — 7.9 ft and 5.9 ft | 1.3 |
| Players' bench | 10 m long × 1.50 m wide (~32.8 ft × 4.9 ft) | 3.1 |

*(IIHF Official Rule Book 2025/26 and the Appendix VI "Rink Lines" infographic, whose measurements are all in centimetres. Every IIHF figure in this document was read in the rule book itself on 29 July 2026.)*

The classic "big ice" is the 30 m (98.4 ft) version — **13.4 ft wider than an NHL rink**. That is the sheet most European domestic leagues and most older Olympic tournaments used.

**Don't assume "international" means "wide" any more.** The 2026 Milano Cortina Olympic tournament was played on 60 m × 26 m ice — about three feet shorter and four inches wider than an NHL rink — and the 2010 Vancouver and 2022 Beijing Games also used NHL-sized sheets. The IIHF's position was that the difference is insignificant for safety and quality of play. So the wide sheet is now the exception at the top level, not the rule.

**The consequence for anyone playing outside North America — Britain included — is that "IIHF-size" is not a size.** Rule 1.2 sanctions everything from 26 m to 30 m wide, so two rinks that are both entirely legal can differ by more than thirteen feet across. Whatever your league's rulebook says, the sheet is a property of the building, not of the book. Measure it or ask, and read the next section.

### The England Ice Hockey amendment — British rinks are outside the IIHF range

This is the single most important thing on this page for a British reader, and it is why you cannot take either table above as a description of your rink.

The IHUK In-House Rules amend IIHF Rule 1 directly. Under the heading *"Rink size and eligibility"*, the In-House Rules 2026-27 state: *"Rinks with a non-standard playing surface (below 56 m × 26 m) are authorised by the NGB for all levels of hockey"* — with two named rinks permitted only up to U16: **Sutton** in England and **Coatbridge** in Scotland. The rule's own scope line reads *"This rule applies to all fixtures"* — where the 2025-26 edition confined it to EIH and SIHA fixtures.

That threshold is not a relaxation of Rule 1.2; it sits **outside** it. The IIHF fixes the **length at 60 m** and offers no shorter option, so a sheet under **56 m** is over thirteen feet short of the only length the book recognises — and shorter than an NHL rink too (200 ft is 60.96 m). **26 m is the IIHF *minimum* width**, so "below 56 m × 26 m" is below the narrowest legal international sheet. Note that 26 m is 85.30 ft, fractionally **wider** than a full NHL rink: a legal international sheet is never narrower than an NHL one, and a British sheet just under the 26 m threshold can still be wider than one. Where the IIHF sheet gives way is length. Note also that 56 m is a British threshold, not an IIHF one.

So the practical position for a British player is blunt: **your working assumption should be that your rink is smaller than both the NHL standard and the IIHF standard, and that neither set of dimensions above describes the building you are standing in.** Everything downstream of rink size shifts with it — how long a rim takes to come round, how far the half-wall is from the slot, how much room there is behind the net, whether a stretch pass is even available.

Two limits on how far that goes, both about the evidence rather than the ice:

- **The two British sources word the authorisation differently, and it matters.** The In-House Rules read as blanket and standing — non-standard surfaces *"are authorised"* by the NGB *"for all levels of hockey"*, with the two venues named as the **exception to** that authorisation rather than as examples of it. But England Ice Hockey's *Rules & Regulations* 22.2 says such rinks *"may be approved by EIH to be used for all levels of hockey"*, which is the language of approval on application, and names only Sutton. **The published documents do not settle which of the two governs.** That sub-standard sheets are the ordinary case rather than the awkward one follows from the In-House wording — but it is an inference, and no published count of British rink dimensions exists to test it.
- **There is still no published census of British rink dimensions**, so nothing here tells you the distribution — how many rinks, or how far below. England Ice Hockey holds the register but does not print it: *Rules & Regulations* 22.8 says only that *"a list of approved rinks can be found by contacting EIH"*. The rule tells you what to expect; it does not tell you what yours measures. That remains something to go and look at.

The full list of England Ice Hockey amendments is in [UK and England Rules](uk_rules.md); the geometry is here.

### What the extra width actually changes

If you move from an 85 ft sheet to a 98 ft sheet, five things change in ways you will feel within one shift:

- **The corners get bigger and further from the net.** A puck rimmed into the corner is a longer retrieval, a longer forecheck sprint, and a longer pass back to the middle. Dump-and-chase is less rewarding.
- **The half-wall is further from the slot.** A pass from the wall to the middle takes longer to arrive, which gives defenders time to close. Offensive-zone play tends to be more perimeter-based and more patient.
- **Angling an attacker to the boards stops being a kill shot.** On narrow ice, the wall is your second defender. On wide ice, an attacker forced outside still has room to keep skating, so gap control and stick position matter more than steering.
- **Backchecking distance goes up.** Cross-ice recovery is longer, so weak-side coverage is harder and late attackers arrive more freely.
- **The neutral zone stays almost the same length.** NHL 50 ft — a derivation from Rules 1.2 and 1.5, not a figure the rule text states — against IIHF 15.0 m (49.2 ft), which Appendix VI states directly: effectively identical. What changes is width, not length, so neutral-zone traps that rely on squeezing the width of the ice get much harder to run.

The reverse also holds: coming from wide ice to narrow ice, you have far less time on the wall, and simple quick plays beat clever slow ones.

**One genuine asymmetry worth knowing:** the IIHF goal line is 4.0 m from the end boards, the NHL goal line is 11 ft (3.35 m). There is about **two feet more room behind the net** on international ice, which makes wrap-arounds, behind-the-net possession and D-to-D behind the net slightly easier there.

### Rec rinks are not standard, and it matters

Most recreational, beer-league, youth and older community rinks are **not** built to either specification. **That sentence and the ranges below it are general observation rather than a sourced standard: no measured distribution of rink sizes stands behind them, so read them as the shape of the variation to expect rather than as a count of it.** Common variations, and what they do to you:

- **Short sheets** (170–190 ft). The neutral zone is compressed; there is barely any time between the blue lines. Stretch passes and regroups mostly stop working; quick chips and immediate forechecking work better.
- **Narrow sheets** (65–80 ft). Everything happens faster and closer. Cross-ice passes are far more available, but so are the sticks trying to intercept them.
- **Missing markings.** Many rec rinks have no trapezoid at all, no neutral-zone dots, or faceoff circles painted to a different radius. If the trapezoid isn't painted, that is a strong hint your league isn't enforcing it — a hint, not a ruling, since the rule turns on the position of the puck rather than the markings — which changes your goaltender's job completely (see [Goaltender](../positions/goaltender.md) and [Breakouts](../systems/breakouts.md)).
- **Boards and glass in poor repair.** Dead spots in the boards eat rims. Learn your home rink's dead spots; they are a real tactical asset.

**Practical instruction:** on the first shift at an unfamiliar rink, deliberately check three things — how far it is from the goal line to the end boards, whether the trapezoid is painted, and how the puck comes off the corner boards on a rim. Those three answers change your breakout.

**If you play in Britain, assume nothing from the diagrams here until you have looked** — and note that this is not a rec-rink caveat, it is the governing body's own position. Rule 1.2 sanctions any sheet from 26 m to 30 m wide, and the In-House Rules then authorise rinks *below* 56 m × 26 m for all levels and, from 2026-27, in all fixtures. So "we play IIHF rules" tells you which penalties get called and nothing at all about the ice — and unlike a North American beer-league rink, a British sub-standard sheet is running the full IIHF rule book on it, with one exception whose status is genuinely unsettled: the trapezoid, below.

## 3. The lines and markings, and what each one does

### The goal line

A **2-inch red line drawn all the way across the ice**, 11 ft from the end boards, with the goal frame standing on it (NHL Rule 1.5). **Under IIHF Rule 1.5 — the British book — it is a 5 cm red line drawn 4.0 m from each end and continued vertically up the boards.** It is one of the most consequential lines on the rink because so many rules key off it:

- A goal is scored when the puck **completely crosses** the goal line between the posts and under the crossbar.
- **Icing** is judged by the puck crossing the goal line (the whole line, boards to boards — not just the goalmouth).
- The **trapezoid** restricts your goaltender only *behind* the goal line.
- Coaches use "**below the goal line**" as shorthand for the area behind the net, which is a distinct tactical space with its own rules of thumb.

### The blue lines

Two **12-inch-wide blue lines**, drawn 64 ft out from each goal line, dividing the ice into three zones (NHL Rule 1.5). **Under IIHF Rule 1.5 they are 30 cm wide — the same foot — and sit 18.5 m out from each goal line.** They are the widest lines on the ice, and that width does work: offside is judged against the *whole* line, so the puck must cross all of it before the zone changes hands.

What the blue lines do:

- **The blue lines define offside.** You are offside only when **both skates** have completely crossed the line into the attacking zone before the puck has completely crossed it. One skate on the line or behind it keeps you onside. And the **puck** must completely cross too — until it has, the line is still live. See [Rules Primer](rules_primer.md) and [Zone Entries](../systems/zone_entries.md).
- **The blue lines are where defencemen live in the offensive zone** — see "the point" below.
- **The blue lines are the reference for the "tag up" reset** on a delayed offside — where your rule set gives you one, which under USA Hockey Rule 630(d) it does not below Youth 15-Only, Girls 16U, High School and Adult. [Language and Glossary](language_and_glossary.md) carries the tag-up rule in full, including what it takes in Britain.

⚠️ **The trail skate is where the books split, and the split changes what you do.** Because the line is a foot wide and offside requires *complete* crossing, attackers deliberately drag a trail skate on the line while their body and stick get ahead of it. That is not a trick; it is the rule working as designed. Dragging the blade **in contact** works under all four rule sets. **Lifting it keeps you onside under the NHL and IIHF, and is offside under USA Hockey (Rule 630(a)) and Hockey Canada (Rule 6.11) — two books of the four**, both of which judge offside on the skate that is in contact with the ice.

### The centre red line

A **12-inch red line** across the middle of the ice, marked with a distinctive pattern so it can never be mistaken for a blue line (NHL Rule 1.5). Its remaining jobs:

- **Icing** is measured from the centre red line: a puck shot from your own half, crossing the opponent's goal line untouched, is icing.
- To nullify a potential icing, a teammate must "**gain the line**" — the puck must contact the centre red line **on a stick**, not a skate.
- The centre red line is the conventional reference for gap control: close to about **a stick-and-a-half** by the red line. **That is a guide, not a law** — it moves with your own backward speed and the attacker's skill — **and it is coaching convention rather than a measured figure:** the one published coaching page found that anchors a distance to the centre red line prints **two** stick lengths there, not a stick and a half. [Defending the Rush](../systems/defending_the_rush.md) has the full set of distances, and says which published pages back each figure and which contradict it.

![](diagram:icing-gaining-the-line)

The centre red line is **no longer a passing restriction** in the NHL. The two-line pass rule was abolished by a playing-rule change for the 2005-06 season, so the centre line no longer determines an offside pass — which is why the **stretch pass** exists as a weapon at all.

### The goal crease ("the blue paint")

In the NHL the crease is built as follows (Rule 1.7): a 2-inch red line runs **1 ft outside each goal post**, extending **4 ft 6 in** straight out at right angles to the goal line, and the two straight sides are joined by a **6 ft radius arc** struck from the centre of the goal line. So it is **8 ft wide at the goal line and 6 ft deep at its deepest point**. The enclosed area is painted light blue and — importantly — the crease is a **volume**, not a patch of ice: it extends **4 ft vertically**, to the height of the top of the goal frame.

![](diagram:the-goal-crease)

There are also two small **5-inch marks** on the inside of the crease lines, 4 ft out from the goal line. Those are the goaltender's reference marks, not player restraining lines.

> **IIHF (Rules 1.5 and 1.7 — the British book):** the same shape at metric sizes — 2.45 m wide at the goal line, marked in 5 cm red line, and *"the marked line belongs to the Goal Crease."* One real difference: the Goal Crease Area *"extends vertically until the top of the crossbar"* — the top of the frame, rather than the NHL's fixed 4 ft.

**Whether the red line itself is *in* the crease is a separate question, and the books do not answer it together: every one that settles it in rule text or glossary puts the line inside the crease, while the NHL — which does not — answers it only in a reference table, the other way.** It is not pedantry: the crease edge is where a goal gets waved off, and "on the line" is exactly where a net-front player stands.

**The IIHF** Rule 1.7 says *"The marked line belongs to the Goal Crease"*, identical in the 2025/26 and 2026/27 editions. **USA Hockey** says it twice — the Note to Rule 625(b) defines the area as *"all the space outlined by the semi-circular crease lines (including crease lines)"*, and its Casebook answers the question in terms, at *The Rink*, Situation 4: *"Is the two inch red line outlining the goal crease considered to be a part of the goal crease? Yes."* **Hockey Canada** answers it in its Glossary rather than in its Rule 1.5(c), which uses the NHL's looser wording: *"The lines of the crease are considered part of the crease."* ⚠️ **And so does CARHA, the book to check if you play adult recreational hockey** — in its **Glossary** entry *Creases*, *"The lines which designate these areas are to be considered as part of the creases"*, while its **Rule 5(c)** carries the same silent *"all the space outlined by the crease lines"* wording the NHL uses. **The entry is written in the plural because it covers the Referee's crease as well as the goalkeeper's**, and ⚠️ **it binds CARHA-affiliated adult leagues rather than rec hockey in general**, so ask your league which book it runs on. **The NHL** is the exception: it says only that the area is *"outlined by the crease lines"*, which does not say which side the outline runs along, and no other rule in the book addresses it — the one place the line's status appears at all is Reference Table 14, which treats a player *"on the crease line"* as **not** in the crease. ⚠️ **The PWHL book reproduces both halves of that** — the same silent rule wording and the same table row — so it lands where the NHL does.

**What that costs a net-front player.** Under **USA Hockey** Rule 625(b) no goal may be scored with an attacking player in the crease, whoever scored it — so a skate on the red line voids your teammate's goal, subject only to that rule's own carve-outs. Under **Hockey Canada** Rule 8.5 standing in the crease is not itself a foul, but the goal survives only where the crease player stays passive and does not *"obstruct the Goaltender's view or movements"* — and a screen obstructs the view by definition, so on the line is no goal there too. **The instruction that works in every book, and needs none of this resolved: keep your skates off the red line, not merely out of the blue paint.**

> ⚠️ **The IIHF's own two answers do not line up, and nothing held here resolves them.** Rule 1.7 puts the marked line inside the crease. Its Appendix IV goalkeeper-interference table — **Table 16** in the 2025/26 book, renumbered **Table 14** in 2026/27 — then prints an attacker planted *"within the goal crease"* to obstruct the goalkeeper's vision as *"Goal is disallowed"*, and an attacker planted *"on the crease line or outside the goal crease"* on otherwise identical facts as *"Goal is allowed."* Read with Rule 1.7, those two rows answer the same play in opposite directions; **no edition of the IIHF Situation Handbook mentions the crease line at all**, and **neither book says anywhere what status these tables have**. [Shooting](../technique/shooting.md) sets out what the tables say and the limits that travel with them. Until it is settled, do not plan a screen on the line under the IIHF book.

Why it matters to you: the crease is the boundary of goaltender interference. Inside it, you must not impair the goalie's ability to make a save. Outside it, *incidental* contact is legal if you made a reasonable effort to avoid it — but ⚠️ **all four books qualify that permission before granting it**, in the NHL's and the IIHF's words at Rule 69.4: *"A goalkeeper is not 'fair game' just because he is outside the goal crease. The appropriate penalty should be assessed in every case where an attacking player makes unnecessary contact with the goalkeeper."* USA Hockey writes it at 607(d), Hockey Canada at 8.5, where the penalty reaches *"anywhere on the ice."* A clean screen — **clear of the crease and of its red line**, in the goalie's sightline, no contact — is left alone by all four books, and two of them say so in a worked situation rather than leaving it to inference: a player who **plants himself outside the crease** and obstructs the goaltender's vision draws *"Goal is allowed"* at **NHL Reference Table 14** and at the IIHF's own Appendix IV goalkeeper-interference table, **Situation 5 E** — while **5 D**, the same planting done *inside* the crease, is disallowed. ⚠️ **Both those rows extend the permission to a player *"on the crease line"*, and only the NHL's book leaves it standing:** the IIHF's own Rule 1.7 puts that line inside the crease, which makes it 5 D rather than 5 E, and USA Hockey and Hockey Canada put it inside too, where it costs your teammate's goal. **Plant outside the line, not on it.** ⚠️ **The other two reach it by silence rather than by a table: USA Hockey writes no goaltender-vision provision at all, in its playing rules or its Casebook, and Hockey Canada's two references to the goaltender's view both reach only a player standing in the crease.** ⚠️ **And the NHL and IIHF reach a screener who stops a goaltender getting back to the crease by a different route: Rule 69.4 carries a clause for it, penalising a player who prevents him from returning *"due to the deliberate actions of an attacking player"* — a clause written for the goaltender who has already played the puck outside his crease.** ⚠️ **That is the reach of that one clause, not a licence for everything outside it, because ordinary interference reaches the same act without that precondition in three of the four books.** A goaltender who has not played the puck is an opponent not in possession — **NHL and IIHF Rule 56.1** say so in terms, *"The last player to touch the puck, other than the goalkeeper, shall be considered the player in possession"* — **that in the NHL's wording, the IIHF capitalising *"Player"* and setting *"possession"* in quotation marks of its own** — and **Hockey Canada's** Glossary writes *"except the goaltender"* — and **moving into his path** is a minor under **NHL and IIHF 56.2(i)**, *"interferes with or impedes the progress of an opponent who is not in possession of the puck"* — **the NHL's characters again, the IIHF setting *"possession"* in quotation marks there too** — and **Hockey Canada 8.3(i)**, whose penalty sits at 8.3(a). ⚠️ **Two limits travel with that, and the first is why this says *moving*.** The same NHL and IIHF Rule 56.1 protects ice you already hold — *"A player is allowed the ice he is standing on (body position) and is not required to move in order to let an opponent proceed"* — and frames the offence on movement, a *"pick"* being delivered by a player who *"moves into an opponent's path without initially having body position"*. **Both of those are the NHL's wording too. In the stand-your-ground sentence the IIHF capitalises *"Player"*, writes *"the ice they are standing on"* and *"are not required to move"*, and prints *"(body position)"* bare exactly as the NHL does; in the *"pick"* sentence, by contrast, it adds quotation marks of its own round *"body position"*.** ⚠️ **The second is that USA Hockey is the book those three do not speak for:** its definition of possession carries no goalkeeper exclusion, and its own Casebook routes this play through **625(a)(8)**, a contact clause, so how far its interference route reaches an attacker who never touches the goaltender is not settled in its terms. **None of that changes what to do. Clear the lane back to his net whether or not he has touched the puck.**

Inside the crease they part company, and there the cost is the goal rather than a penalty. ⚠️ **USA Hockey Rule 625(b) is a flat position bar:** no goal with an attacking player in the crease, whoever scored, *"unless the puck has preceded the player(s) into the goal crease or the goalkeeper is out of the goal crease area."* **That second carve-out reverses the moment the goalkeeper starts back** — its Casebook disallows the goal *and* assesses an interference penalty where an attacker prevents a returning goalkeeper from making a play. ⚠️ **And being shoved in by a defender answers only for your position, not for what you do next:** under USA Hockey the goal stands, but its Rule 625(a)(8) is still a minor for interfering with the goalkeeper once you are there. **Hockey Canada Rule 8.5 is not flat** — the goal stands where a teammate outside the crease scores and the crease player stays completely passive. **So work just outside the paint, not on its edge and not in it.** [Rules Primer](rules_primer.md) carries the full four-book comparison. See [Winger](../positions/winger.md) and [Offensive Zone Play](../systems/offensive_zone_play.md).

### The referee's crease

A **10 ft radius red semicircle** on the ice in front of the Penalty Timekeeper's seat (NHL Rule 1.7). Players may not enter or remain in it while the referee is reporting to the off-ice officials — doing so is a misconduct under Rule 39.4. It is the one marking on the rink with no tactical purpose whatsoever; it exists so officials can do paperwork without being surrounded.

> **IIHF** calls it the **On-Ice Officials' Crease** and marks it with a 3.0 m radius (Rule 1.7).

### The faceoff spots and circles — all nine

There are **nine faceoff locations** on the ice. Every stoppage results in a draw at one of them.

| Name | Location (NHL) | Circle? |
|---|---|---|
| **Centre ice dot** — spot 1 | Exact centre of the rink; 12-inch blue spot | Yes — 15 ft radius blue circle |
| **Neutral-zone dots** — spots 2–3, defensive-zone end | 5 ft outside your own blue line, 44 ft apart | No |
| **Neutral-zone dots** — spots 4–5, offensive-zone end | 5 ft outside the attacking blue line, 44 ft apart | No |
| **End-zone dots** — spots 6–7, one end | 20 ft out from the goal line, 22 ft either side of the centre of the goal | Yes — 15 ft radius red circle |
| **End-zone dots** — spots 8–9, other end | Same as spots 6–7 | Yes |

*(NHL Rule 1.9, which fixes the end-zone spots "along a line twenty feet from each goal line and parallel to it", twenty-two feet either side of the line joining the centres of the two goals.)*

The **four neutral-zone dots have no circles** — just 2 ft red spots. That is deliberate: neutral-zone draws are meant to restart play quickly with everyone spread out, so there is no restraining circle.

How players name them in conversation:

- **"Centre ice"** or **"the middle dot"** — the opening draw of each period and every draw after a goal.
- **"Our end" / "their end"**, then a side: *"D-zone draw, bench side"* or *"O-zone draw, far side."* "Bench side" means the half of the ice your players' bench is on; "far side" is the other. This is the most common way to specify an end-zone draw, because your bench side determines who can change.
- **"Outside the blue"** or **"neutral-zone dot"** for the four dots flanking the blue lines.
- **"Strong-side dot" / "weak-side dot"** when the puck is already in play near one of them.

> **IIHF equivalents (Rule 1.9 and Appendix VI) — the operative set in Britain:** centre spot 30 cm across with a 4.50 m radius blue circle; neutral-zone spots 60 cm across, 1.50 m from each blue line and 14.0 m apart; end-zone spots 6.0 m from the goal line and 7.0 m either side of centre, with 4.50 m radius red circles. The same nine locations, metricated, and the four neutral-zone spots have no circles there either.

### Hash marks

The **hash marks** are the short lines outside each end-zone faceoff circle. In the NHL they are two red lines, 2 inches wide and **2 ft long, placed 5 ft 7 in apart**, at the outer edge of each side of the circle, running parallel to the goal line (Rule 1.9). Four per circle — one pair on the boards side, one pair on the middle-of-the-ice side, both roughly level with the dot.

They exist to keep players apart on a draw. **Encroachment into the area between the hash marks before the puck is dropped is a faceoff violation** (Rule 76.7(ii)). And, the part that catches people out, a winger who creeps over the hash marks gets **the centre** thrown out of the dot, not themselves.

![](diagram:faceoff-dzone-alignment)

They are also the depth reference coaches lean on most in the defensive zone — that it is the one leaned on *most* is coaching observation, but the line itself is painted and its depth is fixed. "**Below the hash marks**" means the band between that line and the goal line: the end-zone dot sits **20 ft** out from the goal line and the blue line **64 ft** out, so that band is roughly the **bottom third** of the zone's depth rather than the bottom half — half the depth would be 32 ft out, a full twelve feet higher. "**Above the hash marks**" is everything from there out to the blue line.

There is a second, smaller set of markings you may not have noticed: an L-shaped pair of lines 1 ft outside each faceoff spot, 4 ft long and 18 in apart, with a 2 ft 10 in foot at the end (Rule 1.9). Those are where the two centres' skates go on a draw, and standing wrong on them is itself a faceoff violation (Rule 76.7(iv)). ⚠️ **But "restraining lines" is not a name for these marks alone, so ask which lines are meant when you hear it** — the NHL book uses the same phrase for the hash marks at Rule 76.6, and its own index reads *"Hash marks (restraining lines)"*. The full faceoff procedure is in [Faceoffs](../systems/faceoffs.md).

> **IIHF (Rule 1.9):** hash marks are 5 cm wide, 60 cm long and 1.70 m apart, at the outer edge of both sides of each circle and parallel to the goal line. The centres' restraining lines start 30 cm out from the spot and are 1.20 m long, 45 cm apart, with a 90 cm foot.

### The trapezoid (goalkeeper's restricted area)

The trapezoid is the marked area behind each net inside which the goaltender may play the puck. In the books that have one, a goaltender who plays the puck behind the goal line **outside** it takes a **two-minute minor for delay of game**, and the position of the puck is the determining factor. **The rule text's one exception is a goaltender playing the puck while maintaining skate contact with the goal crease** — there is no teammate-based exception. ⚠️ **The IIHF's Situation Handbook adds two more, and they are not in the rule text**: a puck stopped *on* the goal line is not in the restricted area at all (Situation 27.6), and a puck that merely deflects off the glass into a retreating goaltender draws nothing, because *"the goalkeeper must **play** the puck to be penalized"* (Situation 27.9). ⚠️ **Both answers misnumber it *"Rule 27.8"*; the IIHF's restricted area is 27.7.** The penalty sits at **NHL** Rules 27.8 and 63.2(viii) and **IIHF** Rules 27.7 and 63.2(VIII); [Rules Primer](rules_primer.md) carries it, and what it means for the goalie who has to live inside it is in [Goaltender](../positions/goaltender.md).

![](diagram:the-trapezoid)

**Current NHL markings (Rule 1.8 and the official rink diagram):** the lines begin **7 ft outside each side of the goal crease — 8 ft from each goal post** — and run diagonally back to points **11 ft from each goal post** at the end boards. In plain numbers: **22 ft wide along the goal line, widening to 28 ft at the end boards**, and 11 ft deep.

> **Two rules, two different numbers — flagged because you will see both quoted.** Rule 1.8 and the official rink diagram give 8 ft from each post (22 ft at the goal line). Rule **27.8**, the penalty rule, still reads "lines that begin six feet (6') from either goal post" — the pre-2014 dimension, left behind when the trapezoid was **widened by two feet on each side for the 2014-15 season** at the NHLPA's request. Neither rule is self-contradictory; 27.8 simply describes the older, narrower shape. Treat **8 ft / 22 ft** as the painted reality and the diagram as authoritative.

**Where the trapezoid applies:** the NHL and IIHF play, both read directly in the rulebooks. **The KHL is commonly said to have one as well, though that one is unverified here: no KHL rulebook was available to check it against.** The IIHF's own 2021 announcement dates its adoption to the **2021-22 season**; **the NHL's commonly given 2005-06 date is in no rulebook held here.** **Neither North American governing body's book has one** — the *USA Hockey Playing Rules 2025-29* and the *Hockey Canada Playing Rules 2026-2028* mark no goalkeeper's restricted area anywhere in their rink sections, and neither limits *where behind the net* a goalkeeper may play the puck.

⚠️ **But "no trapezoid" is not "no restriction", and two differences will still cost your goaltender two minutes.** Both books stop the goaltender dead at the **centre red line** — deliberately participating in the play beyond it is a minor under USA Hockey **407(c)** and Interference under Hockey Canada **4.13(c)**. ⚠️ **And both judge that on the goaltender's *skates*, where NHL 27.7 and IIHF 27.6 judge it on the puck**, so a goaltender who reaches back across the line for a puck still on their own side is legal under the professional books and a minor under both amateur ones. ⚠️ **Neither 407(c) nor 4.13(c) says the word, though, so look the skates test up in the right volume:** USA Hockey's is in the separate *Casebook*, at **Rule 407, Situation 13**; Hockey Canada's is in playing-rule text at **Rule 8.3(a)(ii)** and in **Interpretation 2 to Rule 4.13(c)**. Freezing is restricted out there too — USA Hockey Rule 614(c) and Hockey Canada's Interpretation 3 to Rule 10.1(a). [Goaltender](../positions/goaltender.md) owns all of it. **How much rec, beer-league and youth hockey those two books add up to is a question about leagues rather than about books, and this document has no count for it.** Find out which book you are under, because it changes your defencemen's retrieval game completely.

> **🇬🇧 Britain: the geometry is the IIHF's.** Rule 1.8 marks the *"Goalkeeper Designated Area"* with two 5 cm red lines, *"6.80m"* along the goal line and *"8.60m"* along the boards — four inches and three inches off the NHL's 22 ft (6.71 m) and 28 ft (8.53 m), so **do not read the two books as diverging on width**. The area is *deeper*, though, simply because the IIHF goal line is 4.0 m from the boards rather than 11 ft. Rule 27.7 restates those current dimensions, so unlike NHL 27.8 it leaves no superseded figure in the penalty rule, and it carries the same rule-text exception in terms — ⚠️ **the IIHF's own Situation Handbook then adds two the rule text does not: a puck stopped on the goal line, and one that only deflects into you** — reading: *"The minor penalty will not be assessed when a goalkeeper plays the puck while maintaining skate contact with their goal crease."*

**Whether it is live in England and Wales is genuinely unsettled, and that is the trap.** The In-House Rules put the whole domestic game on the IIHF book, recreational and junior hockey included, and amend Rule 1 only on rink size and benches — so on those alone the restricted area would be live at every level. England Ice Hockey's own *Rules & Regulations* (2024-2025), Rule 22.3, then suspends it: *"Goalkeepers' Restricted Area is not currently enforced to allow ice rinks the necessary time to make the changes to implement this rule."* That explains the missing paint. But the In-House Rules do not mention the restricted area at all in either edition searched, and nothing in them suspends it for SIHA or BUIHA hockey, **so the published documents do not settle whether the suspension still stands. Ask your league** before assuming either that your goaltender is free behind the net or that they are not; [UK and England Rules](uk_rules.md) records the same uncertainty. **Nor does anything establish what a sub-standard British rink's trapezoid measures** — its depth is set by that 4.0 m goal line, which a sheet below 56 m may not have room for.

⚠️ **And read what Rule 22.3 actually suspends, because it is much narrower than "free behind the net".** It names the **Goalkeepers' Restricted Area** and nothing else. **IIHF Rule 63.2(I) is a separate delay-of-game minor, and no British document held here touches it** — it penalises any player, *"including the goalkeeper"*, who *"holds, 'freezes' or plays the puck"* so as to cause a stoppage, and for a goalkeeper *"this rule applies **outside of their goal crease area**"*. ⚠️ **Note where that boundary sits — the goal crease, not the trapezoid**, so the whole designated area and all the ice behind the net is outside it. **The suspension frees where your goaltender may *play* the puck; it does not free where they may *freeze* it** — and the IIHF's own Appendix IV settles that without inference, listing *"Playing puck outside restricted area"* and *"Deliberately 'freezes' the puck inside or outside the goal crease"* as separate rows against different rule numbers. ⚠️ **Both carve-outs travel with that, or you produce a goaltender who will not cover a rebound:** coming out to *"cut down the angle"*, saving, and *then* covering is legal (63.2(VI)'s Note), and 63.2(VII) excepts a goalkeeper *"actually being checked by an opponent"*. [Goaltender](../positions/goaltender.md) owns the freezing rules in full.

Tactically, the trapezoid is why dump-and-chase works. A puck rimmed into the corner cannot be calmly collected by the goaltender; a defenceman has to go and get it with a forechecker arriving. **Aim your dump-ins away from the trapezoid.**

### The boards, the glass, and the benches

- **The boards** (also "**the wall**") are 40–48 inches high, ideally 42, under **NHL Rule 1.3**. They are in play: the puck off the boards is live, and a skilled player uses the wall as a passing partner. **IIHF Rule 1.3 gives one figure rather than a range** — *"The ideal height of the boards above the ice surface shall be 1.07m"*, which is the NHL's ideal 42 inches in metric, with any variation requiring IIHF authorisation.
- **The glass** is 8 ft above the boards at the ends and at least 6 ft along the sides (**NHL Rule 1.3**). **"Off the glass and out" — caroming the puck off the glass and out of your zone — is legal.** Putting it ***over*** the glass is a different act: a puck shot or batted directly out of the playing surface from your own defending zone is a **two-minute minor** (**NHL** Rule 63.2(iii), **IIHF** Rule 63.2(III)), and the carve-outs are narrower than players assume — so keep the clear down.
- **🇬🇧 The IIHF's glass is lower, and that is the figure a British player needs.** **IIHF Rule 1.3**: behind each goal it *"must be 2.4m and must extend at least 4.0m from the 'goal line' towards the 'blue line'"*, and along the sides *"must be 1.8 m, except in front of the Players' benches"* — about 7.9 ft and 5.9 ft. Where the NHL writes a **floor** of six feet down the sides, the IIHF writes a **height**, slightly under it, and the tall glass behind each goal is guaranteed only for the first four metres, a **minimum rather than a maximum**. **So keep a clearing attempt down rather than up:** the glass you are trying to beat is lower than the NHL number in your head, and the same two-minute minor applies. ⚠️ **The rule's *"except where there is no protective glass"* carve-out is not available at the bench** — a puck shot **over** the bench glass is penalised, and only one shot **into** the bench, or one leaving *"directly off a 'face-off'"*, is let off. [Rules Primer](rules_primer.md) owns the delay-of-game rule and its carve-outs.
- **The players' benches** are both on the **same side of the ice**, opposite the penalty benches, each about 24 ft long, seating at least 14. The **penalty benches sit in the neutral zone**, on opposite sides of the timekeeper. **IIHF Rule 3.1 says each bench** *"should be 10m in length and 1.50m in width"* — longer than the NHL's, and "should" rather than "shall". In Britain the In-House Rules soften it further: benches and penalty boxes must, *"within the fixed constraints of the rink, conform as closely as reasonably practicable to IIHF dimensions and layout"*, and any unavoidable deviation must be *"communicated to both teams and the officials prior to warm-up"* (In-House Rules, Rule 3).
- **The "change zone" is a rule, not a marking, and nothing is painted for it.** A player leaving the ice must be at their own bench and out of the play before their replacement steps on, or it is a bench minor for too many men. **How close is a book question, and the four books give four answers:** **NHL Rule 74.1** says *"within five feet (5')"* and **IIHF Rule 74.1** *"within 1.50m"* — the same distance to within an inch, so do not read those two as a divergence; **USA Hockey Rule 204(b) states no distance at all**; **Hockey Canada Rule 6.1(a)** and **CARHA Rule 19(b)** both give 3.05 m (10 ft). [Rules Primer](rules_primer.md) carries the full four-book position.

Because both benches are on one side, one half of the ice is the "**bench side**" and the other is the "**far side**". This is a real tactical fact, not trivia: changing is safe on the bench side and dangerous on the far side, and coaches call faceoff plays differently depending on which side the draw is.

## 4. The three zones — and the thing that confuses every beginner

The two blue lines divide the ice into three zones. The NHL rulebook names them from the perspective of one team (Rule 1.6 and the Glossary of Terms):

- **Defending zone** — the zone containing the goal your team is defending. It runs from your own blue line to the end boards behind your own net.
- **Neutral zone** — the centre ice area between the two blue lines. Neither defending nor attacking.
- **Attacking zone** — the zone containing the goal you are attacking, from the opponent's blue line to the end boards behind their net.

![](diagram:the-three-zones)

**Here is the part that confuses everyone: the zones are named relative to your team, not to the building.** The same physical piece of ice is *your* defensive zone and *their* offensive zone, simultaneously. Nothing about the ice changes; the label depends on who is speaking.

Two consequences you must internalise:

1. **"Offensive zone" and "defensive zone" swap ends every period.** Teams change ends after each period, so the end you defended in the first is the end you attack in the second. The label follows the team, not the paint.
2. **When a teammate or coach says "get it deep," you have to know whose zone they mean.** In practice, hockey speakers almost always mean *their own team's* perspective. If in doubt, use the unambiguous terms: **"our end" / "their end"**, or **"end zone"** for either.

Some documents and commentators use **"offensive zone"** where the rulebook says "attacking zone", and **"D-zone"** as shorthand for defending zone. All three are common; they mean the same thing.

**The zone includes its blue line.** For offside purposes the puck is not in the attacking zone until it has *completely* crossed the line, and a player standing on the line is not yet in the zone. The line is painted a foot wide, 30 cm under the IIHF book — **reading that width as existing *for* the offside rule is this document’s inference, though: the rules that set the width do not themselves say why it is that wide.**

**"End zone"** is the neutral term for either zone that contains a net — useful precisely because it carries no ownership.

## 5. Named areas of the ice

![](diagram:rink-map-end-zone)

This map is a close-up of **one end zone**, drawn to scale. The picture starts at the centre red line, so about a quarter of it is neutral zone: the end zone itself begins at the blue line and runs from there to the end boards at the right.

**Not everything named below is painted on the ice.** The crease, the trapezoid, the faceoff dots and circles and the hash marks are real markings — §3 covers all of them — while the slot, the high slot, the low slot, the goalmouth, the point, the half-wall, and the split above and below the dots are vocabulary read off those markings, not lines of their own.

### The slot

**Where:** the area directly in front of the net, between the two faceoff circles, running from the top of the circles down to the goalmouth.

![](diagram:the-slot)

**Why it matters:** it is where most goals are scored from — see [Shooting](../technique/shooting.md) for the conversion rates by location and the samples they come from. It is the shortest route to the net and the widest angle on it. Everything in defensive-zone coverage is ultimately about who is standing in the slot, and everything in offensive-zone play is ultimately about getting a puck and a body there at the same time.

The slot is layered, and the layers have different jobs:

### The high slot

**Where:** the upper part of the slot — from the level of the **faceoff dots** up to the **top of the circles**.

![](diagram:the-high-slot)

**Why it matters:** this is the shooting layer. A player unmarked here gets a clean look with time to pick a corner, and a puck arriving here from the half-wall is a one-timer. **Who covers it is a coaching choice rather than a rule of hockey.** In a low zone collapse (zone) defensive system — the default assumed throughout this guide — the high slot belongs to the centre, or to the weak-side winger collapsing down when the centre goes to help in the corner. Under a **man-on-man** system nobody holds the area at all: you go with your check wherever they go. **Find out which system your team plays** — [Defensive Zone Coverage](../systems/defensive_zone_coverage.md) sets out each one.

### The low slot

**Where:** the lower part of the slot — from the dots down toward the goalmouth, outside the crease.

![](diagram:the-low-slot)

**Why it matters:** tips, rebounds, and screen position, and defenders cannot let anyone stand unmarked in it. This is the ice a net-front player fights for. **The scoring evidence points here more heavily than anywhere else on the ice — but note what has actually been counted, because it is a share of goals rather than a conversion rate for this layer on its own.** Close to half of all NHL goals came from the inner slot and just over a quarter from the outer slot, on Sportlogiq tracking of three seasons at five-on-five that is not public and cannot be re-derived; separately, **26.8%** of goals were scored within two stick-lengths of the net — about **10.5 feet** — and **51.6%** within twenty feet, computed from the NHL play-by-play feed over 400 games of 2024-25, from the 2,498 goals in it that carry coordinates. [Shooting](../technique/shooting.md) owns the shot-location figures and [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md) the counted distances, and both hedge every one of them. **Treat "highest-percentage ice" as the shape those figures agree on rather than as a measured ranking of this layer against every other one.**

### The goalmouth

**Where:** the immediate front of the net, at the edge of the crease — a much smaller area than the low slot.

![](diagram:the-goalmouth)

**Why it matters:** it is where tap-ins and jam plays happen. Under a **low zone collapse** — the zone defensive-zone coverage assumed as the default throughout this guide, and a coaching choice rather than a law — the **weak-side defenceman** owns the goalmouth and does not leave it. That is not universal: under **man-on-man** the net front belongs to whoever's check went there, and under a **hybrid** — man-on-man below the dots, zone above — to whichever of the low three is matched to the net-front forward. A **collapsing box** is a state rather than a system — what a low zone collapse turns into under maximum pressure — and in it both defencemen are inside together, plus whoever else is in there. **Find out which system your team plays** — [Defensive Zone Coverage](../systems/defensive_zone_coverage.md) sets out who has the net front under each. Getting "goalmouth" and "slot" mixed up is one of the most common causes of a blown assignment — coaching observation rather than a counted ranking. They are two different jobs, one layer apart.

### The "home plate" / high-danger area (also "the house")

**Where:** "home plate" is not an official marking — it is a shape used by analysts and increasingly by coaches. It runs in along the **tops of the two faceoff circles**, straight down the dot lines to the **two faceoff dots** — 44 ft apart, so that part of it is 44 ft wide — and then angles in to the **two goalposts**, which are 6 ft apart on the goal line. Drawn out, it looks like a baseball home plate with its **point at the net**: square across the top, tapering to the width of the goal mouth. [Offensive Zone Play](../systems/offensive_zone_play.md) and [Defensive Zone Coverage](../systems/defensive_zone_coverage.md) describe the same shape and are built around it.

![](diagram:home-plate-the-house)

**Why it matters:** it is the working definition of a "high-danger chance." The exact boundaries vary a little between analytics providers (Natural Stat Trick, Evolving Hockey, MoneyPuck all draw it slightly differently), so treat it as a concept rather than a measurement. The concept is simple and worth having: **shots from inside home plate are worth several times a shot from outside it.** Take shots from inside; work the puck inside rather than shooting from outside.


### The crease / "the paint"

**Where:** the light-blue area in front of the net, 8 ft wide and 6 ft deep, extending 4 ft upward.

**Why it matters:** it is the goaltender's space. "**Stay out of the paint**" is the instruction; "**live just outside the paint**" is the skill. Note where the edge is: **the red boundary line counts as crease under the IIHF, USA Hockey and Hockey Canada, and the NHL never says either way** — see the crease section above for that and for what is actually legal.

### The corners

**Where:** the rounded ends of the rink beside and behind the net, outside the faceoff circles.

**Why it matters:** most loose-puck battles happen here, and corner possession is the start of nearly every offensive-zone cycle and nearly every defensive-zone breakout — coaching observation rather than a measured share. On NHL-sized ice the corners are tight and a two-man forecheck can seal them; on wide ice they are much harder to seal.

### The half-wall

**Where:** the boards **roughly level with the faceoff dot**, between the corner and the point. There is one on each side of each end zone. You will also hear it called the **half-boards**; it is the same place.

**Why it matters:** the half-wall is the most important non-scoring area in the offensive zone — coaching observation rather than a measured ranking. It is the natural distribution point — from here a player can see the point, the slot, the corner and the net-front, and every one of those passes is available. Wingers live here in offensive-zone possession; half-wall players are the two "wings" of a 1-3-1 power play.

**"Half-wall" is not "the point."** The point is the area just inside the blue line, not the line itself; the half-wall is level with the dot, roughly 20 ft from the goal line. Conflating them is a common error and it produces a real mistake — a defenceman drifting down to the half-wall has abandoned the blue line.

![](diagram:the-point-and-the-half-wall)

### The boards / the wall

**Where:** the perimeter of the rink.

**Why it matters:** the wall is both a weapon and a trap. As a weapon, it is a passing partner (bank passes, chips, rims, glass-and-out clears). As a trap, a player pinned against it has no escape on one side and can be sealed by a single forechecker. "**Get off the wall**" means stop battling and move the puck to open ice.

### The point

**Where:** the area just inside the blue line, toward each set of boards, where the defencemen set up in the offensive zone. Two of them: the **strong-side point** (puck side) and the **weak-side point**.

**Why it matters:** it is the top of the offensive-zone structure. From the point you can shoot for tips and rebounds, walk laterally to open a lane, or feed either half-wall. Defensively, "**covering the point**" means denying the opposing defenceman time and space up there — normally a winger's job in a zone system.

**Note the precision:** "the point" is an *area*, not the blue line itself, and it is not the same as "high". A forward who rotates high is not "at the point" unless they are actually occupying that spot.

### The top of the circle

**Where:** the edge of the faceoff circle closest to the blue line.

**Why it matters:** the top of the circle is the standard depth marker for a defenceman supporting a cycle. Picture three depths in the offensive zone — just inside the blue line (default), top of the circle (the activated position, still recoverable), and **below the circles** (a commitment: you are a fourth forward, you cannot recover, and a forward must have taken the blue line for you *before* you went). "Step down to the top of the circle" is a specific, bounded instruction, which is why coaches use it. [Offensive Zone Play](../systems/offensive_zone_play.md) sets out these three depths in full.

### The dots

**Where:** the four end-zone faceoff spots. Used loosely to mean the depth-line they define across the zone.

![](diagram:above-and-below-the-dots)

**Why it matters:** "**above the dots**" and "**below the dots**" is how coaches split the zone in half — though not into two equal halves: the end-zone dots sit 20 ft out from the goal line, so above the dots runs the 44 ft back to the blue line (64 ft − 20 ft) and below the dots runs the 31 ft on to the end boards (20 ft + 11 ft), the larger share on top. Unlike "high" and "low", which are always relative, the dots are painted, so once you know which end is meant, above and below them name a fixed line rather than a feeling. In a hybrid defensive system, for example, coverage is man-on-man **below the dots** and zone **above** them — one coaching choice among several, so find out which system your team plays; [Defensive Zone Coverage](../systems/defensive_zone_coverage.md) sets out the rest.


### Behind the net

**Where:** the strip of ice between the goal line and the end boards.

**Why it matters:** it is the safest place in your own zone to compose yourself, because a turnover there produces no direct shot. ⚠️ **Its middle is the far end of the goalkeeper's privileged area under USA Hockey — but not the corners either side**, which 607(d) Note 2's lines from the end-zone faceoff spots leave outside. Inside it, **Rule 607(c) writes no bare minor** for a deliberate body check or charge on a goalkeeper; accidental or unavoidable contact is interference instead (607(d) Note 1). ⚠️ **Outside it a goalkeeper *"can be legally checked"* — the *Casebook* at Rule 607, Situation 5 — and is still never *"fair game"*, so play the puck, not the goalie.** [Language and Glossary](language_and_glossary.md) sets that area out in full, under **Privileged area**. It is also a pivot point in the offensive zone: a puck carrier who goes behind the net can emerge on either side, and the defence has to commit. In your own end, **carry behind the net rather than through the slot** — and this one is not team-dependent: a turnover with the puck on your stick in your own slot is a goal against. The *pass* between partners is the one some teams take high in the zone instead; **D-to-D** is set out in [§7, D-partner and defence pairs](#d-partner-and-defence-pairs).

### "The office"

**Where:** behind and beside the opponent's net, in the offensive zone.

**Why it matters:** the name comes from Wayne Gretzky, who set up there because the defence cannot easily pressure a player behind the net without opening the slot. It is a phrase you will hear in commentary and it means "attacking possession behind the goal line" — not the same as the neutral space "behind the net" in your own end.

### The neutral-zone dots

**Where:** the four dots 5 ft outside each blue line, 44 ft apart.

**Why it matters:** they mark the standard lateral lanes for a neutral-zone setup. Defencemen defending the rush position roughly between them; forwards in a 1-2-2 neutral-zone forecheck align on them. Faceoffs land here after offside, after most neutral-zone stoppages, and after a whistle in the attacking zone.

### The bench area

**Where:** both players' benches on the same side of the neutral zone; penalty benches opposite.

**Why it matters:** the bench side is where you change safely. The far side is where lines get caught. If you are stranded on the far side and tired, stay in position and finish the shift — a bad change is worse than a long shift.

### The trapezoid, as an area of the end zone

**Where the trapezoid sits:** behind the net, between the goal line and the end boards. The markings, the dimensions, the penalty and its unsettled status in England and Wales are all in [§3, The trapezoid (goalkeeper's restricted area)](#the-trapezoid-goalkeepers-restricted-area); this entry is only its place on the map.

**Why it matters:** it defines which corners your goaltender can help you in — under NHL and IIHF rules, none of them. Aim your dump-ins outside it.

## 6. Directional and relational vocabulary

These words are used in every other document here. They are all **relative**, which is exactly what makes them hard at first and useful once they click.

### High and low

- **High** = closer to the blue line you are facing, away from the net.
- **Low** = closer to the goal line, toward the net.

This holds in **all three zones**, and it is always relative to the net that matters in that moment. "Swing low in your own zone" means come down toward your own goal line. "Rotate high" means climb toward the blue line.

Related: **"third forward high"** means the third forward stays in the upper part of the zone as a safety valve. **"Low-to-high"** describes a pass from the corner or half-wall out to the point — the setup for most point shots.

### Strong-side and weak-side

- **Strong-side** = the side of the ice the puck is currently on.
- **Weak-side** (also "**off-side**", "**back side**") = the opposite side.

**Nothing is painted to mark this split** — the boundary is simply the middle of the ice, wherever the puck currently is. These flip the instant the puck crosses the middle of the ice, which is why defencemen have to talk constantly: one is always the strong-side defender (on the puck) and the other the weak-side defender (at the goalmouth), and they trade those jobs several times a shift.

![](diagram:strong-side-and-weak-side)

The **backdoor** is the weak-side net-front area. What makes it dangerous is that a player arriving there is behind the goaltender's field of view — how it ranks against every other unmarked space is not something anyone has counted.

### Inside and outside

- **Inside** = toward the middle of the ice; also, "on the inside of" an opponent means between them and your net.
- **Outside** = toward the boards.

"**Get to the inside**" is a defensive instruction: put your body between your check and the net. "**Force them outside**" is the same idea applied to the puck carrier: take away the middle so the only route is to the wall — and both of those describe the route you skate and the lane you hold, not a licence to finish the play with a hit.

The middle of the ice is where goals come from, so the whole defensive game reduces to: **be inside, force them outside** — and the body that gets you there is the body you *place*, not the one you *throw*. Putting yourself between an opponent and the net is position; finishing the play by hitting them is a separate act, and body checking is barred at 12U and below and in all Girls'/Women's and non-check Adult classifications under **USA Hockey 604(a)**, at U13 and below, throughout female hockey and in *"any other divisions approved by a Member of Hockey Canada"* under **Hockey Canada 7.3(a)**, and in Britain in the IHUK junior leagues at U12 and below and in the women's NIHL at both levels. ⚠️ **Neither age line is the answer, because both lists are open at the end:** USA Hockey **604(a)** also lets a local governing body *"prohibit body checking in any classification"*, **604(b)** bars checking for both teams in any game where one side is in a no-check classification, and Hockey Canada's own scope sentence hands the rest of its divisions to its Members — so find out what your own league permits before you finish anything with a check.

⚠️ **The route is the half that travels between books. The contact is not.** Two books license the route in terms. **USA Hockey's** Glossary defines **angling** as *"[a] legal defensive skill used to direct/control the puck carrier to an area that closes the gap and/or creates an opening that is too small for the puck carrier to advance"*, and the Note to **Rule 604(c)** puts it inside legal contact even where checking is banned: competitive contact *"also occurs when a player has established an angle (Angling - see Glossary) on the opponent and closes the gap"*, and *"[l]egal competitive contact shall not be penalized under this rule."* **Hockey Canada's Interpretation 1 to Rule 7.3(a)** lets a player *"steer or direct an opposing player into the boards, without actually touching or body-checking them"* and gives the defender *"the right to close off the boards, in order to force the puck carrier to slow down or move towards the middle of the ice"* — but only *"as long as either player does not use their body to intentionally body-check, bump, push, or shove an opponent."* ⚠️ **Neither the NHL nor the IIHF book prints the word "angling" at all.** What they print instead is the *Body Position* paragraph of Rule **56.1** — in the NHL's words, a player *"is allowed the ice he is standing on (body position) and is not required to move in order to let an opponent proceed"*, and the IIHF's is the same sentence with plural pronouns. ⚠️ **The CARHA book grants that same permission in its own interference rule — and then adds, in a separate rule, a duty the *Body Position* paragraph does not.** The second Note to CARHA **Rule 66(a) Interference** says *"the defending players are entitled to 'stand their ground' or 'shadow' the attacking players"*. The separate rule is **Rule 49(a)**, *Boarding and Body Contact*, which assesses a minor or a major to a player who *"intentionally bodies, pushes, shoves, stands in front of an opponent for the purpose of making contact, and/or does not avert body contact with an opponent"* — while **49(c)** keeps genuinely accidental contact outside it, so a scramble for a loose puck is not what it is aimed at. ⚠️ **Read those two rules together rather than either alone**, because 49(a) on its own reads like a ban on standing still and is not one: in a CARHA-affiliated adult league you may hold your ground to take away a lane, but a seal you deliberately ride through is inside 49(a)'s words. ⚠️ **That the last limb amounts to a duty to get out of the way is a reading of the rule's grammar — CARHA does not gloss it** — and it binds CARHA-affiliated adult leagues rather than rec hockey in general, so ask your league whether CARHA is your book.

🇬🇧 **In women's hockey under the IIHF book, Rule 101.1 is a permission with a carve-out rather than a ban:** a bodycheck *"is allowed when there is a clear intention of playing the puck or attempting to 'gain possession' of the puck"*, but players competing for possession *"are not allowed to use the boards to make contact with an opponent to eliminate her from the play, push her into the boards, or pin her along the boards"*, and *"[a]ny move by a Player to step or glide into an opposing Player will be assessed at least a minor penalty"*. **Rule 101.1 also states the hold-your-ground entitlement in terms, and the one duty to avoid contact it imposes falls on the player who is moving rather than on the one standing still.** [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md#where-body-checking-is-and-is-not-permitted) owns whether you may check at all; [Switching Positions](../positions/switching_positions.md) and [Defender](../positions/defender.md) own the position shift to shift; [UK and England Rules](uk_rules.md) owns the British checking formats and names the competitions it does not cover.

### North-south and east-west

- **North-south** = up and down the ice, goal to goal. Direct, fast, low-risk.
- **East-west** = across the ice, boards to boards. Slower, higher-risk, but it moves the defence.

"A north-south player" plays direct and straight. "Too much east-west" is a criticism — passing sideways lets the defence reset. But east-west movement is also what beats a set defence, so the useful version is: **north-south to gain ice, east-west to break structure once you're there.**

### Defensive side of the puck, and above/below the puck

- **On the defensive side of the puck** = between the puck (or your check) and your own net. If an opponent is between you and your net, you are on the wrong side and cannot defend anything.
- **Above the puck** = between the puck and the blue line you are defending — i.e. in a position to stop a counter-attack.
- **Below the puck** = closer to the net than the puck is; committed, and unable to help against a rush the other way.

The working rule of thumb: **somebody stays above the puck.** In the offensive zone that is usually a defenceman at the point plus the high forward; in the defensive zone it is whoever has not committed to the battle. "Everyone below the puck" is the single most reliable way to concede an odd-man rush.

**It is a coaching convention rather than a law, and two things about it vary.** *Who* it is: under a five-man rotation the line is covered by whichever player happens to be highest rather than by a defenceman, so do not hear "above the puck" as "a defenceman" — see [Offensive Zone Play](../systems/offensive_zone_play.md). And *whether*, because benches suspend it outright on a called situation: on a full-ice press, a delayed penalty, or 6-on-5 with your own goaltender pulled, the safety is given up on purpose, because in each of those the counter-attack has stopped costing you anything. [Forechecking Systems](../systems/forechecking_systems.md) sets out those three and the reason for each. Outside them, assume the convention — and find out what your team calls.

### Wide and middle

- **Wide** = out near the boards, in the outside lane. "Beaten wide" means an attacker got around you on the outside.
- **Middle** = the central lane. "Middle drive" means a forward attacking the central lane on a rush; "take away the middle" means close that lane and force the play wide.

Note the asymmetry: **wide is safe for you and bad for them.** A shot from wide is a low-percentage shot; a pass to the middle from wide is a high-percentage play. That is why defenders force wide but keep a stick in the middle passing lane.

## 7. Positions and shorthand

### The six positions

| Code | Position | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **C** | Centre | Takes faceoffs, covers the middle in all three zones |
| **LW** | Left wing | Left-side forward |
| **RW** | Right wing | Right-side forward |
| **LD** | Left defence | Left-side defenceman |
| **RD** | Right defence | Right-side defenceman |
| **G** | Goaltender / goalie | The only player who may deliberately cover the puck to stop play — within limits that differ by book: a cover you did not need can be a minor. See [Goaltender](../positions/goaltender.md) |

**The centre is normally the most demanding defensive role among the forwards** — coaching craft rather than a measured comparison, and different coaches will tell you different things about it.

**The goaltender's permission to cover the puck is not unconditional, and every book limits it.** Under the **NHL** and **IIHF** it is a delay-of-game minor to freeze the puck outside the crease, and inside the crease a deliberate cover that causes a stoppage is still penalised *"unless he is actually being checked by an opponent"* — though no game is officiated on that literal reading. ⚠️ **But both books expressly allow the act that matters most in traffic:** come out to cut down the angle, make the save, and *then* cover the puck — *"this shall be legal"* (the Note to Rule 63.2(vi), and IIHF 63.2(VI) in the same words). **Hockey Canada** and **USA Hockey** each add a pressure test, but not the same one: USA Hockey's Rule 610(b) is a minor with no warning for freezing a puck you had the chance to play before being pressured, while Hockey Canada's reaches the crease only and comes *"after one warning"*. Out of the crease Hockey Canada turns flat — Interpretation 3 to Rule 10.1(a): *"they will not be allowed to freeze the puck. If they do, they will be assessed a Minor penalty. **No warning will be issued.**"* — while its second clause permits that same save-then-cover, and the book never says which of the two governs. **USA Hockey's Rule 614(c)** has four triggers, and only two of them turn on where you are: the puck behind the goal line with your body entirely outside the crease, and the puck outside the *"goalkeeper's privileged"* area. ⚠️ **Out of the crease but in front of the goal line and inside the privileged area, being pressured is what takes a cover out of the penalty rather than what puts it in — but pressure is not the whole test.** The line USA Hockey's *Casebook* draws is the **"act of playing goal"**: smothering a puck at your own net front with attackers coming is inside it, and racing an attacker to a loose puck and falling on it is not, because *"[t]he goalkeeper has an obligation to play the puck with their stick, just like any other player"* (Rule 614(c), Situation 6). **Save, not fetch** — and the fourth trigger, holding the puck against the goal frame, the boards or the netting, turns on neither pressure nor where on the ice you are. [Goaltender](../positions/goaltender.md) owns this and sets out how each one is actually enforced.

**Five skaters and a goaltender** per side at even strength. An NHL team dresses **20 players — 18 skaters and 2 goaltenders** (Rule 5.1), normally organised as four forward lines and three defence pairs.

Note that **"defender", "defenceman", "defenseman" and "D" all mean the same position** and all appear throughout, as do **"centre"** and **"center"**.

### F1 / F2 / F3 — roles, not people

**F1, F2 and F3 are jobs defined by order of arrival, not by position.** This is one of the most important pieces of shorthand in hockey and one of the most commonly misunderstood — coaching observation rather than a counted ranking.

- **F1** = the first forward to the puck. Pressures it.
- **F2** = the second forward in. Supports, seals the escape route F1 has taken away.
- **F3** = the third forward. Stays higher as the safety valve and covers the counter.

If the centre is usually F1 on a forecheck but a winger gets there first, **the winger is F1 and the centre takes the support role.** Nobody swaps positions; they swap jobs. Teams that treat F1 as "the centre's job" end up with two players chasing and nobody high.

![](diagram:forecheck-roles-rotate)

**The "third forward high"** names that same F3 job from the other direction: the shorthand for keeping F3 — the third forward to arrive — above the puck. Which body it is changes shift to shift, and how high F3 sits is a coaching choice — see [Forechecking Systems](../systems/forechecking_systems.md), which sets out where the third forward stands under each forecheck.

### D-partner and defence pairs

Your **D-partner** is the other defenceman on your shift. Pairs are usually kept together because the pair's whole job is a two-role rotation: one on the puck, one at the net front, trading continuously. **D-to-D** means a pass between partners. The usual instruction is to go behind your own net rather than through the slot — a coaching default, not a rule, and one some teams relax high in the zone.

### Forward lines

A **line** is a set of three forwards (LW, C, RW) who play together. Lines are numbered by role: the first line takes the most offensive minutes, the fourth is typically an energy and defensive-matchup line. **"Changing as a unit"** means the whole line goes off together, which is what keeps the structure intact.

### Left-shot, right-shot, and why a defenceman's handedness picks their side

A **left-shot** player holds the stick with the left hand low, so the puck sits on their left; a **right-shot** is the mirror. This is about which side of your body the puck lives on — it has nothing to do with whether you write with your left or right hand.

For defencemen, handedness normally determines the side:

- **A left-shot defenceman plays left defence; a right-shot plays right defence.** On your natural side, the puck is on the **inside** of your body — between you and the middle of the ice — when you face the play along your boards.
- **On your natural side, retrieving pucks behind your own net comes to your forehand**, which means you can pass out of the corner immediately rather than turning your back to the play.
- **On your natural side, D-to-D passes and shots from the point are on your forehand**, so you can move the puck across the line quickly and take a one-timer.
- **On your natural side, you can defend the wall with your body between the attacker and the middle**, with your stick in the passing lane.

Playing your **off-side** (a left-shot on right defence) is common and not a disaster — plenty of NHL defencemen do it, and it gives you a better angle for cutting to the middle and for defending pucks along the wall on your forehand. But your retrievals and D-to-D passes come to your backhand, which costs a beat. One study of NHL shot-location data found that pairs with each defenceman on their natural side out-performed same-handed pairs — 50.71% against 49.32% Corsi For. The effect is real but small, around one percentage point, and some analysts argue teams have gone too far in chasing it; [Game Management](../systems/game_management.md) owns that figure and its caveats. It is still why coaches build pairs left-shot/right-shot when they can.

For **forwards** the convention is looser. Many wingers deliberately play the **off wing** (a left-shot on right wing), because it puts the puck on the inside for cutting to the net and opens up the one-timer from the circle. On the power play, the off-wing half-wall is standard.

## Common Mistakes

- **Assuming "defensive zone" is a fixed end of the building.** It follows your team, and it swaps every period. Say "our end" and "their end" if there is any doubt.
- **Confusing the slot with the goalmouth.** They are two layers of coverage, and who owns each is a coaching choice rather than a law: under the low zone collapse assumed here, a defender who leaves the goalmouth to cover the slot has just left a tap-in unmarked; under man-on-man you go with your check, and the goalmouth belongs to whoever's check is standing in it.
- **Confusing the point with the half-wall.** The point is the area just inside the blue line, not the line itself; the half-wall is level with the dot — a little under **44 ft** apart, because the blue line is 64 ft from the goal line and the end-zone dots are 20 ft from it, and the point sits inside the line rather than on it. A defenceman drifting to the half-wall has abandoned the line.
- **Hearing "off-side" as one thing.** Four things share the word, and two of them are rule violations with different remedies: the zone-entry rule, your unnatural handedness side, the weak side of the ice, and lining up wrong at a faceoff.
- **Treating F1 as "the centre".** F1 is whoever arrives first. If two players both think they're F2, nobody pressures the puck.
- **Thinking of the trapezoid as the goalie's problem.** Where you have one, it is the defencemen's problem: it means nobody is coming to help you in the corner. Under the USA Hockey and Hockey Canada books there is no trapezoid at all, and in England and Wales its status is unsettled — so find out which book you are under first.
- **Learning "high" and "low" as absolute directions.** They are relative to the net that matters right now, and they invert when the puck changes ends.
- **Hearing "be inside, force them outside" as permission to hit.** It is an instruction about the route you skate and the lane you hold; the check that finishes it is a separate act, and body checking is barred at 12U and below and in all Girls'/Women's and non-check Adult classifications (USA Hockey 604(a)), at U13 and below, throughout female hockey and in *"any other divisions approved by a Member of Hockey Canada"* (Hockey Canada 7.3(a)), and in Britain in the IHUK junior leagues at U12 and below and in the women's NIHL — ⚠️ **which covers the leagues IHUK runs and not the EIHL, BUIHA or Scottish domestic competitions outside that structure; for those, ask your league before you hit anyone.** ⚠️ **And neither North American list is a closed one:** USA Hockey 604(a) lets a local governing body *"prohibit body checking in any classification"*, 604(b) bars checking for both teams in any game where one side is in a no-check classification, and Hockey Canada's scope sentence hands the rest of its divisions to its Members — so the age line does not settle it in either book. **The IIHF book does not treat women's hockey as a non-check format at all:** its Rule 101.1 allows the check where playing the puck is the clear object, while barring any use of the boards to eliminate or pin an opponent and any move to step or glide into one. Under the CARHA adult rec book — CARHA-affiliated adult leagues only, never a general rec-hockey standard — the line is drawn further back still, Rule 49(a) reaching a player who *"stands in front of an opponent for the purpose of making contact, and/or does not avert body contact with an opponent"*, though Rule 49(c) keeps genuinely accidental contact outside it. **That is about riding a seal through, not a ban on holding your ground** — the same book's Rule 66(a), second Note, grants the ground in terms, and that the limb amounts to a duty is a reading of the grammar CARHA does not gloss. Find out which book and which classification you are in before you use anything but your skates.
- **Assuming your rink is standard.** Before your first shift at a new rink check three things: how far it is from the goal line to the end boards, whether the trapezoid is painted, and how the puck comes off the corner boards on a rim.
- **Treating the red crease line as the safe side of the boundary.** Every book that settles the question in its rule text or glossary puts the line itself inside the crease — the IIHF at Rule 1.7, USA Hockey in the Note to 625(b) and in its Casebook, Hockey Canada in its Glossary, and CARHA in its Glossary entry *Creases*, though that book's own Rule 5(c) does not mention the lines. ⚠️ **It is the NHL's *rule text* that leaves it unsaid, and that is not the same as the NHL being silent:** its Reference Table 14 treats an attacker planted *"on the crease line"* as outside the crease and allows the goal — and the IIHF's own table says the same, against that book's own Rule 1.7. **So what stands on the safe side of the line is a pair of reference tables, against every book that answers the question in rule text or glossary — and that is not a margin to stand on.** Keep your skates off the red line, not merely out of the blue paint.
- **Quoting the wrong trapezoid width.** The current painted dimension is 22 ft at the goal line; the "18 ft / 6 ft from the post" number is pre-2014.
- **Hearing "the trapezoid isn't enforced here" as "our goalie can do what they like behind the net".** England Ice Hockey's Rule 22.3 suspends the **Restricted Area** and nothing else. **IIHF Rule 63.2(I) is a separate minor** and no British document held here amends it: a goalkeeper who *freezes* the puck to cause a stoppage is penalised anywhere *"outside of their goal crease area"* — the **crease**, not the trapezoid. Playing it out there may be free; sitting on it is not. Coming out to cut the angle, saving, and *then* covering stays legal.
- **Treating the crease as the whole of the goaltender's protected ice.** It is not, in any of the four books. Each says in terms that a goalkeeper is not *"fair game"* outside the area it names, and **what the crease decides is the tier, not whether you may hit them.** The **NHL** and the **IIHF** key their charging sentence on the crease (Rule 42.1 in both); **USA Hockey** keys Rule 607(c) on the larger *privileged area*, where it writes **no bare minor** for body checking or charging a goalkeeper; and **Hockey Canada 8.5(b)** keys on nothing at all, making a major plus a game misconduct mandatory for charging the goaltender wherever it happens.
- **Assuming a goalie who has left the crease to play the puck is fair game as long as you never touch them.** NHL and IIHF Rule 69.4 penalises an attacker whose *"deliberate actions"* prevent a goalkeeper who has already played the puck outside the crease from returning to it — *"may be penalized"* for goalkeeper interference — and that clause names no contact at all. **Hockey Canada's own goalkeeper-interference rule does turn on contact** — the Note to Interpretation 3 of Rule 8.5(a) says outright, *"No penalty would be assessed unless the attacking player's body or stick makes actual physical contact with the goaltender."* **But its ordinary interference rule sits underneath that and needs none**: a goaltender who has not played the puck is not in possession by Hockey Canada's own Glossary, so moving into his path is a minor under Hockey Canada 8.3(i) with no contact required. USA Hockey is the one book whose reach here is genuinely unsettled. Find out which book you're under, and do not assume any of them clears you for standing in a scrambling goalie's lane.

## Key Takeaways

1. **Zone names are relative to your team, not the building** — and they swap ends every period. "Our end / their end" is never ambiguous.
2. **High means toward the blue line you're facing; low means toward the goal line.** True in all three zones, and it inverts when the puck changes ends.
3. **Strong-side is wherever the puck is.** It flips several times a shift, which is why defence pairs have to talk constantly.
4. **The slot has layers.** High slot (dots to top of the circles), low slot (dots to the goalmouth), goalmouth (at the crease). Different jobs — and who owns each is a coaching choice rather than a law: under the low zone collapse assumed here the goalmouth belongs to the weak-side defenceman and the high slot to the centre, or to the weak-side winger covering down for them, while under man-on-man nobody holds either area and you go with your check.
5. **The point is the area just inside the blue line, not the line itself; the half-wall is level with the dot.** Never use them interchangeably.
6. **F1 / F2 / F3 are roles defined by order of arrival, not people.** Whoever gets there first is F1.
7. **The trapezoid runs 22 ft along the goal line, widening to 28 ft at the boards** (NHL Rule 1.8, never 27.8; IIHF 6.80 m to 8.60 m). Playing the puck outside it behind the goal line is a **two-minute delay-of-game minor**, and the rule text's one exception — a goalie keeping skate contact with the crease — **excuses playing the puck, never freezing it**, with two more in the IIHF's Situation Handbook, for a puck stopped on the goal line and for one that only deflects into you. It applies in the NHL and under the IIHF book — the KHL reportedly too, but unverified here — and in neither the USA Hockey nor the Hockey Canada book. **In England and Wales its status is unsettled:** England Ice Hockey's Rule 22.3 says it is *"not currently enforced"* and the current In-House Rules are silent, so check locally. Until you know, keep your own goalie inside it and plan as though theirs may come out.
8. **“No trapezoid” is not “no restriction”, and neither is “not currently enforced”.** The USA Hockey and Hockey Canada books both stop the goalkeeper dead at the **centre red line** — deliberately playing the puck beyond it is a minor under USA Hockey **407(c)** and Interference under Hockey Canada **4.13(c)** — and both judge that on the **goaltender's skates**, where NHL 27.7 and IIHF 27.6 judge it on the puck, so a reach back across the line for a puck still on your own side is legal in one pair of books and two minutes in the other. ⚠️ **Neither 407(c) nor 4.13(c) says the word, so look the skates test up in the right volume:** USA Hockey's is in the separate *Casebook* at **Rule 407, Situation 13**, Hockey Canada's in playing-rule text at **Rule 8.3(a)(ii)** and in **Interpretation 2 to Rule 4.13(c)**. Freezing is restricted out there too: USA Hockey Rule 614(c) turns on four triggers, only one of them pressure, and Hockey Canada's Interpretation 3 to Rule 10.1(a) bars it once the goaltender has left the crease — a minor, **no warning** — while its second clause permits a save-then-cover and the book never says which governs. ⚠️ **And England Ice Hockey's Rule 22.3 suspends the Restricted Area only.** IIHF Rule 63.2(I) is a separate minor, unamended in Britain, and it still penalises a goalkeeper who *freezes* the puck anywhere *"outside of their goal crease area"* — the crease, which is far smaller than the trapezoid. **The suspension frees where a goalie may *play* the puck, never where they may *freeze* it.** Coming out to cut down the angle, making the save and *then* covering the puck stays legal (IIHF Rule 63.2(VI)'s Note).
9. **The neutral zone is the same length on any full-size rink** (NHL 50 ft; IIHF 15.0 m). Note which of those two the books actually state: the IIHF figure is the rule book's own, from the Appendix VI "Rink Lines" drawing, which marks the neutral zone *"¼ L = 1500"* against a stated *"Length (L) = 6000"* in centimetres. **The NHL's 50 ft is nowhere stated as a figure in the rule text** — it is a derivation from the 200 ft sheet of Rule 1.2 and Rule 1.5's 11 ft goal line and 64 ft blue line (200 − 2 × 75). What changes on big international ice is the width, which is why wide ice punishes forechecks and rewards skating. On a short sheet — which the 2026-27 In-House Rules authorise in *"all fixtures"* across the Home Countries, though no census of British rinks has been published — the neutral zone shrinks with the building.
10. **Somebody stays above the puck — a coaching convention, not a law.** Which body it is changes, and under a five-man rotation it is whichever player is highest rather than a defenceman. Benches also suspend it deliberately on a called situation — a full-ice press, a delayed penalty, or 6-on-5 with your goaltender pulled. Outside those, "everyone below the puck" is the most reliable way to concede an odd-man rush.
11. **Be inside, force them outside — with your feet and your position, never as a licence to hit.** The whole defensive game compresses into that — and what does not travel between books is the contact that finishes it: body checking is barred at 12U and below and in all Girls'/Women's and non-check Adult classifications under **USA Hockey 604(a)**, at U13 and below, throughout female hockey and in *"any other divisions approved by a Member of Hockey Canada"* under **Hockey Canada 7.3(a)**, and in Britain in the IHUK junior leagues at U12 and below and in the women's NIHL at both levels — ⚠️ **a list covering the leagues IHUK runs and no others, so for the EIHL, BUIHA, NIHL National or Scottish domestic hockey, ask your league before you hit anyone.** ⚠️ **Neither North American list is a closed one:** USA Hockey **604(a)** lets a local governing body *"prohibit body checking in any classification"*, **604(b)** bars checking for both teams in any game where one side is in a no-check classification, and Hockey Canada hands its remaining divisions to its Members. ⚠️ **Holding your ground, by contrast, travels between every book named here but one.** The NHL says at **Rule 56.1** that a player *"is not required to move in order to let an opponent proceed"*, and the IIHF book, USA Hockey's Glossary entry *Collision* and the second Note to CARHA's **Rule 66(a)** say the same — ⚠️ **but Hockey Canada writes no equivalent to it — its only other body-position language, Rule 8.1 (Holding), permits arm-blocking specifically and says nothing about standing your ground against an oncoming skater, so do not hold a lane in a Hockey Canada game on a permission it never grants.** What **CARHA's Rule 49(a)** adds on top is a limit none of the other three carries, reaching a player who *"stands in front of an opponent for the purpose of making contact, and/or does not avert body contact with an opponent"* — CARHA-affiliated adult leagues only, with Rule 49(c) keeping accidental contact outside it, and that the limb amounts to a duty is a reading of the grammar CARHA does not gloss. 🇬🇧 **The IIHF book, meanwhile, does not treat women's hockey as a non-check format:** Rule 101.1 allows the check where playing the puck is the clear object, but bars using the boards to eliminate or pin an opponent, and any step or glide into one. **Find out which book you are under and what your own league permits before you finish anything with a check.**
12. **The goalkeeper's privileged area is USA Hockey's alone, and it is not the crease.** Rule 607(d) Note 2 runs it from the end-zone faceoff spots straight back to the end boards, so it takes in the net front, the goal line and the ice behind the net, but not the corners. Inside it, **Rule 607(c) writes no bare minor** for body checking or charging a goalkeeper — it is a minor plus a misconduct, or a major plus a game misconduct, with a match penalty available under 607(e). ⚠️ **Not every touch is that rule** — 607(d) Note 1 sends *"any accidental or unavoidable contact"* to interference, a 625(a)(8) minor, and reserves 607(c) for *"deliberate body contact or check"*. The NHL, IIHF and Hockey Canada books have no privileged area at all — ⚠️ **which is not the same as no protection outside the crease.** All four say a goalkeeper is not *"fair game"* out there, and **Hockey Canada 8.5 is the widest of the four**, calling a penalty for unnecessary contact with the goaltender *"anywhere on the ice."* **A goalie who has come out to the wall or the corner for a puck is not free to hit — and the one book that says in terms they *"can be legally checked"* out there, USA Hockey's *Casebook* at 607 Situation 5, penalises any avoidable contact in the same answer. Play the puck, not the goalie.**
13. **The red crease line is part of the crease in every book that settles the question — so keep your skates off the line, not merely out of the blue paint.** The IIHF (Rule 1.7), USA Hockey (the Note to 625(b) and its Casebook), Hockey Canada and CARHA all count the line in; the NHL's rule text leaves it unsaid and its **Reference Table 14** answers the other way, as the PWHL's copy of that table does. Staying off the line is the only position safe whichever book you are under, and a net-front player standing on it can cost their team the goal.
