## Key focus

**Treat the bench as a working position.** Know the score, the clock and the situation before you step over, and watch who is on the ice for them. Sit in line order so your line changes as a unit.

**Change while you still have legs, and name the person you are replacing out loud.** Never go while they have the puck in your end, or when you are the last player back. Two players claiming one spot is how you get too many men.

**When a teammate leaves their spot to go after the puck, fill it — and when somebody has filled yours, get back to it.** That exchange is what trust looks like on the ice. Which spot you fill is your team's system, so find out what yours plays.

**Talk early on the ice, and use the bench to say what you will do.** Call "man on" in your team's words, even when you are not sure. Between shifts, say one specific thing — "I'm coming low on the breakout" — and never anything evaluative.

**Adapt to your linemates; they will not adapt to you.** Learn where each one wants the puck and which way they turn under pressure. Then pass to the player who exists, not the one you wish you had.

**Be where you said you would be, every shift.** A predictable teammate beats a brilliant unpredictable one. Play the system even when you would prefer another, and if you change what you do, say so on the bench first.

**Keep giving a struggling teammate the puck, and give them an easy one first.** Cutting someone out confirms what they already fear. Show frustration at your own errors at most, and at a teammate's never.

**Never retaliate, never go over the boards during an altercation, and stay in the penalty box until an official releases you — even if your time is up.** Retaliation is what escalates. If you are nearest the door, say the name of whoever is halfway over it.

**Say nothing to the referee; tell your captain, and win the next faceoff.** Arguing almost never reverses a call, and most books penalise the dispute itself.

**When someone is down and not getting up, stop playing and wave for the officials. With a head or neck injury, phone for medical help, do not move them except to keep their airway clear, and leave their helmet on.** Assume a possible spinal injury. One person keeps them still; somebody else phones.

## Overview

Hockey changes players during live play, out of a roster you did not pick. Almost everything you do depends on somebody you cannot see doing what you expected. That is why team culture shows up in the score, and why reliability matters more than skill.

**Trust is a prediction, not a feeling.** A defenceman who passes to a spot is betting on where the winger will be. When trust breaks, players stop leaving their own job to cover someone else's, because they no longer believe the cover will come back. Filling a teammate's spot, and returning to yours, keeps that exchange alive. Talking early keeps it informed — and a bench that mocks a wrong call is how a team goes quiet.

**The bench is a working position.** Too many men on the ice is usually two players believing the same spot is theirs, which is why you name who you are replacing. Step on only once that player is at the bench **and out of the play**. And do not ice the puck to get a tired line off: in many leagues, British league hockey included, a team that ices it cannot change. Get it out under control instead; [Rules Primer](../foundation/rules_primer.md#the-consequences) has which books bar the change.

**Discipline is a team skill.** Arguing with the referee never reverses a call and is a penalty in itself in most books. Retaliating, and going over the boards for a teammate, are the same instinct at two scales: they add you to the penalty and help nobody. In every book here the first player off a bench for a fight is thrown out of the game — in four, for any altercation — and none stops at the first. Agree as a team, before the season, that nobody leaves.

**The injured-player convention is the one where getting it wrong is permanent.** With a head or neck injury: **call for immediate medical help, do not move them except to keep their airway clear, and leave their helmet on.** Assume a possible spinal injury. Waving at the bench summons your teammates, not an ambulance, and in a rec game nobody trained may be at the rink — so make sure somebody has actually phoned.

**Almost everything else a team expects of you is custom rather than rule** — keep giving a struggling teammate the puck, never show frustration at one, and copy your team's most respected players. Never quote a custom to a referee as though it were a rule. ⚠️ **But the handshake line is priced: keep your helmet on, keep your hands off an opponent's, and if it goes up, back off and let the officials in first.** The section on it below sets out what each book charges.

## Why This Matters Practically, Not Sentimentally

**In short:** When a teammate leaves their spot, fill it, and get back to yours quickly so the next cover is worth making.

Skip the poster slogans. There is a mechanical reason culture shows up in the score.

Hockey is played five skaters at a time out of a roster of **twenty** in the NHL — eighteen skaters and two goalies — and typically twelve to twenty in rec leagues. Shifts last **roughly 30 to 80 seconds** ([Conditioning and Recovery](conditioning_and_recovery.md) covers that figure; a well-managed one is nearer 30 to 45), and players change **during live play**. That has two consequences no other major team sport shares to the same degree:

- **You are constantly playing with people you did not choose and cannot see.** A defenceman starting a breakout has to release the puck to a spot before the winger arrives at it. That pass is a prediction. It is only a good prediction if you know what that particular winger does. See [Passing and Receiving](../technique/passing_and_receiving.md) and [Puck Support and Spacing](../hockey-iq/puck_support_and_spacing.md).
- **Every shift is an act of delegation.** When you leave the ice, five different people inherit your job. When you go to the corner, someone else has to take the slot you vacated.

So "trust" in hockey is not a feeling. It is a **prediction accuracy problem**. And distrust has a specific, visible symptom: **players stop leaving their own assignment to cover someone else's**, because they no longer believe the cover will be returned.

What that looks like on the ice:

- **The weak-side winger stops dropping into the high slot** when the centre goes to the corner, because last time they did it, the centre didn't come back and the winger got blamed for the goal. Under this guide's standard low zone collapse, that rotation is the whole coverage. Without it you concede slot shots. See [Defensive Zone Coverage](../systems/defensive_zone_coverage.md).
- **Defencemen stop pinching**, because they don't believe the third forward will rotate back to cover the blue line. A team that never pinches never sustains offensive-zone pressure. See [Offensive Zone Play](../systems/offensive_zone_play.md).
- **Nobody talks.** Calling "man on" or "reverse" requires a small act of exposure — you might be wrong. On a bench where being wrong gets you mocked, players go quiet, and the goals that follow are the ones nobody called. See [On-Ice Communication](../foundation/on_ice_communication.md).
- **Shifts get longer.** Players who don't trust the next line to hold on stay out an extra twenty seconds, arrive at the bench exhausted, and are still tired when they go back over the boards.

None of that requires anybody to dislike anybody. It just requires uncertainty. **A team that trusts each other plays a structurally different, more aggressive, higher-support game than a team of equal skill that doesn't.**

**So the thing to do on the ice is keep the exchange going.** When a teammate leaves their spot to go after the puck, fill it; when somebody has filled yours, get back to it as soon as the play lets you, so the next cover is worth making. Which spot you fill is set by your team's system — the rotations in [Defensive Zone Coverage](../systems/defensive_zone_coverage.md) and [Offensive Zone Play](../systems/offensive_zone_play.md) are one version, and yours may differ — so find out what your team plays.

## The Bench

The bench is where most of a game happens to you. Most new players treat it as a rest area. It is a working position.

### Where to sit and why

**Convention, not rule — but a near-universal one.** Nothing in any rulebook says where you sit; the NHL rulebook specifies only the bench itself.

**Sit in line order.** Forwards sit grouped by line, defencemen usually at one end (commonly the end nearest the door the defence uses). Your centre and two wingers are the people either side of you.

The convention exists because:

- **The change happens as a unit.** All three forwards go over the boards from adjacent positions and arrive at the play together rather than trickling on.
- **You can talk to the people you play with** between shifts, which is the only time you can have an actual conversation.
- **The coach can address one line** without shouting down the length of a bench. NHL benches are specified at twenty-four feet long with seating for at least fourteen (NHL Rule 3.1) — that is a long way to shout.

If you are new and don't know where to sit, **ask**. Sitting in someone's spot is a trivial offence; guessing silently and being in the wrong place for a change is not.

### Staying alert

Three things you should be doing every second you are on the bench:

1. **Watch the puck and the clock.** You need to know the game situation *before* you step on — score, time, whether you're on a penalty kill, whether the faceoff is coming in your own end. See [Game Management](../systems/game_management.md).
2. **Watch your matchup.** Who is on the ice for them? Are they changing? Who came off their bench last? This is the raw material of a change on the fly.
3. **Watch your own line's shift when you're not on it.** You are learning your teammates' tendencies for free.

**Do not sit on the boards.** This one *is* a rule: the NHL, the IIHF and the PWHL make sitting on the boards with your skates exposed during play an unsportsmanlike minor, after one warning per team per game. Coaches also flag it as a way an exposed blade ends up near someone's face. No published source measures that as an injury mechanism, but the caution is worth carrying anyway.

**Do not stand up and reach onto the ice.** Playing the puck from the bench, or interfering with it, is a penalty, and it is one of those things that only ever happens to people who weren't paying attention.

### Being ready at the door

Benches have **two doors**. ⚠️ **Only the NHL book requires them to swing inward.** The IIHF requires two doors but says nothing about the swing, and the other books write no door rule at all — so in a rink you did not build, look before you lean on one. Most benches use one door for "on" and one for "off", but which is which varies by rink and team — **find out at your first game, don't guess.**

When your line is next up:

- **Get to the door before you need to.** Standing, gloves on, stick in hand, weight forward.
- **Know who you are replacing.** Not "a forward" — a specific person. This is what prevents too many men.
- **Watch that player, not the puck**, in the last few seconds. You are waiting for them to break for the bench.

### The mechanics of a change on the fly

**In short:** Skate straight to the bench, and step on only as your man arrives there and is out of the play.

This is the part the rules govern, and an honest mistake costs a two-minute bench penalty.

**There are two conditions on the player coming off, not one.** They must be **at the bench** *and* **out of the play** before the replacement steps on. Most players remember the first and forget the second, and the second is the one that gets called.

What that means for you, in order:

1. **The call comes.** Either from the ice ("change!") or from the bench. See the bench and line-change call list in [On-Ice Communication](../foundation/on_ice_communication.md).
2. **The player coming off skates directly to the bench** — no coasting, no last look at the puck. Whatever limit your book writes, it punishes hesitation.
3. **The player going on waits at the door**, watching their man arrive, and steps on *as* they arrive, not before.
4. **Announce it.** "I've got Sam", "I'm coming", "last man". This stops two people believing the same spot is theirs. **How much of the too-many-men total that failure accounts for is not a figure this guide has a source for** — say it because it is free.

**When not to change** matters more than how. Never change when the opposition has the puck in your zone, when you are the last defender back, or after your own team has iced the puck. When your goalie comes off for an extra attacker, the skater waits until the goalie is almost at the bench — and the goalie keeps going.

**After your own icing you may not change** under NHL Rule 81.4, IIHF Rule 81.4 and PWHL Rule 83.4 — except to replace an injured player, to put your goalie back after pulling them, or when a penalty changes either team's strength. The tired five stay out for a defensive-zone draw, and the same bar follows delaying the game. 🇬🇧 **It holds in British league hockey, which plays the IIHF book.** **USA Hockey and CARHA impose no bar after an icing**, and **Hockey Canada applies it only in U18 AAA, Junior and, at a Member's option, Senior** (Rule 6.1(f)).

**The IIHF adds a fourth exception — *"a Player with a broken skate"* — at Rule 81.4 but not at Rule 82.1, and which one controls has not been published.** Do not plan a change around it: if your blade goes on an icing, tell the official and be ready to stay out. ⚠️ **Never put a replacement over the boards on it** — the officials send them back and warn your coach, and **that warning is one per team per game**, so a bench that has already spent it gets a bench minor for delaying the game.

**The on-ice five is fixed when the puck leaves the offending player's stick.** [Game Management](../systems/game_management.md) covers it, and [Rules Primer](../foundation/rules_primer.md#the-consequences) has the rest.

**What it costs.**

- **"At the bench" is five feet (1.50 m) in the NHL, IIHF and PWHL books, and twice that in Hockey Canada and CARHA.** ⚠️ **USA Hockey writes no distance at all**, only *"at the players' bench and out of the play"* — **and its Rule 204(b) adds a trigger the NHL does not have.** A change that *"causes their team to gain a competitive advantage"* is too many players even if nobody touched the puck, so the change that turned a 2-on-1 against into a 2-on-2 **is** a penalty there. [Rules Primer](../foundation/rules_primer.md#changing-on-the-fly) has the detail.
- **A deliberate extra player late in the game is a penalty shot, not two minutes, in every book here** (NHL and IIHF Rule 74.4, PWHL Rule 76.4). USA Hockey Rule 204(d), Hockey Canada Rule 10.7(a) and CARHA Rule 19(f) tie it to the last two minutes and overtime, and CARHA also needs the extra player to play the puck or check someone on purpose. ⚠️ **An honest botched change in the last minute is still the ordinary two minutes. Sending a sixth body over the boards to break up a rush is a penalty shot against you.**
- **A mistimed goalie change is a stoppage and a faceoff, not a penalty, in every book here** — provided the goalie keeps going (NHL and IIHF Rule 71.1, PWHL Rule 73.1, USA Hockey Rule 205(a), Hockey Canada Rules 5.3(a)(x) and 6.3(c)(v), CARHA Rule 19's third note). ⚠️ **But a goalie who turns back to play the puck after the skater is on costs you too many players** under the NHL, IIHF and PWHL books, USA Hockey's Casebook and Hockey Canada — and Hockey Canada says the distance from the bench then stops helping. **Go to the bench and keep going.**

### Gate and door etiquette

Conventions, not rules, and they vary by rink:

- **Whoever is nearest the door works it.** In most beer leagues there is no dedicated door operator, so the player sitting closest opens and closes it. If that's you, that's your job for the shift, and it means you are watching the ice rather than your phone.
- **Hold the door for the player coming off**, then close it. An open door during play is a hazard and, in some rinks, a delay-of-game conversation with the referee.
- **Don't block the door.** Standing in the gateway "getting ready" while someone is trying to come off is the single most common bench annoyance in amateur hockey.
- **Sticks off the floor at the door.** Someone will step on yours and snap it, and it will be your fault.

### Changes at stoppages

Different mechanism entirely. At a whistle the **visiting team puts its line on the ice first**, and the home team answers — which is the whole of what "last change" means, and why the home coach gets to match their line against yours. The windows are short, and the exact seconds differ by book: the NHL gives the visitors five and the home side eight, USA Hockey gives five to each. ⚠️ **Last change is not unconditional** — the home team loses it after an icing **it** committed. See [Rules Primer](../foundation/rules_primer.md#line-changes-at-a-stoppage).

Most rec leagues do not run this procedure formally at all; if yours doesn't, your only matchup tool is the change on the fly. See [Game Management](../systems/game_management.md).

## Being a Good Linemate

### Know their tendencies, and adapt to them

Everything below is convention and craft, not rule.

Every player has a small set of things they reliably do. Your job is to learn your linemates' set and **pass to the player who exists**, not the player you wish you had.

Specific things worth knowing about each linemate:

- **Where do they want the puck?** Skates, or out in front? Forehand or backhand side? A player who takes everything on their forehand needs the puck led differently from one who catches it in their feet and protects it.
- **Do they hold or move it?** A holder needs you to get open twice. A mover needs you moving already.
- **Do they go to the net or stop at the top of the circle?** This changes whether your rim from the corner should be hard around the boards or soft to the net-front area.
- **How fast do they actually skate?** Not how fast they think they skate. A pass into space is only good if they get there.
- **Which way do they turn under pressure?** Almost everyone has a preferred escape direction.
- **On the backcheck, do they take the middle or their man?** This decides what *you* have to take.

**The adaptation runs from you to them, not the other way.** This is the whole point and it is where most players get it wrong. You cannot make a linemate change their game mid-season. You can change what you give them tomorrow. If you find yourself explaining, repeatedly, that they should have been somewhere else, you have chosen the impossible half of the problem.

### Talk before the shift, not after

The bench, between shifts, is the highest-value communication window in hockey — you are stationary, the whistle isn't blowing, and the opposition can't hear you.

Useful things to say in ten seconds:

- "On the breakout I'm coming low, not to the wall."
- "Their D-man is stepping up at the line every time — I'll chip it behind them."
- "If I've got it in the corner, get to the net, I'm not passing it back out." Whoever goes arrives under control, head up, and pulls up short of the goalie.
- "Their number 8 is quick, I'll take them back." On the backcheck, play the puck, not the player. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call; never hit anyone who can't see you coming, never from behind, and if you can see a player's numbers, pull up.

Useless things to say: anything evaluative. "You need to be sharper" is not information.

### The value of consistency

**A predictable teammate is worth more than a brilliant unpredictable one**, and this follows directly from the mechanics described at the top of this document. If passing is prediction, then variance is the enemy. A winger who is in the same place every time the team runs a given breakout lets the defenceman release the puck early, under pressure, without looking. A winger who is somewhere different every time forces the defenceman to look up first — and looking up first is how you get hit and turn the puck over.

The practical version: **decide what you do, and then do it every time.** If you support the wall on the breakout, always support the wall. If you change your mind, say so on the bench first.

## Line and Defence-Pair Chemistry

### What it actually is

Chemistry gets talked about mystically. Stripped down, it is two things:

1. **Complementary tendencies.** The pieces fit. A puck-carrying centre and a winger who gets open beats two of either. A defenceman who wants to activate and one who is happy to stay home covers both jobs; two activators leave the blue line empty.
2. **Shared assumptions.** All three players believe the same thing about what happens next. When the puck goes into the far corner, everyone already knows who is first in, who supports, and who stays high at the top of the zone — without anyone deciding. **"High" there means the third forward holding the top of the offensive zone behind the play**, which is a different job from the defensive-zone winger who "stays high" on the opposing point. F1, F2 and F3 are **roles defined by order of arrival, not by position**, and a line with chemistry sorts those roles out silently and instantly. See [Forechecking Systems](../systems/forechecking_systems.md).

**It is mostly not friendship.** Plenty of effective NHL lines are made of players who don't socialise, and plenty of best friends can't play together. Friendship helps with the *communication* that builds shared assumptions, which is a real but indirect contribution.

### How it develops

- **Repetition.** Chemistry is learned tendencies, and tendencies are learned by watching. This is why coaches keep lines together through a rough patch, and why constant "line blender" juggling has a cost even when each individual pairing looks sensible on paper.
- **Talking about specific, recurring situations** — the breakout, the forecheck entry, the net-front assignment — rather than about hockey in general.
- **Watching your own shifts back**, if your league films games.

### Why some pairings don't work

- **Duplicated tendencies.** Three players who all want to carry the puck through the neutral zone; two defencemen who both want to jump into the rush.
- **Mismatched pace.** A player who plays north fast paired with a player who wants to slow it down and cycle will spend the whole shift a half-second out of phase.
- **Incompatible risk appetite.** One player forcing low-percentage plays through the middle while their partner is trying to manage the game conservatively is a recipe for both being angry. See [Risk Management](../hockey-iq/risk_management.md).

On defence pairs specifically, **handedness** matters, but less than people claim, and it is about the first pass out rather than the shot from the point — see the treatment in [Game Management](../systems/game_management.md).

**If a pairing isn't working, that is information, not a verdict on anyone's character.** Say it to the coach that way.

## The Dressing Room

All convention. It varies enormously — a junior team, a men's league team and a mixed-ability drop-in have different norms, and a rink in Helsinki has different norms from one in Toronto.

**If this is your first time in one**, [Getting Started](../getting-started/getting_started.md) walks through the practical mechanics of a first session — when to arrive, what to bring, how a drill line works — and points back here for the conventions themselves.

### Arriving on time

**"On time" in hockey means dressed and ready before the stated time, not arriving at it.** The widespread convention is to arrive **45 to 60 minutes before a game** and **20 to 30 minutes before a practice**, because you need to get boots off, gear on, tape done, and be on the ice at the first second of a rented, expensive, immovable ice slot.

There is no rule anywhere that says this. There is simply a coach who will notice, and a bench that will notice sooner.

If you are going to be late or absent, **tell someone** — the ice time is paid for and rosters may be tight. Repeated silent no-shows are the fastest way to become unwelcome at any level.

### The dressing order

Most teams have **assigned or habitual stalls**. Common patterns: goalies at the end, defence together, forwards by line, veterans in the corners. Some teams deliberately mix so cliques don't form.

**Rules of thumb if you are new:** take a spot nobody has claimed, ideally near the door, and ask if it's someone's. If the room is small, keep your bag under the bench and your gear on your hooks — sprawling into your neighbour's stall is the room's version of a bad change.

### Noise and music

- **Music is normal**, and there is normally one person whose job it is by long habit. Don't take over the speaker in your first month.
- **The volume and mood convention varies wildly by level.** Loud and joking is standard in beer league. Many competitive teams go quiet for the last ten minutes before the ice.
- **Goalies often want to be left alone** to get their head right in the last stretch before warm-up. This is one of the most consistent conventions in hockey and one of the most consistently violated by new players trying to be friendly. See [Mental Game](mental_game.md).

### A new player's first few sessions

- **Say hello to the room when you walk in.** One sentence. It costs nothing and its absence is noticed.
- **Listen more than you talk.** The first three weeks are for finding out how this team does things.
- **Don't critique anything.** Not the systems, not the line combinations, not the goalie's rebound control, however right you are.
- **Learn names fast.** You cannot make the calls in [On-Ice Communication](../foundation/on_ice_communication.md) without them, and "hey!" doesn't identify anybody.

## Unwritten Conventions of the Game Itself

**Everything in this section is convention, not rule, unless explicitly cited.** These practices differ between North America and Europe, between age groups, between competitive and recreational levels, and between individual leagues. Treat this as a description of what is common, not a code you can hold anyone to.

### The handshake line

**In short:** Go down the line with your helmet on and fastened, say "good game" and nothing else, and back off if it goes up.

**What to do:** helmet on and done up properly, gloves and stick left at the bench, shake everyone's hand, say "good game", and keep moving. Say nothing else, and keep your hands to yourself. ⚠️ **If it goes up, back off and let the officials in first**, however obvious it looks that somebody should pull them apart. If you are watching from the bench, stay on it. If you lost badly or there was an incident, this is exactly when doing it well matters.

**Check your chin strap before you go down the line.** USA Hockey's Casebook says that if more than one finger fits between your jaw and the strap, it is not being properly worn (Rule 615, Situation 19).

The line is widely observed after almost every game in youth and much of recreational hockey. In the NHL it is **essentially a playoff custom**, after a series ends, not after regular-season games. ⚠️ **Nothing cited here establishes the NHL half of that, though** — no NHL source stating the regular-season position either way was found for this document.

**The handshake itself is not a rule.** The USA Hockey Casebook calls it a *"common practice"* that a referee may call off after a rough game: *"The safety of the players and preventative measures should be the priority"* (USA Hockey Casebook 2025-29, Officials, Situation 2, rule reference 502(a)). The line is called off to keep two teams apart, not to punish them.

**What it costs.** The handshake is custom, but how you go through it is priced, and the game is not over for the rulebook. Under USA Hockey, any game misconduct below also costs your next game (404(b)), and a second major in one game at least two:

- **Bare-headed in the line.** 🇬🇧 **Under IHUK, in all four Home Countries, it is a misconduct** *"regardless of league/age group"* (England Ice Hockey Rule 24.13, and the In-House Rules). ⚠️ **That is the sanctioning body's rule, so it does not follow you into an Elite League fixture** — though the EIHL Casebook lets referees call penalties *"until 30 minutes after the end of the game"*. England Ice Hockey Rule 24.12 also makes equipment left on the ice after the buzzer a misconduct if you refuse to move it.
- **Outside Britain, two books reach it without naming the line.** Hockey Canada (3.6) and CARHA (24(b)) require a helmet *"[w]hile on the ice"*, so taking it off for the line is an equipment offence — a minor under Hockey Canada, whose officials can still penalise after the game until the players reach their dressing rooms. ⚠️ **The NHL, PWHL and IIHF scope their helmet rule to the game and warm-up. USA Hockey's 304(c) has no time limit but exempts adults**, so for a youth player the question is open there — and only while the line stays peaceful.
- **Taking your own helmet off in an altercation.** The NHL, PWHL and IIHF make deliberate removal before a fight an unsportsmanlike minor, and do not penalise a helmet that simply falls off. Hockey Canada makes deliberately removing it, or undoing your chin strap so it comes off, in a fight a gross misconduct (10.6(f)(iii)), and undoing it to challenge an opponent a misconduct (10.6(d)). ⚠️ **USA Hockey 615(c) is a match penalty at every age for deliberately removing your helmet before an altercation — and an altercation there is any confrontation that draws a penalty, not only a fight.** A shoving match in the line that draws one penalty counts.
- **Hands off an opponent's head.** Under USA Hockey, removing an opponent's helmet before or during an altercation is ⚠️ **a match penalty at every age** (both match penalties if you do both), and causing it to come off is a game misconduct plus a two-game suspension. Hockey Canada makes removing another player's helmet, or undoing their chin strap, in a fight a gross misconduct (10.6(f)(iv)). Grabbing their helmet or cage is a double minor under Hockey Canada and CARHA, or a match penalty if you use it to gain an advantage or punish them (Hockey Canada Interpretation 15, CARHA 48(d)). Hockey Canada can make it a major and game misconduct depending on how violent it is, and must if it injures them (7.1(b)). A match in either book keeps you out of further games until your case is dealt with (Hockey Canada 4.10(a), CARHA 34(a)). The NHL, IIHF and PWHL penalise it too: usually a minor, though the PWHL makes grabbing a helmet, cage or chin strap a major and game misconduct, and it can go higher in all three if it is violent or hurts them.
- **A loosely worn helmet.** ⚠️ **USA Hockey makes it a game misconduct if your helmet is not worn as designed and comes off in an altercation**, even when an opponent pulled it. A properly worn helmet that comes off through no action of yours is not penalised (Situation 15). CARHA names no such rule.
- **What a USA Hockey match penalty costs.** ⚠️ **You leave the game and are suspended from all USA Hockey games and practices until your case is dealt with** (Rule 405). The hearing is offered within 30 days, a suspension is at least one game, and you are reinstated automatically after 30 days if no hearing could be held — though one can still be held later. The discipline policy recommends three to five games for helmet removal.
- **Fighting in the line.** A fight is a major plus a game misconduct under USA Hockey (615(a)), Hockey Canada (7.10(b)) and the IIHF (46.8, outside the periods; 46.9 in 2025/26). CARHA gives the instigator or aggressor a match penalty and suspends anyone with a fighting penalty (59(a), 59(d)); after the game the NHL and PWHL make it a major plus a game misconduct too (46.9), though the instigator's opponent keeps only the major.
- **Going in to separate them.** Every book here prices the peacemaker. USA Hockey 615(d) gives a game misconduct to the first player into any altercation, peacemaker or not, and two players arriving together can both be "first" (Situation 24, Situation 22). Hockey Canada (7.10(e)(ii)) and CARHA (59(b)) make it a mandatory game misconduct once there is a fight. The NHL (46.16), IIHF (46.7; 46.14 in its 2026/27 book) and PWHL (46.15) leave it to the referee. ⚠️ **No book here makes it free.**
- **When the referee sends you somewhere, go.** USA Hockey gives a minor for not going straight to the bench when told during an altercation (615(e)), and another for dropping your stick or gloves if you were not in the original altercation (615(a)).
- **Coming off the bench.** USA Hockey's major plus game misconduct for leaving a bench during an altercation (629(a)) has no time limit, and fighting off the playing surface is a major plus a game misconduct *"before, during or after the game"* (615(b)). **Coming over the boards costs the same after the handshake as in the first period.**
- **Your mouth.** USA Hockey makes abusive language after the game a game misconduct (601(d)(10)) — harsher than a misconduct for the same words during it — and disputing, taunting or inciting after the game starts at a misconduct. ⚠️ **Hateful or discriminatory language is a match penalty anywhere in the rink, before, during or after the game** (601(e)(3)), with a recommended three-to-five-game suspension that the authorities may increase, or require a hearing.
- **The shove.** Under USA Hockey avoidable contact after the whistle is a minor (640(c)), and a major plus a game misconduct if it recklessly endangers an opponent (640(g)) — and if it does, plan on a match (602(a)).

### Not running up the score

There is **no rule against scoring goals** in the NHL or USA Hockey books — neither has a mercy or running-clock rule. Many local leagues do. CARHA publishes one: running time when there is a five-goal spread *"with 10 minutes remaining in regulation time"*, with penalties stretched to 3, 7 and 12 minutes to match the faster clock (Section Seven, item 16). The threshold varies wherever these exist, so **check your own league's rules.**

The convention, where a game is out of hand, is a set of self-imposed restraints rather than deliberately playing badly:

- Roll all four lines evenly and give the least-used players more shifts.
- Stop forechecking hard in their end; play the neutral zone instead.
- Work on things — complete passes, cycle, defencemen keeping the puck in — rather than hunting chances.
- Don't celebrate goals demonstratively.
- Don't pull anybody's stick or take needless hits.

**The point is not to insult the other team by visibly easing off** — that is worse. The point is to stop trying to increase the margin.

### The goalie's crease

**In short:** Screen without contact from outside the crease, stay out of it after the whistle, and get out of a returning goalie's lane.

**Convention:** during a stoppage, don't skate through, stand in, or shoot pucks at the opposing goalie's crease. Goalies treat that space as theirs, and standing in it after a whistle is the standard way to start something.

**During play, screen from outside the crease and keep your skates out of the blue paint.** Screening without contact is normally legal; contact with the goalie, or stopping them moving in their crease, is what costs your team the goal. [Rules Primer](../foundation/rules_primer.md#goaltender-interference) works through how the books differ.

**When a goalie has played the puck outside their crease and is skating back,** ⚠️ **get out of their lane.** No book here tells you it is free just because you never touched them. **Do not turn that round into a belief that standing still wins you a call, either.** In the NHL and IIHF books it is moving into their path that does the work; Hockey Canada writes no stand-your-ground limb, and CARHA's belongs to the defenders, not to you. **Get out of the way.**

**What it costs.** After the whistle, and for a goalie skating back:

- **After the whistle, the books say almost nothing about the crease, and custom does the work.** ⚠️ **One thing they do name is flipping a puck at the net after the whistle** — a minor at the referee's discretion under Hockey Canada (11.1), and a minor anywhere on the ice under USA Hockey if the shot was avoidable (601(a)(4)). Standing in the paint to start something is reachable too: Hockey Canada and USA Hockey both name inciting an opponent, and 🇬🇧 the IIHF's open unsportsmanlike list covers stoppages in terms (75.2(II)). All of it is at the referee's discretion.
- **A returning goalie.** NHL and IIHF Rule 69.4 let the referee penalise an attacker whose deliberate actions stop a goalie who played the puck outside their crease from getting back. USA Hockey's Casebook sets no such precondition: block their return and the goal is disallowed and you take an interference penalty (Rule 625, Situation 9). ⚠️ **The rule that Situation cites is a contact clause, though, and USA Hockey does not exclude the goalie from its definition of possession** — so how far it reaches an attacker who never touches them is not settled in its terms.
- **Hockey Canada and CARHA write no returning-goalie clause**, but impeding an opponent who does not have the puck is ordinary interference — a minor under Hockey Canada 8.3 and CARHA 66(a). CARHA counts the last player to touch the puck, *"other than a goalkeeper"*, as the one in possession, so a returning goalie never has it there. ⚠️ **And at CARHA the minor is the floor:** the referee may make it a major and a game misconduct, so the act that costs two minutes elsewhere can end your night.

### Your own goalie, in the warm-up and in drills

**In short:** Aim warm-up and drill shots at the pads and gloves, never at your own goalie's head, and stay in your own end.

**Convention — and one of the few in this section where breaking it hurts somebody.** **Don't shoot at your own goalie's head in the warm-up, and don't fire pucks at them from ten feet in a drill.** Both are more common than they should be: the warm-up is where a shooter is loosest and least aimed, and in a drill queue the distance quietly shrinks each time round. Aim at the pads, the glove and the blocker, and take the shot from where the drill puts you.

**No rulebook names shooting at your own goalie as an offence — which is a reason to keep the convention, not to relax it.** Only two books stop play for a hit to the mask. 🇬🇧 **The IIHF — your book in Britain — lets the referee stop play** when a goalie is *"hit on the face mask by a hard shot"* and may be dazed. It is a *may*, and **an immediate scoring chance for the attacking team cancels it.** **Hockey Canada makes it a *shall*, but only in U13 and below.** ⚠️ **The NHL, USA Hockey's stand-up rules and CARHA write no mask stoppage at all.**

**Every book stops play for an *injured* goalie**, though all but CARHA usually wait until the goalie's team has the puck, and each asks an official to judge that the goalie is hurt, and a cage hides much of what an official would judge it by. ⚠️ **A teammate who does not shoot there is the only protection that does not depend on somebody noticing.**

**Keep to your own end in the warm-up.** All six books here confine each team to its own end, so shooting at the other team's goalie breaks a rule in every one of them — and the NHL's, PWHL's and IIHF's warm-up rules cross-reference their fighting rules at that point, which tells you what it usually turns into. ⚠️ **Do not read the absence of a whistle as permission.** Most books have the referee report it to the league, which decides whether you play next week (NHL and IIHF 86.6, PWHL 88.6, Hockey Canada 2.2(i), CARHA 16(h)). USA Hockey (502(b)), Hockey Canada (2.2(j)) and CARHA (27(b)) also let the referee penalise you on the spot — the ordinary rulebook applied early, usually as unsportsmanlike conduct.

🇬🇧 **In Britain, the Elite League adds a suspension or fine for warm-up conduct on top of anything a referee sees and calls — and it can reach your head coach if your team started it.** Its Rule 5.6(1) covers crossing the centre red line in warm-ups for unsportsmanlike behaviour, *"including shooting pucks at opponent’s end"*. Its Rule 86.6 has warm-up violations the officials do not see reviewed after the game, and a standby referee oversees contact and the red line. Everywhere else in Britain, IIHF 86.6 has a half-ice breach reported to the Proper Authorities rather than penalised. Either way, plan on a fight being a major and an automatic game misconduct (IIHF 46.8; 46.9 in 2025/26). [Goaltender](../positions/goaltender.md) has the rest of what a goalie needs from their skaters.

### Helping an injured player

**In short:** Stop for a player who stays down; for a head or neck injury, call an ambulance, helmet on, moving them only for the airway.

Conventions: stop playing when someone is down and not getting up, and wave for the bench or the officials. If it's a head or neck injury, **call for immediate medical help** and **do not move them** beyond keeping their airway clear, unless you are trained to do more. Kneel next to them, keep them still, and leave their helmet on.

**Waving at the bench is not the same as calling for help** — it only tells your own side something has happened. In a rec game there may be nobody trained at the rink, so one person stays with the player and somebody else phones for an ambulance. The full on-ice response — the ABCs, leaving the helmet on, and why you assume a spinal injury in any head injury — is in [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md).

Where this connects to the rulebook (**NHL Rule 8.1**):

- Play is **not** stopped for an injured player until their own team has secured control of the puck — unless the officials judge the injury obviously serious, in which case they may stop it immediately.
- **But there is a second limb people forget, and it cuts the other way.** If the injured player's team *already has control of the puck at the moment of injury*, "play shall be stopped immediately **unless his team is in a scoring position**." So a team attacking on a two-on-one with a teammate down behind the play gets to finish the rush; a team cycling harmlessly in the corner does not.
- An injured player who leaves during play to be replaced must go via the **players' bench**, not any other exit. Using another exit is a bench minor under the NHL, IIHF and PWHL, and under USA Hockey, Hockey Canada and CARHA if you are replaced.
- Once a player is attended to on the ice by the trainer or medical staff, they **must be substituted for immediately** and cannot return until play has resumed.

**Tapping the pads and boards.** Tapping a goalie's pads after a good save, or a teammate's shins after a blocked shot, is the standard nonverbal "well done" — it exists because you can't hear anything through a helmet at speed. Tapping sticks on the boards from the bench is the bench's applause, for a goal, a good shift, or a teammate leaving the ice injured. **Keep it a tap.** The NHL and PWHL make it a minor for players who *"bang the boards with their sticks or other objects at any time"* (39.2(iii)), and the IIHF's 39.2(II) adds the glass. The same clause separately makes showing disrespect for a call in any manner a minor. The referees' discretion covers only banging to get their attention for something like a serious injury. USA Hockey gives a bench minor for banging the boards after a body check to escalate the play (601(b)(5)), and a game misconduct for banging them in protest of a call (601(d)(3)). ⚠️ **Never bang the boards at a call, or to cheer a hit.**

### How much of this varies

A lot.

- **Fighting conventions barely exist outside North American professional and junior hockey.** 🇬🇧 **The current IIHF book, the 2026/27 edition, makes a fight an ejection by default**: a major plus an automatic game misconduct is *"the standard penalty"* for a willing combatant (Rule 46.1). ⚠️ **The older wording, which left ejection to the referee, is the one most people remember** — and the In-House Rules still adopt it, saying a willing combatant *"may be ejected"*. The referee alone may waive the game misconduct for a player who only *"defended"* themselves.
- **The Elite League and the NIHL price the fight itself lower — but not starting one, or going on punching.** In the NIHL, the SNL and BUIHA Varsity the In-House Rules make a fight a five-minute major, with an automatic game misconduct in the NIHL's last five minutes or overtime; the instigator and the aggressor are still ejected, and so can anyone who keeps fighting after being told to stop. The Elite League's Casebook prices a willing combatant at *"at least a Major Penalty"*, and ejects the aggressor, a player on their second fighting major, and an instigator in the last five minutes or overtime. Elsewhere on the IIHF book, and under collegiate rules, expect ejection and suspension — so the North American "code" around fighting has almost no purchase in European or international play.

## Talking to Officials

**What to do is short: during play say nothing, and at a stoppage let your captain ask one calm question.** The rules behind that are stricter than most players realise.

### What actually works

- **During play: nothing.** Say nothing. There is no version of this that helps.
- **At a stoppage, if you are the captain: ask a question, don't make a statement.** "Can you tell me what you had on that one?" gets an answer. "That was a terrible call" gets you two minutes.
- **Calm and quiet beats loud and right.** Referees at every level respond to tone before content.
- **Ask once.** Persistence is what the escalating penalties exist to punish.
- **Do it through the letter.** If you have no letter, tell your captain and let them handle it. That is what the letter is for.

**Arguing has essentially never reversed a call.** NHL officials cannot rescind a penalty because of a protest — the only reversal route is video review, and complaining does not trigger it. Amateur hockey has even less. What arguing reliably does is get you a second penalty and make the next fifty-fifty call go the other way.

### What the rulebook actually says

**In short:** Leave rule questions to your captain, and never dress up a complaint about a penalty as one.

**Only the captain may raise a rule interpretation, and only when invited.** NHL Rule 6.1 gives the captain alone *"the privilege of discussing with the Referee any questions relating to interpretation of rules"*, and then only *"when invited to do so by the Referee"*.

**An alternate captain has that privilege only when the captain is not on the ice.** How many letters a team may carry differs by book: a captain and two alternates is the usual limit; the NHL, the PWHL and the IIHF's 2026/27 edition also allow three alternates and no captain; and Hockey Canada, CARHA and the Elite League allow a captain and three alternates. **Check which book and edition your competition runs.** **Goalies may not be captain or alternate** under the NHL, IIHF, PWHL and Hockey Canada books. **USA Hockey and CARHA bar a goalie only from *exercising* the privilege, not from wearing the letter** — see Captains and Leadership below.

**The popular story that this rule exists because Montreal's Bill Durnan kept leaving his crease to argue is folklore, not documented fact.** Durnan was genuinely captain, and did complain about that habit in his own words — but ⚠️ **no authority anywhere connects the rule's introduction to him specifically**, and the rule's own wording, grouping goalies with coaches and managers, reads at least as much like general tidying-up as one goalie's temper.

**Complaining about a penalty is not a rule interpretation.** Rule 6.1 is unusually blunt: "A complaint about a penalty is NOT a matter 'relating to the interpretation of the rules' and a minor penalty shall be imposed against any Captain, Alternate Captain or any other player making such a complaint."

**Coming off the bench to argue is an automatic minor.** Rule 6.1 again: "Any Captain, Alternate Captain or any player who comes off the bench and makes any protest or intervention with the officials for any purpose shall be assessed a minor penalty for unsportsmanlike conduct under Rule 39 – Abuse of Officials." This is repeated in Rule 39.2(iv).

**Other codes are close but not identical.** The IIHF restricts the privilege to the captain the same way, but doesn't require the referee to invite the conversation first — the restriction there is purely about *who* may talk, not about waiting to be asked. A team cannot change its captain or alternates mid-game; if the captain is ejected or injured, an alternate takes over. **USA Hockey is looser**: the privilege attaches to whichever one designated player the team names, not specifically the captain-when-on-the-ice, and rec leagues frequently run without any letters at all.

### What gets you a penalty

**In short:** Never touch or gesture at an official, and go straight to the box when you are told.

In the NHL, each of these is an **unsportsmanlike minor** on its own: disputing a ruling (no rudeness needed), obscene or abusive language or gestures at an official, banging the boards with a stick at any time, showing disrespect for a decision in any manner, coming off the bench to protest, and banging the glass at an off-ice official. An obscene gesture is also a game misconduct (Rule 75.5(ii)). Hockey Canada catches disputing only when it is done *"in an unsportsmanlike manner"*.

**Don't count on the usual ladder of minor, then misconduct, then ejection — several things skip straight to the top.**

**Contact with an official.** ⚠️ **Never put a hand on an official.** Deliberate force against one is a match penalty under USA Hockey, Hockey Canada and CARHA (CARHA's reaches an intentional touch). Under the NHL and PWHL it is a game misconduct plus an automatic suspension under Rule 40: at least ten games in the NHL and four in the PWHL, or twenty and eight with intent to injure. The IIHF writes a game misconduct for force likely to injure, for demeaning an official and for pushing free of one after an altercation, and leaves the suspension to supplementary discipline. USA Hockey makes touching or holding an official a misconduct, and physical contact with one, or resisting a linesperson in an altercation, a game misconduct.

**Other things skip the ladder too.** Leaving the penalty box to challenge a ruling is a game misconduct in the NHL, PWHL and IIHF, with an automatic three-game suspension for a first offence in the NHL and PWHL. Throwing a stick, bottle or puck toward an official is at least a game misconduct under the NHL, PWHL, IIHF and USA Hockey — and a USA Hockey match penalty for a puck shot at one recklessly. Hockey Canada and CARHA make even a threat without contact a match penalty. Throwing equipment out of the rink in protest is a minor plus a game misconduct in the NHL, PWHL and IIHF, and abusing an official after the game can draw a game misconduct too.

**The one most likely to catch you comes just after you are penalised:** a misconduct for ⚠️ **not going straight to the penalty box when an official tells you to**, or for entering or staying in the referee's crease while the call is reported. [Rules Primer](../foundation/rules_primer.md#talking-to-officials) has the clause-by-clause version.

**An obscene gesture ends your game**, anywhere in the rink, under the NHL, the PWHL, USA Hockey and the IIHF. Under USA Hockey, so does banging the boards, glass or goal frame in protest of a call (601(d)(3)). Spitting at an official is a game misconduct plus an automatic suspension in the NHL and PWHL, and a match penalty under USA Hockey, Hockey Canada and CARHA. 🇬🇧 **In Britain, the IIHF book writes a first-offence game misconduct** for spitting or smearing blood at an official, or for obscene gestures, racial slurs, sexual remarks or abusive language at one — anywhere in the arena, during or after the game. Its minor-penalty clause also lists abusive language at an official, so the book contradicts itself; play to the game misconduct.

## Handling Teammates Who Are Struggling

Convention and judgement, not rule. The overlap with [Mental Game](mental_game.md) is deliberate — that document covers what to do when the struggling player is you.

### The goalie who let in a soft one

Goaltending is the position where one error is instantly visible, permanently on the scoreboard, and impossible to hide. Every goalie knows exactly how bad the goal was without being told.

**What helps:**

- **Say something short and move on.** "Next one." A tap on the pads. That's it.
- **Clear the puck. Win the next faceoff. Play a simple shift.** The single best support you can give a goalie is a period of boring, structured hockey where nothing comes from the slot.
- **Tell them what you're doing in front of them** — "I've got the net front," "shot coming, I'm screening, going left." Information restores their sense of control.

**What makes it worse:**

- **Analysing the goal.** Even sympathetically. Especially sympathetically.
- **Overcompensating** by suddenly collapsing five players to the crease. It tells the goalie you don't trust them and it concedes exactly the screened, deflected chances that produce another soft goal.
- **Silence from the whole bench** after the goal, which reads as blame whether or not it is.

### The player having a bad night

- **Keep giving them the puck.** Cutting a struggling player out of the play is the most common and most damaging reaction. It confirms what they already fear and guarantees the night stays bad.
- **Give them an easy one first.** A short pass they cannot miss. Confidence is rebuilt with completed simple actions, not with a chance to redeem themselves on a hard one.
- **Praise effort, not outcome** — "great backcheck", "good stick there." Outcome praise after a bad night sounds like pity.
- **Say the thing that lowers the stakes.** "It's Tuesday, mate." In a rec league, this is genuinely the right intervention.

**What makes it worse:** "just relax," public coaching from the bench, replaying the turnover, and — worst — a teammate visibly rolling their eyes. See the next section.

## Handling Conflict

### Visible frustration is a tactical error

You will at some point be furious with a teammate mid-shift. The mistake is not the feeling. The mistake is **showing it**, and the argument against it isn't etiquette — it's competitive.

Throwing your head back, slapping your stick on the ice, or gesturing at where the pass should have gone does four things, all bad:

1. **It tells the opposition exactly where your weakness is.** A bench that sees your defence pair arguing will attack that pair. This is free information you are giving away.
2. **It demoralises your own bench.** Everyone can see it. The next player who makes a mistake now knows what happens to them.
3. **It stops you playing.** The two seconds you spend gesturing are two seconds you are not backchecking, and the goal usually comes from that.
4. **It makes the teammate worse.** A player who is worried about your reaction plays slower and passes it away sooner.

**The rule for yourself: react to your own errors visibly at most, and to teammates' errors never.** Even that has limits — see [Mental Game](mental_game.md).

### Deal with it after, not during

- **During the game: nothing.** No conversation in a hockey game has ever improved anybody's next shift.
- **After the game, once you have both calmed down** — not in the car park at full volume, and preferably not in a full dressing room.
- **Talk about the specific play, once.** "On the D-to-D at the blue line I'm expecting it flat — it kept getting chipped and I was reaching." Not: "you never look up."
- **Frame it as what you will do differently**, because that's the only half you control. Most conflicts of this kind are actually mismatched assumptions, and naming the assumption fixes it.
- **If it's genuinely not resolvable between you, take it to the coach or captain** — quietly, as a problem to solve, not as a complaint.

**The escalation you must not make** is retaliating on the ice, or shorting a teammate you're annoyed with by not passing to them. Both are visible to everyone and both convert a private irritation into a team problem.

### The bench that empties

**In short:** When it goes up on the ice, sit down and stay sat down; leaving the bench helps nobody.

**A bench that empties is a culture failure before it is a penalty, and it is the version of everything above that ends games rather than shifts.** The instinct is the same one this section has been about, at team scale: a teammate is in trouble, the door is right there, and going through it feels like loyalty.

**It is not loyalty, and the arithmetic is against it.** The teammate you went out for is already being separated by two linespersons and is going to the box or to the room whatever you do. What you have added is yourself, plus whoever followed you through the door, plus a team that finishes the game short. **From the bench, the only useful thing you can contribute is not being on the ice.**

- **Sit down, and stay sat down.** If you are the player nearest the door, that is a job rather than a seat — somebody halfway over the boards will usually stop if a teammate says their name.
- **The player in the penalty box has the hardest version**: the whistle may be the altercation itself, and the box door is not a release just because your time is up. ⚠️ **The NHL suspends any player who leaves the penalty box illegally during an altercation and is not the first to do so for the next five games** (Rule 70.10) — any of them, not just the second.
- **Do not go out afterwards to settle it either.** That is on-ice retaliation one tier up, and everyone can see who started the second incident.

**The teams this never happens to did not decide it in the moment.** Somebody said out loud, before the season, that nobody leaves the bench — and that is the whole of the culture half of it.

**What it costs.** This one is a rule to know, not a convention:

- **At least a game misconduct in all six books for the first player off a bench.** The NHL, IIHF, USA Hockey and PWHL trigger it on any *altercation*; Hockey Canada (10.4(e)) and CARHA (70(a)) on a *fight*. **And not only the first:** the NHL, IIHF and PWHL reach the first **or second** player off, USA Hockey every player who leaves, and Hockey Canada and CARHA any later player who picks up a penalty of their own once out there.
- **The game misconduct is the floor,** ⚠️ **not the bill.** USA Hockey 629(a) is a *major* plus a game misconduct, and a USA Hockey major is served with no on-ice substitute (Rule 403(a)), so your team kills it shorthanded too. Hockey Canada (10.4(e)(i)) and CARHA (70(b)) add a double minor for the first player off either bench. Leaving the penalty box during a stoppage and an altercation is a minor plus a game misconduct in the NHL and IIHF (70.4 and 70.6) and the PWHL (72.4 and 72.6). Hockey Canada and CARHA cap the game misconducts at five players per team per stoppage, and CARHA allows a sixth only for the identified first player off.
- **And the bill arrives after the game:** under USA Hockey the game misconduct for leaving the bench also costs your next game (404(b)), and NHL Rule 70.10 ⚠️ **suspends the first player off automatically for ten games and the second for five**. [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md) sets out what each book costs; if you play in Britain, [UK and England Rules](../foundation/uk_rules.md) is the one that applies to you.

## Captains and Leadership Without a Letter

### What a captain actually does

The formal duties are small and precise. Under NHL rules the captain is the only player entitled to discuss a rule interpretation with the referee, and then only when invited (Rule 6.1). Beyond that the letter picks up a short administrative list — making the formal complaint that triggers a stick measurement, choosing which of two simultaneously expiring minor penalties releases its player first, and naming the penalty-shot taker whenever the shot was not awarded to a specifically fouled player — and almost nothing else the rulebook recognises.

The real job is off the sheet:

- **Set the standard for what a normal shift looks like.** Not the best shift — the normal one. Teams calibrate to their captain's floor, not their ceiling.
- **Be the interface** with the coach and, where relevant, with the league or club.
- **Own the officials conversation** so nobody else has to, and so nobody else takes the penalty for trying.
- **Notice who isn't talking.** The player quietly having a terrible season is the captain's problem before it's the coach's.

Note the goalie restriction, and note that the books word it differently:

- **NHL Rule 6.1**, **IIHF Rule 6.1**, **PWHL Rule 6.1** and **Hockey Canada Rule 2.3(d)** all bar a goalie from *being* captain or alternate captain at all. IIHF: "No playing Coach or playing Team Manager or goalkeeper shall be permitted to act as Captain or Alternate Captain."
- **USA Hockey Rule 202(a) is narrower.** It says "No goalkeepers shall be entitled to **exercise the privileges** of Captain" — it bars the goalie from doing the job of talking to the referee, **not** from wearing the letter. A USA Hockey team may designate its goalie as captain; someone else just has to do the talking. **CARHA Rule 18(d)** is written the same way.

Either way, a goalie who is the obvious leader of a team ends up leading without the referee-facing part of the role — the situation the next section describes.

### Most leadership has no letter

On any team, the letters go to three or four people at most, depending on the book, and the behaviour that determines a team's culture is distributed across everyone. In practice, the people who most shape a room are:

- **The player who is always the first one dressed and the last one off the ice.** Nobody announces this. Everybody notices it.
- **The player who talks on the ice.** The organising principle in [On-Ice Communication](../foundation/on_ice_communication.md) is that the player who can see it makes the call — which means the loudest useful voice on your team may well be your least experienced player, and that is correct.
- **The player who welcomes new people.** One person doing this changes a room.
- **The player who doesn't sulk after a bad shift.** Emotional weather is contagious in both directions.
- **The player who does the unglamorous thing** — blocks the shot, takes the defensive-zone draw, kills the last minute — without being asked and without mentioning it afterwards.

None of those require skill, seniority, or permission. **If you want to lead a hockey team, the entry requirement is to be reliable in public.**

## Rec and Beer League Specifically

Most adults reading this play here, and the culture is genuinely different from organised competitive hockey.

### The range on one bench

A single beer-league bench routinely contains a former junior player, someone who learned to skate at thirty-five, and everything between. This is unlike almost any other sport's amateur scene, and it means:

- **Adjust the pass to the receiver.** A hard, flat pass to someone who can't receive it is not a good pass. It is a turnover you caused.
- **Cover more ice than your job description if you can.** If you are the strongest skater on your line, you are the one who backchecks — for the puck, not the player. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call; never hit anyone who can't see you coming, never from behind, and if you can see a player's numbers, pull up.
- **Don't coach from the bench.** Nobody paid league fees to be taught by a teammate. If someone asks, help; otherwise don't.

### Playing against much weaker or stronger opponents

- **Against weaker teams:** roll lines evenly, stop forechecking, make five passes before shooting, let your least experienced players take the offensive-zone shifts. See "not running up the score" above.
- **Against stronger teams:** simplify. Get pucks deep, keep the shifts short, take away the middle, and treat it as a chance to work on structure. Trying to trade rushes with a much better team is how a 4-1 becomes 9-1. See [Game Management](../systems/game_management.md).
- **Either way, keep your stick down — and find out whether you may hit at all before you set about softening one.** In a non-check category a body check is a penalty however gently it lands, and under USA Hockey which category you are in is a question for your league rather than for the age list; the next section is the detail. The mismatch is exactly when injuries happen.

### Non-check league etiquette

**In short:** Ask your league exactly what contact it allows, then play the puck rather than the body and never step into anyone.

**Whether your league is non-check depends on its adult classification, and the age list does not answer it — so ask.** USA Hockey names four adult classifications, one of them *"Adult non-check"*. Its Rule 604(a) bars body checking in the 12-and-under youth classifications and below, all girls' and women's classifications, and all non-check adult ones. ⚠️ **That list is a floor, not an answer:** a local body may ban checking in any classification, and 604(b) bans it whenever a Competitive Contact team plays a Body Checking team — so it can change per fixture. **How much adult rec hockey is played non-check is not something a rulebook records**, so ask your league, and get the answer in writing.

**"Non-check" does not mean "no contact."** USA Hockey says so in terms, and permits **angling**, **physical engagement** (two players legally leaning into each other while both play the puck) and **collisions** where players hold legal position. What it bans is intentional contact with *"overt hip, shoulder, forearm or torso action"*, forcing an opponent off the puck with no effort to play it, and deliberate contact with no effort to play the puck. It calls **"finishing the check"** unacceptable, because it is meant to punish rather than win the puck.

**British women's hockey is not under that rule.** Women's hockey on the IIHF book — every level of it in Britain — is **restricted contact, not a blanket ban**. IIHF Rule 101.1 permits body checking *"when there is a clear intention of playing the puck or attempting to 'gain possession' of the puck"*, lets a player hold ground, and penalises stepping or gliding into an opponent. **It separately penalises using the boards to eliminate, push or pin an opponent, which matters most in a corner.** An illegal hit is a minor, or a major plus an automatic game misconduct.

**In the Women's National Ice Hockey League,** ⚠️ **play it as no body checking.** Its rules label both formats *"Full ice, non-checking"* without saying what that means, so do not test the permission in 101.1 there: hold your ground, lean only while you are both playing the puck, and never step into anyone or use the boards on them. Reading USA Hockey's rule as the women's rule is the commonest way to get this wrong; [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md) covers the distinction.

**So under USA Hockey: take and hold your ice, angle someone off, and lean while you both chase a puck — but never step into someone.** The convention on top of the rule is that **the physical intensity is set by the least willing player in the battle, not the most.**

**In a CARHA-affiliated league, never use your body on anyone.** Skate your own line, but do not lean, do not plant yourself in a player's path to take the contact, and get out of the way of contact you can avoid. When your team has the puck, do not skate into a defender's path to free the carrier — CARHA lets a defender hold position but does not let the side with the puck run interference (66(a), Note 2).

**What it costs.** Under USA Hockey, a body check in a non-check category is a minor (604(c)); a reckless one is a major plus a game misconduct (604(d)), and plan on a match penalty (602(a)) and a likely multi-game suspension. CARHA Rule 49(a), which has no classification scope, reaches a player who intentionally bodies, pushes or stands in front of an opponent to make contact, or does not avert contact. That is a minor or a major at the referee's discretion, and a mandatory major when intentional contact injures; any major ejects you with at least a one-game suspension (Rules 30(a) and 32(d)). Contact you genuinely could not avoid is not a penalty there (49(c)) — except to the head, face or neck, still a minor (50(a)), or driving a player from behind into the boards or net, a match penalty (53(b)). See [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md).

### Everyone has work in the morning

The single most useful sentence in adult hockey. It underwrites most of the culture:

- **An 11pm game is not worth an injury.** Nobody is getting a contract.
- **No slashing, no cross-checking, no retaliation.** The retaliation is what escalates, every time.
- **Sticks down, blades on the ice, no follow-through above the waist.**
- **Let the ref referee.** They are usually a teenager or a volunteer and are on their fourth game of the night.
- **Be somebody people are pleased to see on the ice.** Rec-league rosters are filled by word of mouth; this is also, incidentally, the entire economics of getting invited back.

## Being New to a Team

**Reliability beats skill, and it isn't close.** A team fills its roster with the people it can count on. Skill decides your line; reliability decides whether you are on the team next season.

**If this is your first team rather than a new one**, [Getting Started](../getting-started/getting_started.md) covers what comes before this list — registration, tiers, fees, and what a season actually commits you to.

The practical checklist:

1. **Turn up, on time, every time.** Dressed and ready before the stated time.
2. **Reply to messages.** Team organisers in amateur hockey are volunteers chasing people for availability. Being the person who answers within the hour makes you disproportionately popular.
3. **Pay your fees before you're asked.**
4. **Ask what the systems are** in your first week: what forecheck, what defensive-zone coverage, who calls the breakout. Then play them, even if you think a different one is better. See [Forechecking Systems](../systems/forechecking_systems.md) and [Defensive Zone Coverage](../systems/defensive_zone_coverage.md).
5. **Ask which door is on and which is off**, and where you should sit.
6. **Play simple for the first few games.** Get pucks deep, complete passes, finish your shifts. Teams form their impression of you fast, and "safe" is a better first impression than "flashy and occasionally catastrophic."
7. **Learn names.** Then use them on the ice.
8. **Say yes to the post-game.** At least sometimes. Half of a hockey team's shared assumptions get built there.
9. **Don't apologise constantly.** A new player narrating every mistake becomes exhausting; make the mistake, reset, next shift.
10. **Don't compare it to your old team.** Ever.

## Coaches

### What coaches actually notice

This is convention and coaching-experience material rather than a measured finding, so treat it as informed opinion: **coaches watch effort and positioning far more than skill.** Skill is largely fixed within a season and is obvious in the first ten minutes. Positioning and effort are the things a coach can change, so they are what a coach is scanning for.

Concretely, what gets noticed:

- **Whether you backcheck all the way**, or coast in at the top of the circles. Backcheck for the puck, not the player, and pull up if you can see their numbers.
- **Where you are when the puck is on the other side of the ice** — the single most revealing tell of whether a player understands the system. See [Playing Without The Puck](../hockey-iq/playing_without_the_puck.md).
- **Shift length.** A player who consistently changes at forty seconds is easier to deploy than a more skilled player who stays out for ninety.
- **Whether your head is up on the bench.**
- **Whether the bad shift changes your next shift.**

### Getting useful feedback

Coaches are usually bad at giving unsolicited individual feedback, not because they don't have it, but because there are eighteen of you and one of them.

- **Ask at a good time** — after practice, not during a game, not in the two minutes after a loss.
- **Ask a narrow question.** "What do you want from the weak-side winger on the defensive-zone collapse?" gets you an answer you can use. "How am I doing?" gets you "yeah, good."
- **Ask about a specific shift.** "Third period, the shift where they scored — where should I have been?"
- **Ask what would move you up.** "What would I need to do differently to play more minutes?" is direct, and most coaches respect it.
- **Then do it, visibly, and don't mention that you're doing it.**

### Asking good questions

- **About systems, ask "what" and "when", not "why not something else."** "When their D steps up at the line, do you want me to chip it or hold?" is a question. "Wouldn't a 1-2-2 work better here?" is a challenge, whether or not you meant it as one.
- **Ask in front of the group when the answer helps everyone** — most system confusions are shared.
- **Ask privately when the answer is about you.**
- **Accept the answer.** You can disagree; you still play the system. A player who plays a system they dislike properly gets listened to later. A player who freelances doesn't.

## Common Mistakes

- **Treating the bench as a rest.** Know the score, the clock, who you're replacing and who you'll face before you reach the door.
- **Changing late.** Go when you decide to go, without one last look at the puck. **Step on only once the player coming off is at the bench *and* out of the play** — [Rules Primer](../foundation/rules_primer.md#changing-on-the-fly) has your book's distance.
- **Two players thinking the same spot is theirs.** Name the person you are replacing, out loud, as you go.
- **Staying out because you don't trust the next line.** Change while you still have the legs to get to the bench.
- **Changing at the wrong moment.** Wait until the puck is out of your end or your team has it, and never change when you are the last player back.
- **Not getting back after a teammate covered for you.** Return to your spot as soon as the play lets you, or your team will stop covering at all.
- **Sitting on the boards.** Sit on the bench with your skates inside it. An exposed blade is dangerous, and the NHL, the IIHF and the PWHL make it a minor after one team warning.
- **Helping a downed player up.** With a possible head or neck injury, **do not move them except to keep their airway clear, and leave their helmet on.** **Call for immediate medical help**, then keep them still. Waving at the bench is not a call for help — make sure somebody has actually phoned.
- **Arguing a call.** Let it go and win the next faceoff. In every book here except Hockey Canada, disputing a ruling is a minor in itself; Hockey Canada's needs it done *"in an unsportsmanlike manner"*.
- **Expecting a warning first.** Keep your hands off officials, never gesture or spit at one, and stay in the box however wrong the call looks. Each of these can end your game with no minor first, and force or spitting brings a suspension in most books. [Talking to Officials](#talking-to-officials) has the detail.
- **A non-captain talking to the referee.** Find out who holds that privilege, and if it isn't you, tell your captain. ⚠️ **Complaining about a penalty is not a rule question, and it draws a minor of its own** — under Hockey Canada when done in an unsportsmanlike manner.
- **Coming off the bench to protest.** Stay on the bench and say it there. Coming off is an automatic minor under the NHL, the IIHF and the PWHL.
- **Going over the boards because a teammate is in trouble.** Sit down, stay in the box until an official releases you even if your time is up, and say the name of whoever is halfway over. **In all six books here the first player off a bench for a fight is thrown out of the game — in four, for any altercation — and none stops at the first.** [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md) has each book; [UK and England Rules](../foundation/uk_rules.md) has the British position.
- **Expecting linemates to adapt to you.** Learn where each one wants the puck, and pass to the player who exists.
- **Visible frustration at a teammate.** Skate straight back into the play; talk about one specific play after the game, once. Showing it tells the opposition where to attack.
- **Going quiet on the ice.** Call "man on" early, in your team's words, even when unsure — a wrong call costs less than no call.
- **Shooting at your own goalie's head in the warm-up, or from ten feet in a drill.** Aim at the pads, the glove and the blocker. ⚠️ **No book makes it an offence, and every stoppage for a hurt goalie waits on an official seeing the injury through a cage — so you are the protection.**
- **Treating a returning goalie's lane as free ice because you never touched them.** Get out of their way. The NHL and IIHF penalise deliberately blocking their return, contact or not; Hockey Canada and CARHA reach it as ordinary interference; under USA Hockey it is a penalty and any goal comes off. [Rules Primer](../foundation/rules_primer.md#goaltender-interference) has the rest.
- **Analysing the soft goal with the goalie.** Say "next one" and win a faceoff.
- **Cutting a struggling teammate out of the play.** Keep giving them the puck, and give them an easy one first.
- **Coaching teammates from the bench in a rec league.** Tell a linemate what *you* will do next shift, and help only when asked.
- **Visibly easing off when the game is out of hand.** Keep playing properly — roll the lines, drop the hard forecheck, work on passes. Coasting insults them more than a goal does.
- **Assuming "non-check" means "no contact."** Angle, lean while you both play the puck, and hold your position — but never step into someone. **In a CARHA league, don't lean or plant yourself in a player's path, and avoid contact you can**; in women's hockey on the IIHF book, never use the boards to push or pin. ⚠️ **In the WNIHL, whose rules label its games *"non-checking"*, play it as no body checking at all.**
- **Being new and having opinions.** Listen for three weeks. Then have them.
- **Treating the priced conventions as custom.** Never quote a custom to a referee — but the handshake line is priced. **Keep your helmet on, keep your hands to yourself, say nothing, and if it goes up, back off.** A fight there ends your game in most books, helmet removal can be a USA Hockey match penalty, the first player in to break up a fight is thrown out under USA Hockey, Hockey Canada and CARHA, and can be in every book, and post-game abuse is a USA Hockey game misconduct. After a whistle, don't stand in the opposing crease or flip a puck at the net either.

## Key Takeaways

1. **When a player is down and not getting up, stop and wave for the officials. With a head or neck injury, phone for medical help, do not move them except to keep their airway clear, and leave their helmet on.** Assume a possible spinal injury. One person keeps them still; somebody else phones. See [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md).
2. **When your line is next, get to the door early, watch the player you are replacing, and name them out loud.** ⚠️ **Step on only once your man is at the bench *and* out of the play** — otherwise it is a bench minor. [Rules Primer](../foundation/rules_primer.md#changing-on-the-fly) has your book's distance.
3. **When your line is tired and pinned in, get the puck out under control rather than icing it.** ⚠️ **Under the NHL, IIHF and PWHL books — and so in British league hockey — a team that ices cannot change, except to replace an injured player.** USA Hockey and CARHA allow it; Hockey Canada bars it at some levels.
4. **In a non-check league, hold your ice, lean only while you both play the puck, and never step into anyone.** In women's hockey on the IIHF book, never use the boards to push or pin. ⚠️ **In the WNIHL, play it as no body checking; under CARHA, don't lean or plant yourself in a player's path, and avoid any contact you can.**
5. **When a call looks wrong, say nothing, let your captain raise it, and win the next faceoff.** Arguing is a minor (in Hockey Canada, if unsportsmanlike). ⚠️ **No warning comes first for a hand on an official, spitting at one or an obscene gesture: each can end your game, and force or spitting brings a suspension in most books.**
6. **If you have no letter, take it to your captain — and find out before the game who holds that privilege.** ⚠️ **Complaining about a penalty is not a rule question and draws a penalty of its own in most books, and coming off the bench to argue is an automatic minor in the NHL, IIHF and PWHL.**
7. **Adapt to your linemates; they will not adapt to you.** Be where they expect you on every breakout, because a predictable teammate lets a defenceman pass without looking up. If you change what you do, say so on the bench first.
8. **Talk.** Between shifts, tell a linemate one specific thing you will do — *"I'm coming low on the breakout"* — never anything evaluative. On the ice, call "man on" early, in your team's words, even when you are not sure.
9. **When a linemate leaves their spot to go after the puck, fill it — and when somebody has filled yours, get back as soon as the play lets you.** That exchange, repeated, is what trust on the ice is. Chemistry is built by repetition, not friendship, which is why constant line juggling has a cost.
10. **When a teammate is struggling, keep giving them the puck, and give them an easy one first.** After a soft goal, say "next one" and play a simple shift — never analyse it with the goalie. Never show frustration at a teammate on the ice; talk about one specific play after the game, once.
11. **When it goes up on the ice, stay on the bench, and stay in the penalty box until an official releases you, even if your time is up.** ⚠️ **In all six books here the first player off a bench for a fight is thrown out of the game — in four, for any altercation — and none stops at the first.**
12. **In a handshake line, keep your helmet on and your hands off an opponent's head, say nothing, and if it goes up, let the officials in first.** ⚠️ **A fight there ends your game in most books, and so does being first in to break one up under USA Hockey, Hockey Canada and CARHA — it can in every book. Taking your helmet off before an altercation, or an opponent's before or during one, is a USA Hockey match penalty.**
13. **When a game is out of hand, stop trying to add to the margin, but do not visibly ease off.** Roll the lines, play the neutral zone instead of forechecking hard, and work on passes rather than hunting chances. **When you are the weaker team, simplify:** pucks deep, short shifts, take away the middle.
14. **Treat the bench as a working position, and keep both skates inside the boards.** Coaches flag sitting on the boards as putting a blade near someone's face, and the NHL, IIHF and PWHL make it a minor after one warning. Lead without a letter: be first dressed, talk on the ice, welcome new players. **Reliability, not skill**, gets you accepted.
15. **In the warm-up and in drills, shoot at your own goalie's pads, glove and blocker, never the head.** ⚠️ **No book makes it an offence, and a cage hides the injury an official would need to see — so you are the protection.** Keep to your own end in the warm-up; every book here requires it.
16. **When a goalie who played the puck outside their crease is skating back, get out of their lane.** The NHL and IIHF penalise deliberately blocking their return, contact or not; Hockey Canada and CARHA reach it as ordinary interference; under USA Hockey it is a penalty and any goal comes off. [Rules Primer](../foundation/rules_primer.md#goaltender-interference) has the rest.
17. **Play the system your coach asks for, the same way every shift.** Coaches commonly say they watch effort and position more than skill: backcheck all the way — for the puck, not the player, pulling up if you can see their numbers — and know where you are when the puck is on the far side. To earn more ice time, ask a narrow question about a specific shift, after practice — then do it.
