# Equipment

> **Rule set:** Written to NHL rules. Where IIHF, USA Hockey or typical rec-league rules
> differ in a way that changes how you play — and equipment is one of the areas where they
> differ most — it is flagged inline. Equipment *rules* vary far more between leagues than
> playing rules do, so the recurring instruction in this document is: check your own league's
> requirements before you buy. **Playing in Britain? The IIHF flags are yours** — every level
> of UK hockey runs the IIHF book, amended locally, and the local amendments bite hardest on
> equipment. See [UK and England Rules](../foundation/uk_rules.md), and read
> [What the Rules Actually Require](#what-the-rules-actually-require) before you spend anything.

## Key focus

**Never wear a second-hand helmet — bought, borrowed or handed down — and a goalie mask counts as a helmet.** Crushed foam does not recover, and you cannot see what a helmet has hit. At a taster session, take your own or ask the host first.

**Replace any helmet that has taken a serious impact, even if it looks fine.** Bauer and Hockey Canada both say so. No helmet is concussion-proof — a retailer's point, not a research finding — so never play as though you are wearing armour.

**Do your chin strap up every time, a finger's gap at most, and strap every pad so it cannot move.** Check the overlaps in a skating stance, not standing in the shop.

**In Britain, wear a neck laceration protector every time you are on the ice — games and training, adults included.** USA Hockey's adult exemption is not yours. ⚠️ **Under the In-House Rules there is no warning: forget it and you sit out ten minutes, and a second offence ends your game.**

**Find out what your league requires before you buy anything** — facial protection, mouthguards and stick length all differ between books, far more than playing rules do.

**Fit beats price, and skates most of all.** In a boot a size too big, your ankle takes up slack before the blade tilts, so you never commit to an edge. Get measured, and allow a growing child half a size at most.

**Keep your edges sharp, and skate on one hollow you know.** Dull steel is the usual reason a player suddenly slides out of turns. Sharpen new skates before first use, then roughly every 20–30 hours on the ice. If unsure, ask for half an inch.

**Use a stick you can bend and control.** Start at chin height in skates, a flex near half your body weight in pounds, and a mid curve. Cutting a stick stiffens it, and a deep curve costs you your backhand and your passing.

**When kit fails in play, let go of it and get off the ice.** Drop a broken stick where you stand — carrying it is a penalty. ⚠️ **Never tidy it over the boards: under the IIHF book British hockey plays, that gets you thrown out of the game.** If your helmet or neck guard comes off, go to the bench.

## Overview

Hockey gear does two separate jobs: it keeps you out of hospital, and it decides what your body is able to do on the ice. Most players get the first roughly right and the second badly wrong.

**Fit beats price at every position on the list**, and skates are where it bites hardest. Pure Hockey's fitting guide: *"a low-end skate that provides good heel lock will outperform a high-end skate that is a size too large."* In a loose boot you never commit to an edge, and you conclude you have weak ankles. You have loose boots.

**Then look after the numbers that decide how your kit plays.** Skate on one hollow you know, and change it a step at a time so you can tell whether the edge or your technique is the problem. Choose a stick you can bend and control: a shaft stiffer than you can load, or a curve you cannot backhand with, costs you shots and passes. These are starting points, not rules.

**Equipment rules differ far more between leagues than playing rules do.** Neck protection is the sharpest difference, and it catches adults. Across England, Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland a neck laceration protector is mandatory for every player, adults included, and the books that reach training say to wear it there too. Under the In-House Rules, which reach university hockey too, there is no warning: forget it and you sit out ten minutes, and a second time you are thrown out of the game. ⚠️ **USA Hockey exempts adults and Hockey Canada covers only minor and female hockey — an adult in Britain who follows those books will be sent off the ice.**

**Replace any helmet that has taken a serious impact, even one that looks perfect.** Foam works by crushing and does not un-crush, and you cannot see the damage. That is also why **nobody should wear a second-hand helmet**, including from a taster session's loaner pool — and **a goalie mask counts**, which is where the usual advice to borrow club gear stops. No helmet is concussion-proof, and a helmet is only partly effective against the rotational forces behind most concussions; the source for both is a retailer's buying guide rather than concussion research.

**A helmet only protects you while it is fastened, and a pad only while it stays where you put it.** Do the chin strap up before you step on the ice. Strap elbow pads so they cannot spin when you fall, and check your pants overlap your shin guards in a skating stance, because that is when the gap opens. Oversized pads are not extra protection — they slide out of the way on impact.

**Kit also fails mid-game, and what you do next is a rule, not a habit.** Let go of a broken stick the moment it breaks; under the In-House Rules, carrying it anywhere on the ice is two minutes. Leave it on the ice clear of the play — putting it over the boards is the tidy instinct, and under the IIHF book it gets you thrown out of the game. ⚠️ **A helmet or neck guard coming off is not the penalty; staying on the ice without it is** — go to the bench. In British play you leave the ice at once, where the NHL, Hockey Canada and the Professional Women's Hockey League (PWHL) let you refit a helmet and play on. Keep a spare stick taped at your bench.

## The Full Kit List

Here is everything a skater needs, roughly in the order you put it on, with what each piece does and where it sits on the price/benefit curve.

**From the skin out, up to the skates:**

| Item | What it does | Price/benefit curve |
|---|---|---|
| **Base layers** | Wicks sweat away from skin; reduces chafing and gear stink | Cheap, high value. Any decent synthetic or merino kit works |
| **Jock (men) / jill (women)** | Protects the groin/pelvis. Available as a traditional strap-and-cup or as compression shorts with a cup pocket | Cheap, non-negotiable |
| **Shin guards** | Cover shin and knee against pucks, sticks and falls | Steep early gains, flat quickly. Fit matters far more than model |
| **Socks** | Hold shin guards in place, carry team colours | Trivial cost |
| **Pants ("breezers")** | Protect hips, thighs, tailbone, kidneys and lower spine | Mid gains. Coverage and fit dominate |
| **Skates** | Everything | **Steepest curve in the bag.** See below |

**Then everything from the waist up:**

| Item | What it does | Price/benefit curve |
|---|---|---|
| **Shoulder pads** | Shoulders, chest, back, sternum, biceps | Depends heavily on whether your league allows body checking |
| **Elbow pads** | Elbow joint, lower tricep, upper forearm | Cheap, but fit is critical — see the rotation problem below |
| **Neck guard** | Reduces the risk of a skate- or stick-blade laceration to the throat | Cheap, and mandatory in many rule sets — **mandatory for every player of every age across the Home Countries** |
| **Jersey** | Team identification; also required to cover most of your pads | — |
| **Gloves** | Hands, wrists, lower forearms — plus all your stick feel | Real gains up the range, mostly in feel and durability |
| **Helmet** | Skull and brain | **Never the place to save money.** See below |
| **Cage / visor / full shield** | Face, eyes, teeth | Mandatory at most levels; cheap relative to what it protects |
| **Mouthguard** | Teeth and jaw | Cheap. Custom-fitted from a dentist is better than boil-and-bite |

**And what you carry rather than wear:**

| Item | What it does | Price/benefit curve |
|---|---|---|
| **Stick** | Every puck touch you will ever make | Long, steep curve — but only after the specification is right |
| **Tape and wax** | Blade protection, puck feel, grip | Pennies. Disproportionately important |
| **Bag** | Carries and ventilates everything | Buy the size you need, not the biggest |

Optional but common: **cut-resistant socks, sleeves or base layers** (IIHF Rule 9.5: "All Players are **strongly recommended** to wear cut-resistant socks, sleeves, or undergarments." USA Hockey Rule 304(g) is a notch softer — players "**are recommended** to wear cut-resistant socks, sleeves or undergarments." Recommended by both; only the IIHF says *strongly*), **skate guards or soakers**, a **shooting pad** for home practice, and **spare laces**.

### What USA Hockey says you are personally responsible for

USA Hockey Rule 304(a) puts the obligation on you rather than on the referee: "Each player is personally responsible to wear protective equipment for all games. Such equipment should include gloves, shin pads, shoulder pads, elbow pads, hip pads or padded hockey pants, protective cup, tendon pads plus all head protective equipment as required by USA Hockey rules."

## What the Rules Actually Require

This is the section people skip and then get sent off the ice for. The three main rule sets differ substantially.

### If you play in Britain, read this before you read anything else in this section

**Your rule book is the IIHF's**, at every level, with the **IHUK In-House Rules** amending the equipment rules more heavily than any other part of the game — ⚠️ **including that adults are not exempt from neck protection.** [UK and England Rules](../foundation/uk_rules.md) has the full list and the evidence; these are the ones that change what goes in the bag.

- **A neck laceration protector is mandatory for everyone, adults included**, unlike under USA Hockey and Hockey Canada. If it comes off in play, ⚠️ **leave the ice and don't retrieve it.**
- **Wear the neck guard at training as well as in games.** In England and Wales *Rules & Regulations* 24.3 covers *"all on ice activities"*, and the EIHL Casebook's Rule 9.12 covers *"warm-ups and practice sessions"*. The In-House Rules say nothing either way about training — **that is silence, not an exemption**, and what is missing at practice is the referee, not the reason for the guard.
- **If you are a junior, keep the neck guard on at the bench — even on nights you are not playing.** IHUK's *Junior Rules of Competition* 2026-27 §10 puts dressed players on the bench in full kit *"including helmet (full face) and neck guard"*, and a non-playing junior may be on the bench *"only if wearing, at minimum: helmet with full-face protection, neck guard, and upper-body protection. Otherwise, they must leave the bench."* The Women's National Ice Hockey League (WNIHL) document says the same of non-playing U16 players.
- **Buy a cage for any junior, 18 or over included**, and full face for a woman until at least the season after her 18th birthday. IHUK's junior leagues require full face whatever the age, Under-19 included, and England Ice Hockey requires a cage of every junior in England and Wales. A half visor for an adult woman is plausibly permitted from that season, but that is this document's own reading of a rule that never says so — IIHF 102.1 closes with *"Players without the mentioned protection are not allowed to participate in a game."* Buy full face if you want certainty.
- **In Scottish Ice Hockey Association (SIHA) Recreational and University hockey, and in British Universities Ice Hockey Association (BUIHA) hockey, a half visor is the minimum**, regardless of age.
- **Keep the chin-strap gap to 2.5 cm or less** (about one finger), and **a full cage's chin cup in contact with your chin**, inside the J-clips — no loose or dangling cages or straps.
- **Shorts that are cut, torn or modified are not permitted**, and jerseys must not be tucked in.
- **Wear full kit, helmet included, in the pre-game warm-up, not just the game.** IIHF Rule 9.5 writes that into the book every British league plays, and England Ice Hockey's *Rules & Regulations* repeat it.

**What it costs.** ⚠️ **The neck-guard mandate is the same across Britain; the punishment is not, so do not carry one league's tariff into another.** Under the **In-House Rules** there is **no warning** — an immediate 10-minute misconduct, then a game misconduct on a second offence. The **Elite League** Casebook keeps the IIHF's lighter first rung: a warning, then a minor. **Being told to fix equipment and refusing is a separate ladder, IIHF Rule 9.5, which no British amendment touches**: minor, then misconduct on returning to the ice unchanged, then game misconduct the third time. A loose chin strap is the bench and a team warning the first time, a 10-minute misconduct after that; a detached helmet in play means leaving the ice immediately, and a misconduct if you don't.

*Source: [IHUK In-House Rules 2026-27](https://englandicehockey.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/IHUK-In-House-Rules-2026-2027.pdf), [England Ice Hockey Rules & Regulations](https://englandicehockey.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/09/1.0-5.0-EIH-Rules-and-Regulations.pdf) Rule 24, and the [Motorpoint EIHL Casebook 26-27](https://www.eliteleague.co.uk/document/191-eihl-casebook-26-27-version-1-1-080926) Rules 9.5 and 9.12 — the League issues updates in season. See [UK and England Rules](../foundation/uk_rules.md) for the full citations and edition caveats.*

### Helmets

- **NHL (Rule 9.6):** a League-approved helmet on the ice, on the bench and in the penalty box. If yours comes off during play, leave the ice or retrieve and replace it "within a reasonable period of time" — a minor if you do neither — and do not leave the penalty box during play without a fastened helmet. ⚠️ **Removing an opponent's helmet is a separate offence here and in the PWHL**: *"A player who intentionally removes an opponent’s helmet during play shall be assessed a minor penalty for roughing"* (9.6 and 51.2 in both), and 51.6 in both bars supplementary discipline for it, so in the NHL the minor is the whole of it. ⚠️ **But PWHL Rule 51.3 makes grabbing an opponent's helmet, chin strap, facial protection or throat protector a roughing major even at minimal force, and 51.5 adds a mandatory game misconduct.** The PWHL book does not say which rule applies when the grab is what takes the helmet off, so read any grab as the major and the ejection.
- **IIHF (Rule 9.6):** stricter. A player whose helmet comes off *"must exit the playing surface immediately"* and is *"not allowed to retrieve and put their helmet back on their head"*. ⚠️ **Losing the helmet is not the offence under any of these books; staying on the ice is**: *"If a Player does not comply with this rule, they shall be assessed a minor penalty without a warning"* (under Rule 12 – Illegal Equipment, identical in the 2025/26 and 2026/27 editions). ⚠️ **Removing an opponent's helmet is a roughing minor here too**, and leaving the penalty box during play without a fastened helmet stops play and costs a minor. Grabbing an opponent's face mask is an unsportsmanlike-conduct minor (Rule 75.2(II)), and Rule 21 can make it a major and a game misconduct if the referee judges the force serious and severe.
- **Britain (In-House Rules, Rule 9.8) — the same duty, a tier higher.** A detached helmet in play still means leaving the ice immediately, but staying on is a **10-minute misconduct** here rather than the IIHF's two-minute minor — and a second misconduct of any kind in the same game is an automatic game misconduct, so an earlier chin-strap violation plus this one ends your night. Go to the bench and leave the helmet where it fell.
- **USA Hockey (Rule 304(c), (e)):** a HECC-certified helmet below Adult level; **adults must still wear one**, but it need not be certified, and for them the requirement is a fastened chin strap. In *all* classifications a helmet or facemask coming off **stops play immediately** — unlike the NHL. ⚠️ **Keep the strap within one finger's width of the chin:** looser, the team is warned once and the next offender on that team takes a misconduct.
  - **Around a fight,** ⚠️ **removing a helmet is punished far harder here than under the NHL, PWHL or IIHF.** A helmet that comes off *during* an altercation because it was not fastened properly is a game misconduct plus a one-game suspension. **Deliberately** removing your own before an altercation, or an opponent's *"prior to or during"* one, is a **match penalty** — ejection plus a suspension pending review. Causing an opponent's helmet to come off without deliberately removing it is a **game misconduct** plus a **two-game suspension**, *"in addition to any other required suspensions incurred during the same incident."*
  - **It is not limited to fights.** ⚠️ **It reaches an ordinary post-whistle shove:** the casebook says an altercation *"does not need to be considered a fight for Rule 615(c) to be applied"*, and applies it *"in any altercation where at least one player will receive a penalty"*. The IIHF, NHL and PWHL give no penalty at all for a helmet that comes off naturally in a fight, so don't plan around that if you might ever play under USA Hockey.
  - **Facemask grabs.** ⚠️ **Grabbing, holding or even rubbing an opponent's facemask anywhere is a mandatory major and game misconduct under Rule 622(b)**, and under 622(c) a match penalty for reckless endangerment may also be assessed for grabbing or holding it. If the grab is reckless, plan on that match: Rule 602(a) says one *"shall be assessed"* to any player who *"recklessly endangers"* an opponent.
- **Hockey Canada (Rule 3.6(d)):** ⚠️ **replace the helmet properly fastened or go to the bench**; take part in the play *"in any manner"* without it and play is stopped, with a minor for illegal equipment. **If you lose your helmet and are too badly hurt to do either, play is stopped straight away** — Interpretation 10 to Rule 10.6(a) overrides the usual injury rule, under which play runs on until your team has the puck — and you still leave the ice until play resumes.
  - **Around a fight,** ⚠️ **removing a helmet is a gross misconduct** — sent to the dressing room for the rest of the game and reported to the league. That covers removing your own, or undoing your chinstrap so it comes off, before or during the fight, and doing either to an opponent (10.6(f)(iii), (iv)).
  - **Helmet and cage grabs.** Grabbing an opponent's helmet, chin strap or facial protector is priced anywhere on the ice: **Rule 7.1** makes it a double minor at minimal force, a major and a game misconduct at the referee's discretion (mandatory if it injures), and a match penalty for more force or a grab used to gain an advantage, punish or injure. **CARHA Rule 48(d)** makes it a double minor, or a match penalty when the grab is used as an advantage or to punish or injure.

### Facial protection

- **NHL (Rule 9.7):** since 2013-14, players with fewer than 25 games of NHL experience must wear a visor.
- **IIHF (Rule 9.7):** visor, cage or full-face visor. ⚠️ **Every player born after 31 December 1974 must wear at least a visor**, fixed along the sides so it cannot flip up, covering "the eyes and nose in its entirety"; tinted or coloured visors are banned. Women's Senior and U18 (Rule 102.1–102.2) and men's junior U18 (Rule 202.2) require **full-face protection**.
  - **A cracked visor ends your shift.** ⚠️ **Leave the ice:** *"A Player whose visor face protection becomes cracked or broken during game action must leave the ice immediately."* Rule 9.7 sets no penalty itself; two other rules do, and both escalate. Under Rule 9.5 the player is told to change it and the team is warned; after that everyone on the team wearing such equipment is penalised under Rule 12, and a refusal is a minor for delay of game, then a misconduct for returning unchanged, then a game misconduct.
  - **In junior hockey,** ⚠️ **the tariff is five times heavier.** Rule 202.7 (men's junior U18) lists *"visor pushed up"* among equipment worn *"not in accordance with the rules"*, warns **both** teams through their coaches, then gives the next offender a **ten-minute misconduct**; Rule 102.7 does the same for women's U18 and under-aged players. Both are in the 2025/26 edition British hockey plays, and **the 2026/27 edition deletes them**, so cite this with its edition.
  - **Dangerous Equipment.** Rule 9.8 also covers *"wearing a visor in a way that may cause injury to an opponent"*: the player goes off, the team is warned, and after that every player on the team wearing it that way is penalised.
- **Britain (In-House Rules, and the Elite League's Casebook where named):** ⚠️ **in women's hockey, wear full face until the season after the 18th birthday**; a half visor is permitted only from then. IHUK's 2026-27 junior rules require full face of every junior from U10 to U19, and in the U19 National Division *"full face cages … regardless of being 18+"*. SIHA Recreational, SIHA University and BUIHA players need a half visor minimum regardless of age, and the Elite League's Casebook (Rule 9.5) drops the IIHF's birth-year line, so every EIHL player wears at least a visor.
  - **Face cages are mandatory in all junior hockey in England and Wales**, and R&R 24.5 says they *"must be tight against the player's chin at all times"* — on the same single warning as the neck guard and the chin strap (24.6), with misconduct penalties after that to the player *"and also potential team head coach in the event of repeat offending."*
  - **The women's half-visor permission is a reading.** ⚠️ **It is the natural reading of the domestic sentence rather than something either book states**, and IIHF 102.1 closes with *"Players without the mentioned protection are not allowed to participate in a game"* — no published document settles which prevails. **Buy a cage if you want certainty** — it meets every one of these rules, junior or adult. See [UK and England Rules](../foundation/uk_rules.md).
- **USA Hockey (Rule 304(d)):** a HECC-certified facemask is required below Adult level. ⚠️ **A half visor carries an age floor that's easy to miss, because it's HECC's rather than USA Hockey's own** — HECC only certifies visors for players 18 and over, so the practical answer under 18 is full-face protection (a cage or full shield). **That last step is a reading of HECC's certification scope, not something the rulebook states:** Rule 304(d) asks only for *"a facemask certified by HECC"* and sets **no age for the visor itself**, and that age limit comes from HECC's certified-equipment list, not from the rulebook.
- **Hockey Canada (Rule 3.6(e)):** a full facial protector is compulsory in every category except Senior and Major Junior, where a CSA-certified half visor is an option — and Note 2 makes facial protection *"recommended but optional for male adult recreational hockey"* altogether — and CSA's visor certification carries the same 18-and-over floor as HECC's — Hockey Canada's own table describes a visor as *"intended for use by a person 18 years old or older"*.

### Neck protection

- **IIHF (Rule 9.12):** mandatory for all players in all categories *"of IIHF Competition"*, minor penalty after a warning. ⚠️ **But in junior hockey the 2025/26 book — the one British hockey plays — routes the violation through Rules 102.7 and 202.7 to a warning to both teams through the coaches and then a TEN-MINUTE MISCONDUCT**, five times the adult tier. The 2026/27 book deletes those rules and that cross-reference.
- **England and Wales (R&R 24.3):** the requirement covers *"all on ice activities"* — **practices included**, not only games — and *"All protective equipment must be worn without alterations and as directed by the manufacturers’ specification."* ⚠️ **R&R 24.6 supplies the tariff, it is shared, and it reaches the bench as well as the player.** A first violation of 24.3, 24.4 or 24.5 — neck guard, chin-strap gap or junior face cage — *"counts as a warning to the player and bench, for any further violation of rule either 24.3, 24.4 or 24.5 misconduct penalties will be assessed to the offending player and also potential team head coach in the event of repeat offending."* **One warning covers all three rules together**, so a chin-strap correction early in the night spends the warning your neck guard would otherwise have had.
- **Practice — only two of these books put the guard on the *player* there, and the silence in the rest is not an exemption.** England and Wales R&R 24.3 covers *"all on ice activities"*, and the EIHL Casebook says *"[t]his requirement also applies during warm-ups and practice sessions."* ⚠️ **A third document reaches training and does something different.** IHUK's *Coaching Regulations 2026-2027* require a Level 2 coach *"for all junior and senior teams, including practices, games, camps, and hockey schools"*, and §9.3 puts a neck guard in the **coach's** own on-ice kit and in a 16–17-year-old foundation coach's — but its list of what the coach must check on **players**, *"approved helmets and face protection, sticks, and gloves"*, names no neck guard. **Read that as an asymmetry between documents, not as a permission.** The In-House Rules, the IIHF book, USA Hockey and Hockey Canada are scoped to competition and say nothing either way about practice — searched for *practice*, *practise* and *training*, the In-House Rules have nothing on the point. What is missing at training is the referee, not the reason for the guard. **So wear it at training whichever book you are under.**
- **IHUK, England Ice Hockey and Scottish Ice Hockey competitions, women's and university hockey included (In-House Rules, Rule 9.12):** the same universal requirement, with the warning removed — an immediate 10-minute misconduct, a game misconduct on a second offence. That is the conservative reading, because England Ice Hockey's own *Rules & Regulations* 24.6 sets out a warning scheme and nobody has published which governs. ⚠️ **And 9.12 is not the whole of your ladder either.** The In-House Rules amend 9.12 and leave **IIHF Rule 9.5** untouched, so 9.5’s escalation is yours as much as the Elite League’s: a player told to fix equipment who refuses takes a minor for delay of game, a **misconduct** for returning to the ice unchanged, and a **game misconduct** the third time. See [UK and England Rules](../foundation/uk_rules.md).
- **Two IHUK *Rules of Competition* carry it onto the bench — including for players who are not playing.** The 2026-27 junior and WNIHL documents each add a §10 *Equipment & Kit* — *"Neck-laceration protector: mandatory and unaltered from manufacturer spec"* — require it of dressed players on the bench, and reach the non-playing player too: *"Non-playing junior players may be on the bench only if wearing, at minimum: helmet with full-face protection, neck guard, and upper-body protection. Otherwise, they must leave the bench"* (the WNIHL says the same of non-playing U16 players). ⚠️ **The junior document also closes the adult-exemption argument in one division:** *"All U19 National Division players must wear full face cages and neck guards for the full season, regardless of being 18+."* **These are competition regulations, so they settle those two competitions and nothing else** — no other British competition's *Rules of Competition* was read for this document. **Where they apply, the guard stays on at the bench.**
- **The Elite League (EIHL Casebook, Rule 9.12):** the same universal requirement, for *"all categories of EIHL Competition"*, with the IIHF's warning-then-minor kept rather than raised. ⚠️ **It reaches further than the IIHF's version**: the requirement *"also applies during warm-ups and practice sessions"*, a sentence neither the 2025/26 nor the 2026/27 IIHF book carries.
  - **The minor is the first rung, not the ceiling.** ⚠️ **A third refusal to fix it ends your game.** Casebook **Rule 9.5** names *"neck laceration protection not being properly worn"* among the things officials respond to, warns the player and team, and then: *"[i]f the Player refuses, they shall be assessed a minor penalty for “Delay of Game” and if they return to the ice without making the change, they shall be issued a misconduct penalty. Should this happen a third time, the Player shall be issued a game misconduct penalty."* That ladder is the IIHF's own Rule 9.5 word for word. The Casebook prints 9.12 and 9.5 side by side without reconciling them, so this document reads 9.12 as the rule for turning up without one and 9.5 as the rule for refusing to fix it — the narrower reading rather than the cautious one, since 9.5's trigger, *"any equipment that does not meet with the IIHF regulations"*, plainly reaches having none at all.
- **USA Hockey (Rule 304(g); 303(b) for goalies):** a **HECC-certified** neck laceration protector is required in every classification except Adults, for whom it is strongly recommended. ⚠️ **BNQ-certified protectors fulfil the rule only *"through the 2026-27 USA Hockey season"*** — after that, HECC.
- **Hockey Canada (Rule 3.6(c)):** a BNQ-certified throat protector is mandatory for all players registered in minor and female hockey, goalies included — a dangler on the mask doesn't substitute. ⚠️ **That BNQ certification programme closes 31 July 2027 with no announced replacement** — check your protector's label and ask your association what it will accept afterward.
- **NHL: none, and that is a whole-book absence rather than a gap in one section.** The 2025-26 rulebook was searched in full, including across line breaks and hyphens: *"throat"*, *"laceration"* and *"cut-resistant"* return **zero**, and the six occurrences of *"neck"* are all about a jersey's neck opening or about high-sticking contact — a fact about the NHL, not a standard to copy.

### Mouthguards

- **USA Hockey (Rule 304(f)):** a coloured internal mouthpiece is required from 12U through Youth/Girls' 18U-19U, on the same warning-then-misconduct ladder as the helmet and neck guard.
- **IIHF (Rule 9.13):** recommended for all; required for men's junior U20 players not wearing full-face protection.
- **The Elite League (EIHL Casebook, Rule 9.5) — mandatory for every skater, with no cage exemption.** *"All players participating in the EIHL must properly wear a mouth guard."* ⚠️ **The IIHF’s full-face carve-out does not survive into the Casebook:** where IIHF Rule 202.3 excuses a player in the Under 20 category who wears a cage or full visor, the Casebook excuses no skater, so an EIHL skater in a cage still needs one. **Goalies are the one exception the Casebook does write** — *"It’s recommended that goalkeepers wear proper mouth protection, but not mandatory."* Chewing it does not count as wearing it, and losing it mid-shift is not an emergency: *"he may continue to play until the 1st stoppage of play."* Refuse to put one in and you are on the ladder Rule 9.5 sets for any non-conforming equipment — a minor for delay of game, then a misconduct, then a game misconduct. **That ladder is the IIHF’s own wording rather than an Elite League invention**, so it is not a tier the League has added.
- **The WNIHL (IHUK *Rules of Competition* 2026-27, §10):** mandatory with a half visor, not with full-face — *"Facial protection (Full-face covering or half visor and mouth guard) is mandatory in IHUK WNIHL Competition"*, and the same pairing is required of dressed players sitting on the bench. **Non-compliance is penalised as illegal equipment.** ⚠️ **That is a competition regulation rather than a playing rule, so it settles the WNIHL and nothing else** — no *Rules of Competition* for any other British competition was read for this document.
- **Hockey Canada (Rule 3.6(e), Note 6):** compulsory wherever a half visor is worn, except Senior, where it is only recommended — so in practice it bites only in Major Junior. ⚠️ **That is a reading of two Notes together, not a sentence either one writes.** And the same rule's **Interpretation 2** says something different again: the guidelines reach mouth guards *"if a Member has made mouth guards mandatory at a specific division of play. **Otherwise, mouth guards are not currently mandatory under the rules.**"* The rulebook prints no reconciliation, so ask your Member association — and wear one either way, whichever way the answer goes.

### Everything else

- **Pads go under the jersey.** Nearly universal across the books, with minor variation in what's excepted (gloves, headgear, goalie leg pads, and in USA Hockey padded pants too, which is why some North American leagues wear pants over the jersey hem). **In Britain**, the jersey must also stay completely outside the pants and cover protective equipment like elbow pads — buy one big enough to do that job.
- **Shorts, in Britain.** Cut, torn or modified shorts are illegal equipment — a warning first, then a misconduct. Recreational hockey in England tolerates small rips, but anything a referee judges safety-relevant is not. The common habit of cutting the inner thigh out of pants for mobility makes them illegal here.
- **Elbow pads (NHL Rule 12.3):** any elbow pad without a soft outer covering of sponge rubber "at least one-half inch (½") thick" is dangerous equipment.
- **Gloves — a minor in five books, a misconduct under USA Hockey.** A glove with the palm cut out to allow bare-hand use is illegal everywhere: a minor penalty under the NHL, the IIHF and the PWHL (Rule 12.2 in all three), under Hockey Canada (Rule 10.6, which lets the referee warn first) and under CARHA (Rule 24(a), after one warning). ⚠️ **Under USA Hockey it's a ten-minute misconduct**, and taping over a cut-out palm is worse, not safer — cutting or injuring an opponent with taped hands during a fight is a match penalty there.
- **Jewellery — banned in two of the books cited here and unmentioned in the rest.** **USA Hockey Rule 305(c):** *"Except for Adults, no player or goalkeeper shall be permitted on the ice while wearing jewelry unless it is completely covered by equipment or taped to the body"*; its Casebook (Rule 305(c), Situation 4) accepts a HECC-certified helmet with ear pieces, or *"clear tape that secures the earring to the body"*. **The EIHL Casebook (Rule 9.5) is stricter and writes no adult exemption**: *"Jewelry cannot be worn, either under a player’s equipment or exposed to the outside of equipment"*, filed under Illegal Equipment, so Rule 9.5's refusal ladder applies. ⚠️ **No other book cited here writes a jewellery rule** — the NHL, IIHF, Hockey Canada, PWHL and CARHA books and both general British documents were searched in full — **but that is an absence, not a permission**, and a referee there may still judge a loose ring or stud dangerous equipment.
- **Shin pads (IIHF Rule 9.9):** must be small enough to fit inside standard socks, with no protrusions or additions.
- **Skates (IIHF Rule 9.10):** exactly four parts — boot, blade, holder, laces. No pick. No mechanical aids. Laces may be any non-fluorescent colour but "must not be so long that they touch the ice." USA Hockey Rule 302(a) simply bans speed skates, figure skates, and anything unsafe.
- **Sticks:** the shaft cap is **written four different ways across the six books.** **Hockey Canada 3.3(b)**: *"The stick will not exceed 1.60 m (63 in.)"*, with **no height exception.** **NHL 10.1**: 63 inches, or 65 for a player 6'6" or taller with written approval. **IIHF 10.1 — the book British hockey plays — 1.63 m, about 64¼ inches**, or 1.65 m for players 2.0 m or taller with written approval. **USA Hockey 301(b): a flat 65 inches for everyone**, no approval needed. CARHA (Rule 21(b)) is 63 inches with no exception, and the PWHL copies the NHL's 63 and 65. ⚠️ **So a 64-inch stick is legal under the IIHF and USA Hockey, and illegal under Hockey Canada and CARHA and — without approval — the NHL and PWHL.** Blade limits (about 12½ inches long, 2–3 inches deep) and the curve limit (about ¾ inch) match across the NHL, IIHF and USA Hockey; Hockey Canada sets no curve limit, a gap in the rule rather than a licence. **CARHA's curve limit is half an inch** (Rule 21(b)), so in a CARHA league measure a deep retail curve rather than assume it is legal. Elsewhere, retail sticks are legal by default.

> **Note on a common retail error:** at least one major retailer's blade guide states the NHL curve limit is ½ inch. It is ¾ inch, per NHL Rule 10.1, and the same retailer's stick-fitting page says ¾ inch. Retail sticks are made legal to that limit by default; outside CARHA's half-inch rule you are extremely unlikely to buy an illegal curve by accident.

## Skates — The Most Important Purchase By a Wide Margin

Nothing else in the bag is close. Your skates are the interface between you and the only surface you play on. Every technique in [Skating](../technique/skating.md) — knee bend, edge angle, extension, recovery — is executed through the boot. A boot that lets your foot move independently of it cannot transmit any of that.

### Fit above everything

Three checks, in order:

1. **Heel lock.** Sit down, kick your heel firmly into the back of the boot, and lace up. Your heel must not lift or shift when you walk. Pure Hockey's fitting guide is blunt about it: "Moving or lifting your heel inside the hockey skate should be impossible." How To Hockey's fitting crash course says the same — "your heel should not move when walking."
2. **Toes just brushing the cap.** With your heel seated back, your big toe should *barely* brush the toe cap — feathering it, not pressing into it, and not floating clear of it.
3. **No dead space anywhere else.** Foot flat on the footbed, snug all round, no gaps at the ankle or under the arch.

Lace properly while you check. Tighten firmly through the first two or three eyelets so the forefoot is snug, then tighten well through the curve from foot to ankle — that is what pulls your heel back into the pocket. Wear the socks you actually play in.

### Sizing: skates are not shoes

**Hockey skates run roughly 1 to 1½ sizes smaller than your street shoe size.** *(A starting point, not a promise. **CCM verifies it**: its Skate Selector publishes a 1.0-size offset through youth and junior and 1.5 through senior. **Bauer deliberately publishes none**, saying finding a skate "isn't as simple as taking your shoe size and looking at a conversion chart", and **True publishes none either.** The retailer guide most people meet the rule on gives eight brand-specific charts — four Bauer, four CCM, none for True — so even there it is not one number.)* Don't be alarmed by the number on the box. Widths are commonly D (regular) and EE (wide); Bauer and CCM now use numbered fit systems (Fit 1 / Fit 2 / Fit 3, and tapered / regular / wide) that map onto them.

For children, **half a size larger than that is acceptable for growth, and no more.** Pure Hockey's sizing guide is specific about what that looks like inside the boot: toes barely touching the toe cap with "no more than 1/4 inch of space in the heel," and "when sizing children, follow the same method while allowing 1/2 size extra for growing room."

> **Do not use the "one finger" check you will also see on Pure Hockey's fitting page** ("for youth sizes, you should be able to place one finger between the inside of the boot and the heel of the foot"). A finger is roughly ½–¾ inch — two to three times the quarter-inch the same retailer's sizing guide allows — and it directly contradicts the heel-lock test above, which says heel movement should be *impossible*. The two Pure Hockey pages conflict; go with the quarter inch and the half size. A child in a skate that loose cannot learn edges and will get blisters.

### Buying a size too big costs you edges and destroys the boot

It is a false economy in two directions.

**It makes you a worse skater, immediately and permanently while you own them.** Pure Hockey's skate sizing page is unusually direct: "Going too big will result in blisters and a noticeable decrease in skating ability due to a lack of ankle stability. A strong stride comes from having a boot working in unison with the foot of the player."

Here is the mechanism, and it is the reason this matters more than any other equipment point in this document. **Edges are controlled by ankle angle.** To get a blade onto a steep inside or outside edge you roll your ankle and drive the knee out over the skate. If there is slack between your foot and the boot, the first several degrees of that ankle movement are spent taking up slack instead of tilting the blade. You feel unstable, so you don't commit, so you never get onto a real outside edge. Players in oversized skates typically diagnose themselves as having "weak ankles." They usually have oversized skates.

If you are working through the edge drills in [Skating](../technique/skating.md) and outside edges feel unsafe no matter how much you practise, get your skate fit checked before you buy another power-skating session.

**It also destroys the skate.** A foot sliding around inside the boot breaks down the sides of the quarter package from the inside. Pure Hockey: wearing skates any larger than the recommended fit "will cause blisters and will break down the sides of the boot."

### Recreational versus performance boots

Skate lines run to many price points. **Counted from the manufacturers' own catalogues on 27 July 2026, current-generation senior skates only: Bauer lists nine models across two lines** — Vapor at four tiers (Fly30 $319.99, Fly40 $579.99, FlyPro $779.99, FlyLite $1,039.99) and Supreme at five (F20 $229.99, F30 $319.99, F40 $579.99, F50 Pro $779.99, Fuse $1,039.99). **CCM lists ten** — Jetspeed at six (FT860 $249.99 up to FT8 Pro $1,229.99) and Tacks at four (XR 70 $319.99 up to XR $919.99). With last generation's clearance stock, each brand runs to about fourteen. The tiers matter more than the count:

- **Entry-level.** Soft quarter package (the boot's structural shell), minimal support, breaks down in a season or two. Fine for a few public skates a year; not fine for learning to play.
- **Upper entry / lower mid-range.** Stiffer polyurethane shells, some ankle support, quick-release holders that let you swap steel.
- **Mid-range.** The first tier with **composite** material in the quarter package, and — importantly — **the first price point that can be heat-moulded (baked)**. This is the value sweet spot for most adult recreational and beer-league players: enough stiffness to develop technique, enough moulding to fit properly.
- **Upper-mid and above.** Stiffer, lighter, better steel, better liners, more customisable fit. **Discount Hockey's skate comparison says second-tier boots are "preferred by most NHL players"**, being "nearly identical to the top-of-the-line model except for a few features" and having "a faster break-in time". ⚠️ **That is a retailer's assertion and it carries no source** — no survey of what professionals skate in is cited for it here.

US retail skate prices run from about **$50–$80** for entry level to **$1,000 and beyond** at the top. (The cited retailer's ladder runs from "Entry-Level Skates ($49.99 - $79.99)" to "Top-Of-The-Line Skates ($599.99 - $999.99+)"; the open-ended plus is theirs, so read the top figure as a floor for that tier.)

### Stiffness — what it is for

A stiffer boot transmits more of your leg's force into the ice and holds your ankle in a consistent position under load. That is an advantage *if you are strong enough and skating hard enough to load it.* If you are not, a very stiff boot simply refuses to let you bend your ankle, which flattens your knee bend and takes away exactly the edge angle you were trying to buy.

The practical rule: **match boot stiffness to how often and how hard you skate**, not to your ambitions. A pro-stiffness boot worn once a week by a beginner is worse than a mid-range boot worn by the same person.

### Baking (heat moulding)

Mid-range and up boots contain thermoformable materials. A shop oven heats the boot — How To Hockey watches a fitter use a shop "cold oven, which puts every piece of the skate to about 200 degrees" — so the material softens and takes the shape of your foot as it cools. Done properly it shortens the break-in. ⚠️ **By how much is not a figure anything cited here publishes** — the familiar "it halves the break-in" is shop lore, not a measured result, and that page does not state it.

How to do it right:

- **Have a shop do it.** Home ovens are not the same thing and you can cook the glue.
- Pull out on the laces, have the fitter lace them, and then **sit still**. Do not stand up, do not flex your ankle forward or back, do not press on the boot.
- **Wait about ten minutes** for the skate to cool and set before moving.
- **Do not bake entry-level skates.** There is little mouldable material in them and the heat can do more harm than good.

### Break-in and lace bite

Expect mild discomfort for the first few sessions while the liner packs out. How To Hockey's threshold is a good one: **if it still genuinely hurts by your fourth time on the ice, go back to the shop.** That is a fit problem, not a break-in problem.

**Lace bite** — a hot spot, blister or cyst on the top of the foot — comes from lace pressure driving straight down onto the tendon. Two fixes:

- **Change your lacing.** Lacing "in-to-out" — feeding the lace up through the eyelet from underneath, the standard way shoes are laced — gives a tighter fit but drives the pressure straight down onto the tongue. Lacing **"out-to-in"** is, in Discount Hockey's words, "when the lace is brought over the eyelet and then turned back in. This method will provide more comfort and reduce the risk of lace bite because the tension is applied laterally instead of downward." You trade a little tightness for it.
- **Gel pads** inside the tongue disperse the pressure.

If you need Achilles or ankle gel pads to fill empty space, that is a signal your skates are too big.

## Skate Sharpening

### What "hollow" means

A hockey blade is not flat across its width. Sharpening cuts a **concave groove down the centre of the blade**, and what is left standing at either side of that groove are your two **edges** — the inside edge and the outside edge that [Skating](../technique/skating.md) is built around.

The **radius of hollow (ROH)** is the radius of the circle that groove is cut to, quoted in inches: ⅜", 7/16", ½", 9/16", ⅝", and so on. Counter-intuitively, **the smaller the number, the deeper the hollow and the sharper the bite.** A ⅜" hollow is aggressive; a ⅝" hollow is shallow.

### Deeper versus shallower — the trade-off

There is no correct answer, only a trade:

- **Deeper hollow (⅜", 7/16").** More bite. Better acceleration, harder stops, tighter turns. Less glide, and the skates can feel "grabby" — harder to transition between edges and harder to hold speed.
- **Shallower hollow (9/16", ⅝").** More glide and more top-end speed for the same effort. Less bite, so stops and hard turns need more attention.

The two variables that push you one way or the other:

- **Body weight.** A heavier player sinks the blade into the ice more for the same hollow, so heavier players generally go shallower and lighter players deeper.
- **Ice temperature — leave your hollow alone.** Your feel for your edges is calibrated to it. If you play regularly at one unusually warm or cold rink and want to experiment, move one step and give it several skates. Everyone agrees cold, hard ice eats edges faster.

  Two of the big retailers flatly contradict each other on which way to move. Discount Hockey says to go sharper at **frigid rinks** "to help them dig more into the harder ice", and duller at **warmer rinks** "to prevent them from 'sinking' into the softer ice." Pure Hockey points the other way: "colder, harder ice wears skate edges more quickly and often provides **more** bite".

  **The evidence favours Discount Hockey.** Bauer's own sharpening guide: *"On colder ice, it'll be harder to dig in and you'll skate on top of the surface. If the ice is warmer and softer, you'll dig in more, which adds to fatigue and less glide."* A 2025 *Scientific Reports* review of arena ice finds that *"ice becomes harder or stronger as the temperature decreases"*, and that *"Ice that is too hard may reduce this penetration, potentially affecting agility and control"*. Two caveats: gliding friction bottoms out and rises again in very cold ice — de Koning and colleagues measured the minimum "at an ice surface temperature of about -7 degrees C" — and **no study crosses hollow radius with ice temperature**.

### Typical hollows

- **½ inch is the usual default.** Discount Hockey's skate guide calls it the best "all-around" hollow because it is "right in the middle of the road - neither too sharp nor too dull", and adds that it "is also considered the industry standard by repair shops" — a trade convention reported by a retailer, not a published standard. If you don't know what you want, ask for a half inch.
- **Recreational players** commonly start at ½". *(Pure Hockey's sharpening guide is the source for this bullet and the two below it.)*
- **Heavier players, or anyone who wants more glide**, often prefer **⅝"**.
- **Players who want maximum edge bite** choose **⅜"**, accepting that it takes more effort to maintain speed.
- Once you know your baseline, move **one step at a time** — to 9/16" or 7/16" — rather than jumping.

**Skate on a consistent hollow.** Your whole feel for your edges is calibrated to it. Changing hollow and technique in the same week means you cannot tell which one caused what.

**Flat-Bottom V (FBV)** is an alternative grind that cuts a flat centre section with angled edges instead of a rounded hollow, aiming for strong bite *and* good glide. Not every machine can do it — ask before you rely on it.

### How often to sharpen

- A common rule of thumb is **every 20–30 hours on the ice**. Many players settle into once or twice a month.
- **Over-sharpening wears your steel out prematurely.** Under-sharpening costs you edges. Pick a routine based on your schedule and adjust.
- **Sharpen new skates before their first use.** They arrive with a factory finish, not a game-ready edge.
- Outdoor ice, which is often harder and carries grit, eats edges faster.
- Using soakers (soft blade covers) between sessions extends the interval.

### How to tell you need a sharpen

- **On the ice:** the blade chatters, pulls to one side, or wobbles. You slide out of turns you normally hold. Your stops go from a clean spray to a scrape. Push-offs feel weak.
- **By hand:** run a finger lightly down the length of the blade — along the steel, not across the edge — feeling for nicks and gouges. The edge should feel sharp.
- **By eye:** hold the blade under a bright light and look straight down at the edge. **A sharp edge is a line and reflects almost nothing. If you can see a reflection along the edge, it has rounded over** — time to sharpen.
- **For parents:** a young player who suddenly can't stop, slides out of turns, or says the skates feel "slippery" or "weird" is usually describing dull steel.

Expect to pay roughly **$10–$20** per sharpen at a US shop, often less if you leave them overnight. At-home stones and re-edgers run **$20–$40** and are best treated as touch-up tools between proper sharpens, not replacements — some of them remove more steel than a machine would.

### Sharpening is not profiling

**Sharpening sets the hollow across the blade. Profiling (contouring) reshapes the runner from heel to toe** — how much steel is on the ice at once, and where the balance point sits. Profiling changes acceleration, stability, agility and stride feel. It is a tuning exercise for players who already know what they want; sharpening is routine maintenance everyone needs.

## Sticks — The Four Variables

A stick is a specification, not a brand. Four numbers determine almost everything about how it plays.

Everything in this section is a **preference**, not a rule of hockey. The starting points below are starting points. Two players of identical build reasonably end up with different sticks.

### 1. Length

**The standard fitting check:** stand the stick vertically on its toe, in front of you.

- **In shoes**, the butt end should reach roughly your **nose**.
- **In skates**, roughly your **chin**.

That is a starting point, and the position you play pushes it either way — though this is convention and preference rather than anything the rules impose:

- **Defencemen** tend to go **longer**. A longer stick extends your reach in the defensive-zone gap, makes poke-checks and stick-lifts effective from further away, gets into passing lanes from further out, and helps you keep the puck away from your body while walking the offensive blue line. See [Defending the Rush](../systems/defending_the_rush.md) for why reach is the currency in a gap.
- **Forwards** often go **shorter**. A shorter stick handles the puck closer to the body, is quicker in tight, and makes it easier to get your hands out in front for a fast release — see [Shooting](../technique/shooting.md) and [Puck Handling](../technique/puck_handling.md).

The costs of getting it wrong are symmetrical: too long and you cannot handle the puck in close, you stand taller (which wrecks the knee bend everything else depends on), and your release slows down. Too short and you give up reach, and you have to bend further than is comfortable to keep the blade flat.

The maximum shaft length is **not one number** — NHL 10.1 and Hockey Canada 3.3(b) set 63 inches, **IIHF 10.1 sets 1.63 m, about 64¼ inches**, and USA Hockey 301(b) allows a flat 65; the full comparison, with the exception routes, is under *What the Rules Actually Require* above. Retail sizes run roughly: youth 38–44", junior 46–53", intermediate 54–55", senior 56–63".

### 2. Flex

**Flex is a stiffness rating: the number of pounds of force needed to bend the shaft one inch.** A 100-flex stick takes 100 lb to bend it an inch. Higher number, stiffer stick. Retail sticks run from about 30 flex for small children to 110 for large adults.

**The rule of thumb: start at roughly half your body weight in pounds.** A 180 lb player starts at 85–90; a 200 lb player at 100. Then adjust:

- **Round the flex up** if you are tall or strong for your weight.
- **Round the flex down** if you are average or below in height or strength.

**Why it matters.** A shot's power does not come only from your arms — it comes from loading the shaft like a spring and letting it unload into the puck. That only happens if you can actually bend it.

- **Too stiff** and you cannot fully load the shaft. You get less power, not more, and a slower release, because you are muscling the puck instead of letting the stick do work.
- **Too whippy** and you overpower it. The shaft bends further than you intended, the puck leaves at an angle you didn't choose, and shots become inconsistent. It also feels vague when you are receiving a hard pass.

The practical test: **you want a flex you can load consistently without overdoing it.**

**Cutting a stick raises its effective flex.** A shorter shaft is a shorter lever, so it takes more force to bend it the same amount — the number printed on the stick no longer describes what you are holding. The retail guidance is to treat about **three inches** as the threshold: if you plan to cut more than three inches off, **buy a lower flex** than the calculation says; if you plan to add more than three inches with an end plug, **buy a higher flex**. This is the single most common way players end up on the wrong flex without realising it — they buy the right number and then saw four inches off it.

Positionally, and again as convention rather than rule: defencemen often prefer stiffer shafts, partly for long point shots and partly for the solid feel when battling; forwards often prefer softer flexes for quicker loading and faster release.

**Kick point** is a secondary variable — where along the shaft the stick is designed to bend most. **Low kick** flexes near the blade for a quick release; **mid kick** is the traditional feel and loads more power over a longer motion; **high kick** loads the whole shaft, favouring big slap shots; **constant** and **dual kick** designs respond to where your bottom hand is. Get length and flex right first; kick point is a refinement.

### 3. Curve

The blade's curve is described by three things: **where** the curve is, **how open** the face is, and the **toe shape**.

**Where the curve sits — heel, mid, or toe:**

The puck naturally settles into the deepest part of the curve, and then has to travel from there to the end of the blade as you shoot. That distance is what changes.

- **Heel curve.** The puck starts furthest from the toe, so it spends longest on the blade — **a longer release, with more time to build velocity.** Traditionally favoured for slap shots and by defencemen.
- **Mid curve.** The all-rounder. Good puck spin, balanced across shot types, generally the safest first choice.
- **Toe curve.** The curve is out near the end, which makes it easier to scoop or lift the puck away from someone's stick and easier to **toe-drag** (dragging the puck back toward yourself on the very end of the blade to pull it around a defender's stick). Favoured for wrist shots and puck handling.

**Face angle — open versus closed:**

Hold the stick upright and look down at the blade. If you can see the face of the blade, it is **open**; if you mostly see the top edge, it is **closed**. This is loft, exactly like the difference between a 9-iron and a 3-iron.

- **Open face.** Lifts the puck sooner and with less follow-through — good for scoring in tight, banging in rebounds from the goalmouth, and going **top shelf** (into the upper part of the net, under the crossbar) from close range without space to wind up. It also **disguises your shot**: the blade looks the same whether you are going high or low, and goalies read blade angle. It makes **saucer passes** (a pass flipped through the air to travel over a stick or skate and land flat) easier, and it makes **receiving passes on your backhand** easier, which matters if you play your off-wing.
- **Closed face.** More accurate, because the puck stays square to the blade through the shooting motion. Better puck control and better for long, flat passes — see [Passing and Receiving](../technique/passing_and_receiving.md). Needs a fuller follow-through to elevate, which telegraphs more. Generally the better choice for defencemen and for all-round players.

**The backhand trade-off is unavoidable.** As How To Hockey puts it, "what helps the forehand shot hurts the backhander." A deep, open curve makes forehand elevation easy and makes backhand shooting harder and less accurate — the backhand is fired off the convex side of the blade with nothing to cup the puck. If you care about your backhand, and [Shooting](../technique/shooting.md) argues strongly that you should, favour a shallower, less open pattern. **Developing players in particular are usually better served by a less open pattern**, because it forces you to learn real shooting and stickhandling mechanics rather than relying on loft.

**Toe shape.** A **round toe** gives more blade surface at the end, which many players find easier for toe-drags and puck control. A **square toe** makes it easier to separate a puck from the boards — a real advantage for defencemen digging dump-ins out of the wall — and gives a slightly bigger surface for blocking shots and knocking down passes.

**Blade length** varies by pattern with no industry standard, capped at 12½ inches by rule. A shorter blade releases quicker (less distance for the puck to travel); a longer blade builds more velocity and gives more surface for receiving passes.

### 4. Lie — the least understood variable

**Lie is the angle between the shaft and the blade** — in practice, how upright the shaft stands when the blade is flat on the ice. Pure Hockey's stick guide gives the numbers as running **4 to 8**; its stick-fitting page narrows the practical range to **4.0 to 6.0 in half increments, with most sticks a 4.5, 5.0 or 5.5**. (The two pages disagree on the range; the fitting page is the one describing what is actually on the rack.) The same stick guide converts them: **a lie of 5 corresponds to roughly a 135° angle, and each lie number up or down changes it by about 2°** (a 6 lie ≈ 133°, a 4 lie ≈ 137°). **Higher number, more upright stick.**

**Why it matters.** With the correct lie, the whole bottom edge of your blade contacts the ice when you are in your normal playing stance. With the wrong lie, only the heel or only the toe touches, and everything degrades:

- **Passes you receive skip off** the raised part of the blade instead of settling.
- **Stickhandling gets loose**, because the puck is bouncing on a tilted surface.
- **Shots go off-line**, because the blade meets the puck at an angle you didn't intend.
- **The blade wears unevenly** and dies early.

**How to check yours in ten seconds:** turn your stick over and look at the bottom of the blade. **If one area — usually the toe or the heel — is significantly more worn than the rest, your lie is wrong.** A correct lie wears evenly along the blade.

**Which way to go:**

- **Lower lie** (shaft more angled) suits a player who skates in a deep crouch, is taller, or carries the puck further from the body. Traditionally more common among defencemen.
- **Higher lie** (shaft more upright) suits a player who skates more upright, or who keeps the puck tight to their feet. Traditionally more common among forwards.

Note the interaction: **lie and length are coupled.** Cutting a stick down effectively raises the lie you need, because your hands come down and the shaft angle changes. If you cut four inches off a stick and your blade suddenly rides on its heel, that is why.

### Shaft finish and construction

**Grip versus clear.** A grip (textured) shaft stops the stick spinning in wet gloves — most players prefer it. A clear shaft lets your bottom hand slide freely up and down, which some players want for stickhandling, and it usually needs tape on the shaft to compensate.

**One-piece versus two-piece.** One-piece is the modern standard. A two-piece (separate shaft and blade) lets you mix and match, and lets you salvage half the stick when the other half breaks — a genuine budget option.

**Composite versus wood.** Composite is lighter and engineered to flex at a designed point, for a faster release and more velocity. Wood is heavier, deader and cheaper, arguably easier to receive hard passes with, and comes in far fewer flexes — wood seniors are usually sold as one stiff "senior" flex. *(The often-quoted "90–100 flex" for wood traces to one discontinued model — HockeyWorld lists the Sher-Wood 5030SC Feather-Light Senior at "Flex:90-100", now "Currently Unavailable" — while the CCM Ultimate ABS senior "comes in 85 flex". Treat the numbers as illustrations.)* The CCM Ultimate senior is sold in 85 flex only, and Sherwood's own 5030 senior page offers **no flex option at all**. Pure Hockey's stick guide suggests practising with wood and playing with composite, because the "heavier feeling wood stick will act like a weighted donut on a baseball bat". ⚠️ **Nothing cited here tests whether that transfer happens**, so treat it as a thing to try, not a training method this document stands behind.

### When your stick breaks

**Open your hand where you stand.** A broken stick is one the referee judges *"unfit for normal play"*. You may play on without a stick, but you must drop the broken one immediately. If you move it at all, push it along the ice to the side of the rink, clear of the play. ⚠️ **Never put it over the boards** — it is the tidy instinct, and every book penalises it. Carrying a stick and disposing of one are two different rules, and one motion can satisfy the first and break the second.

**Get a replacement handed to you.** It must come from your bench or from a teammate on the ice. Only the NHL, the IIHF and the PWHL also let you pick an unbroken stick up off the ice; USA Hockey, Hockey Canada and CARHA allow only one from your bench or handed to you by a teammate. A teammate may not throw or shoot one to you — the IIHF adds tossing and sliding — and you may not use an opponent's stick or a goalie's.

**If your goalie's stick breaks, skate a spare out and put it in their hand.** A goalie cannot simply skate to the bench for one. In the meantime a skater's stick is legal under all six books — within goalie-stick measurements under USA Hockey's Casebook (Rule 605, Situation 5), and only until the next stoppage under Hockey Canada 3.2(c) and CARHA 51(c). ⚠️ **Only the NHL, the IIHF and the PWHL let a goalie play on with a broken stick.** The In-House Rules write no rule of their own here and send whatever they do not cover to the IIHF book — *"Unless otherwise stated, all rules will be enforced in line with the IIHF Rule Book"* — **so that permission stands in British play.**

**Carry a spare stick to the bench.** A break with no spare means a stickless shift or a borrowed stick with the wrong flex, curve and lie.

**What it costs.**

- **Carrying it on.** Under **NHL Rule 10.3**, and the PWHL's Rule 10.3 in the same terms, carrying the broken stick or playing the puck with the stub is a **minor penalty**. ⚠️ **Under the In-House Rules the rule is stricter, has no discretion in it, and is new for 2026-27.** The In-House Rules' own **Rule 10.3**: *"Skating with a broken stick anywhere on the ice is not permitted under IHUK In-House Rules. A player whose stick is broken must drop their stick immediately. Failure to do so will incur a 2min penalty for illegal equipment"*. So USA Hockey's casebook allowance for carrying it to your bench when you are close and out of the play is not yours.
- **Putting it over the boards.** All six books let you put the broken portion **to the side of the ice, clear of the play**, with no penalty. **NHL Rule 53.2(iii) Note 2**, **IIHF Rule 53.2(III)** and **PWHL Rule 54.2(iii) Note 2** are word for word: *"When a player discards the broken portion of a stick or some other object by tossing it or shooting it to the side of the ice (and not over the boards) in such a way as to not interfere with the play or opposing player(s), no penalty will be imposed for so doing."* **USA Hockey Rule 637(a) Note**, **Hockey Canada Rule 3.2(a) Note 1** and **CARHA Rule 51(a) Note** say the same.
  - **USA Hockey Rule 637(c)** makes throwing any part of a stick *"outside of the playing area"* a misconduct — ten minutes, which affiliates may shorten for short-period games below Adults. Its Casebook takes this exact case and answers: *"Even though the player may be trying to keep the broken stick off the ice, the Referee has no choice but to assess a misconduct penalty to that player"* (Rule 637, Situation 11). **Hockey Canada Rule 10.5(d)** is a misconduct on the same facts.
  - **The NHL, the PWHL and the IIHF — your book in Britain — are a full tier harsher:** ⚠️ **an intentional throw gets you thrown out of the game.** Their **Rule 53.5** (PWHL 54.5) gives a **game misconduct** to a player who *"intentionally throws"* a stick or any part of it *"outside the playing area"*; an accidental throw is a misconduct under 53.4 (PWHL 54.4). Depositing a broken stick over the glass is intentional on its face, and **the In-House Rules say nothing about throwing equipment out of the playing area**, so IIHF 53.5 is what reaches you. *(USA Hockey 637(c) escalates to a game misconduct for a throw in protest of an official's decision or toward a spectator, while its Casebook at Rule 637, Situation 10 words the trigger as "a violent act or in the direction of any spectators".)*
- **A goalie playing on with it.** **USA Hockey, Hockey Canada and CARHA give a goalie no permission to play on.** USA Hockey's **Rule 605(a)** reaches the goalie by name and makes *"participating in play with a broken stick"* a minor, with no goalie carve-out. *(Its sled hockey rules do let a goalie play on until the next stoppage, but they do not govern stand-up hockey.)* **Hockey Canada Rule 3.2(c)** says it outright — *"A goaltender may not continue to play with a broken stick of any kind"*, a minor — and **CARHA Rule 51(c)** carries the same sentence, a minor under its Rule 51(a).
- **A goalie going to the bench for one.** The NHL, IIHF and PWHL bar it at Rule 10.4; USA Hockey (Rule 605(c)) and CARHA (Rule 51(e)) make going during a stoppage a delay-of-game penalty; and Hockey Canada makes a goalie who goes to the bench for equipment a delay-of-game minor (Rule 10.1) unless the backup replaces them.
- **A slid stick, under Hockey Canada.** ⚠️ **The goalie must leave it where it lies.** Interpretation 1 to Rule 3.2(c) gives a goalie who picks it up a minor for illegal equipment, and nothing *"if they do not pick up the stick."* ⚠️ **The skater who slid it faces worse than a minor:** the same interpretation gives them a minor for throwing the stick, and adds *"A Penalty Shot may be awarded under this rule, if the player throws, shoots, kicks, or slides the stick at the puck or puck carrier in their defending zone."* Full treatment is in [Rules Primer](../foundation/rules_primer.md).

## Protective Fit — Where the Gaps Are

The general principle: **protective gear that is too big protects less, not more.** Oversized pads slide out of position on impact, so the pad arrives somewhere other than where the puck does. They also drag against each other, restrict your stride and shooting motion, and expose the joints they were bought to cover.

### How each piece should sit

- **Shoulder pads.** **The tips of your shoulders must be centred in the shoulder caps.** Try them on standing, with your pants — there should be a small gap between the bottom of the pads and the top of the pants when you stand upright, because you will be bent forward in your skating stance and that gap closes. Sized by chest measurement plus height or weight.
- **Elbow pads.** Sized by measuring from the cuff of your glove to the middle of your bicep. Try them on **with your shoulder pads and gloves on** — that is the only way to see whether the three pieces actually meet. Tighten the straps so the velcro stays fastened under your jersey through a full game, and so the pad **cannot rotate** if you fall on it.
- **Gloves.** Sized in inches from your fingertip to the end of your elbow pad — typically 8" youth to 15" senior. Round up if you are between sizes. The **cuff of the glove should meet the bottom of your elbow pad with no gap**. Your fingertips should just reach the end without being forced.
- **Pants.** Sized close to your jeans/waist size, confirmed by measuring your waist just above the hips. For length, Pure Hockey's fitting guide is explicit: standing upright, **"the bottom of the pants should overlap the kneecap area of the shin guards by roughly one-third to halfway."** Reaching the kneecap is not enough — you want the pants sitting *over* the top of the guard, because the gap opens as soon as you bend into a stance.
- **Shin guards.** Put your skates on and tie them, sit with your feet flat and your knees bent to 90°, and measure **from the top of the skate tongue to the centre of your kneecap**. **If you wear the guard over the skate tongue, add about an inch** on top of that. Your **kneecap must sit centred in the knee cup at all times** — strap them tight enough that they cannot rotate during play.

### Three gaps between pieces of kit, and how to close each one

Protective gear only protects you where its pieces overlap. Fitting guides keep returning to three overlaps, and each is checkable at home. ⚠️ **That these three matter most is fitting-guide emphasis, not a counted ranking** — nothing cited here classifies hockey injuries by the gap they came through.

**1. The gap between shin guard and pants.** This is the classic. Pants that are too short, shin guards that are too short, or both, leave a strip of bare thigh just above the knee — precisely where a rising puck, a stick and the top of the boards all arrive. **The pants must overlap the top of the shin guard while you are in a skating stance, not while you are standing in a shop.** Bend into a hockey stance in the changing room and check. If a gap opens, one of the two pieces is wrong.

**2. Elbow pad rotation.** An elbow pad that spins on your arm when you fall puts its hard cap on the inside of your arm and presents bare joint to the ice. It is how a player breaks or badly bruises an elbow while wearing an elbow pad — **that is the mechanism, not a measured frequency; nothing cited here counts how often it happens.** It is a strap-tension and sizing problem, and it is entirely preventable: strap it so you cannot twist it by hand.

**3. Wrist exposure.** The strip between the top of the glove cuff and the bottom of the elbow pad. Slashes land there, and it is the part of your arm you cannot see. Both the IIHF and USA Hockey now **recommend cut-resistant sleeves or undergarments** for exactly this reason — the IIHF "strongly" (Rule 9.5), USA Hockey simply "recommended" (Rule 304(g)). Long elbow pads and correctly sized gloves close the gap for free; a cut-resistant sleeve closes it properly.

A fourth worth noting: **tendon guards** — the padding at the back of the ankle above the boot — are on USA Hockey's list of equipment you are responsible for. Most modern skates include them; check that yours does and that your socks are not holding them out of position.

## Helmets and Facial Protection

### Certification

Three separate systems, one for each of the big markets. **The mark you need is determined by where you play, not by where the helmet was made.**

**United States — HECC.** The **Hockey Equipment Certification Council** is a US non-profit whose sticker American leagues reference. *(HECC's homepage says it was "created at the request of USA Hockey in 1978"; USA Hockey's own article "What the Heck is HECC?" dates it to 1976 — "The Hockey Equipment Certification Council (HECC), organized in 1976…" — and does not mention requesting it. HECC's account is used here.)* HECC does not write standards; it certifies against **ASTM** ones, which is what most "check the sticker" advice leaves out:

| Item | Standard HECC certifies to | Title |
|---|---|---|
| Helmets | **ASTM F1045** (HECC currently certifies to F1045-22; ASTM's current edition is **F1045-24**) | *Standard Performance Specification for Ice Hockey Helmets* |
| Face protectors | **ASTM F513-22** | *Standard Specification for Eye and Face Protective Equipment for Hockey Players* |
| Goalie head and face | **ASTM F1587-22** | *Standard Specification for Head and Face Protective Equipment for Ice Hockey Goaltenders* |
| Visors | **ASTM F513-22** on HECC's certified-equipment list — but its testing page says CAN/CSA Z262.2; see below | *Standard Specification for Eye and Face Protective Equipment for Hockey Players* |
| Neck laceration protectors | ISO/DIS 10256-5 and CAN/BNQ 9415-370 | — |

- **A visor's HECC certification carries an age condition, and it is HECC's rather than any rulebook's.** HECC files visors as *"Eye and Face Protectors Type C"* and certifies them *"only for persons age 18 and over"*. [Facial protection](#facial-protection) above sets out that scope in full, and what it does and does not settle under each rule set.
- **HECC's own two pages disagree about which standard a visor is certified against, so do not repeat either as settled.** Its certified-equipment list files every visor against **ASTM F513** — the same standard it lists against a full cage. Its certification-testing page says the opposite: full-face protectors are evaluated to ASTM F513, and *"the difference is the standard used to evaluate the product, visors are evaluated to CAN/CSA Z262.2."* Nothing published reconciles them. **What you actually check is unaffected** — look for the HECC mark itself, and for the Type C age scope above.
- **HECC certification expires.** This is written into the USA Hockey rulebook itself: "HECC certification includes an expiration date on the sticker and a helmet that has an expiration date that has expired is no longer considered certified. The player may not wear a helmet that does not have a valid and current certification sticker." HECC's own site gives the interval: **"The expiration date of the label is 6.5 years after the date of manufacture."** That figure is confirmed for **helmets**. **HECC-certified neck laceration protectors are five years, not 6.5** — HECC published that as a correction to a previously stated seven-year figure.

**Canada — CSA, and it is mandatory.** Only CSA-certified helmets are approved for Hockey Canada play; HECC certification **does not apply in Canada**, and Hockey Canada says so explicitly. The standards are:

- **CSA Z262.1:26** — *Protective equipment for use in ice hockey — Head protectors for skaters*, which adopts ISO 10256-2:2024 with Canadian deviations. CSA Group's store now lists the edition it replaced, Z262.1:15 (reaffirmed 2025), as withdrawn.
- **CSA Z262.2:15 (R2025)** — *Face protectors for use in ice hockey*, published 2015 and reaffirmed 2025. CSA Group's store now lists this edition as withdrawn too, and its page names no successor.

**Hockey Canada Rule 3.6(b)** covers the cage as well as the helmet: *"While on the ice, including pre-game warm-ups, all players, including goaltenders, will wear a CSA-certified hockey helmet, to which a CSA-certified facial protector must be securely attached and not altered in any way. Any alteration to a CSA-certified helmet or facial protector automatically destroys the certification."* ⚠️ **On the helmet that is flat; on the facial protector the same rule's Note 2 carves out one group**, making it *"recommended but optional for male adult recreational hockey"*, and the rulebook does not say which governs. See [Facial protection](#facial-protection) above. **A certified facial protector satisfies both readings and cannot be wrong.** There is also **federal law**: the *Ice Hockey Helmet Regulations* **SOR/2016-186**, made under the *Canada Consumer Product Safety Act*, require helmets sold in Canada to *"meet the requirements of standard Z262.1… as amended from time to time"* — an open reference that tracks CSA's current edition.

**Europe — CE marking against EN ISO 10256.** The IIHF rulebook names no certification body, requiring only "a helmet manufactured and approved specifically for ice hockey", but the CE mark you see on equipment sold in Europe now has a specific standard behind it:

- **EN ISO 10256-1:2024** — *General requirements*
- **EN ISO 10256-2:2024** — *Head protectors for skaters* (the helmet standard). *It supersedes **EN ISO 10256-2:2018**: the Polish standards body PKN, a CEN member, marks PN-EN ISO 10256-2:2018-08 **"Wycofana i zastąpiona przez PN-EN ISO 10256-2:2025-06"** — withdrawn and replaced — with a **withdrawal date of 11 June 2025**, and ISO lists ISO 10256-2:2016 as **Withdrawn** with "New version available: ISO 10256-2:2024". **Watch the edition years:** EN ISO 10256-2:**2018** is the European adoption of ISO 10256-2:**2016**; there is no 2018 ISO edition. The 2018 EN was never harmonised under the PPE Regulation, so its absence from the list below is expected.*
- **EN ISO 10256-3:2024** — *Face and eye protectors for skaters*
- **EN ISO 10256-4:2024** — *Head and face protectors for goalkeepers*

All four were cited in the *Official Journal* by **Commission Implementing Decision (EU) 2026/1279 of 12 June 2026**, giving **presumption of conformity with the EU PPE Regulation (EU) 2016/425** from **16 June 2026**. That is very recent — no earlier edition of EN ISO 10256 appears in the harmonised list at all — so equipment on shelves may still predate it.

**Britain — no certification mark is specified anywhere, so buy for fit and condition, not for a sticker.** Buy hockey-specific gear sold into the European market, CE-marked against EN ISO 10256 where the mark applies, keep it unmodified, and fit it as the manufacturer says. A HECC or CSA sticker is no problem here, but British referees check something else: whether the item is present, unaltered and correctly fitted. **Ask your league what it accepts, and if you tend goal, ask before you buy a mask.**

The IIHF book names no **outside** certification body: Rule 9.7 asks for "a helmet manufactured and approved specifically for ice hockey", Rule 9.6 for "a helmet of design, material and construction approved for ice hockey", and Rule 9.12 for a protector "designed for this specific purpose". "Certified", "certification", "HECC", "CSA", "BNQ", "ASTM" and "10256" appear nowhere in either edition. England Ice Hockey's R&R 24.3 asks only for "an approved Ice Hockey Neck Guard/Protector", worn "without alterations and as directed by the manufacturers' specification", and 24.1 for equipment to "confirm to IIHF safety standards".

**What it costs.** ⚠️ **The IIHF book does write approval by itself, and you can be sent off the ice under it.** **Rule 9.8 – Dangerous Equipment** covers *"[e]quipment that does not conform to IIHF standards"* and lists *"wearing non-approved equipment"*: the player goes off, the team is warned, and after that everyone on it wearing such equipment is penalised. **Rule 11.8** requires goalies' face masks to be *"of a design approved by the IIHF"*. None of these names a mark, a standard or a list, and "approved" does not appear in the In-House Rules at all, which name no standard number either — so nothing domestic tells you how approval is established.

**Neck protectors are where the three systems diverge most, and the Canadian rule is the strict one.** ⚠️ **If you play in Canada, or buy for a child who does, treat BNQ as a requirement.** **Hockey Canada Rule 3.6(c)**: *"[t]he wearing of a BNQ-certified throat protector is required for players registered in minor and female hockey"*, and *"[g]oaltenders who wear an attachment to the mask or helmet designed to protect the throat, must still wear a BNQ-certified throat protector"* — a dangler is not a substitute. The standard is **CAN/BNQ 9415-370**, *Neck Protectors for Ice Hockey and Ringette Players*. USA Hockey merely **accepts** BNQ protectors alongside HECC ones through the 2026-27 season.

**If you tend goal,** ⚠️ **wear a certified protector whatever your division.** The rulebook does not say whether that goalie sentence carries the minor-and-female scope, and Hockey Canada publishes no interpretation. It is read here as carrying it, because Hockey Canada's Glossary already counts a goalie as a player, so the sentence closes the dangler loophole rather than adding an obligation. **But the other reading is not a misreading**, so if you tend goal outside minor and female hockey, ask your association.

***Change in progress — check before you buy, and check again before 2027-28.*** ⚠️ **BNQ is closing the BNQ 9415-370 certification programme on 31 July 2027 and has said it will not open a replacement.** Its customer notice of **6 August 2026** reads: *"following the recent publication of the international standard ISO 10256-5, the BNQ conducted a review of its Program and decided to discontinue it. Likewise, the BNQ has decided not to implement a new certification program based on the new ISO 10256-5 standard."* Until then *"[n]o new applications for certification will be accepted"*, and existing certificates hold *"until their expiry date or until the end of the Program, whichever occurs first"*. A Standards Council of Canada notice of 7 May 2024 also records the standard itself as being withdrawn, naming no successor.

Keep two things apart: **a successor *standard* exists; a successor BNQ *certification programme* does not.** The standard is **CAN/BNQ 0102-565/2023 (ISO 10256-5: 2017, MOD)**, *Protective Equipment for Use in Ice Hockey — Part 5: Neck Laceration Protectors for Ice Hockey Players*, a National Standard of Canada dated **7 February 2023**. In 2024 BNQ said a new programme *"will be announced later on our website"*; what arrived instead was the closure notice.

**Hockey Canada Rule 3.6(c) still requires a "BNQ-certified" throat protector** in minor and female hockey, and names no standard number. ⚠️ **Nothing published says what Hockey Canada will require once BNQ stops issuing certificates — and the closure is not permission to skip the protector.** **Buy a BNQ-certified protector now, keep the label legible, and ask your association before the 2027-28 season what it will accept after 31 July 2027.**

**Check the sticker before you buy, and especially before you accept a hand-me-down.** A cheap clearance helmet is often cheap because its certification has expired, which makes it illegal in USA Hockey play at every age classification except Adults — Rule 304(c) requires a HECC-certified helmet, and the rulebook's own Note says an expired sticker "is no longer considered certified" — even though the helmet is physically fine.

### Cage versus visor versus full shield

- **Cage.** Metal bars. Maximum protection, no fogging or scratching, full airflow; almost everyone stops noticing the bars within a few sessions. **The default unless you have a reason to wear something else** — though below adult level a certified full shield is legal too: USA Hockey Rule 304(d) asks for a **HECC-certified facemask**, and IIHF women's and U18 hockey ask for **full-face protection**, not a cage. **Junior hockey in England and Wales is the exception**: England Ice Hockey's R&R 24.5 makes **face cages mandatory for all junior games**, so a full shield is not a substitute there.
- **Visor (half shield).** Clear polycarbonate over the eyes and nose. Best optical clarity, no chin or mouth protection — wear a mouthguard. The IIHF requires at least a visor of everyone born after 31 December 1974, fixed so it cannot flip up, and bans tinted ones so opponents and officials can make eye contact.
  - **In Britain:** in women's hockey a half visor is permitted only from the season after the player's 18th birthday, so **a woman buys full face until then, and a player in IHUK's junior leagues wears full face whatever their age, Under-19 included — a cage, in England and Wales.** A half visor is the **minimum** in SIHA Recreational and University hockey *"regardless of their date of birth"*, and in BUIHA hockey. **The women's permission is a single In-House sentence that never says it lifts the IIHF's full-face requirement, so full face is the only choice that cannot be wrong for an adult woman, and a cage the only one for a junior.** See [UK and England Rules](../foundation/uk_rules.md).
  - **Under USA Hockey**, HECC certifies visors — *"Eye and Face Protectors Type C"* — *"only for persons age 18 and over"*, so under 18 the HECC-certified facemask Rule 304(d) requires is a cage or a full shield. **HECC recommends full face at every age**, because a visor gives *"no protection for the mouth, teeth, lower face, and jaw."*
- **Full shield (full visor).** A clear polycarbonate cage-equivalent. Full facial coverage with better sightlines than a cage; can fog and can scratch. Permitted by IIHF as one of the three facial-protection types.
- **Combination cage/shield** hybrids exist and split the difference.

**A cage or shield is sized separately from the helmet.** A medium helmet does not guarantee a medium cage, even in a combo package — fit both. **Under the In-House Rules this carries a tariff:** *"Helmet–facemask combinations must match manufacturer sizing. Mis-sized combinations are considered Dangerous Equipment, and the player will be removed until corrected."* Come back with the same violation and it is a **Minor Penalty – Illegal Equipment**, then **10-minute misconducts, the second of which is a Game Misconduct.** The Elite League's Casebook writes no sizing rule, but that is not a permission: it defaults to the IIHF book, whose Rule 9.8 sends a player with *"Dangerous Equipment"* off the ice and warns their team, penalising everyone on it wearing the same after that. The same rule tells teams to carry spare screws, straps, clips and a spare facemask or visor — worth a corner of the bag.

### The fit test

1. **Measure** your head circumference with a soft tape about ½"–1" above your eyebrows. Your fitted hat size is a usable proxy.
2. **Open the helmet to its largest adjustment**, put it on, and then tighten it down gradually until it is snug. Most modern helmets adjust with side tabs or a rear dial, and adjacent sizes overlap substantially — a small at maximum is close to a medium at minimum.
3. **The front edge should sit about ½ inch above your eyebrows**, flat and level. It must not tilt forward, slide back, or wobble.
4. **Shake your head.** The helmet should move with your skull, not on it. If it shifts, it is too big. If it creates pressure points or a headache, it is too small.
5. **Wear it for a few minutes** before you commit. Hot spots reveal themselves slowly.
6. **Fit the cage.** **Chin centred in the chin cup, and the cup strapped snugly against your chin** — Pure Hockey says the cage should "fit snugly around the chin cup", with no gap figure. A cage that doesn't close to your chin lets the whole helmet shift when you are hit. Looking straight ahead, your eyes should be **between the bars**. **In Britain it is written down:** the chin cup must be *"properly fitted and in contact with the chin, within the helmet's J-clips"*, and loose or dangling cages and straps are banned. Referees allow small compromises only where adjustment cannot improve the fit.
7. **Chin strap fastened, with no more than a finger's gap.** Unfastened, a helmet is a hat. ⚠️ **If your helmet comes off in play, leave the ice — do not put it back on.** In Britain a coach or manager must check helmet and facemask fit, chin straps **and neck laceration protection** before the warm-up (In-House Rule 5.4), so expect to be asked.

   **What it costs.** Almost every rule set requires the strap fastened whenever you are on the ice (the NHL and PWHL, Rule 9.6, write it only for a player returning from the bench or penalty box). **Three books set a fit test, all one finger.** In Britain the gap must not exceed **2.5 cm — about one finger** (In-House Rule 9.8, and England Ice Hockey's R&R 24.4): the first offence sends the player to adjust and warns the **team**, and **once your team has been warned, a loose strap is a 10-minute misconduct, even if it is your first.** USA Hockey's Casebook sets **one finger width** (Rule 304, Situation 14): off the ice until corrected, a team warning, then a misconduct for the next violation. For a helmet that comes off, In-House Rule 9.8 requires you to *"immediately leave the ice"*, and failure *"will be penalised, with a misconduct penalty, under Rule 12 – Illegal Equipment."* The IIHF book has the same duty with a minor; the NHL allows a refit *"within a reasonable period of time"*.

### Replacing a helmet after an impact

**A helmet that has taken a serious impact should be replaced.** Protective foam absorbs energy by crushing, and crushed foam does not un-crush, so a helmet that looks fine can hide a compressed liner and hairline shell damage. The same goes for one dropped hard onto concrete, left in a hot car, or worn well past its certification date.

A manufacturer and a governing body both say it outright. **Bauer's own helmet manual:** *"Helmet and liners should be inspected prior to each use and the helmet must be replaced if the foam or internal attachments have broken down. The helmets must be discarded if the shell is cracked or deformed. **If helmet is severely impacted, it should be replaced immediately.** Exposure to violent impact or stress may reduce its protective function."*

**Hockey Canada's helmet-certification bulletin:** *"Helmets that are cracked, have loose fittings or missing liner pieces, or that have been subjected to a severe impact should be replaced."* It adds that neither sticker measures lifespan: *"There is currently no expiration on CSA certification and the date on the CSA sticker represents the year the helmet was manufactured"*, and the HECC date *"is related only to the length of certification granted by HECC for a given helmet and is not an indication of the lifespan of a hockey helmet."*

Three things worth being honest about:

- **There is no concussion-proof helmet, and a helmet is only partly effective against the rotational accelerations behind most concussions.** ⚠️ **That comes from a retailer's buying guide rather than from concussion research.** Discount Hockey's helmet comparison: *"No two impacts in hockey are exactly the same, which makes it impossible for manufacturers to create a 'concussion-proof' helmet."* The same page says Bauer's suspension liners and CCM's liquid-filled bladders are *"designed to minimize brain movement during rotational impact - the most common type of concussion-causing impacts"*, and that the likelihood of concussion in an expensive helmet *"should be vastly lower (but still possible)"* than in a cheap one. Pure Hockey's helmet guide adds that *"there is no such thing as a perfect or totally injury-proof helmet."* **The risk never goes to zero.**
- **Helmets are extremely good at preventing skull fractures and lacerations.** ⚠️ **Nothing cited here establishes that, so read *"extremely"* as unmeasured.** Discount Hockey says only that *"[a]ll hockey helmets are primarily designed to protect the skull and brain"* — what a helmet is for, not how well it does it. **A helmet does the job it was built for and only part of the hardest one. That is the reason to wear it, and the reason not to play as though you are wearing armour.**
- **Spending sense.** Pure Hockey's helmet guide puts it sharply: it makes little sense to spend $200–$250 on a stick and $600–$800 on skates, then protect your head with a $60 bucket. (That is their picture of a keen player's spend; scale it to what you paid.) Helmets in a US retail catalogue have ranged from about $27 to about $300. Buy the best-fitting helmet you can afford, and fit before features.

## Goalie Equipment — An Overview

Goaltending gear is a different sport's worth of kit. This section is deliberately a summary; see [Goaltender](../positions/goaltender.md) for how it is actually used.

**The list:** mask (with optional throat protector / "dangler" hanging from the chin), chest and arm protector, leg pads, catch glove (trapper), blocker, goalie pants, knee and thigh protectors, goalie jock, goalie skates, and a goalie stick.

**Three things a skater should know about it:**

**1. It is sized completely differently.** Goalie leg pads are not sized like shin guards. Sizing starts from a **floor-to-knee measurement taken seated**, plus barefoot height, plus your skate size, and the fit is judged by **where your knee lands in the knee cradle** in both your stance and your **butterfly** (the save position where the goalie drops to both knees and fans the pads flat across the ice) — not by height or age. Pads also come in **+1" and +2"** thigh-rise options, adding an inch or two at the top for extra **five-hole** coverage (the five-hole is the gap between a goalie's legs) in the butterfly, at some cost in mobility. Unlike a chest protector, which should be snug and shouldn't shift, **leg pads should be secure but able to rotate freely** into and out of the butterfly.

**2. It is heavily regulated by dimension.** Where skater equipment rules are mostly about safety, goalie equipment rules are about how much net you are allowed to cover. NHL Rule 11.2 caps leg pads at **11 inches** in extreme width and sets each goalie an individually calculated maximum height based on their floor-to-knee and knee-to-pelvis measurements. Rule 11.5 caps knee protection at a contoured **9 inches**. USA Hockey Rule 303 caps leg guards at **11 inches wide and 38 inches long**, the blocker at **8 × 15 inches**, and the catch glove at an 8-inch cuff, 45-inch circumference and 18-inch catching portion. IIHF Rule 9.11 requires goalie skates to have a **protective cowling** at the front of each boot and bans "cheaters" added to gain ice contact. **It is compulsory for all goalies to wear helmets and full facemasks** (USA Hockey Rule 303(b)); no form-fitted facemasks are permitted.

**3. It is a much larger investment.** A full set of goalie gear costs several times a skater's kit, wears out on a similar timescale, and is far harder to buy secondhand in the right size. Many clubs own a set of house goalie gear for exactly this reason — ask before you buy, **with one exception, which is the next paragraph.**

**The mask is the exception to that:** ⚠️ **own it rather than borrow it, because a goalie mask is a helmet.** The rules say so: **USA Hockey Rule 303(b)** makes helmets and full facemasks *"compulsory for all goalkeepers"* at every age, and **Rules 304(c) and (d)** require a HECC-certified helmet and facemask of *"[a]ll players, including goalkeepers"* below Adults. **Hockey Canada Rule 3.6(b)** covers *"all players, including goaltenders"* with a CSA-certified helmet and facial protector, and *"[a]ny alteration to a CSA-certified helmet or facial protector automatically destroys the certification."* So [New versus used](#new-versus-used) and [Replacing a helmet after an impact](#replacing-a-helmet-after-an-impact) apply to a borrowed mask exactly as to a borrowed helmet — and **a club mask has been on more heads and stopped more pucks than any hand-me-down, with nobody recording which shot landed where.** The certification date is checkable; the impact history is not.

**So borrow the pads, blocker, trapper, chest and arm protector and pants to find out whether you like the position — and get your own mask.** Ask a club mask's age and impact history anyway, but expect the honest answer to be that nobody has kept track — and **a mask nobody can account for is one you do not put your head in.** *(Applying the used-helmet reasoning to a goalie mask is a reading of the certification rules above, not a sentence any rule book writes — no book distinguishes a loaned mask from an owned one.)*

One rule does run the goalie's way, and it is worth knowing because the skater version is different: under **USA Hockey Rule 304(e)**, if a goalie's helmet or facemask comes off during play *"the officials shall stop play immediately"* — and, unlike a skater, *"the goalkeeper shall not be ruled off the ice prior to the face-off."* Deliberately removing it to cause that stoppage is a minor for delay of game.

## Buying Strategy

### New versus used

**Safe to buy used**, provided you check fit and condition:

- **Sticks** — as long as the length, flex, curve and lie are right for you, and there are no cracks, soft spots or rattles. A cheap way to try a curve you have never used.
- **Pants, shin guards, shoulder pads, elbow pads** — inspect for cracked plastic caps, compressed foam and broken straps, then wash them.
- **Bags, socks, jerseys** — obviously.
- **Skates**, with real caveats: only if they fit you properly (the boot has already moulded to somebody else's foot, which is the problem), the quarter package is not broken down, the eyelets are intact, and there is enough steel left. A well-cared-for second-tier boot in the right size is a bargain; a broken-down boot in the wrong size is worse than useless.

**Do not buy used:**

- **Never buy a used helmet — and that includes a goalie mask, which is a helmet.** This is the firm one on the list. You cannot see a compressed liner or a hairline crack, you usually cannot know the impact history, and the certification may have expired — which under USA Hockey Rule 304(c), reaching *"[a]ll players, **including goalkeepers**"*, makes it illegal below Adults as well as unsafe. A sibling's hand-me-down has the same problems, and **so does a club's shared house mask** — see [Goalie Equipment](#goalie-equipment--an-overview). ⚠️ **And the label itself can be fake.** CSA Group reports that most counterfeit CSA-certified helmets are *"purchased online directly from manufacturers or retailers, or at used sporting goods stores and direct sellers"*, and that a helmet which *"feels unusually light in weight, flimsy, has loose parts, or appears to be modified"* needs checking **even if it carries a CSA label** — look the model up in CSA Group's own product listing before you trust the sticker.
- **Never buy anything with an expired certification sticker** — helmets, facemasks, neck guards.
- **Never buy a used mouthguard or jock/jill.** For reasons that need no explanation.
- **Never buy a cage or visor with deep scratches or cracks.** A cracked visor must be replaced; the IIHF requires a player whose visor cracks in play to leave the ice immediately.

**At a taster session, take your own helmet if you have one.** Taster sessions hand out club helmets, and a loaner pool — a one-off taster, or a club's loan-and-hire shelf — means a helmet that has been on other heads with nobody logging what it stopped. **USA Hockey's Try Hockey For Free site answers this in your favour:** *"Most host locations will have loaner equipment, however, you are welcome to bring your own if you have it"*. **So take a helmet if you have one, ask the programme before the session if you do not, and make a helmet the first thing you buy.**

**Where the real choice is a loaner helmet or no ice at all,** ⚠️ **that decision belongs to the parent and the programme.** Nothing published says a loaner helmet is safe for a single supervised session, and nothing rules it out; the sticker is checkable and the impact history is not. Kit **given to keep** is a separate question — NHL/NHLPA Learn to Play and The First Shift both supply head-to-toe equipment, and [Getting Started](../getting-started/getting_started.md) found that neither Learn to Play's own pages nor Hockey Canada's say it is new, so ask the programme.

**Where the used-helmet rule comes from.** No rule book cited in this document writes it. The NHL, IIHF, USA Hockey and its Casebook, Hockey Canada, CARHA, the IHUK In-House Rules and England Ice Hockey's Rules & Regulations were each searched in full, including across line breaks, for *second-hand*, *secondhand*, *used helmet*, *hand-me-down*, *loaner*, *reconditioned*, *refurbished*, *previously owned* and *borrowed helmet*, and every string returns zero in every book. Hockey Canada's bulletin goes only as far as *"[p]articipants should exercise good judgment as to the suitability of a hockey helmet for play"*, and CSA Group's helmet FAQ treats a used helmet as something to verify rather than refuse.

**So the used-helmet rule here is a firm caution, not a law of hockey.** It rests on what those sources do say — a severely impacted helmet must be replaced, and alteration or an expired sticker voids certification — plus the fact that nothing lets you read a helmet's impact history off the outside of it.

### Where to spend and where to save

**Spend on, in this order:**

1. **Skates** — the biggest performance difference in the bag, and the one piece where a bad purchase actively holds back your development.
2. **Helmet** — worth spending on because it carries the biggest consequence of any piece in the bag.
3. **Stick** — but spend on getting the *specification* right first. A correctly specified $80 stick beats a wrongly specified $300 one, every time.
4. **Gloves** — you feel the puck through them, and cheap gloves wear through at the palm quickly.

**Save on:**

- **Socks, base layers, bag, tape** — commodity items.
- **Shin guards and elbow pads** — fit dominates; the model barely matters at recreational level.
- **Pants** — unless you play a contact league, mid-range is plenty.
- **Shoulder pads** — in a **non-checking** adult league, lightweight pads are entirely adequate. In a checking league this moves up the priority list substantially.

### Kitting out a beginner on a budget

1. **Ask the club first.** Many associations and rinks loan gear to new players or hold a used-gear swap, and goalie gear is often club-owned. **Some beginner programmes supply kit outright** — [Getting Started](../getting-started/getting_started.md) names them and what each includes — so check before you buy. Whether kit you are given to keep is new is unresolved — no source consulted here confirms it either way — so ask the programme. ⚠️ **Two things a loan or a swap must never cover: a helmet, and a goalie mask, which is a helmet.** Borrow the pads and buy the head protection new — see [New versus used](#new-versus-used) and [Goalie Equipment](#goalie-equipment--an-overview). At a one-off taster, take your own helmet or ask the host site first.
2. **Buy last season's models.** Manufacturers refresh lines annually; last year's mid-range boot at clearance is usually the best value in the shop.
3. **Get properly fitted in person for skates and helmet**, even if you buy online afterwards. These are the two you cannot guess.
4. **Buy the rest used or entry-level**, and buy fit over features — but "the rest" excludes the do-not-buy-used list above: **helmets and goalie masks, facemasks and neck guards (new, every time), mouthguards, jocks/jills, and any cage or visor with deep scratches or cracks.**
5. **Buy a mouthguard and a neck guard.** They cost almost nothing. **The neck guard is required in every British league, for every player of every age** (IIHF Rule 9.12). ⚠️ **The mouthguard is not, and it varies by competition:** the IIHF recommends it for everyone and requires it only of a men's Under-20 player without full-face protection (Rules 9.13 and 202.3), the Elite League requires one of every skater, and the WNIHL of anyone in a half visor. Ask your league — and wear one either way.
6. **Buy two rolls of tape and a puck.** You will use both immediately.

### The order to upgrade in

1. **Skates**, once you are skating more than about once a week — move up to a mid-range, bakeable boot.
2. **Stick specification** — get length, flex, lie and curve right. This costs nothing extra if you buy the right stick next time.
3. **Helmet**, if yours is old, expired, or doesn't fit properly.
4. **Gloves**, when the palms go.
5. **Steel** — better runners on a decent holder are a cheap upgrade to an existing skate, and having two sets of steel doubles your time between sharpenings.
6. **Everything else**, when it wears out.

## Maintenance and Care

### Drying — the one habit that matters

**Take everything out of the bag as soon as you get home. Every time.** Wet gear left zipped in a bag is a warm, dark, humid environment full of organic material, which is exactly what bacteria and fungi want. As How To Hockey puts it: "The longer you leave wet ANYTHING in your bag, the more it will stink."

The smell is the visible symptom; the real reasons are more serious. Damp gear degrades faster — foam breaks down, stitching rots, steel rusts. And hockey supplies a steady stream of small cuts, blisters and abrasions, the openings skin bacteria need. The NHS notes that staph and MRSA live harmlessly on skin, spread mainly by touch, and cause infection only when they get in through a break in the skin. A *Sports Medicine* review by Tlougan and colleagues reports that the infections most often seen in ice-skating athletes are *"tinea pedis, onychomycosis, pitted keratolysis, warts and folliculitis"*, and that *"[a]wareness, prompt treatment and the use of preventative measures are particularly important in managing such dermatoses that are easily spread from person to person in training facilities."* Its prevention advice matches this page: keep gear ventilated and dry, keep your skin clean, and wear sandals in shared changing areas. *(The review establishes which infections occur and how to prevent them; there is no published infection **rate** attributable to unwashed equipment.)*

**Practical routine:**

- **Hang everything** — a drying rack, a spare rail, or hooks in a garage. **Run a fan.** Airflow does more than time.
- **Air-dry base layers**; wash them after every single use.
- **Full wash every few weeks.** How To Hockey's routine: "every piece of equipment in the wash except for your skates and helmet", on the longest cycle with a pre-wash and an extra rinse, and **no chlorine bleach**, which "can deteriorate some of the bonding agents and foams in your equipment"; oxygen bleach is fine. It says the result lasts "3-6 weeks before you'll need to repeat" — **a claim about the smell, not about the foam.** ⚠️ **Check each item's care label before you machine-wash it.**
- **Wash the neck guard exactly as its label says — your rule book ties you to it.** Bauer's neck- and wrist-laceration-protector booklet, the only hockey care instruction traced to a manufacturer here, permits the machine "at cold temperature (30°C) with like colors", fasteners closed, and then: "[d]o not use bleach, detergents with bleach or fabric softeners. Do not dry clean. Reshape and lay flat to dry. Do not tumble dry, do not iron and do not expose to direct sunlight." **A long hot cycle and a tumble dry both sit outside that.** It matters most for the neck guard, because England Ice Hockey's R&R 24.3 requires equipment worn "without alterations and as directed by the manufacturers’ specification", and Bauer certifies its protectors only "provided they have not been reconditioned or altered in any way". No manufacturer instruction forbidding machine washing was found for anything on this list, and Bauer publishes no care booklet for shoulder, elbow or shin pads — so this is *check the label*, not *don't wash it*.
- **Wipe helmets and skates by hand.** Anti-bacterial spray on already-dry gear helps with odour.
- **Shower promptly after you play**, and don't share towels.

### Blades, holders and rust

- **Wipe your steel and holders dry after every skate, before the skates go back in the bag.** Water sitting on steel is what rusts it.
- **Use soft blade covers (soakers)** — the terry lining absorbs residual moisture. Hard guards let you walk on concrete but do nothing about rust; some soft guards with a rubberised sole do both. **Never store skates in soakers**, though; take them off once the blades are dry.
- **Air the skates out at home**, and if you are being thorough, **pull the footbeds out** so moisture isn't trapped underneath.
- **Replace your steel** when it is chipped, cracked or broken (usually from a blocked shot or a step onto concrete), when repeated cross-grinding has worn it very short, or when rust has eaten into it.
- **Never walk on concrete, tile or metal in bare blades.** One step ruins an edge and can gouge the steel.

### Laces, tape and general lifespan

- **Replace skate laces before they break**, which they will do in the changing room five minutes before a game. Waxed laces hold tension better and let you tie sections of the boot at different tightnesses; unwaxed laces give a more uniform, looser feel. Keep a spare pair in the bag.
- **Re-tape the blade when the tape wears through** — some players re-tape before every game, which is preference, not necessity. Clean the adhesive residue off first with warm water and mild dish soap, or by warming it with a hairdryer and wiping it away.
- **Rough lifespans**, which vary enormously with use: **tape** — a game or a few; **laces** — a season; **sticks** — unpredictable, and they break without warning; **skates** — entry-level boots break down in a couple of seasons, good boots last years with care; **helmets** — replace at certification expiry or after a significant impact, whichever comes first; **protective pads** — until the foam is compressed, the plastic is cracked, or you have outgrown them.

## Taping

Tape does four things: it protects the blade from wear, it stops water and ice building up on it, it gives the puck something with a bit of grip to sit against, and — on the shaft — it gives you a knob and a grip that your hands can find without looking.

**There is genuinely no right way to tape a stick.** Players tape differently because they want different things, and the variation is preference, not technique.

### Blade taping

The mechanics that are not preference: **wrap smoothly, overlap evenly, and don't let it wrinkle or bunch.** An uneven tape job gives you an unpredictable blade surface, which shows up on receptions and on shots. Trim creases and toe flaps off with scissors.

Most players start by laying a single strip **around the toe edge** — over the top and along the bottom — flattened smooth, before wrapping the blade itself.

Then pick a pattern:

- **The classic.** Heel-to-toe, leaving a little of the heel and toe exposed, covering the working middle of the blade.
- **The sock.** Heel-to-toe with full coverage from end to end, so the blade looks like it is wearing a gym sock. Maximum protection, slightly more weight.
- **Toe tape job.** Starts at the centre of the blade and wraps forward, covering only the toe. Popular with players who use the toe of the blade for handling and want a clean heel.
- **The five strand.** Five wraps just past the centre towards the toe. Minimal, quick, and easy to redo.

**Direction** — toe-to-heel or heel-to-toe — is personal preference for skaters. **Goalies** are conventionally advised to wrap **toe-to-heel** so the puck rolls off the blade cleanly when they pass and shoot.

**Colour** is preference with a story attached: black tape is said to hide the puck from the goalie; white tape is equally popular, and the black scuffs a puck leaves on it are claimed to do the same job. **Neither is a proven effect — no published study backs either.** The one authoritative voice argues the *opposite* of the black-tape folklore: David Marcoux, a former NHL goaltending coach, writing for NHL.com, says *"from a goalie's standpoint, it's simply easier to see the black puck coming off a white stick blade"*, and that *"there is at least one team in the Quebec Major Junior Hockey League, the Sherbrooke Phoenix, that mandates white tape on stick blades of defensemen for this very reason."* That is a coach's belief, not data, but it is better sourced than the black-tape claim, which rests on retailer copy hedged as "may also help".

**Wax** goes on after the tape — four to six horizontal passes across the face of the blade, repeated a few times, and the same on the back. It keeps water and ice off the tape, adds grip on the puck, and makes the tape job last longer. Use hockey-specific wax; general-purpose waxes gum up. Rubbing a puck back and forth along the blade does a similar job. (Wayne Gretzky's baby-powder finish, which dulls the tackiness slightly, is a real thing but is not a substitute for wax and is easy to overdo.)

### Knob and grip

The **knob** at the butt end stops the stick sliding out of your top hand and gives you something to grip when you pick the stick up off the ice. Build it how you like it:

- Start a base with a folded scrap of paper or napkin over the butt end.
- **Tear the tape in half lengthwise** and wrap the thinner strips around the top until the knob is the size you want.
- **Compress it in your hand** and smooth it down.
- Then run full-width tape down the shaft **10–12 inches** for grip.

A **ribbed grip** is a common variation: twist a foot or so of tape into a rope, spiral it down the shaft below the knob, then cover the spiral with a normal wrap. Finishing with clear tape reduces friction on your glove palms, which are the first thing to wear out. The **candy cane** is a spaced spiral down the shaft, about 1½–2 inches between wraps.

Goalies tape a **large knob** specifically so the stick stays in hand during poke checks, and many tape the paddle for rebound control.

### Shin pad and sock taping

**Clear hockey tape (shin tape) around the sock over the shin guard** does two jobs: it holds the guard in position so it cannot rotate mid-game, and it holds the sock up. Two or three wraps at the top of the calf and two or three at the ankle is the standard. Do not wrap so tightly that you restrict circulation, and be aware that a full wrap at the top of the calf can be uncomfortable over a long game — most players tape below the widest part.

Some players also tape a wrap around the top of the skate tongue or across the boot; that is preference.

## Bag and Pre-Game Routine

### Choosing a bag

Match the bag to your gear and your body, not to the biggest one on the shelf. A bag too large for your kit is annoying to carry and lets everything slide around; kids in particular end up hauling bags that strain a shoulder.

- **Carry bags** with a shoulder strap are the classic and still the most common.
- **Wheeled bags** take the load off your shoulders entirely and are the norm for young players and goalies. Most also have a carry strap.
- **Backpack bags** suit players travelling light; some have wheels too.
- **Ventilation matters more than it sounds.** Modern polyester bags dry faster and breathe better than old leather or PVC ones, and bags with mesh panels limit how much stink builds up between the changing room and home. Fabric weight is quoted in **denier** (420D, 600D, 1000D) — higher is stronger but stiffer.
- A **skate compartment** keeps sharp steel away from soft gear, which is otherwise how bags and gloves get cut open.
- A **stick bag** is worth it if you travel to tournaments by bus or plane.

### The checklist

Run this before you leave the house. Nothing on it is optional and every one of them has been forgotten by somebody, in the car park, twenty minutes before a game:

**Torso and arms:** base layers · jock/jill · shoulder pads · elbow pads · gloves · jersey
**Legs:** socks · shin guards · pants · sock tape
**Head and neck:** helmet (with cage/visor attached) · mouthguard · **neck guard — mandatory for every player across the Home Countries (IIHF 9.12), and under the In-House Rules forgetting it is an immediate 10-minute misconduct with no warning**
**Feet:** skates · spare laces · skate guards/soakers
**Sticks:** stick (plus a spare if you have one) · stick tape · wax
**Other:** water bottle · towel · shower kit · rink pass/club card · any tape or straps you use

### The dressing order most players use

There is a reason this order is nearly universal: each layer has to go on before the thing that overlaps it, and you cannot bend down to lace skates once your shoulder pads and jersey are on.

1. **Base layers**
2. **Jock / jill**
3. **Shin guards**, then **socks** pulled over them and taped
4. **Pants**
5. **Skates** — laced properly, while you can still reach your feet
6. **Shoulder pads**
7. **Elbow pads**
8. **Neck guard** (not optional anywhere in the Home Countries, at any age — see above)
9. **Jersey** (pulled down over the pads — remember most pads must be under the uniform by rule, and in Britain the jersey must sit outside the pants on tie-down straps, must not be tucked into your shorts, and must cover your elbow pads)
10. **Helmet**, chin strap fastened
11. **Mouthguard**
12. **Gloves**, last, so your hands are free for everything above

Then check three things before you step on: **chin strap fastened, shin guards under the pants and not sticking out, and gloves meeting your elbow pads.** Those are the three that referees and injuries both notice. **And step on wearing all of it, warm-up included** — England Ice Hockey's Rules & Regulations 24.1 require that *"[f]ull equipment, including helmets, must also be worn properly during the pre-game warm-up"*, and in Britain a coach or manager is required to check helmet and facemask fit, chin-strap tightness and neck laceration protection **before** the warm-up under In-House Rule 5.4.

## Common Mistakes

- **Buying skates a size too big.** **Start 1 to 1½ sizes below your shoe size and check heel lock** — CCM publishes that offset, Bauer publishes none. Too big costs you edge control, gets blamed on "weak ankles," and wears the boot out from the inside.
- **Buying a half visor for a junior, or for a woman before the season after her 18th birthday.** **Buy a cage for a junior, and a cage or full shield for anyone else** — that clears every book's face-protection rule, provided it carries the certification mark your book names. In Britain every player in IHUK's junior leagues, Under-19 included, wears full face whatever their age — a cage, in England and Wales — and nothing published confirms that a woman may switch to a half visor after that birthday. HECC certifies visors only for players 18 and over, and Hockey Canada makes full face compulsory in every division except Senior and Major Junior.
- **Never getting skates baked or broken in**, then deciding the fit is wrong. Have a shop bake mid-range or better boots, expect a few uncomfortable sessions, and go back only if they still genuinely hurt by your fourth time on the ice.
- **Standing up, or flexing your foot, during a skate bake.** Sit still for the ten minutes or so the boot takes to cool and set, or you mould it to the wrong shape.
- **Skating on whatever hollow the shop last gave you.** Pick one, write it down, and ask for it every time — otherwise you can't tell whether your edges or your technique are the problem.
- **Chasing a deeper hollow because "sharper is better."** Start at half an inch and move one step at a time. Deeper means more bite *and less glide*, and heavier players usually want the opposite.
- **Buying the flex the calculation says, then cutting four inches off the stick.** Buy lower if you plan to cut more than about three inches, because cutting stiffens it.
- **Assuming a stiffer stick shoots harder.** Buy a flex you can bend on every shot: start near half your body weight in pounds, and round down unless you are tall or strong for your weight. If you can't load it, it shoots softer.
- **Buying a deep, wide-open curve as a beginner.** Start on a mid curve with a less open face. A deep, open curve flatters your forehand and wrecks your backhand and your passing — and the backhand is the shot goalies read worst.
- **Never looking at the bottom of your blade.** Turn the stick over now and then: if the toe or heel is wearing faster, your lie is wrong, so try a different one on your next stick.
- **Wearing pads that are too big "for extra protection."** Buy the size that fits and strap each piece so it cannot rotate. Pads that are too big slide out of the way on impact.
- **Leaving a gap between shin guards and pants.** Check it in a skating stance, not standing up.
- **Loose elbow pads.** Strap them so you cannot twist them by hand, and check with your shoulder pads and gloves on. A loose one rotates when you fall and puts the hard cap somewhere useless.
- **Any second-hand helmet — bought, borrowed or handed down, goalie masks included.** Buy yours new: you cannot see a crushed liner or a hairline crack, or know its history. **An in-date sticker does not make a hand-me-down safe** — the date is a certification clock, not a condition report — and under USA Hockey an expired sticker makes the helmet illegal below Adults as well.
- **Taking the club's spare goalie mask along with the club's spare pads.** Borrow the pads, blocker, trapper and chest protector; **buy the mask.** A shared house mask has taken more pucks than any hand-me-down, with nobody logging them, and a mask nobody can account for is not one to put your head in.
- **Assuming your cage is the same size as your helmet.** **Fit the cage to your head, chin cup against your chin and inside the helmet's J-clips** — the sizes often differ, even in a combo pack. Under the In-House Rules a mis-sized helmet–facemask combination is *"Dangerous Equipment"* and you are off the ice until it is fixed.
- **Putting a child in a taster session's loaner helmet without asking.** **Ask whether you may bring your own, and buy one first if you can** — nobody can tell you what a pool helmet has stopped. Kit a programme **gives** a child to keep, as NHL/NHLPA Learn to Play and The First Shift do, is a separate question: ask whether it is new.
- **Leaving the chin strap slack.** **Do it up before you step on the ice, with no more than a finger between strap and chin.** A loose strap turns a helmet into a hat. Under the In-House Rules and USA Hockey the first loose strap is a warning to your team, and after that a loose strap costs ten minutes. ⚠️ **Under USA Hockey a loose helmet that comes off in an altercation throws you out of the game and costs you the next game too.**
- **Taking your own helmet off before a fight, because that is what you see on television.** **Keep it on, and never grab at an opponent's helmet, cage or chin strap.** ⚠️ **Under USA Hockey, removing your own before a fight or any scrum, or an opponent's before or during one, is a match penalty at every age** — thrown out, and suspended until the case is heard. Grabbing, holding or even rubbing a facemask anywhere throws you out there too, and plan on a match penalty if the grab is reckless. Hockey Canada sends you to the dressing room for removing a helmet around a fight, and in the PWHL any grab of an opponent's helmet or chin strap throws you out. ⚠️ **Under Hockey Canada and CARHA, a grab anywhere on the ice is a double minor at the least, and a match penalty if used to gain an advantage, punish or injure. Under Hockey Canada it is also a major and game misconduct if it causes an injury, even by accident, and a match penalty if done with any more than minimal force.**
- **Playing on for "just one more second" after your helmet comes off.** **Leave the ice at once, and leave the helmet where it fell.** Losing it is never the penalty — staying on without it is. ⚠️ **Under the In-House Rules that costs ten minutes**, and two minutes under the IIHF book; neither lets you put it back on. The NHL, Hockey Canada and the PWHL do, so do not carry either habit across.
- **Taking the neck guard off to sit out.** **Keep it on for as long as you are on the bench.** In IHUK junior and WNIHL competition it is required on the bench as well as the ice, and a junior who is not playing may sit there only in a helmet with full-face protection, a neck guard and upper-body protection; *"[o]therwise, they must leave the bench."*
- **Bending down for a neck guard that has come off in play.** **Go to the bench and let somebody else collect it.** The In-House Rules: *"If a neck guard comes off during play, the player must leave the ice and cannot retrieve it."* Playing on without it costs ten minutes with no warning, and the second time you are thrown out of the game.
- **Assuming a BNQ certification mark will still be issued in 2028.** **Buy a BNQ-certified protector now, keep the label legible, and ask your association before 2027-28 what it will accept.** Hockey Canada requires one in minor and female hockey, and BNQ closes that programme on 31 July 2027 with no replacement announced.
- **Leaving gear in the bag between sessions.** Empty it the moment you get home, hang everything and run a fan. A packed bag is the single habit behind rot, rust, smell and skin problems.
- **Storing skates in soakers while still wet.** Wipe the steel dry first, and take the soakers off once the blades are dry — they absorb moisture, they do not seal it out.
- **Spending big on a stick and cheap on a helmet.** Put the helmet first, and buy the stick on specification rather than price.
- **Skating to the bench with your broken stick still in your hand.** **Open your hand the moment the stick goes**, and keep a spare taped at your bench so a break costs you seconds, not a shift. ⚠️ **Under the In-House Rules carrying it anywhere on the ice is two minutes**, with none of the allowance USA Hockey's casebook gives near your own bench; the IIHF book already made it a minor.
- **Getting rid of a broken stick by putting it over the boards.** **Let go of it, or move it along the ice to the side of the rink, clear of the play.** Over the boards costs ten minutes under Hockey Canada and USA Hockey, where the Referee *"has no choice"*, and ⚠️ **under the NHL, the PWHL and the IIHF — your book in Britain — an intentional throw out of the rink gets you thrown out of the game.**
- **Telling your goalie they can play on with a broken stick.** **Carry a spare out and put it in their hand — never throw, shoot or slide it.** Only the NHL, IIHF and PWHL let a goalie play on with a broken stick until the next stoppage; under USA Hockey, Hockey Canada and CARHA it is a minor. ⚠️ **Under Hockey Canada a slid stick can cost more than two minutes:** the teammate who slides it takes a minor and risks a penalty shot, and a goalie who picks it up takes a minor too.
- **Forgetting a mouthguard or neck guard** and being sent off the ice for it. **Pack the neck guard for every session, training included, and find out what your competition asks of a mouthguard.** In Britain the neck guard is mandatory at every age: under the In-House Rules forgetting it costs ten minutes on the spot, the Elite League warns and then gives two minutes, and a third refusal to fix it ends your game in every British league. The Elite League requires a mouthguard of every skater, the WNIHL of anyone in a half visor.

## Key Takeaways

1. **Buy your own helmet new, and never wear a second-hand one — a goalie mask counts.** You cannot see what it has hit, so borrow the club's goalie pads but get your own mask. That is a firm caution, not a rule-book requirement. ⚠️ **Replace any helmet that has taken a serious impact, even one that looks fine.**
2. **Do the chin strap up before every skate, a finger's width at most, and keep the helmet on.** ⚠️ **Never take your own helmet off before a fight or scrum, and never remove or grab an opponent's.** Under USA Hockey, removing one throws you out at every age, with a suspension until a hearing.
3. **Wear the helmet, but never play as though it were armour — and never go into a collision head down.** ⚠️ **No helmet is concussion-proof** — a retailer's point, not a research finding. USA Hockey's *Heads Up Hockey* guide warns that *"there isn’t any protective equipment that can keep a player from getting a serious spinal injury"* in a collision taken head down.
4. **Before you buy protection, find out what your league requires** — facial protection, neck guard, mouthguard and certification mark all change by book. Buy a cage for any junior, Under-19 included, and a cage or full shield for anyone else. **Under Hockey Canada, buy a BNQ-certified neck protector now and ask what your association will accept after 31 July 2027.**
5. **🇬🇧 Pack the neck guard for every session and wear it every shift — adults and training included.** It is mandatory for every player across England, Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland. Where a British book says nothing about training, **read that as silence, not an exemption.** USA Hockey's adult exemption and Hockey Canada's minor-and-female scope are not yours.
6. **Fix the neck guard when an official tells you, and if it or your helmet comes off in play, go to the bench.** ⚠️ **Under the In-House Rules there is no warning: ten minutes, and thrown out of the game the second time.** The Elite League warns first, but a third refusal to fix it ends your game in any British league.
7. **Buy pads that fit rather than a size up, and strap each so it cannot rotate — too-big pads protect less.** Check three gaps: shin guard to pants, in a skating stance; a rotating elbow pad; and the wrist between glove cuff and elbow pad. Between two glove sizes, round up.
8. **Buy skates that lock your heel, toes just brushing the cap — a cheap skate that fits beats an expensive one a size too big.** Start about one to one and a half sizes below your shoe size and get measured. Allow a growing child half a size at most.
9. **If your edges will not hold, check the boot before you blame your ankles.** Slack in a loose boot is taken up before the blade tilts, so you never commit. Players in oversized skates usually decide they have weak ankles; they have loose boots.
10. **Pick a hollow and a stick, write the numbers down, and change one thing at a time.** Start at a half-inch hollow, sharpened every 20–30 hours on the ice, and a stick at your chin in skates with a flex near half your body weight. Cutting a stick stiffens it; uneven blade wear means your lie is wrong.
11. **Empty the bag every time you get home.** Hang the kit, run a fan, wipe your steel dry, and keep chlorine bleach away from the gear. Clean, dry kit removes most of the chance for the skin infections that circulate in hockey.
12. **When your stick breaks, open your hand and leave it on the ice clear of the play.** ⚠️ **Never tidy it over the boards** — under the NHL, PWHL and IIHF books that throws you out of the game. Under the In-House Rules, carrying it anywhere on the ice is two minutes. Take your goalie a spare and put it in their hand.

*Limitations worth knowing:*

- ***No British certification mark exists to check for.** Every UK equipment requirement in this document is a requirement that the item be present, unaltered and correctly fitted — not that it carry a particular sticker. The words "certified", "certification" and "10256" appear **nowhere** in the In-House Rules; the IIHF book names no **outside** certification body either. ⚠️ **It does require its own approval, though, and an earlier form of this note overstated the absence: "approved by" returns eleven hits in each IIHF edition, not none.** Two of them reach equipment a reader buys — Rule 11.8's goalkeeper face mask *"of a design approved by the IIHF"* and Rule 9.8's post-injury mask or protector — and Rule 9.8 also classifies *"[e]quipment that does not conform to IIHF standards"*, and *"wearing non-approved equipment"*, as Dangerous Equipment. Re-searched in full in the 2025/26 v1.1 and 2026/27 editions on 10 September 2026, including across line breaks; "approved" does **not occur** in the In-House Rules. England Ice Hockey's Rules & Regulations go as far as "an approved Ice Hockey Neck Guard/Protector" (Rule 24.3) and equipment that must "confirm to IIHF safety standards" (Rule 24.1) without saying approved by whom or against what. The advice here — buy hockey-specific gear CE-marked against EN ISO 10256 where the mark applies — is therefore an **inference from the EU/UK product-safety regime**, not something any British rule book states. If your league specifies a mark, its specification wins.*
- ***The two general British rule documents the UK guidance here is written from are of different vintages, and one has moved on.** The In-House Rules are Revision 1 of the **2026-27** season, published 25 July 2026; England Ice Hockey's Rules & Regulations are the 2024-2025 edition. Where they overlap they agree in substance (neck guards, the 2.5 cm chin-strap gap, cut shorts, jersey tucking), but the In-House Rules carry the current penalty escalations and are the operative document. ⚠️ **That caution has already been proved out once**: the 2025-26 edition scoped itself to England and Scotland, wrote Rule 9.12's competition list as *"all IIHF categories and SIHA competitions"*, and allowed a player whose helmet came off to refit it *"if safe to do so"*. The 2026-27 edition reaches all four Home Countries, writes the list as *"all IIHF categories and IHUK/EIH/SIH competitions"*, deletes the refit option **and names a misconduct penalty where the 2025-26 edition named no tier at all**, and adds a **Rule 10.3 on broken sticks** that the 2025-26 edition did not contain at all — the word "broken" appears nowhere in it. IHUK issues Rule Bulletins and further revisions in-season, so **check for a later revision before relying on a penalty detail.** The **EIHL Casebook** was obtained on 22 September 2026 — *Motorpoint EIHL Casebook 26-27, Version 1.1 of 8 September 2026*, from the League's own Casebook page — and **its Rule 9 equipment sections were read for this document**: Rule 9.5 Protective Equipment, which sets the Elite League's own mouth guard, facial protection, jewellery and neck protection requirements and the escalation for refusing to change a non-conforming item, and Rule 9.12 Neck Laceration Protection, which is where the Elite League's warning-then-minor tier above comes from. ⚠️ **Its rule sections outside Rule 9 were not read**, so nothing here should be taken as describing what the EIHL does with any other rule. The League issues updates in season. **One competition regulation is cited above as well** — the [IHUK WNIHL Rules of Competition 2026-27](https://englandicehockey.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/WNIHL-Rules-of-Competition-2026-2027.pdf), read in the primary text on 22 September 2026 for its §10 *Equipment & Kit* only, which is where the WNIHL’s half-visor-and-mouth-guard requirement comes from. ⚠️ **It governs the WNIHL and nothing else, and no other British competition’s Rules of Competition was read for this document** — so where a mouth guard is compulsory in any other British league is not settled here. **That neither general British document requires a mouth guard was established by search rather than assumed:** England Ice Hockey’s *Rules & Regulations* and both the 2026-27 and 2025-26 editions of the In-House Rules were searched in full, including across line breaks, and the word *mouth* does not occur in any of the three.*

- ***Whether the neck-guard mandate reaches practice was settled by search, and the answer is partly silence.*** *England Ice Hockey's* Rules & Regulations *24.3 and the EIHL Casebook's Rule 9.12 are the only documents cited here that say anything about on-ice activity outside a game. The IHUK In-House Rules 2026-27, the IIHF 2025/26 book, the USA Hockey playing rules and Casebook and the Hockey Canada playing rules were each searched for "practice", "practise" and "training"; none of them either extends the requirement to training or withholds it, so this document treats their competition scoping as silence rather than as an exemption. The In-House Rules' one training-adjacent provision is a duty on the coach or manager, not on the player. ⚠️ **That third document is now held, and it has been read.** The IHUK* Coaching Regulations 2026-2027 *were obtained on 22 September 2026 and §9 read in the primary text. They do make an ice hockey neck guard mandatory for a coach’s own on-ice kit, and for a 16–17-year-old acting as an on-ice demonstrator, and §9 is scoped in terms to* "junior and senior teams, including practices, games, camps, and hockey schools"*. **What they do not do is put the neck guard on the player at training** — the coach’s stated duty toward players covers* "approved helmets and face protection, sticks, and gloves"*, with skates, elbow pads and shin guards only* "wherever possible… (full kit recommended)"*. So the player-side training question is now a searched negative rather than an unread document, and the answer above is unchanged. Only §9 was read; the rest of that document has not been.*
- ***Only two rule sets put a measurable standard on chin-strap fit, and that was established by searching all of them.** The USA Hockey playing rules and Casebook, the Hockey Canada playing rules, the CARHA Hockey rules, the NHL book, the PWHL book and the IIHF book were each searched in full, including across line breaks and hyphenation. Only **USA Hockey's Casebook** attaches a fit test — the one finger — and only the **two British documents** attach the 2.5 cm. Hockey Canada Rule 3.6(b) (*"securely fastened under the chin"*), CARHA Rule 24(b) and IIHF Rule 9.6 require the strap to be fastened, and NHL and PWHL Rule 9.6 require it of a player returning from the bench or penalty bench; none of those five sets a measurement, so under them it is the referee's judgement and nothing more.*
- ***HECC's founding date is disputed between two primary sources.** HECC's own homepage states it in two places: "We are a 501(c)(3) non-profit organization, created in 1978 at the request of USA Hockey." USA Hockey's own article "What the Heck is HECC?" (10 October 2017) says the council was "organized in 1976" and does not mention having requested it. Both parties are primary sources and they disagree by two years; the text uses HECC's own account and flags the conflict inline. A caution for anyone re-checking this via the Internet Archive: hecc.org hosted an unrelated organisation before roughly 2018, so earlier captures of that domain are not the hockey body.*
- ***Which standard HECC certifies a visor to: HECC's own two pages disagree, and the disagreement is left visible here rather than resolved.** HECC's **Certified Equipment** list files every visor as "Eye and Face Protectors **Type C**" against **ASTM F513** (F513-22 for current products, F513-12 for older ones) — the same standard it lists against a full cage, with the type letter rather than the standard carrying the distinction — and the string "Z262" appears nowhere on that list. But HECC's **About Product Certification Testing** page, under its own *Visors* heading, says the opposite: full-face protectors are evaluated to ASTM F513, and *"[t]he difference is the standard used to evaluate the product, visors are evaluated to CAN/CSA Z262.2."* So one HECC page assigns visors an ASTM standard and another assigns them the Canadian one. Nothing published reconciles them, and no claim is made here about which is current. **None of this changes what a buyer does:** the mark to look for is the HECC certification itself, and the Type C age scope above — certified only for persons 18 and over — is stated on the Certified Equipment list either way. CSA Z262.2 is separately a real and correctly cited standard elsewhere in this document: it is the Canadian face-protector standard, mandatory for Hockey Canada play.*
- *The **6.5-year HECC certification period** comes from HECC's own site. Three caveats: HECC states it on a page about **helmet** labels, in a sentence that reads "approved helmets **and other equipment**" rather than naming face shields or masks; it does not hold across the range, because HECC separately puts neck laceration protectors at **five** years; and **USA Hockey** publishes no statement of the 6.5-year figure at all — its rulebook defers to the printed sticker date.*
- ***Annex ZA of EN ISO 10256-2:2024*** *was not read directly — the standard is paywalled. The presumption of conformity asserted in the text rests on the Official Journal citation itself (Commission Implementing Decision (EU) 2026/1279), which is what confers it; a harmonised EN cited in the OJ necessarily carries an Annex ZA, but the annex text was not inspected.*
- ***CSA Z262.1 and Z262.2 editions.*** *CSA Group's store, re-read on 15 October 2026, lists **CSA Z262.1:26** as the active helmet standard — *"Head protectors for skaters (Adopted ISO 10256-2:2024, second edition, 2024-07, with Canadian deviations)"* — and marks the 2015 edition, **Z262.1:15 (R2025)**, as withdrawn. The same store marks **Z262.2:15 (R2025)**, the face-protector standard, withdrawn as well, and that page names no successor; none is asserted here. The **(R2019)** citations you will see elsewhere were correct before the 2025 reaffirmation, and **no R2024 edition ever existed** in the store or in any archived capture of it. Hockey Canada Rule 3.6(b) asks for a CSA-certified helmet and facial protector and names no edition, so what a buyer checks is unchanged as of 15 October 2026; the federal helmet regulation tracks Z262.1 *"as amended from time to time"*.*
- ***CAN/BNQ 9415-370 currency, the successor standard, and the closure of the certification programme.*** *BNQ's own pages state that the current text of 9415-370 "is a reaffirmation (reapproval) of the edition dated September 5, 2013", and that the certification programme runs against it under protocol **BNQ 9415-970**. The SCC notice of intent (posted 7 May 2024) confirms that the standard is being withdrawn — activity "Withdrawal", project need "It was decided to withdraw this document" — and **that notice alone does not name a successor**. Two things then have to be kept apart. The **standard** has a successor, fully documented by BNQ: **CAN/BNQ 0102-565/2023 (ISO 10256-5: 2017, MOD)** is a published National Standard of Canada, first edition 7 February 2023, whose preface and Canadian-deviation notes repeatedly describe CAN/BNQ 9415-370 as the standard "which was in effect prior to" it. The **certification programme** does not: BNQ's customer notice of 6 August 2026 states that BNQ "conducted a review of its Program and decided to discontinue it" and "has decided not to implement a new certification program based on the new ISO 10256-5 standard", closing it on 31 July 2027 with no new applications accepted in the meantime and companies left to arrange their own transition "to other accredited certification bodies". BNQ's earlier statement that "the new edition of the program and the transition process… will be announced later on our website" is superseded by that notice, and both are quoted above so the change of position is visible. Two limitations: BNQ's English standardization page for 0102-565 is gone, and it is worth being precise about how, because a link checker reading status codes alone will call it live — the URL redirects to `bnq.qc.ca/404.html` and serves a not-found page under an **HTTP 200** status rather than returning a 404. It is therefore cited from the Internet Archive capture of 13 October 2024, and the standard document itself from BNQ's own PDF retrieved via the same archive; and **nothing published says what Hockey Canada will require once BNQ stops issuing certificates**, so no prediction is offered here.*
- ***EN ISO 10256-2:2018 supersession.*** *The 2024 edition is the current harmonised helmet standard, which the Official Journal list confirms (EN ISO 10256-1 through -4, all :2024, cited by Commission Implementing Decision (EU) 2026/1279, applicable 16 June 2026). The supersession of the 2018 edition is recorded by **PKN**, the Polish CEN member, whose entry for PN-EN ISO 10256-2:2018-08 reads "Wycofana i zastąpiona przez PN-EN ISO 10256-2:2025-06" — withdrawn and replaced — with withdrawal dated **11 June 2025**; that 2018 adoption "implements EN ISO 10256-2:2018 [IDT], ISO 10256-2:2016 [IDT]", and the replacing document implements EN ISO 10256-2:2024. ISO's own catalogue lists ISO 10256-2:2016 as Withdrawn with "New version available: ISO 10256-2:2024". **Two traps to watch for:** the EN and ISO edition years differ (EN:2018 adopts ISO:2016, and there is no ISO edition dated 2018), and BSI's page for the 2024 edition carries no supersession statement. And the absence of any 2018 edition from the harmonised list proves nothing either way — the 10256 series was only harmonised under the PPE Regulation on 16 June 2026, so no earlier edition would appear there.*
- ***Hockey Canada Rule 3.6 sub-clause lettering*** *has moved between editions (Hockey Canada's own 2022 bulletin cites the throat-protector clause as 3.6(f); the 2023-24 through 2026-28 rulebooks have it at 3.6(c)). The rule number 3.6 is stable; cite the letter with care. Note also that the rule text itself names **no standard number** — "BNQ-certified" is all it says; the mapping to CAN/BNQ 9415-370 comes from BNQ and HECC, not from the rulebook.*

One caution about where the 6.5-year figure lives, because HECC's pages are not interchangeable: its **certification-testing** page carries no numeric duration at all, while its **Important Notices Regarding Labels** page does — *"The expiration date of the label is 6.5 years after the date of manufacture"*, re-read in the primary text on 2 September 2026. - ***A translation trap in the Bauer manual, for anyone re-checking the used-helmet rule against it.** Bauer's booklet is printed in a dozen languages, and they do not all say the same thing. The **English** *Additional Information* paragraph reads that BAUER helmets meet the CSA, ASTM and CE standards *"provided they have not been reconditioned or altered in any way"*; the French says *"remis à neuf"* — refurbished — and the Spanish *"reparados"* — repaired. **Only the Italian says second-hand**: *"a condizione che non siano di seconda mano o modificati in alcun modo."* The German paragraph mentions neither. So Bauer is a primary source for replacing a severely impacted helmet and for what voids certification, and it is **not** a source for a rule against second-hand helmets; the Italian line is an outlier against the manufacturer's own English text and nothing here rests on it.*

- ***Skin infection and dirty gear.*** *The hockey-specific peer-reviewed source is Tlougan et al., "Skin conditions in figure skaters, ice-hockey players and speed skaters: part II", *Sports Medicine* 2011, which names the infectious dermatoses common in these athletes and the hygiene measures that prevent them. - *Claims about **black tape hiding the puck from the goalie** are widely repeated in the sport and are reported by retailer guides, but **no published study supports the effect.** PubMed returns nothing on blade tape colour and puck visibility; the goaltender visual-search literature (Vickers' quiet-eye work, including Panchuk, Vickers and Hopkins 2017 on deflected shots) concerns gaze location and fixation timing, not chromatic contrast, and contains no mention of tape, contrast, colour or luminance; Salmela and Fiorito's 1979 "Visual cues in ice hockey goaltending" **is** a temporal-occlusion study of cue availability, and its own abstract says so: *Can J Appl Sport Sci* **1979 Mar;4(1):56-9**, thirty-four goaltenders averaging 15.8 years watching filmed shots edited so that *"2, 4 or 8 images were occluded prior to the impact of the stick with the puck"*, with the finding that *"performance precision was aided by the increased availability of pre-shot visual cues."* **Two limits on that.** **Crossref does not index the paper — PubMed does** (PMID 498402), so a search of one database is not a search of the literature. And **only the indexed abstract was read here, not the full text.** ⚠️ **It does not bear on the tape question either way**: the abstract mentions no tape, no colour, no contrast and no luminance — it is about *when* cues become available, not *what* makes the puck visible. It is listed because a reader checking the literature will meet the title. The puck-tracking papers that surface in search are computer-vision work on broadcast footage, not human perception. **Treat the absence as real.** The only authoritative statement on the question runs the other way and is itself belief-based: NHL.com's David Marcoux on white tape, quoted in the text.*
