## Key focus

**If you are in crisis — any thought of harming yourself or of suicide — stop reading and get help now, tonight.** Call your local emergency number (999 in the UK and Ireland, 112 across the EU, 911 in the US and Canada), or go to [§13, When to seek real help](#13-when-to-seek-real-help) for crisis lines.

**After every mistake, run the same reset, and name your next job before your skates hit the ice.** A physical trigger, one long breath out, a cue word, then one concrete sentence: *"first forechecker, take the D's inside shoulder."* The plan pushes out the replay.

**On the bench, recover, watch and plan one thing — never replay the mistake.** Watch which defenceman panics under a forecheck and which forward has stopped backchecking. Save the analysis for after the game.

**Decide early and commit: simple in your own end, creative in theirs.** Hesitation is a mistake too: the half-second you take to be sure is all a forechecker needs. Off the glass and out (not over it), chosen early, is a good play; the same chip late is a turnover.

**Judge your night on process goals you control, and keep an outcome goal on the list.** Pick three for your role — your gap, your first touch, arriving at the net — once you know which system your team plays.

**In a slump, shoot more, not less.** Set a shot-attempt target and hit it whether or not anything goes in. Rebuild confidence with reps of the exact thing you lost.

**Switch your focus on triggers; nobody concentrates for sixty minutes.** Over the boards, at a whistle, as the puck goes behind your net: snap back up and check your shoulder. That fixes puck-watching where "try harder" does not.

**Use a routine and the warm-up to set your level, up or down.** Keep a routine that runs in any rink, and prune any step whose absence would wreck you. Call nerves *"my body getting ready"*: flat, go short and hard; over the top, skate long and breathe out slowly.

**Keep your frustration to yourself, on the bench and with the officials.** Linemates read a slammed stick in half a second. After a bad call, skate away and run your reset: arguing earns at least a power-play penalty, and keeping at it can get you thrown out.

## Overview

The mental side closes the gap between your worst game and your best. It does not raise your best. Your speed and your shot barely move between Saturday and Sunday; your attention and your composure after a mistake move a lot. Mental training is no substitute for skill work.

**Most of it is about the next shift, not the last one.** A mistake you carry costs you twice: once when it happens, and again while you replay it and the play moves on. So the reset ends by naming a job, and the bench is for planning, not post-mortems. The four-step order is coaching craft that no study has tested as a sequence, though each step has support of its own.

**Careful players make more turnovers.** Fear of a mistake looks tidy, but it makes you late, and every half-second you spend making sure gives the forechecker time. A process target you can hit gives the fear something to do.

**Process goals are what you score yourself on, because you control them.** Keep the outcome goal in view too: the one study that tested all three kinds of goal — small, and not in hockey — found the combination won. In a slump that means shooting more, because confidence comes from having actually done the thing. Self-talk helps, moderately, but it multiplies evidence rather than replacing it.

**Attention is switched, not held.** Good players have reliable moments when their focus snaps back, and a shoulder check tied to those moments beats puck-watching.

**Your state before the game is something you set.** How much nervous energy suits you is individual, so track it over a run of games. Then use a routine and the warm-up to get there each week. Calling the feeling readiness has evidence behind it and costs nothing.

**Frustration is not private.** Your linemates read it, and an official can turn it into a penalty or worse.

**And if it has spread beyond the rink, it is not a hockey problem.** See a qualified professional; looking for one is not a failure of toughness. Any thought of harming yourself or of suicide means getting help now, tonight — the numbers are in [§13, When to seek real help](#13-when-to-seek-real-help).

## How claims are labelled in this document

Three labels are used throughout.

- **"The research supports this."** Published, peer-reviewed work — usually a meta-analysis or a well-replicated finding — named where the claim is made.
- **"The evidence here is thin"** or **"contested."** Popular and maybe true, but the studies are few, small, outside sport, or disputed. Use it, but don't build your season on it.
- **"This is coaching craft."** What experienced coaches and players do, untested. Most of hockey is coaching craft — just don't present it to a teammate as science.

**No statistics in this document are invented.** Every number comes from a named, cited study.

## 1. Why the mental side is not optional

### Hockey is unusually unforgiving of a wandering mind

Three features of the game make attention matter more here than in most sports.

**It is fast and it never stops.** There is no set piece and no huddle. The situation changes every two or three seconds, and the game never waits for you. Spend four seconds on the pass you just missed and you have missed about two more decisions.

**Your mistakes are public and immediate.** A blown coverage is a puck in your net thirty seconds later, with your name on it in front of your own bench. The feedback arrives before you have finished processing the error.

**You are tired when it matters.** A shift is short — the measured range is **roughly 30 to 80 seconds**, with a well-managed shift nearer 30 to 45 — because you cannot keep up the output for longer. (Vigh-Larsen and Mohr's 2024 physiology review collected those figures from three earlier studies; [Conditioning and Recovery](conditioning_and_recovery.md) owns them and names the studies.) The last ten seconds, when your legs are gone, test your decisions hardest. Mental discipline stands in for judgment when you are too tired to think.

### The honest version of "it's all mental"

You will hear that the gap between your best and worst game is all mental. That overstates it. Here is the defensible version.

Your physical capacities — speed, shot, edges, strength — barely change in a week. Your attention, decision speed and composure after a turnover can swing a lot between Saturday and Sunday. So on a bad night when you are healthy and rested, the cause is more likely in the second list. That is a reasonable inference, not a measured finding, and it is why mental skills are worth training.

**What the mental side cannot do.** It will not make you faster, fix a bad first step, or rescue a wrist shot with no weight transfer. Anyone selling mental training as a substitute for skill work is selling you something.

## 2. The shift reset

This is the single most valuable mental skill in hockey, and the one most worth practising.

### The shift reset: the problem it solves

You lose a wall battle, fan on a one-timer, get beaten wide. Play does not stop. Within two seconds you have a new job, and if you are still holding the last two seconds, you are not doing it.

Then you sit down, and the real cost lands. **The mechanism here is a theory, not a measured finding, and it was not tested on hockey players.** Attentional control theory (Eysenck, Derakshan, Santos and Calvo, *Emotion*, 2007) holds that *"adverse effects of anxiety on processing efficiency depend on two central executive functions involving attentional control: inhibition and shifting."* In plain words, anxiety eats the capacity you use to block out distractions and to move your attention.

A brain re-running a turnover has less left for what the bench is for: watching their defence pair under pressure, hearing the coach's adjustment, tracking who you are out against next. So one bad shift becomes two.

### The reset itself

No study has tested this exact four-step sequence, so the sequence is coaching craft, but each step has support of its own and the whole thing is close to universally taught. Build it as a fixed routine so it runs without thinking.

**One: a physical trigger.** One specific, repeatable movement: tap both shin pads with your stick, squeeze the shaft once, adjust your right glove. What it is does not matter; that it is always the same, and physical, does. It makes the reset something you do rather than a mood you wait for.

**Two: one breath out, long.** Not a deep breath in — a long breath *out*. Slow exhaling raises cardiac vagal activity, the body's signature of coming down from a spike.

**The research supports the physiological effect.** Laborde and colleagues' meta-analysis (*Neuroscience & Biobehavioral Reviews*, 2022, **223 studies**) found that slow breathing raises vagally-mediated heart rate variability — in general populations, not athletes, and not measured as anxiety. **The anxiety claim sits a tier lower**: the athlete studies cited below are single trials, not a meta-analysis. **And the evidence for a direct performance benefit is thin.** Use it to change how you feel, not to make your next pass better.

**Three: a cue word.** One or two words, said to yourself, pointing at what you are about to do — not at what you just did. "Next." "Feet." "Inside." "Wall." **The research supports self-talk in general:** Hatzigeorgiadis and colleagues' 2011 meta-analysis (32 studies, 62 effect sizes) found a moderate positive effect, ES = 0.48.

Two of its findings matter for how you pick a cue, and the first is often misreported.

- **Match the cue to the task.** The paper supports a *matching* hypothesis — its figure is captioned *"The matching hypothesis on the effectiveness of self-talk: Interaction between task demands and self-talk content."* Instructional self-talk beat motivational **only for tasks with fine motor demands**. On gross tasks its table runs the other way (instructional d = 0.22, motivational d = 0.33), though it names no winner there. (Those cell figures are **second-hand** — the paper is paywalled and was not read for this document; see Sources.) So use **instructional cues for precise things** — shot placement, faceoff hands, a saucer pass — and **motivational cues for effortful things** — a hard forecheck, a battle, the end of a shift.
- **Self-talk's effect was larger for novel tasks than for well-learned ones.** Neither kind of cue will do much for a skill you have already automated.

**Four: name your next action, not your last one.** Before your skates hit the ice, say what you are doing this shift in one concrete sentence: "First forechecker, take the D's inside shoulder." "Get above the puck." This step is the point; the first three exist to make it possible.

### Do not try to suppress the thought

Deciding *not* to think about the mistake tends to backfire. Suppressing a thought takes effort, the effort uses up attention, and the thought often comes back. The alternative is to notice it, let it be there, and move your attention to the next action anyway. That is the core of the **Mindfulness-Acceptance-Commitment (MAC)** approach.

The practical translation: "I made a bad read, and I am now going to win this wall battle" works better than "stop thinking about the bad read."

**The evidence for MAC is partial, and the support for acceptance itself is the weakest part.** A robust Bayesian meta-analysis of **eleven controlled trials** (Int J Sport Exerc Psychol, 2023) found:

- **Mindfulness: g = 0.64 (95% CI 0.00–0.91), with strong evidence.**
- **Sport performance: g = 0.37 (95% CI 0.00–0.79), with moderate evidence.** The authors put it no higher than "might be effective."
- **Experiential acceptance: g = 0.36 (95% CI 0.00–0.92), with only anecdotal evidence.**
- **Psychological flexibility: not supported.** The authors say current trials "do not provide sufficient evidence" for it, for acceptance, or for well-being.

Every one of those intervals starts at **0.00**: on eleven trials, none of the effects is clearly separated from zero. So use the acceptance stance — it beats suppression — but file it under **"the evidence here is thin."**

### What to do on the bench

You have three jobs on the bench, and none of them is a post-mortem.

1. **Recover.** Get your breathing down. See [Conditioning and Recovery](conditioning_and_recovery.md).
2. **Watch.** The bench is the best seat for reading the game — which defenceman panics under a forecheck, whether their centre is cheating for offence, which forward has stopped backchecking.
3. **Plan one thing.** Decide the one thing you are doing next shift. As your change comes, check the clock and the score: protecting a late one-goal lead and chasing one ask for different risks. See [Risk Management](../hockey-iq/risk_management.md#risk-and-game-state).

**Analysing the mistake is a post-game job.** It helps then and hurts during. If you cannot let it go, park it: "I'll look at that after." Some players jot a note on their phone afterwards — coaching craft, but cheap, and it turns an intrusive thought into a scheduled task.

## 3. Playing to a process rather than an outcome

### The distinction

An **outcome goal** is a result you do not fully control: score, win, don't get scored on. A **process goal** is an action you do control: keep your **gap** — the distance between you and the carrier skating at you — at roughly **two to three stick lengths** through the neutral zone; take your first touch away from pressure; shoot within half a second in the slot; angle every carrier off the middle. **Those distances are a guide, not a law.** They move with your backward speed and the attacker's skill, and coaching pages disagree; [Defending the Rush](../systems/defending_the_rush.md) owns the full ladder.

One goal does not belong on a list of process goals: ⚠️ **"finish every check" — you may not be allowed to check at all.** Your age **and** your league's category decide it: age can bar it outright, and clearing the age line does not settle it. [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md) §1 sets out which classifications allow it, including the restricted form the IIHF writes for women's hockey. **In Britain neither the IIHF book nor the In-House Rules states an age.** Your competition's Rules of Competition do: §1 quotes IHUK's, which put the junior line between U12 and U14 and make the Women's NIHL non-checking. Outside the leagues IHUK runs, ask and get it in writing.

Even where you may check, *"finishing the check"* on a player who has already passed or shot is a late hit. The books allow finishing a check already being delivered, not starting one after the puck has gone. [Body Contact and Battles → The late hit](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md#the-late-hit--and-the-narrow-window-for-finishing-a-check) shows how narrow that window is.

Angling — steering a carrier with your route rather than hitting them — is allowed even where checking is not. Hockey Canada says a defender *"has the right to close off the boards"* to force a carrier to slow down or turn back inside. ⚠️ **But only *"as long as either player does not use their body to intentionally body-check, bump, push, or shove an opponent"*,** and the same book counts it as body checking when a player *"leaves their skating lane to make contact"*. In CARHA adult leagues the rule reaches a player who merely *"does not avert body contact with an opponent"*. So *"angle every carrier off the middle"* is a process goal in any league; *"ride them off the puck"* is not. See [Game Management](../systems/game_management.md).

You cannot control whether the puck goes in — the screen, the deflection and the save are not yours. You can control your route, your gap, your first touch, your shot selection and your feet.

### Why outcome focus makes you more inconsistent

Three proposed reasons. The first is plain reasoning, the second has a reasonable evidence base, and the third has recently been undermined.

**Outcomes are noisy, so outcome-based confidence is noisy.** If your test is "did I score", a night of four good looks and two posts is a failure, and your confidence takes damage it has not earned. Judging the process is stable because the input is stable.

**Outcome focus makes you watch yourself, and watching yourself breaks automated skills.** **The research supports this:** the leading account of choking is the self-focus or explicit-monitoring theory. Pressure pushes your attention onto movements that normally run on their own, and a fluent skill turns slow and deliberate.

The main experiment is Beilock and Carr (*Journal of Experimental Psychology: General*, 2001). Choking hit golf putting, a proceduralised skill, but not a non-proceduralised arithmetic task, and self-consciousness training removed it. The authors themselves call the idea a *"popular but infrequently tested belief"*, so this is a real experiment, not a large replicated literature. Worrying about the result makes you watch yourself shoot, and that is how you shoot badly.

**Outcome focus points your attention inward.** **This one is contested, and recently became much more so.** The claim is that an **external** focus — on the effect of the movement — beats an **internal** focus on the body part doing it. It rests on Gabriele Wulf's body of work and a 2021 *Psychological Bulletin* meta-analysis (Chua and colleagues) that supported it. That meta-analysis pooled **73 performance and 40 learning studies** across clinical and non-clinical groups, children and adults, so it is not an athlete-specific result.

In 2024 McKay and colleagues re-analysed the same literature, also in *Psychological Bulletin*, and found **"moderate to strong evidence of publication bias for all analyses."** Corrected for that, the effects fell to near zero — g = 0.01 for performance, 0.15 for retention, 0.09 for transfer. Their conclusion: the effects "on average" are "small to nil."

**How to treat this.** External cueing is free and there is no evidence it *hurts*, so phrase a cue as "drive the ice away" rather than "extend your leg". But stop hearing it called settled, and if an internal cue is the only way you can feel a correction, use it.

### Wording your cues externally

Same instruction, two wordings. The right-hand column is the usual recommendation, with the caveat above.

| Internal (worse) | External (better) |
|---|---|
| "Snap the wrists" | "Rip it off the bar-down corner" |
| "Bend your knees" | "Get your chest over your toes" — or better, "drive the ice away" |
| "Follow through high" | "Finish the stick at the far post" |
| "Push with the outside edge" | "Push the ice sideways" |
| "Soften your hands" | "Cushion it like an egg" |

The pattern: point the cue at the puck, the ice, the target or the opponent, not at your own limbs.

### Building your own process list

Pick **three** process goals for the season, specific to your role — three, and no more.

> **These examples are system-dependent, not rules of hockey.** They assume this guide's default: a **low zone collapse** in your own zone and a **2-1-2 forecheck**. Under **man-to-man** coverage the centre follows their check, even up to the point, and the winger's job becomes "stay attached to my man" rather than "hold the high slot." Under a **1-2-2 forecheck** the winger contains on the wall instead of arriving at the net front. **Ask your coach which system you play and rewrite these.**

- **Defenceman:** maintain gap through the neutral zone; get shots through from the point rather than looking for the perfect lane; first touch on retrievals goes to the wall, not the middle.
- **Winger (low zone collapse / 2-1-2):** arrive at the net front on every entry; support the wall battle rather than waiting at the blue line; first pass out of your own zone is up the boards.
- **Centre (low zone collapse):** be the lowest forward in your own zone; win the puck-side draw or tie it up; get above the puck on every turnover.

Score yourself on those three after the game — **but keep the scoresheet**, for the reason below.

**What the evidence says — and it is not "process goals only".**

**Process goals have measured support.** Kingston and Hardy (1997, *The Sport Psychologist* 11:277–293) trained **37 club golfers** with either process or performance goals, and the process programme improved **self-efficacy, cognitive anxiety control and concentration** more than the performance programme did. Note the comparison: process against **performance** goals, not against **outcome** goals.

**The study that tested all three points somewhere more interesting.** Filby, Maynard and Graydon (1999, *Journal of Applied Sport Psychology* 11:230–246) split **40 participants** into five groups on a soccer task over five weeks and a competition. Their conclusion: *"The superior performance of the groups using multiple-goal strategies provided evidence to support the efficacy of maintaining a balance between the use of outcome, performance, and process goals."*

**So:** score yourself on process goals, because they are stable, controllable and what improves under training — but keep outcome goals in the mix, not as the thing you judge a night by. Both studies are small, neither is in hockey, and neither covers a season.

**One citation that does *not* support any of this:** Kyllo and Landers' 1995 goal-setting meta-analysis (*Journal of Sport and Exercise Psychology* 17:117–137) — 36 studies, an overall benefit of **0.34 of a standard deviation** — looks at goal difficulty, absolute versus relative goals, and time frame. **It does not compare process and outcome goals at all.** Do not cite it for that.

## 4. Confidence

### Where it actually comes from

The most useful model is Albert Bandura's four sources of self-efficacy, and its ranking matters more than the list.

1. **Mastery experiences — by a distance the strongest source.** Having actually done the thing. Not having imagined it, not having been told you could; having done it.
2. **Vicarious experience.** Watching someone comparable do it. Weaker, but useful when you are very unsure — a demonstration from a teammate at your level beats one from the best player on the ice.
3. **Verbal persuasion.** Being told. The weakest of the three, and Bandura's work notes an asymmetry: **discouragement lowers self-efficacy more effectively than encouragement raises it.** So one dismissive comment outweighs five compliments, and what you avoid saying to a struggling teammate matters most.
4. **Physiological state** — and specifically **how you interpret it**. A racing heart before a big game does not set your confidence; reading it as "I'm ready" or "I'm falling apart" does. Section 5 builds on this.

So **confidence comes after preparation and evidence.** Self-talk helps (ES = 0.48, moderate), but with no bank of real successes behind it, it is a thin layer over nothing. To feel confident taking a puck off the wall under pressure, take two hundred off the wall under pressure in practice. See [Practice and Development](practice_and_development.md).

### How confidence collapses

Confidence rarely fades evenly. It collapses in one narrow area and leaks outward. You fan on a shot, so you stop shooting, so you handle the puck less, so your touch goes. Now you feel bad at hockey, not just at one shot.

Two things make this worse. Fatigue — a tired brain rates everything more negatively. And comparison — watching the player who took your spot in the lineup is a reliable way to feel worse without learning anything.

### The hockey version: the scoring slump

This is the classic, and the trap is invisible from inside it.

A forward stops scoring and gets careful: wait for a better look, don't waste chances. So they **shoot less**. That means fewer rebounds, deflections and ugly goals that break slumps, and a smaller sample, so the drought lasts longer even if the scoring rate has not changed. They also carry the puck into contact looking for the perfect play, which produces turnovers and hurts confidence further.

**That slumping players shoot less is coaching craft, not a measured finding** — no published study shows it, and there is no honest number for it. But coaches and sport psychologists describe it constantly, and the prescription holds either way.

### Rebuilding after a bad run

**Shoot more, not less.** Set a process target — five shot attempts a game, whatever suits your role — and hit it regardless of whether anything goes in. You are deliberately rebuilding the sample. See [Shooting](../technique/shooting.md).

**Change the unit of success.** Add things you can actually collect: a puck retrieved under pressure, a clean zone entry, a shift that ends in their zone, a wall battle won. That is section 3, applied under duress.

**Contribute in other ways.** A forward who is not scoring can forecheck hardest, be first back, win draws. That helps the team, and it rebuilds confidence where the feedback is immediate.

**Rebuild the evidence bank.** In practice, do reps of the exact thing you lost — the specific release you fanned on, not general shooting — until you have twenty good ones. That is the strongest lever you have.

**Do not add more analysis.** Thinking harder about an automated skill makes it worse. Do the technical work in practice and play on instinct in games.

## 5. Nerves and arousal

### What the inverted-U actually says — and what it does not

You will be told that performance follows an inverted U against arousal: too little and you are flat, too much and you fall apart, peak in the middle. The picture is useful, but the usual version is not accurate.

**Where it comes from.** Yerkes and Dodson, 1908, training mice — the "dancing mouse" — on a discrimination task with electric shock. It was cited about ten times in the next fifty years, and some of those citations already called it a law.

**What the original distinguishes, and almost nobody repeats.** It depends on task difficulty. **Simple, well-learned tasks tend to improve roughly linearly with arousal.** Only **complex or unfamiliar tasks** show the inverted U. The textbook curve is a later version that drops this, and the literature calls it frequently miscited.

**What is contested.** The correlation is real; the cause is not established, and flow theory and reversal theory offer rival accounts. Most useful for you: different athletes read the same physical state completely differently, so one universal curve is too general to act on.

**What this means on the bench.** Not "aim for the middle". Instead:

- **Your best state is individual, and it may not be calm.** That is the core of Yuri Hanin's **Individual Zones of Optimal Functioning (IZOF)** model, which replaces the universal curve with personal zones. Some players play best furious, some serene. Find yours — see below.
- **It also depends on the task.** Simple, physical tasks — a hard forecheck, a shot block, a puck battle — tolerate high arousal: crank it up. Fine or new tasks — a shootout attempt, a power-play look installed on Tuesday, a saucer pass — want you lower.

### The catastrophe model — the version that best matches how hockey actually falls apart

The catastrophe model (**Fazey and Hardy, 1988**, *The Inverted-U Hypothesis: A Catastrophe for Sport Psychology*, a British Association of Sports Sciences monograph) describes a bad period better than the inverted U. **But the evidence for it is thin and often overstated.** The study usually cited, **Hardy and Parfitt (1991)**, *British Journal of Psychology* 82:163–178, had **eight basketball players**. That does not reach this document's top tier, so treat the model as a useful picture, not an established mechanism.

Two claims matter.

**Under high worry — cognitive anxiety, as distinct from physical arousal — performance does not decline gently past the peak. It falls off a cliff.** In the high-anxiety condition the best scores were higher, the worst were lower, and the drops were far larger. That is the period where your team concedes three in six minutes.

**Getting back takes more than easing off slightly.** Once you are over the edge, you have to come down well below where you fell, not just back to it. That is the case for the **thirty-second timeout** (the NHL gives each team one per game), for shortening the bench, changing lines or having the goalie freeze the puck — and for you taking a full, deliberate reset rather than "calming down a bit."

### Nerves versus anxiety

These are not the same thing and conflating them is unhelpful.

**Nerves** are the physical ramp-up before something that matters: heart rate up, quick breathing, a hollow stomach, needing the toilet. They are proportionate and usually fade inside the first shift. They are the body preparing to work hard, and they are a *good sign*.

**Anxiety** is the worry: catastrophising, rehearsing failure, "what if I'm terrible tonight." It does not fade at puck drop. It is the dangerous variable in the catastrophe model, and it is what eats your ability to block distractions and shift focus.

So **manage nerves, don't eliminate them** — a player with none before a big game is often flat — and **address anxiety**. Anxiety that is there on Wednesday about Saturday, or that has stopped being about hockey, is section 13.

### Practical regulation

**Reframe the arousal rather than fighting it.** **The research supports this, and it is one of the sturdier findings here:** telling people that the physical signs of stress are the body mobilising to help them improves performance and produces a healthier stress response. Jamieson and colleagues have shown it repeatedly, though mostly in academic testing rather than sport, so the move to a rink is an inference. It fits Bandura's fourth source: it is how you read the state that moves confidence.

The move is small and concrete. Standing on the blue line for the anthem with your heart going: **"That's my body getting ready"** rather than **"I'm nervous."** Same sensation, different label.

**Breathe out longer than you breathe in** — in for four, out for six, six to ten times. The long exhale is the active part. As above, the vagal and anxiety effects are supported; a direct performance effect is not established.

**Use the warm-up to set your level, in either direction.** Flat: short hard bursts, quick feet, loud talk, hard shots. Over the top: long smooth skating, easy passes, slow breathing, less talking.

**Learn your own zone.** This is IZOF in practice, and it takes about eight games. After each, write two lines: how you felt beforehand (flat, edgy, calm, angry, distracted) and how you played. Patterns show up fast and often surprise: some players play best mildly irritated, and some find their "focused and calm" nights are their worst.

## 6. The pre-game routine

### What a routine is for

A routine makes your **state** consistent, so the only variable left is the game.

**Routines work; what nobody can tell you is which parts do the work.** Those are two different claims.

**The research supports the effect.** Rupprecht, Tran and Gröpel's meta-analysis (*International Review of Sport and Exercise Psychology*, online 2021, in print 2024; **112 effect sizes**) found **significant positive effects of pre-performance routines on sport performance**: **SMC = 0.31 (95% CI 0.18–0.44)** in pre–post designs, **Hedges' g = 0.64 (95% CI 0.45–0.83)** in experiments under low pressure, and **g = 0.70** under pressure, though with a much wider interval (**95% CI 0.24–1.16**).

The effects were "not moderated by the type of PPR, age, gender, skill level, or intervention characteristics." That means no difference was detected, not that novices and experts benefit equally. It is a reason not to assume routines are only for experts.

**What is unsettled is the anatomy.** Cotterill's 2010 review said understanding of routines "falls somewhat short of that required to recommend with confidence," and which components matter is still open. So build one, and distrust anyone who says exactly which parts are essential.

### Routine versus superstition — one test

They look identical from outside. The test is simple: **if you cannot do it, does it wreck you?**

A routine is what you do for its effect — a warm-up that raises body temperature, breathing that settles you, imagery that primes the patterns. In a different room, or when you are late, you compress it and you are fine.

A superstition is what you do because of what you fear will happen if you don't — same socks, same tape job, tapping the goalie's pads a set number of times. Harmless in itself, but it makes your composure depend on something you do not control, and one day the sock will be in the wash.

**Keep the routine; prune anything that would break you if it were unavailable.** In an away rink, a different room or on an odd schedule, your routine must still run.

### A workable timeline

Times are relative to puck drop. The specific numbers are coaching craft; adapt them to your league and your travel.

**The night before**
- Kit packed and checked. Skates sharpened if needed. See [Equipment](equipment.md).
- Sleep. This has a stronger evidence base behind it than anything else in this section — see [Conditioning and Recovery](conditioning_and_recovery.md).
- No tactical cramming. Reviewing systems at 11pm the night before does not install them.

**Three hours out**
- Last full meal.
- If you use imagery, this is a good slot for the longer version.

**Sixty to ninety minutes out — arrival**
- Arrive at the same point in the timeline every week if you can. Rushing is an easy, self-inflicted way to arrive over-aroused.
- Dress in the same order. Not superstition — one less decision.

**Twenty to thirty minutes out — off-ice warm-up**
- Raise body temperature. Dynamic movement, not static stretching.
- Hands: puck on a stick, passing off a wall, stickhandling.
- The off-ice warm-up is where you deliberately set your arousal up or down — see [Nerves and arousal](#5-nerves-and-arousal) above.

**On-ice warm-up**
- Skate to actual game speed at least twice — a warm-up entirely at 60% teaches your body that tonight is a 60% night.
- Goalies: track pucks properly from the first shot. See [Goaltender](../positions/goaltender.md).

**The last sixty seconds**
- Two or three slow exhales.
- **One** thought about the game, and make it a process cue. Not "we need this one." Something like "first pass up the wall" or "gap early."

**Between periods**
- Recover, make one adjustment, take one process cue.
- Do not spend the intermission on the referee.

### Imagery, if you use it

**The research supports imagery**, and the best-supported framework is **PETTLEP** (Holmes and Collins, 2001), examined in systematic reviews. Its core idea: imagery works better the closer it matches the real thing — in your kit, in a realistic posture, in real time, from your own eyes, including how the movement *feels*, with a real game's emotion attached.

Keep it short and specific — two or three minutes on situations you will actually face tonight. "Winning a puck off the left wall against a hard forecheck" is useful. "Scoring a hat-trick" is daydreaming.

## 7. Focus and attention

### Four kinds of focus, and hockey needs all of them

Robert Nideffer's model, which remains the standard framework, splits attention along two dimensions: **width** (broad versus narrow) and **direction** (external versus internal). That gives four styles, and hockey demands all four within a single shift.

| Focus | What it is | When you need it in hockey |
|---|---|---|
| **Broad-external** | Taking in the whole environment | Scanning before you receive the puck; reading the rush as a defender; the moment you step over the boards |
| **Narrow-external** | Locking onto one specific external cue | Receiving a pass; picking the far corner; watching your man's chest in a battle |
| **Broad-internal** | Analysing, planning, tactical thinking | On the bench; at a whistle; between periods |
| **Narrow-internal** | Single cue word, feel of a movement | The last half-second before a faceoff or a shot |

**The skill is not being focused. The skill is switching.** A defender who stays narrow-external on the puck carrier through a three-on-two has stopped seeing the trailer. A forward who stays broad-external as the pass arrives will not catch it. The transitions are the game.

### Attentional cues

A cue is a very short instruction that forces the right focus at the right moment.

- **Short.** One or two words. Anything longer arrives too late.
- **External, as a default.** Point at the ice, the puck, the target or the opponent rather than your body. Wulf argues this strongly; McKay and colleagues' 2024 re-analysis says it barely matters (section 3). It is free, so do it, but an internal cue is not an error.
- **Match the cue to the task.** **Instructional** for fine actions — placing a shot, a faceoff grip: "far post", "stick down". **Motivational** for effortful ones — a forecheck, a battle: "go", "work". The self-talk meta-analysis shows an instructional advantage on fine tasks only and names no winner on gross ones, so a motivational cue there is a sensible default, not a proven edge.
- **Tied to a trigger,** so you do not have to remember to use it: over the boards, at a whistle, as the puck goes behind your net.

You cannot hold high focus for sixty minutes and nobody does. What good players have is reliable **trigger points** — moments where focus snaps back up — rather than continuous concentration.

### Puck-watching is a focus failure, not a laziness failure

Puck-watching is standing still, eyes on the puck, with an unmarked opponent behind you. It is the defensive error named most often below the professional level — a coaching observation, not a counted ranking — and it is usually blamed on effort. It is an attention problem, and seeing that is what lets you fix it.

The puck is the brightest, fastest thing on the ice and where the danger seems to be, so your attention locks onto it. Hockey asks you to override that pull several times a shift and check the space you cannot see.

In the model above, puck-watching is being stuck in **narrow-external** when you need **broad-external**. Anxiety makes it worse, because it degrades exactly the shifting you need to break your gaze off the puck. A nervous player puck-watches more.

The fix is shoulder checks tied to triggers, not an order to "concentrate." [Scanning and Anticipation](../hockey-iq/scanning_and_anticipation.md) covers building that habit; see also [Playing Without the Puck](../hockey-iq/playing_without_the_puck.md).

## 8. Fear

Fear is common in hockey and rarely discussed. **There is no published estimate of how common**, so distrust any figure you hear, including the claim that "something like half" of an adult rec dressing room has a version of it. Coaches and players describe it constantly. If you are afraid of something on the ice, you are not soft and it is not a character defect. It is a normal response to a fast game with hard surfaces, and it is workable.

### Four fears, four different problems

**Fear of contact** — of being hit, or of hitting. Common in late starters, players returning after time away, and anyone who has just moved up a level. Body checking is barred in a non-check classification — ask your league which it runs, since no rulebook records how many leagues sit in each — but body *contact* (battles, pins, collisions) is in every version of the game. **In Britain**, ask which format your competition's Rules of Competition set: by age group in the junior leagues, by the league in the senior ones. [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md) §1 has both routes.

**Fear of injury** — usually specific and usually earned. Someone with a bad shoulder is afraid of that shoulder, not of hockey, and most of all in the first few games after it has healed.

**Fear of the puck** — flinching on shot blocks, turning away from a point shot, not wanting to be in the slot when the puck is coming. Goalies have their own version.

**Fear of making a mistake** — the most damaging of the four, and the least visible, because it does not look like fear at all. It looks like a careful, quiet, tidy player who never does anything wrong.

### Why fear of mistakes is self-defeating

The loop is counter-intuitive from the inside.

Afraid of a mistake, you play safe — which really means you play late. You hold the puck an extra half-second, refuse the tight pass, skip the pinch the read calls for, then chip it out anyway once the forechecker has arrived. **Hesitation is a mistake too, and hockey punishes it fast.** The half-second you take to be sure is all a forechecker needs, and a late chip with a stick on you comes straight back. So the passive player makes *more* turnovers, and each one confirms the fear.

**The chip itself is not the problem.** Off the glass — not over it — and out, chosen early under pressure in your own end, is a good play, and simple in your own end is sound hockey; [Risk Management](../hockey-iq/risk_management.md) sets out when to take it. The same chip half a second late is a turnover.

There is a slower cost too. A player playing not to lose the puck stops trying the plays that would improve them, so they stall, fall further behind, and the fear grows.

**The prescription is not "be brave."** Turn the vague fear into a specific process target you can hit (section 3), and accept some errors as the price of playing properly. Good coaches want mistakes of commission, not omission. If yours does not, that tells you about the coach, not about you.

### Working on physical fear: graded exposure

The general approach that works for fear — in and out of sport — is **graded exposure**: controlled, gradually harder contact with the feared thing, at a level where you stay in control. Its clinical base for anxiety is strong; **applying it to hockey contact fear is coaching craft, not cited to a study here.** It matches the checking progressions Hockey Canada and USA Hockey teach. Hockey Canada's *Checking Resource Guide* calls checking *"a 4-step progression"* — **positioning and angling, stick checks, contact confidence and body contact, then body checking** — and USA Hockey's *Introduction To Body Contact* uses the same shape as a pyramid. ⚠️ **Take the order from that USA Hockey manual, not the ages:** it introduces body checking at 12-and-Under and quotes the 2007-09 rule book. Today **USA Hockey Rule 604(a) prohibits body checking "in the 12 & under youth age classifications and below"**, in all girls'/women's classifications and in non-check adult ones, and lets a local governing body bar it anywhere. Current ages are in [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md) §1. These are teaching sequences for a skill, not tested treatments for fear.

**For contact.** Work up in steps: leaning and pushing against a stationary partner, then angling along the boards at half speed, then one-on-one wall battles, then game pace. Stay at each level until it bores you. The technique is in [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md).

**Technique reduces fear more than courage does.** Much fear of contact is a sound fear of getting hurt because you do not know how to take a hit. See it coming, get your weight low **with your head up and your chin off your chest**, keep your feet moving, and come into the wall on an angle, not straight on. If the contact is coming anyway, get your **skates parallel to the boards, feet apart and knees bent, with your forearm and hip to the wall**.

**Head up and chin off your chest is the non-negotiable part, and it is the half of "get low" that players drop: never turn your back to the checker, and never duck.** A chin tucked to the chest straightens the cervical spine, and USA Hockey's *Heads Up Hockey* guide says the injury from that head-down, head-first collision with the boards or a goal post *"can occur at walking speed"*. A back turned to the checker makes the contact a hit from behind, and USA Hockey warns: *"Even a light hit from behind could inflict a severe head or neck injury."*

Turn your shoulder toward *the checker*, never into *the boards*. USA Hockey's flat prohibition is the narrow one — *"Never hit the boards or glass with the tip of your shoulder"*, the tip only — and its checklist for taking a check asks for *"No shoulder contact on the boards, if possible"*. [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md) has the full treatment. The fear then drops for a good reason.

**For the puck.** Same idea: tennis balls, then slow pucks, then real shots from distance, then closer. Learn to block properly, which is nearly the opposite of instinct: **head up and out of the shot lane, square to the shooter, on your feet, close, presenting your shin pads, hands tucked with the backs of the gloves out.** Never turn your back, never turn sideways, never lift a leg — all three expose unarmoured parts and turn you away from the puck. **Head up and out of the shot lane is the one that does not bend**, whatever else your team asks of you.

Going to the ice at the *shot* takes you out of the play whether or not the block works. A timed slide at the *pass* on a 2-on-1 is a different act, and if that is your team's method, ask your coach how they want it done rather than working it out at speed. [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md) covers the technique and when *not* to block. A player never taught to block a shot is right to be afraid of it.

**Check your equipment.** Ill-fitting or thin protection makes you flinch, correctly. Someone flinching at point shots in thin shin pads is not being irrational. See [Equipment](equipment.md).

### Fear as information

Not all fear should be overridden. Fear that says "this is genuinely dangerous" — a player out of control, a hit from behind toward the boards, going into a corner head down with someone arriving — is doing its job. The goal is to stop fear operating where you are actually safe. See [Risk Management](../hockey-iq/risk_management.md).

### Coming back from injury

**This section assumes you have been medically cleared to play.** If you have not, that is the first step and it is not yours to make — see [Conditioning and Recovery](conditioning_and_recovery.md).

Your mind takes longer to recover than your body. It is normal to be cleared and still protecting the injury three weeks later. Two things help: a staged return through the same graded steps rather than straight into a game, and knowing that the first real contact on the healed area usually resolves most of it. The IOC consensus statement adds that mental health symptoms **increase injury risk and delay recovery**, so a lingering fear is a physical risk factor, not just discomfort.

## 9. Goaltender psychology

Goaltending has a strong claim to being the most psychologically demanding position in team sport, and writing on goalkeepers across sports treats it as a distinct problem.

> **A source warning for this section.** Everything below is much less well sourced than the rest of this document. The hockey-specific references are an **applied-practice blog post** (the Association for Applied Sport Psychology), a **commercial goalie-coaching site**, and an **unpublished doctoral dissertation** (University of North Dakota, 2022). There is no meta-analysis.

One peer-reviewed review is nearby. De Castro, de Castro and Coutts (2021) ran a **scoping review of psychological interventions with football goalkeepers**: of 178 articles screened, **nine** qualified, mostly about the penalty kick, and **only one actually applied psychological techniques to goalkeepers — and it detected no improvement.** They conclude goalkeepers "may benefit", reasoning from other sports rather than goalkeeper evidence.

So **somebody has looked, in the better-researched sport, and found almost nothing.** Read this section as **structured coaching craft with a plausible rationale**, not research findings.

If you play out, read this section anyway — most of it is about how to treat your goalie.

### Why the position is different

**Your mistakes are the scoreboard.** A skater's error may be covered; yours has nobody behind it, and it is announced, replayed and recorded against your name.

**The failure is public, still and prolonged.** After a goal, play stops and everyone looks at you. You stand for thirty to sixty seconds with no task — full attention on the error and no action to reset with.

**Your work rate is nothing like the team's.** You can be idle for eleven minutes and then need an elite reaction save. You have to stay engaged through empty stretches and produce peak output instantly.

**You are structurally isolated.** You train separately, you often warm up separately, and you frequently have no positional peer on the roster except the person competing with you for the net.

**Blame lands on you by default.** The applied goalkeeper writing describes goalies absorbing a disproportionate share of blame, guilt and self-criticism — practitioner description, per the warning above, not measurement.

### The short memory

The one non-negotiable goaltending skill. It is trainable and it is a routine, not a personality trait.

**Build a fixed post-goal routine and run it every time**, including on goals that were not your fault. If you only run it after bad goals, it becomes a marker of failure. A suggested shape, coaching craft in its specifics:

1. Get the puck out of the net yourself, or don't — but pick one and always do it.
2. Skate a short, deliberate loop out to the top of the crease or to the corner. **Move.** Movement gives the reset a physical form and breaks the frozen posture.
3. Water bottle, one long exhale, wipe the mask.
4. One factual sentence about the goal — "screen, didn't find it" — and then stop. No evaluation, no blame, no "that was terrible."
5. Return to the crease, tap both posts, cue word, set for the faceoff.

Steps 4 and 5 are the whole thing. One clean description satisfies the urge to process, so your brain stops re-running the goal all period. The rest is scaffolding.

The better-supported parts — controlled breathing, a short cue, accepting rather than suppressing the thought — are the ones from section 2, with the same caveats: the acceptance evidence is thin, and a cue for an effortful reset need not be instructional. The goalie-specific packaging is craft.

### Confidence and the goalie

Bandura's ranking applies with force: goalie confidence is built on **saves made**. After a bad night, a goalie should face lots of makeable shots in the next practice before hard ones. Volume matters more than difficulty.

From applied practice rather than a study: busy nights are often *easier* for goalies, because engagement is continuous and rhythm builds. The dangerous game is fifteen shots with a soft one in the second period.

### How a team should treat its goalie

Most teams get this wrong in one of two directions.

**Do not pile on.** A defenceman's head shake, stick slam or skate-away after a goal reads as blame whether meant or not, and (section 11) it does not stay between the two of you.

**Do not over-console either.** Skating over after every goal marks each one as an event to manage — the opposite of what the short-memory routine is for. Default to normal: a tap on the pads, one word, back to your job.

**Break the isolation off the ice.** Include the goalie in the room, the pre-game and the social side. It costs nothing.

**Talk to them during play.** The goalie is the best-placed communicator on the ice, and a job in the flow of the game keeps them engaged through quiet spells. See [On-Ice Communication](../foundation/on_ice_communication.md).

For the technical and positional side, see [Goaltender](../positions/goaltender.md).

## 10. Coaches, benchings and criticism

### Separate the message from the delivery

Treat feedback as two things: **the content** — is it true, and can I use it? — and **the delivery** — was it fair, kind, well-timed, public?

They are independent. A coach who shouts "you're standing still!" across the bench has delivered it badly and may be entirely right. Discard the message because you resent the messenger and you lose the information.

**The reverse matters too.** Pleasant delivery does not make advice correct. Plenty of coaching is a preference presented as a rule — "wingers never go below the goal line" is a system choice, not a law. Ask which system, and ask why.

**What the research says about delivery.** Controlling coaching — public criticism, punishment, pressure — is linked to more psychological **need frustration**, lower engagement, and more defiance and anger. Autonomy-supportive coaching, built on choice and collaboration, is linked to better motivation and resilience. **Be precise:** the Delrue et al. vignette study cited below **did not measure stress**, and burnout, though well attested in this literature, is not from that paper either.

One further idea has **no source behind it**: that a **closer** coach-athlete bond can *amplify* the harm of a controlling style, because the athlete complies without accepting the demand. **None of the coaching references below states it**, so treat it as a reasonable caution, not a finding.

**And there is a line.** Demanding is not the same as abusive. Criticism of your play is coaching. Personal degradation, routine humiliation in front of the group, or anything that leaves you dreading the rink is not, and it is not something to absorb quietly. Take it to your club's safeguarding lead if it has one, another club official, the league or the governing body.

### When you are not playing

**Start with an observation — and there is no study behind it: most players believe they deserve more ice time than they get.** Whatever the real proportion, it cannot be true of everyone. You remember your good shifts, you never watch yourself play, and you miss the plays where you were beaten because you were behind them. That does not make your coach right; it makes you an unreliable judge of your own case.

Given that, here is what actually helps.

**Ask for specifics, at the right time.** Not on the bench, not right after the game, not in front of the group — a day or two later, in private: "What do I need to do to get on the second power play?" A vague "why am I not playing more?" gets a vague answer and sounds like a complaint.

**Then work on the answer where the coach can see it.** If it is "you're getting beaten back," backcheck hard in practice, not just in the two shifts you get.

**Be excellent in the minutes you do get.** Conspicuously good in four minutes gets six; sulking through four gets two. Coaching craft, but close to universal.

**Stay physically ready.** A cold player who goes over the boards after eight minutes and gets beaten has confirmed the coach's decision. Keep your legs alive.

**Know the limits of your control.** Some benchings are political, some coaches have favourites, and some rosters are simply deeper than your level. If you asked the specific question, did the work, and nothing changed over a real stretch, moving teams is a legitimate answer, not an admission of failure.

### Frustration has a price on the scoreboard

**Skate away from a bad call and run your reset.** Keep your hands off officials, keep your stick off the boards and glass, and never throw anything onto the ice from the bench — not a water bottle, not a towel, not even a stick to a teammate who has lost theirs. Your frustration, shown, hands the other team a power play, and the goal they score will be blamed on the call rather than on you. And a power-play penalty is the cheap end: plenty of these acts end your game.

**What it costs.**

- **Disputing a call.** NHL Rule 39 gives a minor to "any player who challenges or disputes the ruling of an official," for obscene, profane or abusive language or gestures at any official, and to "any player or players who bang the boards with their sticks or other objects at any time, or who, in any manner show disrespect for an official's decision." Banging to get the officials' attention for a serious injury or illness is the exception: there *"discretion must be exercised by the Referees."* Banging the glass in protest of an off-ice official's ruling gets the same minor. Keeping at it is a misconduct, then a game misconduct (Rules 39.4 and 39.5), and the IIHF, PWHL, USA Hockey, Hockey Canada and CARHA books write the same ladder. Hockey Canada, whose minor is for disputing a call *"in an unsportsmanlike manner"*, lets the referee go straight to either without a minor first; CARHA can go from the minor straight to a game misconduct.
- **Gestures and language at an official.** An obscene gesture ends your game in the NHL: Rule 75.5(ii) makes it a game misconduct, not a referee's option, even though Rules 39.2 and 75.2 also name it as a minor; the PWHL overlaps the same way at 77.5(ii). USA Hockey's Rule 601 makes an obscene gesture, or banging the boards, goal frame or glass in protest of a call, a game misconduct, and obscene, profane or abusive language a misconduct; its Casebook prices a stick banged on the glass at the goal judge as a minor plus a misconduct or game misconduct. The IIHF gives a game misconduct for spitting at an official, or for aiming obscene, profane or abusive language, an obscene gesture, a racial slur or taunt, or a sexual remark at one, and spitting on or at one is also reported for suspension. CARHA makes obscene, profane or abusive language or gestures a misconduct, and Hockey Canada does the same for abusive conduct towards the referee.
- **Spitting, slurs and threats.** Spitting at an official or an opponent is a match penalty under USA Hockey, Hockey Canada and CARHA and a game misconduct under the IIHF. In the NHL and PWHL spitting on or at an official is an automatic suspension, and at an opponent can be a game misconduct. Hateful or discriminatory language is a match penalty under USA Hockey and a gross misconduct under Hockey Canada and CARHA; a racial slur is a game misconduct under the IIHF and can be one in the NHL and PWHL. A threat of physical harm to an official is a match penalty under USA Hockey, Hockey Canada and CARHA — you do not have to touch an official to be thrown out.
- **A hand on an official** — to argue, or to pull free of a linesperson in a scrap. Under USA Hockey, physically contacting an official or resisting a linesperson is a game misconduct and deliberate force is a match penalty; the book also prints a misconduct for a touch or hold, so do not count on the lower rung. Deliberate force is a match penalty under Hockey Canada, and even force applied by accident while you keep at a scrap is a game misconduct there. CARHA's match penalty reaches an intentional touch, hold or push. In the NHL and PWHL it is a game misconduct plus an automatic suspension. The IIHF gives a game misconduct for deliberate force likely to injure an official, for physically demeaning one, and for force used to get free of one during or after an altercation, and the referees report it for suspension; resisting a linesperson in any other way is a misconduct or game misconduct at the referee's discretion.
- **Language or gestures at anyone else.** NHL Rule 75 gives a minor for obscene, profane or abusive language or gestures "directed at any person." The IIHF makes an obscene gesture a game misconduct whoever it is aimed at.
- **Throwing things.** NHL Rule 75 gives a minor to "any player who throws any object onto the ice from the players' or penalty bench" — the cheapest price in any book — and the IIHF and PWHL charge the same. Under Hockey Canada it counts as interference from the bench: a bench minor plus a game misconduct for the player who threw it (Rule 8.4(a) and (e)). CARHA gives an identified thrower a game misconduct (Rule 66(d)), and USA Hockey gives the team a bench minor, or a misconduct to a thrower the referee can identify (Rule 601(b)(4) and its Note). A stick or other equipment thrown out of the playing area in protest of a call is a minor plus a game misconduct in the NHL, IIHF and PWHL and a game misconduct in USA Hockey (637(c)); Hockey Canada (10.5(d)) and CARHA (83(d)) make any stick thrown out of the playing area a misconduct. Thrown at the puck in your own end, or at a player on a breakaway, it gives the other team a penalty shot — or the goal itself, if your net is empty.

## 11. Team dynamics, and your contribution to them

### Your emotional state is not private

**The evidence here is real but a tier below this document's top label.** It comes from Tamminen and colleagues' **scoping review** (*International Review of Sport and Exercise Psychology*, 2022), which maps a literature rather than pooling effects — there is no meta-analysis behind it. That literature finds that emotions spread between athletes, that coaches' emotions are linked to players' emotions and team performance, and that teammates regulating each other's emotions affects anxiety and results. Closeness and status matter, so a senior player's body language carries further than a fourth-liner's. Treat the direction as well documented and the size as unquantified.

So **when you slump on the bench, throw your head back or bang your stick, you are not privately processing a bad shift. You are broadcasting.** Your linemates read it in half a second as one of three things — the coach is wrong, my linemate let me down, or this game is gone. None of those helps them on the next shift.

Fake enthusiasm convinces nobody. Hold a **neutral floor** instead: upright, eyes on the ice, engaged. Feel what you feel; don't project it.

### Being a good teammate on a bad night

Your bad nights are when you most easily become a net negative for the group.

**Say the thing that helps, which is usually about the next shift.** "Get above me next time and I'll hit you" is useful. "You've got to be there" is not.

**Absorb, don't transmit.** When a teammate makes an error, say nothing, or one word of support. The head shake costs more than the error did.

**Take responsibility out loud, once, and then stop.** "That's on me" from the player who turned it over ends the conversation. Repeated self-flagellation makes everyone manage your feelings instead of playing.

**Your bad night is not the main event.** Seventeen other people came to play. On a night when you have nothing, your job is not to make it their problem.

The wider subject — roles, standards, how groups set their own norms — is [Team Play and Culture](team_play_and_culture.md).

## 12. Motivation over a season

### Improvement is not linear, and expecting it to be is what breaks people

Learning usually climbs fast early, then runs in long flat stretches broken by sudden jumps. The flat stretches are the normal shape of learning, not failure, but they arrive just as the early enthusiasm wears off.

Two things help you sit through them.

**Know what a plateau usually is.** Often you are consolidating: the skill is becoming automatic, which does not feel like progress but makes it usable under pressure. Often you are doing more with the same skill against better players, so your output is flat while your ability has risen. And sometimes it is a real ceiling on your technique, which needs a new method rather than more of the same. See [Practice and Development](practice_and_development.md).

**Measure something that moves.** Wins, points and ice time can sit still for months; process markers move weekly. This is section 3 across a season.

### About "10,000 hours"

**The popular version — that 10,000 hours of practice makes an expert — overstates the research.** Macnamara, Moreau and Hambrick's 2016 sport meta-analysis found that **deliberate practice accounted for 18% of the variance in sports performance** (95% confidence interval 12–25%). The rest lies in starting age, physical attributes, coaching, the standard of the players around you, genetics, injury history, and *how* you spend the hours.

That cuts both ways. **Practice is not pointless** — 18% is large for a single factor, and it is the one you control. **But hours alone are not the mechanism**, so two players with the same ice time can improve at very different rates. The full argument, including the disputed 1%-among-elites figure and Ericsson's reply, is in [Practice and Development](practice_and_development.md).

### The grind

Mid-season, with early mornings and cold rinks, motivation stops being about ambition and becomes about habit.

- **Rely on structure, not feeling.** Turning up because it is Tuesday beats turning up because you feel like it.
- **Have an off-season.** Hockey twelve months a year is a good way to stop wanting to play. Breaks are a recovery tool, not a lapse.
- **Keep one thing you are working on.** A technical project gives you something to care about on a bad night besides results you do not control.
- **Watch for wanting to play turning into feeling obliged to.** That drift is an early sign of burnout, which the coaching-climate research links to controlling environments. If it persists, take it seriously — see the next section.

## 13. When to seek real help

This document is about performance. It is not a substitute for care, and some of what looks like a mental-game problem is not one.

**The IOC's 2019 consensus statement on mental health in elite athletes**, the standard reference, makes several points that matter here. Mental health symptoms and disorders are common in athletes and can impair performance. **Depression and anxiety** are broadly as common in elite athletes as in the **general population** — worth saying because both opposite claims circulate. But that does not hold for everything: **eating disorders and disordered eating are *more* common in athletes**, especially in weight-sensitive, aesthetic and endurance sports. Hockey is not usually a weight-making sport, but roster weights and body-composition targets happen.

**Mental health cannot be separated from physical health**: symptoms raise the risk of injury and delay recovery. And the statement stresses telling the traits of a committed competitor apart from genuine problems that need treatment.

**Signs it is time to talk to a qualified professional rather than reading more about mental skills:**

- Anxiety that is present well before game day, or that does not settle once play starts.
- Low mood, loss of interest, or hopelessness that persists for weeks.
- Sleep that is consistently disrupted, or appetite that has changed markedly.
- Dread of going to the rink, in someone who used to enjoy it.
- Performance problems that have spread into work, study, relationships or family life — this is the clearest signal in the list, because a genuine hockey problem stays in the rink.
- Using alcohol or anything else to manage how you feel about playing.
- Any thought of harming yourself or of suicide. This one is not a threshold to weigh up.

**For anything less acute.** Speak to your GP or family doctor, or to a qualified clinical or sport psychologist — in many countries sport psychologists are regulated and you can check registration. ## Common Mistakes

- **Treating the mental game as something you either have or don't.** Composure is a routine, not a temperament: run the shift reset after every mistake until it runs without thinking.
- **Analysing a mistake during the game.** On the bench, recover, watch and plan one thing; park the mistake — "I'll look at that after" — and analyse it after the game.
- **Trying to suppress the bad thought.** Notice it, let it be there, and move your attention to your next action. Suppressing it uses up the attention you need for the next shift.
- **Judging yourself on outcomes you don't control.** Two posts and four good looks is a good game. Score the night on your three process goals, with the outcome goal on the list but not the verdict.
- **Shooting less in a slump.** Shoot more: set a shot-attempt target and hit it whether or not anything goes in. Fewer attempts mean fewer ugly goals and a longer drought. That slumping players shoot less is **coaching craft, not a measured finding**.
- **Believing the textbook inverted-U.** It depends on the task, and the best level is individual. Track how you felt and how you played over about eight games to find yours.
- **Trying to be calm before every game.** Some players play best angry. Find your own state and use the warm-up to push towards it.
- **Confusing a routine with a superstition.** If missing it would wreck you, it is a superstition. Prune it.
- **Mismatching the cue to the task.** Use an instructional cue ("far post") for fine actions and a motivational one ("go") for effortful ones. The evidence for an instructional edge covers **fine tasks only**.
- **Treating external-focus cueing as settled science.** "Chest over your toes" is a fine default, but the bias-corrected re-analysis found the effect close to zero. Use whichever wording lets you feel the correction.
- **Calling puck-watching a work-rate problem.** It is an attention problem, and worse when you are anxious. Tie a shoulder check to triggers: over the boards, at a whistle, as the puck goes behind your net.
- **Playing safe to avoid mistakes.** Hesitation is a mistake too, and passive play makes more turnovers, not fewer. Decide early and commit, and turn the fear into a process target you can hit.
- **Putting your head down when you get low into the boards.** Get low with your head up and chin off your chest, come in on an angle with skates parallel to the boards — **never turn your back to the checker, and never duck.** A tucked chin straightens the cervical spine, and USA Hockey says that head-first injury *"can occur at walking speed"*. See [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md).
- **Blocking a shot with your head in the lane.** Head up and out of the lane, square to the shooter, on your feet and close, shin pads presented, backs of the gloves out. Never turn your back, turn sideways or lift a leg. How much your team blocks is a coaching choice, so ask your coach — and your goalie.
- **Broadcasting a bad shift on the bench.** Your linemates read the slump and the stick slam. A stick slammed on the boards or glass can also be a penalty of its own — in USA Hockey a misconduct, and in protest of a call a game misconduct, which USA Hockey's Casebook pairs with an unsportsmanlike minor; in the NHL a minor. Hold a neutral floor and talk only about the next shift.
- **Over-consoling your goalie.** A tap on the pads and back to work beats skating over after every goal.
- **Arguing with officials.** Skate away and run your reset. Arguing a call earns a power-play penalty, and keeping at it escalates until you are thrown out; in Hockey Canada one argument can do it. An obscene gesture, a slur, spitting or a threat can end your game outright, and under Hockey Canada and CARHA so can throwing anything from the bench. Usually, your frustration becomes their power play.
- **Assuming you deserve more ice time than you get.** That most players believe this is an observation with no source behind it, but nobody judges their own case well. Ask one specific question in private, then do the work.
- **Absorbing abuse because it comes from a demanding coach.** Criticism of your play is coaching; personal degradation or routine humiliation is not. Take it to your club's safeguarding lead or another club official, the league or the governing body.
- **Treating flat stretches as failure.** Plateaus are usually consolidation. Track process markers and keep a technical project going. Hours alone were never the whole story: deliberate practice explains about 18% of performance variance — see [Practice and Development](practice_and_development.md).
- **Managing a clinical problem with performance techniques.** If it has spread beyond the rink, talk to your GP or family doctor, or a qualified clinical psychologist. Any thought of harming yourself or of suicide means get help now, tonight: call your local emergency number (999 in the UK and Ireland, 112 across the EU, 911 in the US and Canada). [§13, When to seek real help](#13-when-to-seek-real-help) has the crisis lines.

## Key Takeaways

1. **If it has spread beyond the rink, see a qualified professional — that is not a failure of toughness.** Any thought of harming yourself or of suicide means get help now, tonight: call your local emergency number (999 in the UK and Ireland, 112 across the EU, 911 in the US and Canada). [§13, When to seek real help](#13-when-to-seek-real-help) has the crisis lines.
2. **After every mistake, run the same reset — trigger, long breath out, cue word, next action — and name that action before your skates hit the ice.** On the bench, recover, watch and plan; analyse after the game. If the thought sticks, notice it and move on rather than fight it. The order is coaching craft, and the evidence for acceptance is thin. *(§2, The shift reset)*
3. **Decide early and commit: simple in your own end, creative in theirs.** Hesitation is a mistake too — the half-second you take to be sure is all a forechecker needs. Off the glass and out (not over it), chosen early, is a good play; the same chip late is a turnover. *(§8, Why fear of mistakes is self-defeating)*
4. **Score your night on three process goals for your role, and keep the outcome goal on the list without judging the night by it.** Check your team's system before you write them. ⚠️ **Never put "finish every check" on the list:** your age and your league's category decide whether you may check at all — see [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md) §1.
5. **In a slump, shoot more: set a shot-attempt target and hit it whether or not anything goes in.** Confidence comes from having done the thing, so rebuild it with reps of the exact release you lost. Self-talk helps, but it multiplies that evidence rather than replacing it. That slumping players shoot less is coaching craft, not a measured finding.
6. **Focus is switching, not holding: tie it to triggers.** Over the boards, at a whistle, as the puck goes behind your net — snap back up and check your shoulder. Puck-watching is an attention problem, not a work-rate one. This is coaching craft built on Nideffer's attention model, not a tested hockey finding. *(§7, Focus and attention)*
7. **Nerves are the body preparing; anxiety is the worry — use the first, address the second.** Call the feeling *"my body getting ready"*; that reframe has evidence, mostly from academic testing rather than sport. Find your level over about eight games, then use the warm-up: short hard bursts if flat, long smooth skating and slow breaths out if over the top. *(§5, Nerves and arousal)*
8. **Keep a pre-game routine that still runs in a strange rink or when you arrive late, and prune anything that would wreck you if it were unavailable.** Routines have real meta-analytic support, but nobody knows which parts do the work. *(§6, The pre-game routine)*
9. **Beat physical fear with technique and graded steps, not courage.** Move up only when the level below feels boring, and only once you are medically cleared. Into the wall: on an angle, head up, chin off your chest — never turn your back to the checker, and never duck. Blocking a shot: head up and out of the lane. Some fear is right; don't override it.
10. **Your emotional state is not private, and neither is your goalie's.** On the bench, hold a neutral floor — upright, eyes on the ice — and talk only about the next shift. After a goal against, tap your goalie's pads and get back to work. Goalies: run the same post-goal routine every time, your fault or not. The goalie material is coaching craft.
11. **Keep your frustration off the officials: skate away and run your reset.** Arguing a call earns a power-play penalty, and keeping at it escalates until you are thrown out; in Hockey Canada one argument can do it. An obscene gesture, banging the boards in protest, spitting, a slur or a threat can end your game at once, and a hand on an official can end your game and bring a suspension. Never throw anything from the bench, and never retaliate physically. *(§10, Frustration has a price on the scoreboard)*
12. **Short of ice time? Ask one specific question, in private, a day or two later — then do the work where the coach can see it.** Be excellent in the minutes you get. But personal degradation or routine humiliation is not coaching: take it to your club's safeguarding lead, the league or the governing body. *(§10, Coaches, benchings and criticism)*
13. **Expect flat stretches, and measure something that moves.** Results can sit still for months; process markers move week to week. Keep one technical project going and take a real off-season. If wanting to play drifts into feeling obliged to, take it seriously — [§13, When to seek real help](#13-when-to-seek-real-help) has the signals. *(§12, Motivation over a season)*

*Limitations worth knowing:*

- *No published study establishes that players in scoring slumps take fewer shots; the mechanism is described as coaching craft.*
- *The specific four-step shift-reset and post-goal goalie sequences are coaching craft with no direct experimental test.*
- *The application of graded exposure specifically to hockey contact fear is craft, though the clinical evidence for graded exposure in anxiety generally is strong. Hockey Canada's four-step guide and USA Hockey's* Introduction To Body Contact *are quoted in section 8, but what remains uncited is the claim that following them reduces **fear** — they are teaching sequences for a skill, not tested anxiety interventions.*
- ***Goal setting.** Kingston and Hardy (1997) measure a positive effect of process goals on self-efficacy, cognitive anxiety control and concentration, but against **performance** goals rather than outcome goals. Filby, Maynard and Graydon (1999) test all three goal types and find **multiple-goal strategies** best, supporting a balance that keeps outcome goals in rather than removing them. Both samples are small and neither is in hockey. Kyllo and Landers (1995) tests no process-versus-outcome contrast and is not cited for one.*
- ***Attentional control theory** is cited here to its primary statement (Eysenck, Derakshan, Santos & Calvo, Emotion, 2007). It is a theoretical account tested outside hockey, not a measured hockey finding, and is labelled as such in section 2.*
- ***"Most players believe they deserve more ice time than they get"** — an observation with no source behind it; flagged inline in section 10.*
- ***Prevalence of fear in adult recreational hockey.** No prevalence estimate is given anywhere in this document, because there is no published one. Be sceptical of the figures you will hear quoted — "about half of any adult rec dressing room" and similar — none of them trace to a study.*
- ***All of section 9 (goaltender psychology)** rests on a blog, a commercial site and an unpublished doctoral dissertation — see the source warning at the head of that section.*
