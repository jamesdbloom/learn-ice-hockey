# Finding More Ice

> **Rule set:** Nothing here changes between rule sets, except whether a league allows body checking. Ask before you join — see [Body Contact and Battles](../technique/body_contact_and_battles.md#1-which-contact-rules-are-you-actually-playing-under).
>
> **Related:** [Getting Started](../getting-started/getting_started.md) · [Practice and Development](practice_and_development.md) · [Skating](../technique/skating.md) · [Team Play and Culture](team_play_and_culture.md) · [Equipment](equipment.md)

## Key focus

**Ask your own club for more ice first** — it is the easiest extra ice to get.

**Add one more session a week if you can**, at a stick-and-puck or a drop-in.

**Ring the rink** — it usually knows every session and club on its ice.

**Use public skating for your skating**, and leave the stick at home unless the rules allow it.

**Before you join anything, ask the level, the gear, the cost and whether there is contact.**

**No team? Ask to be placed as an individual, and get on a spare list.**

**If you might play goal, say so** — goalies are scarce, so they get offered more ice.

## Overview

**Most players get far less ice than they need to improve, and most of the extra ice is found by asking, not searching.** Start close and work outwards: your club, then your rink, then the national body, which can tell you which clubs exist but not when they skate.

**Match the ice to the job.** Public sessions are for skating. Stick-and-puck is for puck work. Drop-in or a second team is for game reads. A learn-to-play block is for anyone still learning to stop.

## 1. Start with the club you already have

**In short:** Your club is the easiest source of extra ice, and the one players forget to ask.

Ask your coach or club secretary:

- **Can I join another team's practice?** A second team or a development session often has room. ⚠️ **For a child moving up an age group, ask whether that group checks** — Ice Hockey UK's junior leagues check from U14.
- **Is there a train-only place?** In England, England Ice Hockey describes a *"Train-Only option"* for players who want to train with a club *"without the pressure of having to play in matches"*. It is aimed at older children who start late.
- **Does the club book any extra ice** — a skills hour or a stick-and-puck?
- **Is there a spare list?** That is a list of players a team rings when it is short on the night. ⚠️ **In Britain, get registered to that club and team before the day of the game** — it cannot be done on the night, and a team that plays an unregistered player can lose the result.

## 2. Public skating

**In short:** The cheapest ice you can buy. Use it for skating, not hockey.

Rinks list their public sessions on their websites, usually under "public skating" or "what's on". Before you go:

- **Check the posted rules on sticks and pucks.** Assume they are not allowed unless the rules say so.
- **Ask the desk which sessions are quietest.** A quiet session gives you room to work.

A public session is full of children and new skaters. Keep fast starts, hard stops and backward skating to a clear patch of ice, if the posted rules allow them at all.

## 3. Stick-and-puck and drop-in

**In short:** The most useful extra ice for most players. Find it through the rink and through teammates.

**Stick-and-puck** is open ice for practising on your own with a puck. **Drop-in** is a pick-up game you pay for on the night. The names are loose, so read the rink's own description — [Getting Started](../getting-started/getting_started.md#4-drop-in-stick-and-puck-and-shinny--what-the-words-mean) explains them.

**How to find one:**

1. **Look on the rink's timetable** under hockey or "ice hire", not the public skating page.
2. **Ring the desk** if nothing is listed.
3. **Ask teammates about the sessions players run themselves.** Some drop-ins are booked by a club or a group of players and filled by word of mouth.
4. **Try other rinks within reach.** One a little further away may run sessions where yours runs none.

Then read [Drop-in and pick-up etiquette](../getting-started/getting_started.md#drop-in-and-pick-up-etiquette) before your first one.

## 4. Recreational and adult teams

**In short:** Ask to be placed as an individual, and ask about checking in the same call.

**Rec** (recreational) hockey is hockey played for fun rather than in the top leagues. Section 7 lists where to find clubs. Elsewhere, ask the rink which clubs and leagues use its ice.

Ask:

1. **What level is it?** Judge yourself on your skating, not your shot, and take the lower level if unsure. See [Working out which tier you belong in](../getting-started/getting_started.md#working-out-which-tier-you-belong-in).
2. **Is it a checking league?** ⚠️ **Ask your coach or league secretary, not the rink desk — being a beginner does not put you in a non-checking league.**
3. **Do you place individual players?** Some leagues do; others take only whole teams. Canada's Adult Safe Hockey League has a sign-up form for individuals. ⚠️ **Still on a junior team in Britain? Ask before registering with an adult team** — under Ice Hockey UK's junior rules, a player who registers for senior membership cannot play junior hockey for the rest of that season.
4. **Is it sanctioned?** ⚠️ **In Britain, unsanctioned hockey is uninsured, and under England Ice Hockey's last published rules a registered player who joins an unrecognised league faces suspension and fines.** In Canada, Hockey Canada cannot insure players in an unsanctioned league, and a child registered in one loses all membership privileges for the rest of the season. See [Getting Started](../getting-started/getting_started.md#why-it-exists-insurance-and-safeguarding).
5. **What does it cost, and do you need a goalie?** If you might play goal, say so — clubs often lend goal pads, though you should buy your own mask. See [A note on playing goal](../getting-started/getting_started.md#a-note-on-playing-goal).

## 5. Learn-to-skate, learn-to-play and youth programmes

**In short:** Learn to skate first or alongside, then learn to play, then join a club team — and tell the club you are coming.

[Getting Started](../getting-started/getting_started.md#3-routes-onto-the-ice--children) covers the children's programmes and [the adult routes](../getting-started/getting_started.md#2-routes-onto-the-ice--adults).

- **Learn-to-play and the club may not be connected.** England Ice Hockey says learn-to-play programmes are *"usually run by the ice rink or sometimes the club"*, and that rink programmes *"are not directly linked to junior clubs"*. So contact the club as well.
- **Skate first if you can.** England Ice Hockey advises *"working up to Level 5 skating before entering Learn to Play"*. Ask the rink what its Level 5 is.
- **For a child, ask whether the coaches are background-checked** (a DBS or PVG check in Britain) **and who the club's safeguarding lead is.** [Getting Started](../getting-started/getting_started.md#why-it-exists-insurance-and-safeguarding) explains both.

## 6. Camps and clinics

**In short:** No camp finder was found on the national sites. Ask your club, your rink and your coach.

Ask who coaches it, what level it suits, how many players there are per coach and whether there is contact. ⚠️ **In Britain, ask whether the camp is sanctioned by Ice Hockey UK** — its insurance covers a camp only if it is sanctioned and you are registered. For a child, ask the same safeguarding questions as in section 5. [Practice and Development](practice_and_development.md#if-you-skate-once-or-twice-a-week-most-adult-players) suggests one **power-skating** session a season — coaching on skating technique alone.

## 7. Where to look, by place

*Checked 22 October 2026. If a link fails, go to the body's home page and look for "play" or "clubs". These lists tell you which clubs exist, not when they skate — for that, ring the rink.*

**Across Britain:** Ice Hockey UK has governed all four home nations since 30 June 2026 — see [Getting Started](../getting-started/getting_started.md#if-you-are-starting-in-britain) — but its website has no club finder. To find a rink and its learn-to-skate classes, use British Ice Skating's [rink finder](https://www.iceskating.org.uk/rink-finder).

### England

> - England Ice Hockey's [Play page](https://englandicehockey.com/play/) explains the English pathway. Its club finder is not live.
> - For adult rec hockey, it links its [Rec section](https://www.eiharec.co.uk/index2.html), whose team list names each team and its home rink.
> - Older pages under the EIHA name are out of date; use the links here.

### Scotland

> - Scottish Ice Hockey, now part of Ice Hockey UK, lists [junior clubs](https://siha-uk.co.uk/find-your-ice-hockey-club/), [recreational clubs](https://siha-uk.co.uk/find-your-recreational-club-2/) and [women's clubs](https://siha-uk.co.uk/find-your-womens-club/).
> - Its [Recreational page](https://siha-uk.co.uk/recreational-section/) says some clubs run *"Hockey For All"* sessions for beginners.

### Wales

> - Welsh clubs play in the English leagues, so use the England links above — the Rec team list includes the Cardiff and Deeside teams.
> - No separate Welsh ice hockey body was found.

### Northern Ireland

> - Scottish Ice Hockey's [junior](https://siha-uk.co.uk/find-your-ice-hockey-club/) and [recreational](https://siha-uk.co.uk/find-your-recreational-club-2/) lists include Northern Ireland clubs.
> - The Irish Ice Hockey Association, which describes itself as the governing body for ice hockey in Ireland, lists Belfast clubs and their training times on its [Clubs page](https://www.iiha.org/clubs.html).
> - Ask the club you join which body it registers with.

### USA

> - USA Hockey's [Find Hockey Near You](https://www.usahockey.com/findhockey) finds local youth programmes by zip code.
> - Its [Come Play Youth Hockey](https://www.usahockey.com/comeplayyouthhockey) page links Try Hockey For Free, [Learn to Skate USA](https://www.learntoskateusa.com/find_a_program) and [NHL/NHLPA Learn to Play](https://www.learntoplay.nhl.com/about/), whose locations are on each NHL team's page.
> - Adults: ring the rink and ask which adult leagues and drop-ins use its ice.

### Canada

> - Hockey Canada's [Pathway to Minor Hockey](https://www.hockeycanada.ca/en-ca/hockey-programs/parents/pathway) links its association finder, The First Shift (learn-to-play) and Esso Fun Days (a free taster for girls and women). Hockey Canada calls the finder *"a starting point"*, so confirm eligibility with the association.
> - Learn-to-skate: Skate Canada's [Find a Club](https://skatecanada.ca/learn-to-skate/find-a-club/), or your municipal parks and recreation department.
> - Adults: Hockey Canada's [Adult Recreational Hockey](https://www.hockeycanada.ca/en-ca/hockey-programs/adult-rec) page and the Adult Safe Hockey League's [individual players](https://www.ashl.ca/individuals/) page.

### Anywhere else

> - The IIHF's [Member National Associations](https://www.iihf.com/en/associations) page lists every national body it recognises. Start there, then ring the rink.

## Common Mistakes

- **Waiting for your team to give you more ice.** Ask your club, and add a session yourself.
- **Only searching online.** Many sessions are never listed centrally. Ring the rink, which usually knows them, and ask teammates about the rest.
- **Taking a stick to a public session without checking the rules.**
- **Turning up to the wrong drop-in.** Ask the level, the gear and whether there is contact first.
- **Joining a league without asking whether it allows checking.** Ask your coach or league secretary before your first game.
- **Playing in an unsanctioned league or camp.** In Britain it is uninsured, and under England Ice Hockey's last published rules a registered player in an unrecognised league faces suspension and fines.
- **Doing a learn-to-play block and never telling the club.** The rink's programme may not lead anywhere on its own.
- **Booking a camp on its name.** Ask who coaches it, what level it suits and how many players there are per coach.

## Check yourself

*Five optional questions — skip to the Key Takeaways if you prefer. Try each before you check: getting an answer wrong and then checking teaches more than recognising a right one. The section to check is named with each.*

2. What do you check before taking a stick to a public session? *(§2)*
3. Your rink lists no stick-and-puck. What next? *(§3)*
4. You finish a rink's learn-to-play block in England. Why might no club know about you? *(§5)*

## Key Takeaways

1. **Ask your own club first** — for extra practices, a second team, a train-only place or a spare list.
2. **Add stick-and-puck or drop-in.** It is the most useful extra ice for most players.
3. **Ring the rink and ask teammates.** Between them they know almost every session.
4. **Use public skating for skating,** and bring a stick only if the rules allow it.
5. **Before joining anything, ask the level, the gear, the cost and whether there is contact.** ⚠️ **For a league, ask your coach or league secretary whether it allows checking, and in Britain whether it is sanctioned.**
6. **No team? Ask to be placed as an individual.** ⚠️ **In Britain, register with the club and team before the day of any game for it.**
7. **Learn to skate first or alongside, then learn to play, then join a club** — and tell the club you are coming.
