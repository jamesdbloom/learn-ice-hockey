## Key focus

**Where you shoot from matters more than how you shoot it.** Shots from the slot — the ice between the faceoff circles — score at roughly 10–15%, perimeter shots at roughly 2–4%. The source names no dataset, so trust the gap, not the decimals. If a teammate is in the slot and you are not, pass; if two strides to the middle make it a slot shot, take them.

**Release speed beats shot speed.** A goaltender reads your body, hands and blade and gets set before the puck arrives. Don't gather the puck, don't stop your feet — shoot from where you already are.

**Make the goalie move sideways before you shoot.** A set, square goalie saves almost everything. Pass across the middle first — cross-slot, D-to-D, low to the far point — or come out from behind the net on the other side, and shoot the moment the puck arrives.

**Aim where the goalie is not.** Take the five-hole while they are moving, reaching or mid-drop, not into a set butterfly, and the far corner low just after a cross-ice pass. With bodies at the net, shoot low and hard at the pads for a rebound; alone in the slot, pick a target and finish. Many good coaches teach "aim for the corners" instead — this document follows the other school, so treat it as a choice between two.

**Get the puck through, not just hard.** A shot into a shin pad is a turnover that usually starts a counter-attack. Find the lane first; if there is none, step sideways, fake to move the blocker, or move the puck. From the point, a low shot that arrives beats a hard one that doesn't.

**Stand in the goalie's sightline on every shot — from outside the crease, without touching them.** Keep your skates off the **red crease line**, not just out of the blue paint: every book that settles the question counts that line as crease, and inside it your position alone can cost the goal. Stay out of the goalie's path back to the net, and take a shove head up, chin off your chest, meeting a post with an arm or a leg first. Find out which book your league runs.

**Get your blade into the puck's path at the net front — changing its direction beats adding speed.** A goalie squared to a shot cannot re-read a tip three feet out. Angle the blade — open to lift, closed to keep it low — and keep soft hands: a rigid stick fires the puck to the corner, a yielding one drops it at your feet.

**Follow the shot to the net for the rebound.** A goalie who has just made a save is recovering, not set. Arrive with your stick on the ice, shoot the rebound at once, and play the puck, not the goaltender — avoid them even when shoved, because contact you start can cost the goal and earn you a penalty.

## Overview

Shooting is two skills taught as one: the mechanics of getting the puck from your stick to the net, and the shot selection that decides which are worth taking at all.

**Where you shoot from is most of a shot's value.** One widely cited breakdown puts shots from the slot — in front of the net, between the faceoff circles and out to their tops — at roughly 10–15%, and from the perimeter — near the boards and the blue line — at 2–4%. It names no dataset, so trust the gap rather than the decimals. Pass to a teammate in the slot, or take two strides to get there yourself.

**Release speed beats shot speed.** A shot that arrives after the goalie is set and square is a routine save at almost any speed. The whole job is to shoot before they get there.

**Make the goalie move sideways, or take away their sight.** A shot straight after a pass across the middle, or through a screen, beats the same shot at a set goalie. So pass across first, attack from behind the net, and put a body in the goalie's eyes with a stick on the ice. The sight half has never been priced and the movement half only in one narrow case, so any multiplier you hear on a screen is folklore. ⚠️ **Plant outside the blue paint and off the red line around it, never touch the goalie, and stay out of the road a scrambling goalie takes back to the net.** Every book that settles it puts that line inside the crease; what a skate inside costs differs by book — [In and around the crease](#in-and-around-the-crease--what-each-book-allows-and-what-it-charges) sets them out. Take away their eyes, not their room.

**A shot into a shin pad is worse than no shot.** It gives up the puck and usually starts a counter-attack. Find the lane first, or step sideways until there is one.

**Aim where the goalie is not, and with bodies at the net, shoot to create.** This document follows the coaching school against corners: the five-hole while the goalie is moving, the far corner low after a cross-ice pass, and — with teammates at the net — a hard low shot at the pads, which can become a goal, a tip or a rebound. Alone with the goalie beaten, pick a target and finish.

**Then go and get the second chance.** A tip beats a goalie who has set for the shot; a rebound catches one recovering. Blade on the ice in the puck's path, soft hands on the tip, and shoot the rebound the moment it comes. Arrive for the puck, not the goaltender.

**The vocabulary is on one page.** [Language and Glossary](../foundation/language_and_glossary.md) defines the words used here — the slot, high slot and goalmouth, the point and half-wall, the five-hole and six-hole, release, flex, cupping — and [Rink Map](../foundation/rink_map.md) draws the ice to scale. **Two you need before the mechanics: the blade's *heel* is the end nearest the shaft, its *toe* the far end.** **And keep the goalmouth and the slot apart: they are different coverage jobs, and a defender who leaves the goalmouth for the slot leaves a tap-in unmarked.**

## The Shot Types

There are six things you can do to a puck to send it at the net. Learn all six; you will use three of them constantly.

---

### The Wrist Shot — the fundamental

```facts
Position: Puck behind your back foot, cupped on the heel-to-middle of the blade
Technique: Pull, don't slap. Sweep the puck forward and never let it leave the blade
Technique: Load the flex — push down with the bottom hand while pulling back with the top
Action: Roll the wrists at release as the puck runs heel to toe, so it spins rather than wobbles
Key: Follow through low for a low shot and high for a high one — for the wrist shot, the measured predictor of height is how the blade turns through contact, not the angle it ends at
Key: Blade turn predicting shot height is one study of stationary wrist shots by twenty-five male university players; nothing in it was measured on a snap shot, slap shot, shot on the move or deflection
Goal: Accuracy and deception from almost any body position, with the least room of any shot
```

This is the shot everything else is built from. If you only ever get one shot right, get this one.

**Mechanics, in order:**

1. **Start the puck behind your back foot**, cupped on the **heel-to-middle** part of the blade. "Back foot" means the foot furthest from the net in your stance — for a right-handed shooter facing the net, that is usually the right foot. Starting the puck back gives you a runway; starting it beside your foot means there is nothing to accelerate through.
2. **Hands comfortably apart on the shaft.** Top hand near the end, bottom hand roughly 30–35 cm (12–14 inches) below it — for most adults that is a little *narrower* than shoulder width, so use the measurement rather than the image. Grip firmly but not tightly — a white-knuckle grip locks your wrists, and the wrists are the whole shot.
3. **Pull, don't slap.** Drag the puck forward along the ice in a sweep, from behind the back foot to in front of the front foot. The puck stays in contact with the blade the entire time. This is the difference between a wrist shot and everything else: nothing ever leaves the ice or leaves the blade.
4. **Transfer your weight** from back foot to front foot as you sweep, the same weight shift as a golf swing or a throw. Power comes from your legs and torso, not your arms. Players who shoot with arms only cap out early and never get past it.
5. **Load the flex.** As you sweep, push down and forward with the bottom hand while pulling back and in with the top hand. Those two opposing forces bend the shaft. That stored bend is the second half of your power.
6. **Roll the wrists at release.** As the puck rolls from heel to toe of the blade, snap your wrists over — the blade face rotates from cupped-over-the-puck to closed-toward-the-target. This is the "snap" everyone talks about, and it is what puts spin on the puck. A spinning puck flies flat and true; a wobbling puck does whatever it likes.
7. **Follow through at the target.** Finish low, with your blade and hands pointing at the ice-level part of the net, and the puck stays low. Finish high, with hands and blade rising toward the top corner, and the puck rises. You do not aim a wrist shot by aiming your eyes; you aim it with where the follow-through ends up.

> **How height actually works — read the caveat with it.** The usual coaching account is that the puck leaves on the line the **blade face is pointing when it separates from the blade**: an open (tilted-back) face lifts it, a square or closed face keeps it down. **For the wrist shot, the one study of it this document has found says otherwise.** Michaud-Paquette, Pearsall and Turcotte tracked twenty-five male university players, varsity down to recreational, taking stationary wrist shots at two high and two low targets, and found: *"the end blade orientation at puck release does not predict shot trajectory, and in turn accuracy outcome."*
>
> Their explanation is **contact time**. A clubface is on a golf ball for roughly **10 ms**, so its angle at impact *is* the launch angle; a blade is on the puck for around **180 ms** and is still turning throughout. In their words, *"the puck in a sense is not impacted so much as guided forward by means of steering with the stick's shaft and cradling in the blade's concave front profile."*
>
> What *did* predict the high shots was the **change** in blade orientation through contact — the flick — with shaft bend, the blade's roll at release and puck release velocity, explaining **76.0%** of the accuracy variance at each top target. At the bottom targets the stick explained much less — **36.7%** and **40.6%** — and the strongest single predictor was **where the puck sat relative to the blade heel**. **Two cautions from inside the thesis:** its Discussion credits **six** parameters with the 76% at both top targets, but its own Results table needs **eight** at the top-ipsilateral one; and its abstract and Discussion report the top-versus-bottom accuracy comparison with different numbers **in opposite directions**, a comparison the peer-reviewed *Sports Engineering* version drops. **So no low-versus-high accuracy figure is quoted here** — though the mechanism above is the same in the abstract, the Results and the Discussion.
>
> **Scope that before you act on it:** stationary shots in a laboratory, twenty-five male university players, varsity down to recreational, one research group, wrist shot only. Nothing was measured on a snap shot, a slap shot, a shot taken while skating, or a deflection — and a deflection is the one case where the golf-like account does apply, because there the puck really is struck rather than carried. No study extending this to the other shots was found in Crossref, OpenAlex or Europe PMC.
>
> Follow-through is not a competing mechanism, and the measurement leaves the coaching cue **stronger, not weaker**. A player can reliably aim a finish but cannot meter a blade angle, and if height comes from how the blade *turns* through a long contact, a whole-arm cue is the right kind of instruction. Finishing low keeps the blade closing through release; finishing high rolls it open.
>
> The practical consequence is unchanged: **use follow-through as your cue for height** (net-front low versus top corner), and treat **deliberate face angle as a tool for deception** — opening the face as though going high and closing it at the last instant, which is covered under deception below.

**When to use it:** Almost anywhere you have a beat of time — off the rush, in the slot, off a rebound, from the half-wall. It is the most accurate shot you have and needs the least room.

**Trade-offs:** Less raw velocity than a slap shot, and it takes marginally longer than a snap shot because of the sweep. In exchange you get accuracy, deception, and the ability to shoot from almost any body position.

### The Snap Shot — the most useful shot in modern hockey

```facts
Position: Puck beside or just behind your skate, chest facing the net, no wind-up
Technique: Bottom hand at or just above the midpoint — the wind-up, not the grip, separates snap from slap
Action: Drop the blade behind the puck and load the shaft — cocking a spring, not winding up an axe
Action: Transfer weight back to front, short and sharp; flat-footed and arms-only is the fault to fix first
Read: Inside the faceoff dots, or a window that will be shut in half a second — this is your shot
Risk: Less power than a slap shot; you will not beat a goalie from the blue line with it
```

The snap shot is the compromise between the wrist shot and the slap shot, and the compromise is so good that at higher levels it has quietly become the default. You get most of the power of a slap shot with most of the release speed of a wrist shot.

**What makes it different from a wrist shot:** there is no long sweep. The puck sits **beside or just behind your skate**, roughly where it would be if you were stickhandling, and you shoot from there. Your chest faces the net rather than your shoulder — you are already in an attacking posture, so nothing about your body tells the goalie a shot is coming.

**Mechanics:**

1. **Puck to the side of your body**, on the blade, no wind-up.
2. **Bottom hand lower on the shaft than for a wrist shot** — at or just above the midpoint. This position is what lets you bend the shaft hard in a very short motion. **Be aware that this overlaps with the slap-shot grip described below** (midpoint or lower): the two hand positions sit close together and often coincide. The grip is not what distinguishes a snap shot from a slap shot — **the wind-up is.** Snap shot: hand near the middle, no wind-up. Slap shot: hand at the middle or below, with a wind-up.
3. **Drop the blade just behind the puck and load the shaft** by pushing down with the bottom hand while pulling back with the top hand. You are cocking a spring, not winding up an axe.
4. **Transfer weight from your back leg onto your front leg**, exactly as in a wrist shot. The difference is not the *direction* of the transfer, it is the *distance*: because there is no long sweep, the shift is short, sharp and finished almost as soon as it starts. Failing to transfer at all — shooting off a flat-footed, arms-only motion — is the most common snap shot fault.

   > **An honest disagreement between coaching schools — power versus release.** [HockeyShot](https://hockeyshot.com/blogs/training-academy/four-types-of-shots) draws a deliberate contrast: for the **snap shot** it says transfer your weight to *"your leg closest to the puck"*, and for the most powerful **wrist shot** it says *"the leg opposite the puck."* That is a considered position, not sloppy wording — the claim is that a quick-release shot trades the long back-to-front transfer for a shorter, closer shift that costs some power and buys you time. Most other coaching sources teach back-to-front for both. [Better Hockey](https://www.betterhockey.com/blogs/shooting/four-types-of-hockey-shots) is one of them, and it *is* a counter-claim, though you have to read the whole article to see it: its snap-shot section gives no weight-transfer instruction at all, and the back-to-front instruction appears in the *wrist shot* section — but the article's own summary generalises it to every shot, *"Every shot is powered by the same engine: weight transfer from your back leg to your front leg and stick flex, not arm strength."* So this is two coaching schools disagreeing, not one source being vague.
   >
   > **What to do with that:** if you have a beat of time, transfer back to front — the extra power is real and this document teaches it as the default. If you do not have a beat, do not spend a stride getting your weight onto the "correct" foot. **Shoot off whichever foot your weight is already on.** That is the same instruction as the deliberate "wrong foot" release under [Release Speed Beats Shot Speed](#release-speed-beats-shot-speed), and it is why the default here is "back to front" rather than "back to front, always". The one point every source agrees on is that a flat-footed, arms-only shot with **no** weight shift is the fault worth fixing first.

5. **Release almost immediately.** Roll the wrists up to open the face and lift it, keep them flat to keep it down. **That the blade's angle at release is what sets the height is the coaching account and not a measured one — on the wrist shot it was measured and did not hold** (see the blockquote under [The Wrist Shot](#the-wrist-shot--the-fundamental)); nothing located for this document has tested a snap shot either way.

**When to use it:** Quick chances in the slot. Off a pass when a defender is closing. Off a rebound. Any time the window is open now and will be shut in half a second. If you are inside the faceoff dots, this is probably your shot.

**Trade-offs:** Slightly less power than a full wrist shot with a long runway, and much less than a slap shot. You will not beat a goalie from the blue line with it. You will beat one from twenty feet (about six metres), constantly.

### The Slap Shot — powerful, and usually the wrong choice

```facts
Technique: Strike the ice two to five centimetres behind the puck, not the puck itself
Technique: Hands wide, bottom hand at the midpoint or below; a three-quarter wind-up is more useful more often, though CARHA still counts it as a slap shot and a penalty
Action: Finish low with the face square — low is what a teammate can tip and what leaves a playable rebound
Read: From the point with a lane and time, on a set one-timer, from the top of an umbrella — roughly that list, and never in a CARHA league, where it is a penalty
Risk: The wind-up flags the shot to goalie and blocker, and a miss from the point clears your own zone
Rule: The NHL, IIHF, PWHL and Hockey Canada ban no slap shot and USA Hockey bans one only in Blind Hockey — but CARHA 79(a) bans it outright, a minor, a major if it injures an opponent, and that major ejects you (30(a)) plus a one-game ban (32(d)); 79(b) is a minor for a fake slap shot used to intimidate
Rule: A CARHA major is not only tonight — 30(a) adds a game misconduct to every major and 32(d) an automatic one-game ban; an accidental high stick is exempt, and one that injures still ejects you (62(b) Note) and escapes the automatic ban, but 32(b) sends it to the Chairman (CARHA adult leagues only)
Rule: The Note to CARHA Rule 79(a) puts the threshold at a backswing "more than fifteen inches either on or off the ice" where CARHA's own Glossary says "off the ice" — the book disagrees with itself, so play the Note; and it binds CARHA-affiliated leagues only
Action: Look before you wind up, and use a three-quarter wind-up in traffic — the carve-out that permits the follow-through is not in every book, and under CARHA a three-quarter is still a slap shot
```

The slap shot is the hardest shot in hockey and the most overused. Be honest with yourself about this: it takes the longest to set up, it is the least accurate, it is the easiest to read and block, and the situations where it is genuinely the best option are narrow.

**Mechanics:**

1. **Wind up** by lifting the stick back and up. A full wind-up takes the blade to around shoulder height; a three-quarter wind-up takes it to around waist height and is more useful more often. **In a CARHA league both are a slap shot and a penalty** — that book counts any backswing past fifteen inches, as set out below.
2. **Hands wide apart** — bottom hand well down the shaft, at the midpoint or below it. The wider the hands, the more leverage you have to bend the shaft. (As noted under the snap shot, the two grips overlap around mid-shaft; the wind-up, not the hand, is what makes this a slap shot.)
3. **Strike the ice two to five centimetres (an inch or two) behind the puck, not the puck itself.** This is the whole trick and it is counter-intuitive. You are hitting the ice to bend the shaft. The bend loads, the shaft springs forward, and the blade catches the puck as it recoils. Hitting the puck directly gives you an arm-powered slap with none of the stored energy — which is why beginners' slap shots are slower than their wrist shots.
4. **Transfer weight onto the front skate** through the downswing. Same principle as everywhere else: legs and torso, not arms.
5. **Contact the puck near the middle of the blade** as the shaft releases, then roll it toward the toe.
6. **Follow through at the target.** Finish low and keep the face square — from the point, low is almost always what you want, because that is what a teammate can tip and what produces a playable rebound. **That the blade's angle at release is what sets the height is the coaching account and not a measured one — on the wrist shot it was measured and did not hold** (see the blockquote under [The Wrist Shot](#the-wrist-shot--the-fundamental)); nothing located for this document has tested a slap shot either way.

**When to use it:** From the point, when you have a lane and time and want the puck to arrive at the net front hard and low for a tip. On a one-timer from a set position. On the power play from the top of an umbrella. That is roughly the list — outside a CARHA league, where the slap shot is a penalty.

**Trade-offs and the honest assessment:** The wind-up is a giant flag telling the goalie a shot is coming and the defender to drop into the lane. The blocked-shot rate is high, the miss rate is high, and a missed slap shot from the point usually goes around the boards and out of the zone — you have just given away possession for nothing. A controlled three-quarter slap shot that hits the net is worth far more than a full wind-up that sails wide. Most of the time, from the point, a low hard **snap** shot is the better play: nearly as heavy, twice as fast to release, far more likely to get through.

> **Rec, beer-league and youth leagues — and the book most likely to catch you out.** Many slap-shot bans really are local league and association rules, and **one is not.** There is no slap-shot ban in the NHL rulebook, none in the IIHF's or the Professional Women's Hockey League's (PWHL), and none in Hockey Canada's, and the **USA Hockey playing rules prohibit slap shots in exactly one place: Blind Hockey** (*"Slap Shots are prohibited in Blind Hockey"*, in both its Playing Rules and its Casebook).
>
> **But the CARHA book bans the slap shot outright, and it is a rule book, not a house rule.** CARHA Hockey's standalone *Official Rule Book* is adopted wholesale by the Canadian adult leagues affiliated to it ([Rules Primer](../foundation/rules_primer.md) sets out what else is in it). **Rule 79(a):** *"Any player who uses a 'slap shot' during the game shall be assessed a Minor penalty. **If an injury to an opponent results, a Major penalty shall be assessed.**"* Its Note sets the threshold — *"The Slap Shot is to bring the stick back behind the puck more than fifteen inches either on or off the ice"* — **so a three-quarter wind-up is a slap shot under this book too: shoot a snap or wrist shot, keeping the backswing under fifteen inches.** **Rule 79(b)** adds a minor for a *"Fake Slap Shot"* used to intimidate a player or goalkeeper. ⚠️ **And that major does not stop at the final whistle.** **Rule 30(a)** puts any player given a major *"off the ice for the remainder of the game (Major penalty plus Game Misconduct)"*, and **Rule 32(d)** adds an automatic one-game suspension for any such major except one under the Accidental High Stick Rule — **so an injuring slap shot costs you the rest of tonight and your next game**, and the offender *"may be subject to further discipline action."* ⚠️ **The book disagrees with itself about where the line sits:** the Note says *"either on or off the ice"*, the Glossary *"more than 15 inches **off the ice**"*, so a fifteen-inch backswing kept low along the ice is caught by one and not the other. **Play the Note.**
>
> **What that does and does not tell you.** CARHA governs CARHA-affiliated leagues only and is not a general rec-hockey standard; how many leagues run its book is a question about leagues rather than about books, and this document has no count for it. Many other adult and youth leagues restrict or ban slap shots by their own house rules, often below a certain age. **So find out which book your league runs, and read its own rules sheet, before you plan your game around a slap shot.**

**The wind-up also carries a cost that has nothing to do with shot selection.** A stick that catches someone above the shoulders is a penalty in every book unless, under the NHL's, the IIHF's or the PWHL's, it came accidentally in a normal wind-up or follow-through — and its size depends on your book. ⚠️ **Look before you wind up; in traffic use a three-quarter wind-up, and under CARHA, where even that is a slap shot and a penalty, shoot a snap or wrist shot** — USA Hockey, Hockey Canada and CARHA write no follow-through carve-out at all, and CARHA has no two-minute rung for contact above the shoulders. What each book charges is below.

#### High sticks and the wind-up — what each book actually charges

```facts
Rule: The windup and follow-through carve-out is NHL, IIHF and PWHL only (60.1; PWHL 61.1) — USA Hockey writes none, even in its Casebook, and in junior and senior hockey Hockey Canada Interpretation 1 to Rule 9.5(a) makes a shooting follow-through above the shoulder a minor where no injury results
Rule: Even where it is written the carve-out has a limit — NHL Rule 60.1: "A wild swing at a bouncing puck would not be considered a normal windup or follow through", and IIHF 60.1 and PWHL 61.1 carry that sentence in their own punctuation
Rule: Hockey Canada Rule 9.5 caps its own tiers at a double minor but does not cap your exposure — Rule 9.5(b) and Rule 9.5(c) send any high stick warranting a major or a match to Rule 7.7 Head Contact for Junior and Senior, and in minor and female hockey Rule 7.6 Head Contact takes over instead
Rule: A raised stick can be penalised with no contact at all under USA Hockey — Casebook Rule 621 Situation 2 against 621(a), the minor. IIHF 2025/26 Rule 60.1 defines a high stick as one "carried above" the shoulders, though its 60.2 minor needs contact
Rule: Under USA Hockey a high stick that recklessly endangers an opponent is a mandatory major plus game misconduct — 621(b), "shall be assessed" — and plan on a match: general Rule 602(a) says one "shall be assessed" to any player who "recklessly endangers or attempts to injure" an opponent
Rule: The IIHF's top in-game tier is not the NHL's — IIHF Rule 60.4 lets the Referee assess a major plus an automatic game misconduct for a high stick that recklessly endangers, no injury needed, where NHL Rule 60.4 is a match penalty only for attempted or deliberate injury
Rule: The IIHF writes no match penalty for anything, so a major plus the automatic game misconduct is its ceiling inside the game only — 60.7 allows Supplementary Discipline above it at the Proper Authorities' discretion, and 20.7 requires the Referee to report every major to them (both editions)
Rule: CARHA has no minor rung above the shoulders — 62(b) is a major for contact there "whether or not injury results", accidental or not, with its Note adding a game misconduct when intentional or injuring; its minor, 62(a), is a different act, a carry above the opponent's waist. CARHA leagues only
Never: Lean on a definition to excuse the swing. Keep the blade down, look before you wind up, and three-quarter it in traffic — though under CARHA a three-quarter is still a slap shot and a penalty
```

**Keep the blade below everyone's shoulders, look before you wind up, and shorten the wind-up in traffic — that is the whole instruction, under every book.** What follows is what each book charges when a stick gets there anyway, and the books differ more than most players expect.

**The permission to swing through is not universal, and the difference is a penalty.** NHL and IIHF Rule 60.1 permit accidental contact *"committed as a normal windup or follow through of a shooting motion"* (the NHL's wording; the IIHF adds quotation marks of its own). ⚠️ **Both write the limit into the very next sentence:** *"A wild swing at a bouncing puck would not be considered a normal windup or follow through and any contact to an opponent above the height of the shoulders shall be penalized accordingly."* A shooting motion is protected; a swing at a jumping puck is not — which is exactly when to abandon the one-timer, under [The One-Timer](#the-one-timer--shooting-a-pass-without-stopping-it) below.

**USA Hockey and Hockey Canada** ⚠️ **write no carve-out.** USA Hockey's Casebook points the other way: Rule 621, Situation 2 — *"It is not a requirement that a stick make contact above shoulder height for high sticking to be called"* — and Situation 3 puts a major plus a game misconduct **or a match penalty** on a falling player whose stick recklessly endangers an opponent, because *"All players are expected to have their sticks under control at all times."* The book's Rule 621 Note defines the offence with contact and the Casebook says contact is not needed; nothing settles which governs, so plan for the stricter reading. **Under USA Hockey, plan on a match penalty for a reckless stick as well as a malicious one:** 621(b) makes it a mandatory major plus a game misconduct, and the general **Rule 602(a)** says a match *"shall be assessed"* to any player who *"recklessly endangers or attempts to injure"* an opponent, though 621(c) says only that one *"may also be assessed"*. **Hockey Canada reverses the carve-out in junior and senior hockey:** Interpretation 1 to Rule 9.5(a) makes it a minor where a follow-through *"hits an opposing player above the shoulder **and no injury has resulted**"*; with an injury 9.5(a) reaches a double minor at the referee's discretion. ⚠️ **And 9.5 is a floor with a redirect, not a ceiling:** 9.5(b) says *"No Major penalty may be assessed for high-sticking"* and sends anything that warrants one to **Rule 7.7(b) – Head Contact – Junior/Senior**, a major plus a game misconduct, as 9.5(c) sends a match to 7.7(c). In Hockey Canada minor and female hockey the same contact is Head Contact under Rule 7.6, which *"supersedes any other rules"* bar fighting. Whether a carry alone is punishable under 9.5 its text does not settle. [Rules Primer](../foundation/rules_primer.md) covers the cross-book comparison.

**The IIHF's two editions disagree about whether a high stick needs contact.** The **2025/26** edition defines it by the carry alone — *"A 'high stick' is one which is carried above the height of the opponent's shoulders"* — and the **2026/27** edition uses the NHL's: *"A 'high stick' is one which contacts an opponent above the shoulders, provided their shoulders are at waist level or higher."* Both require contact for the **minor** (Rule 60.2), and both carry the same **Rule 60.4** major. 🇬🇧 **Which applies in Britain depends on the competition.** The *Motorpoint EIHL Casebook 26-27* adopts the 2026/27 book for every rule it does not mention, and it has no Rule 60 section, so an Elite League player is under the contact definition. **Everywhere else nothing published settles it**: the In-House Rules' cover names the *"Current IIHF Rule Book"*, their Introduction the 2025/26 one. [UK and England Rules](../foundation/uk_rules.md) sets that out. ⚠️ **Either way, keep the blade down, and do not lean on a definition to excuse the swing.**

**And the carve-out is not a ceiling on what a high stick costs in the books that write it.** **NHL and IIHF Rule 60.2** is a minor for *"any contact made by a stick on an opponent above the shoulders"*. **Rule 60.3**, and PWHL 61.3, is a **double minor** where the stick contacts the *"neck, face or head"* and *"injury results, in the manner of drawing blood or otherwise"* — and here the newer IIHF rule is the wider: 2025/26 reaches only a player who *"carries or holds any part of their stick above the shoulders"*, while 2026/27, like the NHL, asks only for contact *"(above the height of the opponent's waist)"*. Above that the books part company. **NHL 60.4 is a match penalty** for a player who *"attempts to or deliberately injures an opponent with a high-stick"*, and the NHL has no major tier. **IIHF 60.4** lets the Referee give *"a major penalty and an automatic game misconduct penalty"* where the player *"recklessly endangers their opponent by 'high-sticking'"* — listed among its automatic-game-misconduct majors in Reference Table 6 (2025/26) and the merged Table 5 (2026/27). 🇬🇧 **So under the IIHF book a high stick judged reckless ends your game on the first one, with no injury needed.** ⚠️ **The IIHF's missing match penalty is only its ceiling inside the game:** Rule 60.7 allows Supplementary Discipline under Rule 28, *"whether or not such offense has been penalized by the Referee(s)"*, and Rules 20.7 and 23.2 require every major and game misconduct to be reported to the body that can impose it — the same in both editions. ⚠️ **Look before you wind up, and use a three-quarter wind-up in traffic.** See [UK and England Rules](../foundation/uk_rules.md) for what a first major does to an adult.

**Keep the blade under the shoulders through the follow-through, because one adult rec book starts at five minutes, not two.** **CARHA Rule 62(b):** *"A player who accidentally or intentionally contacts an opponent above the normal height of the opponent's shoulders with a high stick shall be assessed a Major penalty, whether or not injury results."* Accidental counts, no injury is needed, and there is no two-minute rung, where every other book here writes one. Its **Note** adds a game misconduct where the contact was intentional or an accidental one injures; **62(c)** is a match penalty for deliberately attempting to injure or injuring with a high stick. Its minor, **62(a)**, is a **different offence, not a lower rung** — checking or intimidating *"while carrying their stick above the waist height of their opponent"*, no contact needed, which its own case situation applies to a swing at the puck that catches someone above the waist. ⚠️ **Under CARHA a major ejects you by default:** **Rule 30(a)** puts you *"off the ice for the remainder of the game"*, and the only exception for a skater is an accidental high stick — which 62(b)'s Note withdraws once injury results. ⚠️ **Rule 32(d) then adds an automatic one-game suspension** to a major *"other than the Accidental High Stick Rule"*, **so an *intentional* high stick costs you this game and the next.** ⚠️ **An accidental one that injures still ejects you, but as written carries no automatic suspension** — though **32(b)** reports every game misconduct *"to the Chairman for further action"*. There is no route back down to a minor: **Rule 50, Head Contact**, excludes contact with a stick and sends it back to Rule 62. **CARHA governs CARHA-affiliated adult leagues only**, so find out whether it is your book; if it is, the three-quarter wind-up is not a refinement.

### The Backhand — the most neglected shot in hockey

```facts
Position: Puck on the back of the blade between middle and heel, on the flat spot rather than the curve
Technique: Hands wider than on a forehand — the extra spread buys the leverage the backhand is short of
Technique: Keep the puck close and sweep in an arc, rolling heel to toe; reaching kills the power
Read: In tight, on breakaways, on rebounds to your off side, or whenever turning to the forehand costs the chance
Key: Goalies calibrate their release read to forehands, so a backhand gives them less to read
Action: Practise it — it is bad because it is under-practised, a pure volume problem with a volume fix
```

Almost nobody practises the backhand, and almost every goalie finds it harder to stop. Those two are related, and together they are an opportunity sitting in plain sight. **Both halves are coaching observations rather than measured findings** — nobody has counted how players split their reps, and no source here counts save percentage by shot type. What is argued below is the mechanism in each case: the development path that produces thousands of forehand repetitions to a few dozen backhands, and the release read a goalie builds out of them.

**Mechanics:**

1. **Puck on the back side of the blade**, positioned between the middle and the heel — on the flat spot rather than the deepest part of the curve. Most blades have very little useful curve on the backhand side, so the flat area is your friend.
2. **Hands a little wider than for a wrist shot** — top hand at the end of the shaft, bottom hand roughly 40–50 cm (16–20 inches) below it, which is somewhat lower than the 30–35 cm you use on the forehand and well above the halfway point you use for a slap shot. The extra spread buys leverage, which is what the backhand is short of.
3. **Keep the puck close to your body to start.** Backhand power comes from a compact motion, not a long reach. Reaching kills it.
4. **Sweep in an arc**, not a straight line — the blade travels across in front of you rather than straight at the net. Roll the puck heel to toe as it goes.
5. **Push down with the bottom hand to create leverage and bend the shaft**, and pull through. You get much less flex on the backhand than the forehand, which is why the technique matters more.
6. **Transfer weight to the front leg, toward the target.**
7. **Follow through pointing at the target** — open the face to lift it, keep it square to keep it low. **That the blade's angle at release is what sets the height is the coaching account and not a measured one — on the wrist shot it was measured and did not hold** (see the blockquote under [The Wrist Shot](#the-wrist-shot--the-fundamental)); nothing located for this document has tested a backhand either way.

**Why goalies struggle with it:** Goaltenders spend the overwhelming majority of their reps facing forehands, so their read on release timing is calibrated to a forehand. A backhand releases from a different point, at a different moment, with less pre-shot telegraph. It also frequently forces a movement across the body — from a shooter cutting to their backhand side, a high backhand is one of the awkward saves in the game.

**Why most players neglect it:** you grow up taking thousands of forehand reps and a few dozen backhands, so the backhand feels bad, so you avoid it, so it stays bad. It is a pure volume problem with a pure volume fix.

**Where it wins:** in tight around the net, on breakaways, on rebounds that land on your off side, and any time you are in a compromised body position and turning to your forehand would cost you the chance. In traffic in front of the net, you will get more backhand looks than forehand ones — take them.

### The One-Timer — shooting a pass without stopping it

```facts
Action: Set your feet before the pass arrives — if you are still skating, you are receiving, not one-timing
Position: Open your body to the passer so the pass reaches your blade without going through your feet
Technique: Start the backswing as the pass is released, not when it arrives, and keep it short — under CARHA, a backswing past fifteen inches is a slap shot and a penalty
Read: A pass behind you — do not reach back and swing at it. Kill the shot, receive it, and take a snap shot half a second later
Read: Bouncing or rolling puck — abandon the one-timer, cup the blade, give with it and shoot second
Never: One-time it with a stick or a shoulder inside your swing. Take the pass and shoot second instead
Convention: Whether you are the one-timer option is a system choice — a 1-3-1 is built around it, an overload usually is not
Rule: A one-timer is a wind-up taken in traffic, and the follow-through carve-out is the NHL's, the IIHF's and the PWHL's alone (60.1; PWHL 61.1) — USA Hockey's book writes none, nor Hockey Canada's, nor CARHA's
Rule: CARHA Rule 62(b), CARHA-affiliated adult leagues only, is a major for contact above an opponent's shoulders whether or not it injures, accidental or not, plus a game misconduct by its Note if intentional or injuring
Rule: Even where it is written, the carve-out stops at a wild swing — NHL Rule 60.1: "A wild swing at a bouncing puck would not be considered a normal windup or follow through", and IIHF Rule 60.1 is that sentence word for word in its own punctuation
Rule: The IIHF's top in-game tier is not the NHL's — IIHF Rule 60.4 lets the Referee assess a major plus an automatic game misconduct for a high stick that recklessly endangers, no injury needed, where NHL Rule 60.4 is a match penalty only for attempted or deliberate injury
Rule: The IIHF writes no match penalty for anything, so a major plus the automatic game misconduct is its ceiling inside the game only — 60.7 allows Supplementary Discipline above it at the Proper Authorities' discretion, and 20.7 requires the Referee to report every major to them (both editions)
```

A one-timer is shooting a pass directly, without receiving it first. It is the fastest way to move a shot from one side of the ice to the other, which — as the section on the royal road below explains — is exactly the thing goalies most struggle with.

> **A one-timer is a wind-up, and you take it in the slot with bodies around you, so everything under [The Slap Shot](#the-slap-shot--powerful-and-usually-the-wrong-choice) about the follow-through carve-out applies here and matters more.** ⚠️ **Keep the backswing short, and if there is a stick or a shoulder inside your swing, take the pass and shoot second.**
>
> **And the carve-out stops exactly where the mechanics below tell you to abort.** **NHL Rule 60.1**, in the sentence after the one that permits the follow-through: *"A wild swing at a bouncing puck would not be considered a normal windup or follow through and any contact to an opponent above the height of the shoulders shall be penalized accordingly."* IIHF Rule 60.1 says the same. ⚠️ **So swinging at a bouncing puck also costs you the only protection the NHL, IIHF and PWHL books give** — and USA Hockey, Hockey Canada and CARHA never gave any. Under CARHA 62(b), CARHA-affiliated adult leagues only, contact above the shoulders is a major, accidental or not, **with a game misconduct where it was intentional or an accidental one injures**. 🇬🇧 **Under the IIHF book one wild swing a referee judges reckless ends your game on the first one** — IIHF 60.4's major plus automatic game misconduct needs no injury, where NHL 60.4's match penalty needs an attempt to injure. ⚠️ **And that ejection is only the IIHF's ceiling inside the game:** Rule 60.7 allows Supplementary Discipline above it, and Rule 20.7 requires every major to be reported. The full ladder is under [The Slap Shot](#the-slap-shot--powerful-and-usually-the-wrong-choice).

**Mechanics:**

1. **Set your feet before the pass arrives.** This is the whole skill. Square to where you want to shoot, feet planted and wide, weight ready to move onto the front leg. If you are still skating when the pass gets there, you are receiving, not one-timing.
2. **Open your body to the passer** so the pass can reach your blade without going through your feet.
3. **Start the backswing as the pass is released, not when it arrives.** This is the timing point everyone gets wrong: if you wait to see the puck reach you, you are already late. Read the passer's release and begin your wind-up then.
4. **Keep the backswing short.** A three-quarter or half wind-up is far more repeatable than a full one and misses less often, and a missed one-timer is worse than no shot. **In a CARHA league, a backswing past fifteen inches makes it a slap shot and a penalty.**
5. **Contact the puck near the middle of the blade** with the blade square to the target and weight moving onto the front leg. Follow through at the net, low.

**When the pass is bad — and it usually is.** Most one-timer chances arrive slightly wrong: too far ahead, behind you, bouncing, or rolling on edge. Have a plan:

- **Pass slightly ahead of you** — extend and take it off the toe of the blade rather than lunging. You lose power; take the shot anyway if the lane is there.
- **Pass behind you** — do not reach back and swing at it. Kill the shot, receive the puck, and take a snap shot half a second later. A snap shot on target beats a whiffed one-timer every time.
- **Bouncing or rolling puck** — abandon the one-timer. Settle it and shoot second: **cup the blade over the puck and give with it**, absorbing the bounce rather than blocking it. ("Soft" means yielding hands, not an open blade face — an uncupped blade is exactly what a bouncing puck rides over. See [Passing and Receiving](passing_and_receiving.md).) One-timing a rolling puck sends it into the corner — and here the rulebook agrees: **NHL and IIHF Rule 60.1 and PWHL Rule 61.1 exclude *"a wild swing at a bouncing puck"* from the follow-through carve-out**, and USA Hockey, Hockey Canada and CARHA write no carve-out at all. No book protects that swing.

**The one-timer is a coaching-system play as much as an individual skill.** Whether you are the one-timer option depends on your team's offensive-zone setup and power-play formation — a 1-3-1 power play is built around the far-side one-timer, an overload usually is not. See [Special Teams](../systems/special_teams.md) and [Offensive Zone Play](../systems/offensive_zone_play.md), and find out what your team runs.

### Tips and Deflections — changing direction beats adding speed

```facts
Position: Blade in the puck's path at the net front, flat on the ice or just above it
Technique: Present an angled face rather than hitting the puck. Open it to lift, close it to keep it low
Technique: Soft hands — a rigid stick fires the puck to the corner, a yielding one drops it at your feet
Action: Aim to tip at knee, waist or chest height, and watch the puck all the way in
Rule: A tip contacting your stick above the crossbar is no goal, at or below it counts (NHL and IIHF 80.3, PWHL 82.3, Hockey Canada 6.9(b)) — but USA Hockey 621(d) sets that line at your shoulders, and its Casebook 621 Situation 7 makes any part of the stick above them high, so that goal dies
Rule: The crossbar rule reaches a puck you send in "either directly or deflected off any player or official" and stops there — a teammate who takes possession and shoots is a new chain and that goal counts (IIHF Handbook 2025/26 and 2026/27, Situation 80.9(c)); above your shoulders Rule 80.1 whistles it
Rule: Batting the puck above shoulder height stops play only if your team then gains possession (NHL 80.1) — USA Hockey 621(d) prints an immediate whistle, but its Casebook 621 Sit. 15 applies the same test, so play the tip on; CARHA 62(d) stops play at once. If both teams leave it, play stops (NHL 72.3)
Rule: Hockey Canada 9.5's preamble reaches a high-sticking penalty "regardless of whether or not contact occurs", but 9.5(a) — the only clause that assesses one — requires contact, so play it as though the carry alone can be penalised; in minor and female hockey Rule 7.6 Head Contact takes over
Rule: A high stick on the puck is one rule; one on a person is another — NHL and IIHF 60, PWHL 61, USA Hockey 621(a)–(c), Hockey Canada 9.5, or 7.6 Head Contact via 6.9(c) in minor and female hockey, none capped at a minor; in CARHA 62(b) is a major, plus a game misconduct if intentional or injuring
Key: Changing the puck's direction late is worth far more than adding speed to it
```

A tip (or deflection) is redirecting a shot from a teammate as it passes you, usually from the net front. It is the single most valuable thing a forward can do without ever possessing the puck, and it is closer to a receiving skill than a shooting one.

**Mechanics:**

1. **Get your stick blade into the puck's path**, flat on the ice or just above it, at the height you expect the shot.
2. **Blade angle does the work.** Open the face slightly and the puck lifts; close it and the puck stays low or drops. You are not trying to hit the puck — you are presenting an angled surface for it to bounce off.
3. **Soft hands.** Do not brace and do not swing. A rigid stick sends the puck straight back out; a stick held loosely, absorbing slightly on contact, keeps the puck low and controlled. If the tip does not go in, a soft blade drops it at your feet for a rebound rather than firing it to the corner.
4. **Keep the blade on or near the ice** unless you are deliberately going up for a high tip. The pucks you get to tip arrive low, because low is what a point shooter is coached to give you — see **Low and hard for tips** under [Shooting Through Traffic](#shooting-through-traffic). No source this document draws on counts tipped goals by height, so that rests on where shots are aimed, not on a tally of goals. Two ceilings limit how high you may tip:
   - **The crossbar decides the goal.** **Rule 80.3** disallows a goal you cause *"by contacting the puck above the height of the crossbar, **either directly or deflected off any player or official**"*; *"[t]he determining factor is where the puck makes contact with the stick."* Contact at or below the bar that goes in is a good goal under the NHL, the IIHF, the PWHL and Hockey Canada — **USA Hockey uses your shoulders instead and disallows it**, set out below. ⚠️ **The rule stops at a deflection.** If a teammate takes **possession** of a puck you contacted above the bar but below your shoulders and shoots it in, the goal counts — IIHF Situation Handbook, Situation 80.9(c), the same in the 2025/26 and 2026/27 editions. Above your shoulders, **NHL and IIHF Rule 80.1** whistle it as soon as your team gains possession, so that escape is closed.
   - **Your shoulders decide the whistle.** **Rule 80.1**: *"Batting the puck above the normal height of the shoulders with a stick is prohibited"* — but play stops only when the puck then *"comes into the possession and control of a player from the offending team"*. A puck that goes to the opposition plays on. IIHF Rule 80.1, PWHL Rule 82.1 and Hockey Canada Rule 6.9(a) work the same way. ⚠️ **USA Hockey Rule 621(d) prints an immediate whistle, but its own Casebook puts it back close to the other books:** Rule 621, Situation 15 says *"an offending team player must be the first to gain possession of the puck"* before play stops, so you play the tip out — except that a high-sticked puck that goes straight into the goal is an immediate stoppage. A deflection off an opponent is not the possession that cancels it (IIHF Handbook 80.2 and 80.15, Hockey Canada Interpretation 1 to 6.9(a)(i)), and USA Hockey's Situation 6 holds the whistle on a defender's high stick to their own goalie until the goalie has the puck. How much North American rec and youth hockey runs on USA Hockey's book is not counted here.
   - **Where the face-off goes afterwards is where the books split hardest — though none of it changes how you tip.** If neither team plays a high-sticked puck, every book stops play (NHL and IIHF 72.3). The NHL, IIHF, PWHL and Hockey Canada then face off near where play stopped; USA Hockey's Casebook (Rule 621, Situations 8, 10 and 12) sends the draw to the offending team's end, so leaving the puck alone buys territory only under USA Hockey. Five more plays split the same books: **your high stick, their goalie freezes it** — the IIHF faces off in your attacking zone (Handbook 80.12), USA Hockey in your defending zone (Situation 9); **a high tip straight in** — USA Hockey your defending zone (Situation 15), the NHL and IIHF the nearest neutral-zone spot (Rule 76.2, Handbook 80.13); **your high stick puts it out of play in the attacking zone** — USA Hockey your defending zone (Situation 8), the IIHF the neutral zone (Handbook 85.31, Rule 80.2); **an opponent then high-sticks it too** — the IIHF keeps your violation (Handbook 80.3), USA Hockey switches it to theirs (Situation 11); **a high stick during a delayed off-side** — the IIHF the defending team's end (Handbook 80.10), USA Hockey the nearest neutral-zone spot (Situation 13, only in age classifications that use delayed off-side). ⚠️ **CARHA Rule 62(d) is strictest:** *"play shall be stopped immediately and a face-off shall take place in the offending team's defensive zone at the nearest face-off spot. No penalty is assessed to the player"* — CARHA-affiliated adult leagues only. Know your book before you argue with an official.
   - **The two ceilings do not stack.** A tip above your shoulders but below the crossbar that goes straight in **is a good goal** — *"If the puck makes contact with the stick at or below the level of the crossbar and enters the goal, this goal shall be allowed"* (Rule 80.3). ⚠️ **That is the NHL's, the IIHF's, the PWHL's and Hockey Canada's answer (Hockey Canada 6.9(b), IIHF Handbook 80.6, Hockey Canada Interpretation 4 to 6.9(a)(i)); under USA Hockey the same tip is no goal.** Rule 621(d) bars a goal from *"an attacking player playing the puck above the shoulder with the stick and directly entering the goal"*, and its Casebook (Rule 621, Situation 7) rules that *"[w]hen any part of the stick is carried above the shoulders, the entire stick is considered to be high."* Do not carry the good-goal half into a USA Hockey game.
   - **All of that is about the puck. The moment the blade reaches a person it is a different rule and a different price** — NHL and IIHF Rule 60, PWHL Rule 61, USA Hockey 621(a)–(c), Hockey Canada 9.5, and in Hockey Canada minor and female hockey **Rule 7.6 Head Contact**, via 6.9(c). ⚠️ **None of those is capped at a minor.** **CARHA 62(b)** makes contact above the shoulders a **major**, accidental or not, *"whether or not injury results"*, with a game misconduct if it was intentional or an accidental one injures; its minor, 62(a), is a separate offence — checking or intimidating with the stick above your *opponent's* waist, no contact needed. CARHA-affiliated adult leagues only. [High sticks and the wind-up](#high-sticks-and-the-wind-up--what-each-book-actually-charges) has each book's ladder. **At the net front you are tipping in a crowd, so the blade comes down after the tip as well as before it.**
   - Practically: **tip at knee, waist or chest height.** That keeps you clear of both ceilings and is where tippable pucks arrive anyway. Above your shoulders risks the possession whistle — an immediate one under CARHA 62(d) — and above the bar loses the goal. ⚠️ **Carrying the stick high with no contact can still be a penalty.** Under USA Hockey its Casebook says so (Rule 621, Situation 2): *"Whenever a player raises their stick above their shoulders in an attempt to intimidate an opponent or is careless in their actions, high sticking must be called"* — so 621(d) is a stoppage with no penalty and 621(a) is a minor needing no contact. Under Hockey Canada the text does not settle it: 9.5's preamble reaches a penalty *"regardless of whether or not contact occurs"*, but 9.5(a) requires contact, so play it as though the carry alone can be penalised; in minor and female hockey Rule 7.6 takes over.
5. **Watch the puck all the way in**, which is hard, because a defenceman is usually trying to move you.

**Why it works:** a goaltender tracks the puck and sets their body for a known trajectory. A deflection changes the trajectory a few feet in front of them, after they have committed. There is no time to re-read it. This is the principle that runs through this entire document — **changing the puck's direction late is worth far more than adding speed to it.** A modest shot tipped six inches sideways in front of the goalie beats a much harder one they have read from the blue line. (No figures are attached to that claim on purpose. You will see comparisons like "a 60 mph tipped shot beats a 95 mph clean one" quoted around the rink; they are illustrations somebody made up, not measurements of anything.)

#### Contact with the goaltender at the net front

```facts
Position: Outside the blue paint and off the red crease line, in the goalie's eyeline, not touching them and never in their path back to the crease — that is the legal and valuable screen
Rule: Screening from outside the crease without contact is legal — 69.1's disallowing sentence is keyed to an attacker who "enters the goal crease", 69.3's screen ground to a significant position within it, and 69.4 takes the goal outside it for contact only (NHL and IIHF Rule 69, PWHL 71)
Rule: Contact you initiate with a goalkeeper who is in their crease costs your team the goal outside a rebound or loose-puck scramble, whatever your own position was — the IIHF writes "a relevant contact" where the NHL writes contact (NHL and IIHF 69.3, PWHL 71.3; the 69.7 exception is next)
Rule: Rule 69.7 is the exception and it is narrow — incidental contact is permitted only where a goalkeeper and an attacker are simultaneously playing a rebound or a loose puck, inside or outside the crease, and only short of driving the goalkeeper in (NHL and IIHF 69.7, PWHL 71.7)
Rule: Even in a scramble the goal dies where a goalkeeper is pushed into the net together with the puck, by an attacking player, after making a stop — all three of those have to hold — and penalties may follow (NHL and IIHF 69.7, PWHL 71.7)
Rule: A goal disallowed for pushing the goalkeeper in has one relief — where the Referee judges that a defending player pushed or fouled you into them, it "can be permitted", which is judgement and not entitlement (NHL and IIHF 69.7, PWHL 71.7)
Rule: With the puck under a player in or around the crease, no goal can be scored by pushing that player and the puck into the net, and penalties may follow (NHL and IIHF 69.7, PWHL 71.7)
Rule: Rule 69's premise is the NHL's, the IIHF's and the PWHL's (71.1) — under Hockey Canada any contact you initiate with the goaltender voids the goal "inside or outside of the goal crease", and USA Hockey 625(b) voids it on your position alone (Hockey Canada Interpretation 1 to Rule 8.5(a))
Rule: Charging a goalie in the crease can eject in every book — discretionary in NHL and PWHL 42.1, IIHF 42.4, USA Hockey 607(c); mandatory in Hockey Canada 8.5(b) anywhere, CARHA 52(b) there or any injuring charge, USA Hockey 607(b) if reckless, NHL, PWHL 42.5 and EIHL 42 if a major hurts face or head
Rule: Arriving into a goaltender who is turned away can be checking from behind, not charging — no minor in NHL, IIHF or PWHL 43.2, a major and game misconduct above it, the IIHF's where the referee judges it reckless (43.3); IIHF 43.1 names the goal frame, and its 5.1 "Player" covers goalkeepers
Rule: Charging or interfering with a goaltender can reach a mandatory match penalty — Hockey Canada 7.4(c) and 8.5(c); for a deliberate injury or attempt, NHL and PWHL 21.1 and CARHA 48(a); USA Hockey 602(a) for an attempt to injure or reckless endangerment. None in the IIHF
Rule: Leave a goalkeeper alone outside their crease — they are not "fair game" there, and every book calls for a penalty on unnecessary contact: NHL, IIHF and PWHL 42.1, USA Hockey 607(d) ("privileged area"), CARHA 52(b) Note, Hockey Canada 8.5 ("anywhere on the ice")
Rule: An attacker whose deliberate actions keep a goalkeeper who has played the puck outside their crease from returning to it "may be penalized" for goalkeeper interference, with no contact required — NHL and IIHF 69.4, PWHL 71.4
Never: Crowd a goalie so they cannot move, or set the screen on the red crease line — every book settling the question puts that line in the crease, and inside it your position alone can cost the goal
```

**Stand in the goalie's eyes from outside the paint, never touch them, and stay out of their road home; in a scramble, play the puck and pull up short of the goaltender.** That is the play under every book. The rest of this section is why, and what each book does when a net-front player gets it wrong.

Screening a goalie — standing outside the crease in their sightline without touching them — is normally legal. **Rule 69.1** disallows a goal only where an attacker *"either by his positioning or by contact, impairs the goalkeeper's ability to move freely within his crease or defend his goal"*, or initiates deliberate contact. ⚠️ **Read that clause whole before leaning on it.** It opens *"Goals should be disallowed **only if**"* — a **ceiling** on when a goal may be taken off, not a direction to take one off — and the sentence that actually disallows a goal is keyed to an attacker who *"enters the goal crease"*. So a clean screen from outside the paint is legal; crowding a goalie inside the crease is not, contact or no contact. **Rule 69.3** voids the goal for contact with a goalie in their crease, incidental or otherwise, and for a significant position *inside* it with no contact at all — a substantial part of your body in there for more than an instant that **both** obstructs the goalie's vision **and** impairs their ability to defend. **Rule 69.4**: a goalkeeper *"is not 'fair game' just because he is outside the goal crease"*, and *"The appropriate penalty should be assessed in every case where an attacking player makes unnecessary contact with the goalkeeper"*; incidental contact is permitted only while the goalkeeper is playing the puck outside the crease and you have *"made a reasonable effort to avoid such unnecessary contact."* **Rule 69.7** permits incidental contact in a rebound or a loose-puck scramble, inside or outside the crease — **but not driving a goalkeeper who has made the stop into the net together with the puck.** ⚠️ **Do not assume either permission carries into a USA Hockey, Hockey Canada or CARHA game:** USA Hockey's Rule 607(d) Note 1 sends *"any accidental or unavoidable contact that occurs with the goalkeeper"* to the Interference rule.

**The rest of 69.7 is what takes a goal off you.** *"In the event that a goalkeeper has been pushed into the net together with the puck by an attacking player after making a stop, the goal will be disallowed. If applicable, appropriate penalties will be assessed."* All three parts must hold — *together with the puck*, *after making a stop*, *by an attacking player* — so it is not a bar on every collision in a scramble. Its one relief is the Referee's judgement, not your entitlement: if *"the attacking player was pushed or otherwise fouled by a defending player"* into the goalkeeper, *"the goal can be permitted"* — read *"can"*, not *"will"*. And with the puck under a player in or around the crease, *"a goal cannot be scored by pushing this player together with the puck into the goal"*, with penalties if applicable — a penalty shot among them where the Referee judges the puck was covered deliberately. The IIHF carries all four limbs in its own wording, so do not quote one book as the other.

**So what may you actually do?** Stand outside the blue paint and off the red line around it, in the goalie's eyes, without touching them. **Under the NHL and the IIHF, the sentences of Rule 69 that take a goal for where you stood all name the crease:** 69.1's disallowing sentence (*"enters the goal crease"*), 69.3's no-contact ground (a significant position *within* it), and 69.4, which takes the goal outside it for contact rather than position. **The clause people quote at you is 69.1's *"only if"* list**, whose positioning limb (*"or defend his goal"*) names no location — but that list is a **ceiling** on when a goal may be taken off, not a direction to take one off. ⚠️ **And 69.4 still reaches your position:** a player whose *"deliberate actions"* keep a goalkeeper who has played the puck outside the crease from returning to it *"may be penalized for"* goalkeeper interference, with no contact at all — so standing in a goaltender's road home can cost two minutes. **That is a reading of the rule's structure rather than its stated text — though both books' tables answer this exact screen at Situations 5 C and 5 E** — and Rule 69 *"will be enforced exclusively in accordance with the on-ice judgement of the Referee(s)"*. Some competitions can review it — the NHL by Coach's Challenge, the IIHF by Video Review and, at selected IIHF Championships only, a Coach's Challenge — while the British In-House Rules allow neither in EIH or SIHA competition. ⚠️ **And stay off the red line.** The NHL's and the IIHF's tables allow a screen *"on the crease line"* (Situation 5 E), but USA Hockey's Note to Rule 625(b) and its Casebook, the Hockey Canada and CARHA Glossaries and **IIHF Rule 1.7** put the line inside the crease, and the NHL's Rule 1.7 never answers it. **Off the line is the instruction that holds under every book.** What you may **not** do is step into the paint and crowd the goalie so they cannot move. See [Rules Primer](../foundation/rules_primer.md).

**All of that is Rule 69, which the NHL, the IIHF and the PWHL (Rule 71.1) run. USA Hockey and Hockey Canada do not**, and they change what a net-front player should do: stricter inside the paint, and more permissive outside it **about a screen with no contact — not about contact**. Hockey Canada Rule 8.5 says a goaltender *"is not 'fair game' just because they are outside their goal crease"* and reaches unnecessary contact *"anywhere on the ice"*, and its **Interpretation 1 to Rule 8.5(a)** takes the goal off you for contact wherever it happened: *"Any goal scored on a play where an attacking player initiates contact with the goaltender will be disallowed, regardless of whether the contact occurs inside or outside of the goal crease"* — the only exception an attacker *"fouled by a defending player and, as a result, is unable to avoid contact with the goaltender."* ⚠️ **So do not carry 69.4's reasonable-effort permission into a Hockey Canada game.** Its 8.5(b) reaches a major plus a game misconduct at the referee's discretion *"based on the degree of violence of impact"* **with nobody hurt**, and **mandatorily** for charging the goaltender or where the foul injures. ⚠️ **But the ejection for charging a goaltender is not Canadian — every book here reaches a major plus a game misconduct for it**, and USA Hockey for a reckless charge, CARHA, and the NHL, the PWHL and the Elite League when a major for it injures the face or head all make it mandatory in named cases. [In and around the crease](#in-and-around-the-crease--what-each-book-allows-and-what-it-charges) quotes each book; read it before you take a tip from inside a crease.

**69.4's returning-goalie clause is not the only rule on standing in a goaltender's way — ordinary interference reaches the same act in most books.** NHL and IIHF Rule 56.1 put the goaltender outside possession — *"The last player to touch the puck, other than the goalkeeper, shall be considered the player in possession"* (the NHL's wording) — and Hockey Canada's Glossary and CARHA's Note 1 to Rule 66 do the same. So a goaltender scrambling home is an opponent **not in possession**, and impeding one is interference under **NHL and IIHF 56.2(i)**, **Hockey Canada 8.3(i)** (the minor at 8.3(a)) and **CARHA 66(a)(1)**. **That is usually two minutes, but a violent or reckless play can cost a major, in some books with an ejection, and one that hurts someone can end your game.** ⚠️ **USA Hockey reasons about possession the other way.** Its Glossary has no goalkeeper exclusion — *"The last player to have intentionally played the puck is considered to have 'possession of the puck.'"* — so a goaltender who has just cleared it is the player in possession, and the next sentence protects them: *"A player considered to be in 'possession of the puck' is NOT eligible to be body checked and/or engage in competitive contact."* ⚠️ **So under USA Hockey a returning goaltender is a player you may not body check at all.** ⚠️ **Its Casebook answers this play at Rule 625, Situation 9, citing 625(b) and the contact clause 625(a)(8)**, while the Situation itself reaches *"any interference"*. How far that reaches an attacker who never touches the goaltender is not settled, **but the price is: the goal and two minutes — and deliberate contact with the goaltender is charging under its Rule 607(d) Note 1, never a bare minor.** **The other half of NHL and IIHF 56.1 keeps a screen legal:** *"A player is allowed the ice he is standing on (body position) and is not required to move in order to let an opponent proceed"*; the offence is the *"pick"*, by a player *"who moves into an opponent's path without initially having body position"*. **The line is between holding ice you already occupy and moving into the road home** — ⚠️ **so stay out of the road back to the crease whatever the goaltender has just done.**

## Release Speed Beats Shot Speed

Here is the insight that reorganises everything else.

**Shot speed** is how fast the puck travels after it leaves your blade. **Release speed** is how long your shooting motion takes, from first movement to the puck leaving. They are different things, and release speed matters far more.

The reason is that a goaltender does not save a puck by reacting to it. They save it by reading it — watching your body, your hands, your blade, and your weight, then getting their body into the right position *before* the puck is on its way. If your shot arrives after they are set and square, it is a routine save at nearly any velocity. If your shot arrives before they are set, it is a scoring chance at almost any velocity.

One coaching resource puts the trade-off as: a 70 mph shot with a 0.3-second release will beat a goaltender more often than a 90 mph shot with a 0.7-second release, because the second one gives the goalie twice as long to be ready ([HockeyMonkey](https://www.hockeymonkey.com/learn/nhl-scorers-deception-quick-release)). Treat those specific numbers as an illustration rather than a measurement — they are not from a published study — but the principle behind them is coaching consensus everywhere.

### How to actually get a faster release

```facts
Action: Stop gathering. Shoot off the puck you already have, in the position you are already in
Never: Stop your feet before shooting. Learn to release in stride, mid-stride, while still moving
Action: Shoot off the "wrong" foot — it costs velocity and buys a moment the goalie's read says no shot is coming
Action: Change your release point — pull the puck 15–30 cm laterally before releasing
Technique: Open the face as though going high and close it at the last instant; look at one corner, shoot the other
Action: Look off to a teammate in the slot to move the goalie's weight, then shoot into the space that opens
```

**Stop gathering.** The most common release-killer is the little pause where you settle the puck, look up, set your feet, and then shoot. Every one of those is a frame of warning. Shoot off the puck you already have, in the position you are already in.

**Do not stop your feet.** Stopping your skates before shooting is the loudest tell in hockey — every goalie reads it, every defender reads it. Learn to **shoot in stride**, releasing mid-stride while you are still moving. It feels less powerful at first because you cannot plant and load; you get that power back from your momentum, and you get a half-second of surprise on top.

**Shoot off the "wrong" foot.** The textbook shot releases as your weight arrives on the front foot — for a right-handed shooter, the left foot. Releasing off the *same-side* foot instead is unconventional, which is exactly why it works: it comes at a moment in your stride when the goalie's read says a shot is not coming. It costs some velocity. It is worth it. Practise it deliberately; it will not appear on its own. (Which foot this is *relative to the boards* flips depending on which wing you are on — see [Shooting Off the Rush](#shooting-off-the-rush).)

**Change your release point.** Do not release from the same spot in your stance every time. Pull the puck 15–30 cm (six to twelve inches) laterally — in toward your feet, or out away from your body — before releasing. A goalie who has set their angle for a shot from point A now has a shot coming from point B, and their positioning is wrong by exactly that much. Moving the puck sideways also often opens a shooting lane past a defender's stick.

**Use blade and body deception.** Open the blade face as though you are going high, then close it at the last instant and go low. Look at one corner and shoot the other. Exaggerate a posture that suggests a shot to one place and release to another. All of these are cheap, all of them are trainable, and all of them work because the goalie is reading you, not the puck.

**Look off, then shoot.** A half-beat glance at a teammate in the slot moves a goalie's weight. Shoot into the space that opens.

## Accuracy and Shot Selection — where to actually aim

```facts
Targets: Five-hole while the goalie is moving, reaching or mid-drop — not into a set butterfly
Targets: The far corner low, in the instant after a cross-ice pass, while they are still moving across
Targets: Low blocker side to generate a rebound; the gaps under the arms and over the pads
Read: Short side high only when the goalie has cheated off their post — not a default
Priority: With bodies at the net, shoot to create — low, hard, at the pads; alone in the slot, pick a target and finish
Convention: "Aim for the corners" is one coaching school and this section follows the other — you are choosing, not correcting
```

"Aim for the corners" is the standard instruction given to almost every young player, and there is a **coaching school that disagrees with it** — the one this section follows. Its argument: corners are the smallest targets on the net, they are guarded by the goalie's fastest-moving limbs, and missing one means the puck goes wide and out of the zone. Aim where the goalie is *not*, and where a miss still does something useful. Plenty of good coaches still teach corners; know that you are choosing between two schools rather than correcting an error.

**The targets that are actually worth having** — and the ordering between them is coaching judgement rather than a measurement, because no source located for this document prices one target inside the net against another:

- **Five-hole — but time it.** The gap between the pads. Whether this is a big target or a nearly sealed one is **contested**, and the answer is that it depends on what the goalie is doing at the moment of release. Against a butterfly goalie who is already **down and set**, the five-hole is largely closed — that is the entire point of the butterfly, and it is why shooters at every level drift toward shooting high in practice. It **opens** while the goalie is moving: pushing laterally, reaching a skate toward a post on a deke, or in the act of dropping. Modern goalies spend a lot of their time doing one of those, which is what makes it worth having. Take it when they are in motion or mid-drop; do not fire it into a set butterfly and call it a plan.
- **The far corner off a lateral pass.** When the puck has just crossed the middle of the ice, the goalie is still moving across to square up. Their momentum carries them, and the space they are moving *away from* — the far side, low — opens up. It is the target worth taking ahead of any other, and it exists only in the instant after a cross-ice pass. **That ranking is coaching judgement, not a measured one** — what has been priced is the pass that creates it, not the corner you aim at; [Offensive Zone Play](../systems/offensive_zone_play.md) owns the cross-slot pass figures and the qualifications on them.
- **Low blocker side for a rebound.** The blocker is a flat, hard surface, and pads and blocker kick pucks back into the slot far more often than the glove swallows them. If you have a teammate at the net front, a hard low shot to the blocker side is a rebound-generating machine.
- **Under the arms, over the pads.** The gaps between glove and body, and blocker and body, are second only to five-hole for size and reliability.
- **Short side high**, but only when the goalie has cheated off their post. Not a default.

**Shooting for a rebound or a tip is often better than shooting for a corner.** A shot at a corner either goes in, is saved, or misses. A hard low shot at the pads with a teammate at the net front either goes in, gets tipped in, or produces a rebound in the most dangerous area on the ice — three good outcomes against one. So with bodies at the net, shoot to *create* (low, hard, on net, at the pads) rather than to *finish*; alone in the slot with the goalie beaten, pick a target and finish. Be explicit with yourself about which one you are doing. [When NOT to Shoot](#when-not-to-shoot) below turns this into a single pre-shot test.

## Shooting Through Traffic

```facts
Action: Look for the lane before you shoot — no sight of the net or of net-front sticks means no lane
Action: Change the angle — step sideways along the blue line until the blocker has to move their feet; their sticks are set for the angle you had
Technique: Lift the puck a few inches to clear sticks; a foot or more is untippable and easy to catch
Technique: Low and hard for tips — knee height or below, with enough pace that a small deflection still carries
Options: Fake the shot to drop the defender into the lane, or move the puck until a lane exists
Goal: From the point, get it through rather than shoot it hard — the shot is there to make a tip, rebound or scramble
Never: Shoot into a defender's shins — you give up the puck without testing the goalie, and the block usually starts the counter
```

A shot that hits a shin pad is worse than no shot at all. **You have given up possession, you have not tested the goalie, and the block usually starts a counter-attack the other way** — three costs and no benefit. It happens because players decide to shoot before they check whether there is a lane, so the fix is an order of operations rather than a skill.

![](diagram:shooting-lane-and-the-step)

**Getting the puck through, in order of importance:**

1. **Look for the lane before you shoot, not after.** You need a clear channel to the net, not a hopeful gap. If you cannot see net or you cannot see your teammates' sticks at the net front, there is no lane.
2. **Change the angle.** Do not shoot from where you received the puck. Step sideways along the blue line — toward the middle usually, or toward the boards if that is where the gap is. Defenders' sticks and bodies are set for the angle you had a second ago. How far is feel rather than a measurement: go until the blocker has to move their feet, which is usually a step or two, and it costs you almost nothing.
3. **Get it off the ice — a little.** A puck kept flat is easy to block with a stick. A puck lifted a few inches clears sticks. A puck lifted a foot or more is unusable for a tip and easy for the goalie to catch. A few inches is the whole target.
4. **Low and hard for tips.** If you have net-front traffic, the shot you want arrives at knee height or below, hard. Low enough for a teammate to redirect with a blade on the ice, hard enough that a small deflection still has pace on it. High point shots are a tipper's nightmare.
5. **Fake the shot to make them commit.** A shoulder drop and a half wind-up drops the defender into the lane and takes their stick out of it. Then step around and shoot for real. In a CARHA league a faked slap shot can be a minor under Rule 79(b), so sell the fake with your shoulders and eyes rather than the backswing.
6. **Consider not shooting.** If there is no lane, the play is to move the puck — D-to-D across the blue line, or down to the half-wall and back — until there is one. See [Offensive Zone Play](../systems/offensive_zone_play.md).

**A note for defencemen at the point:** the goal of a point shot is almost never to score directly. Point shots produce around **1–3 goals per 100 shots** ([Hockey's Arsenal](https://hockeysarsenal.substack.com/p/shot-quantity-or-shot-quality) — a range read off a shot-location map, not a computed rate; see [Where Shots Are Worth Taking](#where-shots-are-worth-taking)). **Two qualifications travel with that figure and both change what it means for you.** The map counts a shot as a goal, a save or a miss, so **misses are in the denominator and this is not a shooting percentage** — it is lower than one. And it is the **even-strength** map: on the power-play version of the same map the 5% contour reaches roughly the blue line, so **none of this is an argument against a power-play point shot.** The goal is to produce a tip, a rebound, or a scramble in the slot — all of which require the puck to actually arrive there. That is why "get it through" beats "shoot it hard" every time.

## Where Shots Are Worth Taking

This is the part that changes how many goals you score, and it has nothing to do with your hands.

Shot value depends overwhelmingly on location. The exact figures vary by model, but every model agrees on the shape. **These are the numbers to use, and each carries its own qualification with it, because they do not all rest on the same kind of evidence.**

- Shots from the **slot** — the ice between the two faceoff circles, from the goalmouth out to their tops — convert at roughly **10–15%**; shots from the **perimeter** at roughly **2–4%**, three to seven times less ([The Coaches Site](https://members.thecoachessite.com/article/shot-distribution-the-importance-of-getting-shots-from-high-quality-areas-as-illustrated-by-the-4-nations-face-off-championship-game)). **Qualification: the source credits *"data from various hockey analytics sources"* and names no dataset**, so treat these as a coaching-level consensus range rather than a measurement you could re-derive.
- **A second source, a wider region and a different denominator:** point shots produce around **1–3 goals per 100 shots** and shots from the **"house"** around **5–15** ([Hockey's Arsenal](https://hockeysarsenal.substack.com/p/shot-quantity-or-shot-quality)). The house is wider than the slot — across the tops of both faceoff circles, down the dot lines to the two faceoff dots, then angling in to the goalposts, so it is widest where the slot is narrowest; [Rink Map](../foundation/rink_map.md) owns the shape. **Qualification: these are Greg Revak's readings off a shot-location map he credits to HockeyViz (Micah Blake McCurdy), given as ranges rather than computed values** — approximate by construction, but the model behind them is at least named. **Two things travel with the number.** McCurdy counts a shot as *"a goal, a save, or a miss"*, so **misses are in the denominator and this is not shooting percentage**. And **it is the even-strength map**: on McCurdy's power-play map the 5% contour reaches roughly the blue line, so **none of this is an argument against a power-play point shot**. [Offensive Zone Play](../systems/offensive_zone_play.md) sets the map out in full.
- Over a three-year five-on-five sample, **close to half of all NHL goals came from the inner slot** — the area closest to the crease — and just over a quarter from the outer slot, the rest of the slot out to the tops of the circles ([The Point](https://www.thepointhockey.com/shot-optimization-how-optimal-shooting-habits-can-improve-individual-goal-scoring-rates/)). **Qualification: this split is computed from Sportlogiq tracking, which is not public**, the same limit as the Kane and Laine figures below. The article's own words are *"nearly half"* and *"just over a quarter"*, and nobody outside Sportlogiq can check them.

![](diagram:oz-home-plate)

**What that means when you are standing on the ice with the puck:** a shot from the slot is worth several times a shot from the point. Not slightly more — several times. If you can turn a point shot into a slot shot by making one pass, make the pass. If you can turn a perimeter shot into a slot shot by taking two strides toward the middle, take the strides.

The Point's shot-optimisation analysis, the source of the inner-slot split above, gave two named NHL examples of what bad shot selection costs. **Patrick Kane** — *"Kane takes over 20 percent of his shots from 'the dead zone' above the circles and has scored less than 2 percent of his goals from there."* In the season before the article (2018-19) he attempted 79 shots from there, of which *"three of these led to a scoring chance, two produced goals and 38 resulted in a loss of possession."* **Patrik Laine** managed *"a mere 2 goals on 232 shot attempts from above the circles over his career"* ([Mike Kelly, The Point, 26 September 2019](https://www.thepointhockey.com/shot-optimization-how-optimal-shooting-habits-can-improve-individual-goal-scoring-rates/), using Sportlogiq tracking of the previous three NHL seasons at 5-on-5). **The tracking is not public**, so the counts are quoted exactly as the article gives them, and the 2018-19 dating is inferred from the publication date rather than stated. The pattern is what matters: these are among the best shooters alive, and if they cannot score from above the circles, you cannot either.

**The practical rule: get to the middle, or get the puck to someone in the middle.** Everything in [Offensive Zone Play](../systems/offensive_zone_play.md) about cycling, net drives and finding the bumper exists to manufacture slot shots. That is what the whole offensive system is for.

## Screens, Goalie Sightlines and the Royal Road

Location is the first half of shot value. **What the goalie can see, and whether they had to move, is the other half.**

A goaltender who sees the puck the whole way, from a set position, with their angle correct, saves almost everything. Take away either the sight or the set position and the same shot becomes dangerous.

### The royal road

Former NHL goaltender Steve Valiquette popularised a concept called the **royal road** — an imaginary line running down the middle of the ice, which a puck crossing forces the goalie to move laterally across. Shots taken immediately after the puck has crossed it, by a pass or by a carrier skating over it, score at a far higher rate than shots that have not, because the goalie cannot be set and square.

![](diagram:oz-royal-road)

**Published descriptions of where exactly that line runs do not agree, and the weight of evidence is against the popular version.** Most secondary accounts, including [OMHA's](https://www.omha.net/news_article/show/486107-the-most-important-line-on-the-ice-you-ve-never-heard-of), describe it as running the length of the ice from the centre of one net to the centre of the other. The two sources closest to Valiquette himself describe something narrower and **zone-limited**:

- [Blue Seat Blogs](https://blueseatblogs.com/2015/01/09/taking-look-steve-valiquettes-royal-road/): *"The zone is divided in half, vertically from the center bar of the net to the top of the circles, and is bisected by a horizontal line going laterally across the top of the circles. The centerline is Valiquette's 'Royal Road'."*
- [NHL.com's own reporting](https://web.archive.org/web/20150924092445/http://www.nhl.com/ice/news.htm?id=744483) — the article in which Valiquette gives the 76% figure directly — calls it *"a line that halves the offensive zone in two below the tops of the faceoff circles."*

On the reading those two sources give, the royal road stops at the top of the circles rather than running the length of the rink, and a lateral pass out at the blue line would not cross it.

This document still uses the **net-centre-to-net-centre** version, because it is the description you will actually meet and because it gives the more conservative coaching instruction — cross the middle earlier and more often than the narrow version requires, and you cannot be wrong. **But be clear that the numbers below are computed against the narrower definition**, and that the narrower definition is the better-attested one. What has never been published is the precise geometry Clear Sight Analytics tracks against — the framework is proprietary — so the last few feet of the disagreement cannot be settled from public sources.

> **One man's word for his own proprietary data — treat with care.** In Valiquette's framework, "green" shots account for roughly **76% of NHL goals** and "red" shots the other 24%. Blue Seat Blogs lists seven green categories — possession across the royal road, passes across it, deflections, one-timers on the same side of it, screens, broken plays and "green rebounds"; red is everything else. So green is not simply "the puck crossed the middle": the crossing categories are two of seven, and 30% of goals is the share Valiquette gave those two. The 76% is **Valiquette's own figure, stated to NHL.com**: *"Valiquette said 76 percent of goals in the NHL this season have been scored off Green Shots, including 30 percent on plays that involved either a pass or possession moving across the Royal Road"* ([Kevin Woodley, "Unmasked: Analytics provide new evaluation tools", NHL.com, 18 December 2014](https://web.archive.org/web/20150924092445/http://www.nhl.com/ice/news.htm?id=744483) — "this season" is 2014-15 to that date). The same article's description of green goals as those *"scored off high-percentage shots, or shots that the goaltender has less than half a second to see"* is Woodley's gloss, not Valiquette's words. [Blue Seat Blogs](https://blueseatblogs.com/2015/01/09/taking-look-steve-valiquettes-royal-road/), where most people meet the number, **cites no source for it at all**. **The tracking underneath cannot be checked:** Clear Sight Analytics' shot database is proprietary, with no published sample size, shot counts or rules for applying the categories, and the figure has never been independently replicated. The direction is coaching consensus; the number is one analyst's claim about data nobody else can see.

Analysts have pushed back that the royal road formalises something coaches already knew rather than revealing something new — and that is fair. It is still the cleanest single way to think about shot value: **make the goalie move sideways, or make sure they cannot see it.** That instruction does not depend on the number or on the exact geometry.

### How to create those shots

```facts
Action: Pass across the middle before shooting — cross-slot, D-to-D, or low-to-high to the far point — or attack from behind the net and out the other side so the goalie must rotate and re-find the puck
Position: Screen in the sightline, feet outside the blue paint and off the red crease line, stick on the ice for a tip — and never in a goaltender's path back to their crease
Never: Meet a goal post head first, or duck at the net front. Head up and chin off your chest instead — head down into a post is the same neck injury as into the boards, and can happen at walking speed
Rule: A no-contact screen loses the goal for where you stood only from inside the crease — NHL and IIHF 69.1's disallowing sentence reaches an attacker who "enters the goal crease", 69.3's screen ground a significant position within it, and 69.4 takes the goal outside it for contact only
Rule: Three books' reference tables allow the planted screen "on the crease line or outside the goal crease" obstructing the goalie's vision — "Goal is allowed" (NHL and IIHF Table 14, Situation 5 E, IIHF 2025/26 Table 16; PWHL Table 14, unlettered). An appendix table, not rule text — stay off the line
Rule: Stay off the red crease line even where the tables allow a screen on it — the IIHF, USA Hockey, Hockey Canada and CARHA all put that line inside the crease, in rule text or glossary (IIHF 1.7, USA Hockey Note to 625(b), Hockey Canada and CARHA Glossaries)
Rule: The screen the tables allow on the crease line is not a safe harbour — with no contact at all, NHL and IIHF 69.3 wants a substantial part of your body in the crease for more than an instant, obstructing vision and impairing defending; the IIHF's own Situation 69.1 disallows a crease screen flat
Rule: NHL and IIHF Rule 69.3, PWHL 71.3, voids the goal for any contact you initiate with a goalkeeper who is in their crease outside a rebound or loose-puck scramble, whatever your own position was
Rule: NHL and IIHF Rule 69.7, PWHL 71.7, permits incidental contact in a rebound or loose-puck scramble, but not where you push the goalkeeper into the net together with the puck after making a stop — unless a defending player pushed or fouled you into them, when the Referee can permit the goal
Rule: Situation 6B in both books is crowding, not screening — the goalie initiates contact to establish position, you do not vacate, and the referee assesses a penalty, a minor in the NHL's example; that player "runs the risk of 'bad things' happening by being in the crease"
Action: Say who you are shooting at, then shoot through your own net-front teammate's legs from the point, knee height or below — the lane past them is also the sightline they are blocking
Action: Shoot off a rebound immediately — do not settle it and pick a corner
Key: A shot the goalie must move for, or cannot see, is worth materially more than the same shot they are set for — direction only. No source this document has searched prices the sight half at all
```

- **Pass across the middle before shooting.** A cross-slot pass, a D-to-D pass at the point followed by a shot, a low-to-high play from the corner to the far point — anything that drags the goalie across.
- **Attack from behind the net and out the other side.** The goalie must rotate their whole body and re-find the puck.
- **Screen.** Stand in the sightline between the goalie and the shooter, feet outside the blue paint and off the red line around it, stick on the ice for a tip. A screen turns a routine save into a guess. ⚠️ **Take the shove head up and chin off your chest, and never duck.** You are standing still beside a steel frame, watching the shooter rather than the defender moving you, and a tucked chin straightens the cervical spine — USA Hockey's *Heads Up Hockey* guide says a head-down, head-first collision with the boards or a goal post *"can occur at walking speed."* Meet a post with an arm or a leg first, never your head. **The screen is legal from outside the crease and without touching the goalie.** Under the NHL and IIHF, the sentence in Rule 69 that takes a goal for **where you stood** is keyed to the crease; outside it, 69.4 takes the goal for contact, not position. ⚠️ **Position can still cost two minutes out there:** under 69.4 an attacker whose *"deliberate actions"* stop a goalkeeper **who has played the puck outside the crease** from getting back *"may be penalized"*, contact or not. ⚠️ **The catch is the line itself** — the reference tables allow a screen *"on the crease line"*, but the IIHF's Rule 1.7, the Note to USA Hockey's 625(b) and the Hockey Canada and CARHA Glossaries all put that line inside the crease, so stand clear of it. **Under USA Hockey and Hockey Canada nothing reaches a screen set from outside the crease — but a skate in the blue, or on the red line, costs you**: outright under USA Hockey 625(b), and under Hockey Canada 8.5 the moment you do anything in there. ⚠️ **And arriving into the goalie is not capped at two minutes in any book.** Charging a goalkeeper in their crease reaches a major plus a game misconduct in every one; Hockey Canada makes that mandatory, USA Hockey for a reckless charge, and the NHL, the PWHL and the Elite League when a major for it injures the face or head; CARHA makes it mandatory in the crease and for a charge that injures anyone, anywhere on the ice. [In and around the crease](#in-and-around-the-crease--what-each-book-allows-and-what-it-charges) quotes each book. Screen with your position, not your body weight.
- **Shoot through your own teammates' legs from the point — deliberately, low, and said out loud first.** If your winger is at the net front, the shooting lane past them is also the sightline they are blocking. Say who you are shooting at and keep it knee height or below, so the teammate you are shooting through knows the puck is coming and has a blade on the ice for the tip.
- **Shoot off a rebound immediately.** The goalie has just committed to one location and is recovering. Do not settle it and pick a corner; hit it.
- **Vary the shot's origin** — the release-point section above is the individual-player version of the same idea.

**Both books' own reference tables work the planted screen through, and they show the crease keying.** Rule 69 points to them — NHL **Table 14**, IIHF **Appendix IV** (Table 14 in 2026/27, Table 16 in 2025/26) — and the PWHL prints a third copy as its own Table 14, for its Rule 71. In the group headed *"5. SCREENING SITUATIONS"*, row **E** — an attacker who *"plants himself on the crease line or outside the goal crease, as to obstruct the goalkeeper's vision and impair his ability to defend his goal"* (the NHL's wording) — reads **"Goal is allowed."** Row **C**, a screen *"outside the crease"* by a player who *"remains in motion"*, is allowed too. **The two rows that disallow both put the attacker inside the paint:** B *"well inside the crease"*, D *"within the goal crease"*. **What decides it is a significant position held in the crease, not motion** — B is in motion and still disallowed. **And a crease location alone does not disallow either:** row **A**, *"at the top of the goal crease"* and in motion, is allowed because that player *"does not maintain a significant position in the crease in front of the goalkeeper"*. ⚠️ **Do not take "in motion is fine" from row C and carry it into row B's fact pattern.**

**Five limits travel with the allowed planted screen, and none of them is optional.**

- **It is a reference table, not rule text.** Neither book says what status these tables have. But they do not override the rule: 69.1 opens *"Goals should be disallowed **only if**"*; its first limb names no location but sits under that ceiling, and the sentence that actually disallows reaches an attacker who *"enters the goal crease"*; 69.3's no-contact ground needs a significant position *within* the crease, and 69.4 takes the goal outside it for contact, not position. **So the rows are the rule applied, not the rule contradicted.** ⚠️ **That is a reading of the rule's structure, not something either book states**, and both say Rule 69 *"will be enforced exclusively in accordance with the on-ice judgement of the Referee(s)"* — so it is what the text supports, not a promise about the call.
- **"On the crease line" is a skate-width from losing the goal, and the IIHF's rule and its own Handbook disagree about how much more 69.3 asks for.** **Rule 69.3** disallows the goal, with no contact, where an attacker *"establishes a significant position within the goal crease, so as to obstruct the goalkeeper's vision and impair his ability to defend his goal"* (the NHL's wording) — the body, *"or a substantial portion thereof"*, in the crease *"for more than an instantaneous period of time"*. That all three elements are needed is a reading of the rule's *and*, not something either book states, and table row B disallows a goal with no vision element at all. ⚠️ 🇬🇧 **The IIHF's own Situation Handbook does not apply that test, and it may be what the official in a British game works from.** **Situation 69.1**, the same in the 2025/26 (v1.1) and 2026/27 Handbooks, puts a *"Team A player [who] is in the crease screening the goalkeeper"* and answers flat: *"The goal shall be disallowed, and no additional penalty assessed."* No duration, no vision, no defending test. The Handbook's Foreword says the Rulebook wins *"in case of any contradictions"*, but ⚠️ **do not plan on a skate in the paint being read that narrowly on the night.** [Playing Without the Puck](../hockey-iq/playing_without_the_puck.md) carries the same ruling. The tables' **Situation 6B**, in both books, is *crowding* rather than a screen: the goalie initiates contact to establish position, the attacker does not vacate, and the referee assesses a penalty — a minor in the NHL's example; that player *"runs the risk of 'bad things' happening by being in the crease."*
- **The other half of Rule 69.3 is about contact, and it asks for no position and no obstructed vision.** *"If an attacking player initiates contact with a goalkeeper, incidental or otherwise, while the goalkeeper is in his goal crease, and a goal is scored, the goal will be disallowed"* — the NHL's words; the IIHF says *"a relevant contact"*. The one exception is **Rule 69.7** in both books (PWHL 71.3 and 71.7): *"In a rebound situation, or where a goalkeeper and attacking player(s) are simultaneously attempting to play a loose puck, whether inside or outside the crease, incidental contact with the goalkeeper will be permitted"*. ⚠️ **So a brush past a goalie in their crease outside a scramble costs the goal, wherever you stood.** The rest of 69.7 is under [Contact with the goaltender at the net front](#contact-with-the-goaltender-at-the-net-front).
- 🇬🇧 **Name the edition when you cite the IIHF table.** It is **Table 16** in the **2025/26** book, which the In-House Rules adopt for England, Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland, and **Table 14** in 2026/27, which the Elite League's *Casebook* adopts. That Casebook is the only British document with its own words on Rule 69 — an *"EIHL Addition to the IIHF Rule Book"* giving three criteria: can the goalkeeper still play their position and recover, who initiated the contact, and *"Was the contact made inside (or outside) of the crease? Contact in the blue paint area calls for disallowing a goal."* ⚠️ **Weigh those as criteria, not a new rule, and do not read "the blue paint area" as the whole of what costs you** — they say nothing about the red line, and **69.2** tells the referee to weigh *"the degree and nature of the relevant contact with the goalkeeper"* above *"the exact location of the goalkeeper"*. Outside the paint, *"intentional or deliberate"* contact is still a minor or a major *"whether or not the goalkeeper is inside or outside the goal crease"*, 69.4 takes the goal for *"relevant contact"*, and the returning-goalie clause needs no contact at all. The In-House Rules' only Rule 69 amendment is the video-review bar — see [UK and England Rules](../foundation/uk_rules.md).
- 🇬🇧 **Under the IIHF book row E collides with the IIHF's own Rule 1.7, and nothing settles it.** **Rule 1.7**: *"The marked line belongs to the Goal Crease"*, in the 2025/26 and 2026/27 editions alike. So a player planted on the line is *within the goal crease* — row **D**, *"Goal is disallowed."* Neither IIHF Situation Handbook mentions the line, so this is an unresolved disagreement inside one rulebook. **Do not plan a screen on the line under the IIHF book. Set it outside the paint and off the red.** The NHL's Rule 1.7 says only that the crease area *"shall include all the space outlined by the crease lines"*, so the collision is the IIHF's. [Rink Map](../foundation/rink_map.md) compares the books on the crease boundary.

The general rule, and act on its direction rather than on any size: **a shot the goalie has to move for, or cannot see, is worth materially more than a shot they are set and square for — from the same spot on the ice.** Two shots from the identical location can differ in value, and that difference is entirely under your team's control.

**How much more a screen is worth is not measured anywhere this document has looked.** The slot-versus-perimeter figures above are a *location* comparison, a different claim. **Movement is priced in one narrow case:** the public [MoneyPuck expected-goals model](https://moneypuck.com/about.htm) uses, for a rebound, *"difference in shot angle divided by time since last shot"*, so a goalie forced across quickly is measurably worse off. **Sight is not priced at all** — none of its fifteen variables is a screen, because league play-by-play data does not record one. Three numbers look as if they price it, and none does. Valiquette's 76% green share and the *"97-plus percent save"* on a clean look are attributed but uncheckable, and neither compares the same shot with and without a screen. The point-shot figure in [Offensive Zone Play](../systems/offensive_zone_play.md) — roughly **3% with traffic in front, about 1% or less without** — has the right shape, but it is Greg Revak's, printed unattributed in 2020 and later credited by him to a shot-location map and an unnamed dataset together; **neither half can be audited**, and the map has no traffic variable. **So take its direction and put no multiplier on it.** Make the goalie move and take away their sight because every coach and goalie agrees it works — but any multiplier you hear is not from a study.

#### In and around the crease — what each book allows and what it charges

```facts
Rule: USA Hockey 625(b) does not run 69.1 — a flat position bar voiding the goal with an attacker in the crease whoever scored, unless the puck got there first or the goalkeeper is out of the crease area; Casebook Situation 9 voids it anyway, plus an interference minor, if you stopped them getting back
Rule: USA Hockey 625(b) costs you the zone before anyone shoots too — a neutral-zone face-off where an attacker stands, holds their stick or skates through the crease, but only with the puck in the attacking zone, their team in possession and the goalkeeper in contact with the crease
Rule: Hockey Canada 8.5 is not flat — a teammate's goal from outside the crease stands if you stay passive in it, and is disallowed once you play the puck, interfere with the play or obstruct the goalie's view or movements
Rule: Neither book reaches a no-contact screen set from outside the crease — USA Hockey writes no goaltender-vision provision, Hockey Canada's reaches only a player in it — but Hockey Canada voids the goal for contact you initiate "inside or outside of the goal crease" (Interpretation 1 to 8.5(a))
Rule: Charging a goalie in the crease can eject in every book — discretionary in NHL and PWHL 42.1, IIHF 42.4, USA Hockey 607(c); mandatory in Hockey Canada 8.5(b) anywhere, CARHA 52(b) there or any injuring charge, USA Hockey 607(b) if reckless, NHL, PWHL 42.5 and EIHL 42 if a major hurts face or head
Rule: Hockey Canada and CARHA make interference that injures a mandatory ejection — Hockey Canada 8.3(b) and 8.5(b) a major and game misconduct, CARHA 66(e) a major, which its Rule 30(a) makes an ejection
Rule: A hit from behind into a goaltender can be checking from behind, not charging — NHL, IIHF and PWHL 43.2 write no minor: a major and game misconduct (NHL and PWHL 43.3, 43.5; IIHF 43.3 if reckless); Hockey Canada 7.5(c) and CARHA 53(b) make it a mandatory match into the net on a helpless goalie
Rule: USA Hockey 608(a): minor plus misconduct or major plus game misconduct for a goalie hit from behind; the major mandatory if it recklessly endangers or puts them head first into the goal frame (608(b)); a match for recklessly endangering a vulnerable or defenceless one with excessive force (608(c))
Rule: Charging or interfering with a goaltender can reach a mandatory match penalty — Hockey Canada 7.4(c) and 8.5(c); for a deliberate injury or attempt, NHL and PWHL 21.1 and CARHA 48(a); USA Hockey 602(a) for an attempt to injure or reckless endangerment. None in the IIHF
Rule: USA Hockey Casebook Rule 625 Situation 10 runs both ways — a momentary stick or skate in the crease is no stoppage unless you interfere with the goalkeeper, but a goal scored while it is in there with the goalkeeper in the crease must be disallowed
Rule: Being pushed into the crease relieves your position only — USA Hockey 617(c)(4) disallows the goal for an attacker positioned in the crease OR "interfering with the goalkeeper", and 617(c)(4)'s push-in parenthetical is written into the first limb alone
Rule: The push-in relief does not reach the penalty either — USA Hockey 625(a)(8) is still a minor for "physical contact, using their stick or body, in a manner that interferes with the movement of the goalkeeper, unless otherwise specified in the rules" (a reading of what 625(b) does not say)
Never: Set your feet in the blue paint or on the red crease line — every book settling the question puts that line in the crease. Set up outside the line, and find out which book your league runs
Never: Play the puck or touch the goalie after a defender has shoved you into the paint. Get your feet back out of the crease first instead
```

**Keep both skates out of the blue paint and off the red line, get out at once if you are shoved in, and never start contact with a goaltender — that holds under every book.** The rest of this section is where the books differ, and what each one charges.

**The rule behind the screen and the net-front work above is Rule 69, and Rule 69 is the NHL's and the IIHF's — and the PWHL's, at its Rule 71.1.** **USA Hockey and Hockey Canada do not run its premise** — and they change what a net-front player should do, in **both** directions.

**Inside the paint USA Hockey is stricter, and there is nothing to argue.** **USA Hockey Rule 625(b)**: *"No goal may be scored with an attacking player in the goal crease unless the puck has preceded the player(s) into the goal crease or the goalkeeper is out of the goal crease area."* No impairment, no contact, no judgement, and no question of who shot it — a skate in the crease is the whole test, and the Note to the rule puts the crease lines inside it. **That second exception ends the moment you are what stops the goalkeeper getting back.** It also costs you the zone first: a neutral-zone face-off *"any time an attacking player stands, holds their stick, or skates through the goal crease provided the puck is in the attacking zone, the attacking team has possession of the puck and the goalkeeper is in contact with the crease"* — all three provisos are limits. Its Casebook (Rule 625, Situation 10) says play is not stopped for a momentary stick or skate in the crease *"unless they interfere with the goalkeeper in any manner"* — **but its next sentence is the one that costs you a goal:** *"However, if a goal is scored while an attacking player has their stick and/or skates in the opponent's goal crease while the goalkeeper is in the crease, the Referee shall have no alternative but to disallow the goal."* The relief is from the **whistle**, not from the disallowed goal.

**And do not stop reading 625(b) at *"the goalkeeper is out of the goal crease area."*** A net-front shooter hears that as a licence to get in ahead of the puck, and the **USA Hockey Casebook 2025-29** answers exactly that play at **Rule 625, Situation 9**. The goal counts — *"If the goalkeeper is completely out of their crease, an attacking player may precede the puck into the crease and any legally scored goal in this situation shall be allowed"* — **unless you are what stopped them getting home:** *"However, if the goalkeeper is attempting to return to the goal crease and there is any interference by the attacking player who prevents the goalkeeper from making a play, then the goal must be disallowed and an interference penalty shall be assessed."* ⚠️ **Getting there first is fine; being the body between a scrambling goaltender and their net costs the goal and two minutes.**

**Hockey Canada's bar is not flat.** Rule 8.5's Goal Crease Area paragraph starts the same way — *"Unless the puck is in the goal crease area, a player of the attacking team may not stand in the goal crease. If the puck should enter the net while such conditions prevail, the goal will NOT BE ALLOWED"* — then carves out the tip-and-screen case: when a teammate outside the crease scores, the goal *"WILL BE ALLOWED provided that the player who was in the goal crease does not attempt to play the puck, interfere with the play or obstruct the Goaltender's view or movements."* **So on a body in the crease with a teammate scoring from outside it, the two books give opposite answers.** Interpretation 3 to Rule 8.5(a) makes it a test of what you *did*: an attacker is *"NOT committing a foul by simply standing in the goal crease"*, but *"if while standing in the crease, the attacking player attempts to play the puck, interfere with the play, or impede the goaltender's vision or movements, then no goal may be scored."* That is an impairment test naming vision — the counterpart of NHL 69.3 — and separate from a penalty: a goal can be disallowed *"without assessing an attacking player a penalty."* The onus is yours: *"The onus is always on the attacking player and players who do not make an effort to avoid the goaltender must be penalized."*

**Outside the paint USA Hockey and Hockey Canada are more permissive than the NHL about a screen with no contact — not about contact.** Neither writes 69.3's no-contact screen ground, though that ground is keyed to the crease too, so out there the gap is narrower than it looks. USA Hockey's playing rules have **no provision about screening a goaltender or obstructing their vision at all**; its grounds are position in the crease and contact (625(a)(8)). Hockey Canada names a goaltender's view only in the two clauses above, and **both reach only a player in the crease** — what it lacks is 69.1's reach to positioning *outside* the paint. For a **penalty** it asks more again: *"No penalty ... assessed unless the attacking player's body or stick makes actual physical contact with the goaltender."*

**And if a defender puts you in the paint, the books disagree about what happens to you.** USA Hockey's relief is the broadest — *"play shall not be stopped and any legal goal scored shall be allowed"* — but it covers your **position** only: 617(c)(4) disallows the goal for an attacker *"positioned in the crease"* **or** *"interfering with the goalkeeper"*, and the relief is written into the first limb alone. ⚠️ **So what you do after you land there is still yours**, and **625(a)(8)** remains a minor for *"physical contact, using their stick or body, in a manner that interferes with the movement of the goalkeeper"* — a reading of what the relief does not say, not a sentence either rule prints. Get out of the paint, and do not play the puck or touch the goalie on your way. **CARHA Rule 66(b)**, CARHA-affiliated adult leagues only, bars a goal with an attacker standing in the crease unless the puck is there first, with no carve-out for a teammate scoring from outside; it allows the goal where a defender's interference put you there, and still gives a minor to an attacker who *“fails to attempt to avoid contact with the goalkeeper”*, in possession of the puck or not. **Hockey Canada penalises you personally:** under Interpretation 2 to Rule 8.5(a), a player knocked into the goaltender who *"does not make an effort to avoid contact with the goaltender"* *"must be penalized for interference with the goaltender."* **And its Interpretation 1 is stricter than anything in Rule 69:** any goal on a play where you initiate contact with the goaltender is disallowed *"regardless of whether the contact occurs inside or outside of the goal crease"*, the only exception a foul by a defender that makes the contact unavoidable. **Hockey Canada has no counterpart to 69.4's reasonable-effort proviso**: contact you initiate costs your team the goal wherever on the ice it happens.

**Under Hockey Canada, skating into a goaltender at the net front is not capped at two minutes** — ⚠️ **a foul that would have been two minutes becomes an ejection the moment it hurts someone.** 8.5(a) is the minor. **8.5(b)** reaches *"A Major penalty and Game Misconduct penalty"* at the referee's discretion *"based on the degree of violence of impact"*, and **mandatorily** — *"will be assessed"* — to any player who **charges the goaltender** (a jump, two or more strides, excessive distance, a violent and unnecessary check, or a blind-side hit) or who **injures an opponent** by an Interference with the Goaltender foul that would otherwise be a minor. **8.5(c)** is a **match penalty** *"to any player who attempts to injure or deliberately injures a goaltender by Interference."* **8.5(e)** makes the game misconduct automatic on any 8.5(b) major, and **8.5(d)** — *"No Misconduct penalty may be assessed for interference with the goaltender"* — removes the ten-minute step, so there is nothing between the two minutes and the ejection.

**And Hockey Canada is not alone: every book reaches a major plus a game misconduct for charging a goaltender.** **NHL Rule 42.1**: *"A minor, major or a major and a game misconduct shall be imposed on a player who charges a goalkeeper while the goalkeeper is within his goal crease"* — and **PWHL Rule 42.1** prints the same sentence in its own pronouns. **IIHF Rule 42.4** lets the Referee assess *"a major penalty and a game misconduct penalty"* where a Player *"recklessly endangers their opponent by 'charging'"* — **no injury required** — and its 42.1 penalises charging a goalkeeper *"while the goalkeeper is within their goal crease"*. **USA Hockey Rule 607(c)**: *"A minor plus a misconduct or a major plus a game misconduct penalty shall be assessed to a player who body checks or charges a goalkeeper while the goalkeeper is within the goal crease or privileged area"* — and **no limb of Rule 607 is a bare minor**: (a) reads the same way, (b) is a mandatory major plus game misconduct for reckless endangerment, and (e) reaches a match penalty. ⚠️ **And the NHL's ladder does not stop at the ejection:** **Rule 42.4** lets the Referee, *"at his discretion"*, assess a **match penalty** where the player *"attempted to or deliberately injured his opponent by charging"*, and **Rule 21.1**, which the PWHL repeats, makes a match mandatory for a deliberate injury or attempt *"in any manner"*; **Hockey Canada 7.4(c)** makes one **mandatory** for charging an opponent *"in such a way that they are unable to defend themselves"* as well as for a deliberate attempt to injure.

**Some books take the referee's choice away.** Hockey Canada 8.5(b) says *"will be assessed"*; USA Hockey 607(b) does the same for a reckless charge; and **NHL and PWHL Rule 42.5** say *"a game misconduct shall be imposed"* once a major for charging injures the face or head, as the EIHL Casebook's Rule 42 does in the Elite League. **CARHA Rule 52(b)**: *"A Major penalty and a Game Misconduct shall be assessed to any player who charges a goalkeeper while the goalkeeper is within the goal crease **or who injures an opponent as a result of a charge**."* ⚠️ **The second limb has no crease condition, so in a CARHA adult-rec league a charge that injures is an automatic ejection anywhere on the ice** — and **52(a)** says it again for a charge on anyone. **CARHA also writes a mandatory injury tier into its goalkeeper-interference rule:** **Rule 66(e)**, *"A Major penalty shall be imposed on any player who injures an opponent by interference"*, and under **Rule 30(a)** any major puts you *"off the ice for the remainder of the game (Major penalty plus Game Misconduct)"*, the only exception an accidental high stick that injures nobody. CARHA 66(b) also lets the referee lift deliberate contact with a goalkeeper to a major. **The other four books' goalkeeper-interference rules carry no injury tier:** NHL and IIHF 69.2 and PWHL 71.2 assess *"minor or major, as the Referee deems appropriate"*, and USA Hockey 625(a) writes only a minor.

**Under CARHA,** ⚠️ **keep your stick off a goaltender in the crease and away from anyone's head, because neither cross-check is the referee's call.** **Rule 54(c)**: *"A Major penalty and Game Misconduct shall be assessed to any player who crosschecks a goalkeeper while they are in the goal crease, or who injures an opponent as a result of a cross-check"*; **54(b)** does the same for a cross-check above the shoulders, *"whether or not injury results."* **USA Hockey's contact rules have a reckless tier, and none excepts a goaltender:** a charge, a check from behind, a cross-check, head contact or roughing that recklessly endangers an opponent is a mandatory major plus a game misconduct (607(b), 608(b), 609(b), 620(b), 640(g)) — and plan on a match penalty, because Rule 602(a) says one *"shall be assessed"* to any player who *"recklessly endangers or attempts to injure"* an opponent, and 608(c) makes one mandatory for a reckless check from behind with excessive force on a vulnerable or defenceless player. Two clauses reach beyond recklessness: 608(b) covers a check that sends a player *"head first into the boards or goal frame"*, and 620(b) anyone who *"intentionally or carelessly contacts an opponent in the head, face or neck"* — so a careless hit to a goaltender's mask is the same major and game misconduct. **Not read here: every other route to an ejection** — NHL 69.8's *"(minor or major and/or game misconduct)"*, and supplementary discipline in every book — so this covers the charging and goalkeeper-interference rules, not the whole of any book.

**Pull up before you reach a goaltender who is turned away from you** — ⚠️ **arriving into a back is a different rule, and in the NHL, the IIHF and the PWHL it has no two-minute option.** **Rule 43.2** removes it in terms in the NHL — *"There is no provision for a minor penalty for checking from behind"* — and in the IIHF: *"there is no option to award a minor penalty for “Checking from Behind”"*. The floor is a **major** with a game misconduct: **NHL 43.3** *"shall be assessed"* on an opponent *"who is unable to protect or defend himself"*, and 43.5 makes the game misconduct mandatory on any such major; **IIHF 43.3** is at the referee's discretion for a check that *"recklessly endangers the fouled Player"*, with *"an automatic game misconduct penalty"*. The PWHL copies the NHL's rule. **IIHF 43.1 names the goal frame** (the goal-frame limb is the IIHF's alone), but what puts you under it is contact *"on the back part of the body"* — a goaltender facing you is not one you are behind. The referee still judges whether it was a check from behind; what goes, if it was, is the two minutes.

**USA Hockey keeps a minor for it,** ⚠️ **and it is not the cheap answer it sounds:** **608(a)** is a minor *plus a misconduct*, or a major plus a game misconduct; **608(b)** makes the major and game misconduct mandatory where you recklessly endanger an opponent **or** put them *"head first into the boards or goal frame"*; and **608(c)** is a mandatory **match penalty** for recklessly endangering an opponent from behind with excessive force while they are vulnerable or defenceless — with no boards-or-net condition. ⚠️ **NHL 43.4 and PWHL 43.4 also let the referee give a match** where the player *"attempted to or deliberately injured"* an opponent from behind, and both books' **Rule 21.1** makes one mandatory for a deliberate injury or attempt. ⚠️ **Under Hockey Canada and CARHA a hit from behind ejects you even at its cheapest:** the minor carries a game misconduct (Hockey Canada 7.5(a), CARHA 53(a)), an injury makes it a major and game misconduct, and driving a player who cannot protect themselves into the boards or the net is a **mandatory match penalty** — Hockey Canada 7.5(c) *"into the boards or goal frame"*, CARHA 53(b) *"into the boards or goal net"*.

**If the hit reaches the head, it is head contact as well,** ⚠️ **and that can end your game in every book — automatically in some.** **USA Hockey 620(b)**: a major plus a game misconduct for any player who *"recklessly endangers an opponent as a result of head contact or who intentionally or carelessly contacts an opponent in the head, face or neck"* — careless is enough. **PWHL 48.2** makes an illegal check to the head a major, and 48.5 says a game misconduct *"must also be imposed"*, unless the officials reduce it to an accidental minor under 48.3. **Hockey Canada** — 7.6 in minor and female hockey, where any foul that reaches the head is Head Contact, and 7.7 in junior and senior — makes it a **match penalty** to hit a player in the head *"in such a way that the player is unable to protect or defend themselves"*, or to try to injure them; 7.6(c) adds a deliberate injury. **7.6(b) and 7.7(b) make any cross-check above the shoulders a major and game misconduct** *"whether or not injury results"* (in minor and female hockey, a deliberate cross-checking action, under 7.6's Interpretation 2); 7.6(b) does the same whenever head contact injures, and 7.7(b) for a high degree of violence, or an illegal hit that drives the head into the glass, ice or boards and injures it. Elsewhere the referee decides: **IIHF 48.3**, a major and automatic game misconduct for a reckless check to the head or neck; **CARHA 50(b)**, a double minor or a major and game misconduct for intentional contact, and **50(c)** a mandatory match for a deliberate attempt to injure or a deliberate injury. The **NHL** writes no major for head contact, and prices it at **48.5**'s discretionary match and **21.1**'s mandatory one.

**The IIHF has no match penalty,** ⚠️ **but its ceiling is still your whole night.** **IIHF Rule 20.4** prices a major plus a game misconduct as being *“ruled off the ice for the balance of the game”*, a substitute after five minutes — the same on-ice outcome **NHL Rule 21.1** writes into a match penalty. There is no rung below it for a hit from behind: 43.2 leaves no minor, and **Appendix IV Table 6** lists checking from behind among the majors *“that result in an automatic game misconduct”*. **The IIHF's 43.4 is not a match penalty** — that is the NHL's and the PWHL's 43.4; above the major the IIHF allows **Rule 28** supplementary discipline, *“at their discretion”*, reported under Rule 20.7 whether acted on or not. *(That Rule 43 reaches a goaltender is a reading of how the book is assembled rather than a sentence the IIHF prints: **Rule 5.1** makes any reference to* "Player" *cover goalkeepers, and 43.1 names the goal frame — but its Appendix II glossary defines a vulnerable position for* "a skater", *and its Situation Handbook answers* "No situation." *under Rule 43.)*

**The instruction is the same under every book and the reason is not: get your feet off the red crease line, not merely out of the blue.** Find out which book your league runs.

## Shooting Off the Rush

```facts
Read: Release as the defender's stick is closing, not after — waiting for a "good spot" shoots into a blocked lane
Action: Release off the foot you do not normally release off; check which foot that is on the wing you play
Key: On your off wing the "outside foot" is already your conventional release, so that cue tells you nothing
Never: Stop your feet — the rush shot is the purest case for shooting in stride
Targets: Far side low on a wide rush; five-hole off a cut to the middle as the goalie pushes across
Options: Delay a beat and feed a trailer into the high slot — it turns a perimeter shot into a slot shot
```

Rush chances — attacking with speed before the defence is set — are among the best shooting opportunities in the game, and most of them are wasted by shooting a beat too late.

![](diagram:shooting-rush-wide-and-the-trailer)

**The timing.** As you carry the puck wide and drive toward the net, the defender is skating backwards, gap closing, stick out toward the puck. There is a window that opens as you approach and closes when their stick arrives. **Release as their stick is closing, not after.** If you wait until you are "in a good spot," you are shooting into a blocked lane from a bad angle.

**Shoot off the "other" foot — and work out which one that is for you, because it depends on your wing.** The textbook shot releases as your weight arrives on the **front foot**, which for a right-handed shooter is the left foot. What "front foot" means relative to the ice depends entirely on which side you are coming down:

- **On your strong-side wing** (a left-handed shot on left wing, a right-handed shot on right wing), your front foot is the one nearer the **middle** of the ice. The unconventional release here is off the **outside** foot, nearer the boards.
- **On your off wing** (a left-handed shot on right wing, a right-handed shot on left wing), the reverse is true: your front foot is already the one nearer the **boards**. Being told to "shoot off the outside foot" therefore tells you to do the completely conventional thing while believing you are doing something clever. The unconventional release for you is off the **inside** foot.

The underlying instruction is the one that transfers: **release off the foot you do not normally release off.** It gets the puck away earlier in the stride, keeps it further from the defender's stick, and arrives at a moment the goalie's read says a shot is not coming. It costs some velocity and it is worth it — but check which foot that actually is on the wing you play, because getting the label wrong means practising your existing habit.

**Do not stop your feet.** The rush shot is the purest case for shooting in stride. Everything you gain from planting, you lose in warning given.

**Pick your target based on the goalie's angle.** On a wide rush, the goalie is hugging the near post to take away the short side and is exposed far side and low. Off a cut to the middle, five-hole is open as they push across. On a genuine breakaway, deception matters more than power — a backhand-to-forehand, a shoulder fake, a change of speed. Full treatment in [Breakaways and Shootouts](#breakaways-and-shootouts--the-shooters-side) below.

**Know what the second wave is doing.** If you have a trailer coming late into the high slot, delaying a beat and passing back to them is often a better chance than your own shot from the wing — because it converts a perimeter shot into a slot shot. See [Zone Entries](../systems/zone_entries.md) for how rush attacks are structured, and [Defending the Rush](../systems/defending_the_rush.md) for the other side of it.

## Breakaways and Shootouts — the Shooter's Side

A **breakaway** is you, the puck, the goalie and nobody else — no defender close enough to interfere. A **penalty shot** is a breakaway awarded by the referee, taken from centre ice. A **shootout** is a series of penalty shots used to settle a tied regular-season game. All three are the same skill, and it is worth practising on purpose rather than improvising. **Do not assume your league even has a shootout, though** — [Rules Primer](../foundation/rules_primer.md) sets out how rec and beer leagues settle a tie, and says there is no general practice — read your league's rules. Ask before the game.

The goaltender's half of this — what they are trying to do to you — is in [Goaltender](../positions/goaltender.md). Read it. **Knowing what the goalie has been coached to do is most of the advantage available to you**, and everything below is written against it.

### The one thing that decides it: who moves first

```facts
Goal: Make the goalie commit first — every technique here is a way of buying that
Read: A well-coached goalie meets you out, retreats on C-cuts, stays on their feet and watches the puck
Mindset: The goalie's entire plan is to make you commit first; yours is the mirror image
Never: Simply wait the goalie out — if neither of you commits you run out of ice and lose
```

A well-coached goalie meets you out, retreats with you on C-cuts to keep the angle closed, stays on their feet as long as possible, and watches the puck rather than your body. **Their entire plan is to make you commit first.** Yours is the mirror image: **make them commit first.** Every technique below is a way of buying that.

If neither of you commits, you run out of ice and lose — so you cannot simply wait. You need a way to force the decision.

### Approach speed — slower than you think

```facts
Action: Arrive at the top of the circles with less speed than you feel like carrying
Risk: Full flight cuts you to one option and hands the goalie their easiest read
Technique: Come in hard to the blue line, ease off through the top of the circles, accelerate into your move
Convention: That a change of pace beats raw pace, and is worth more than any hand skill, is coaching craft rather than a measured finding — no source held here measures either ranking against a goaltender
Read: A deke needs room; you cannot pull the puck across your body once you are past the post
```

**Arrive at the top of the circles with less speed than you feel like carrying.** Speed is the enemy of a breakaway for three reasons:

- **It shortens your decision window.** At full flight you get roughly one option; at three-quarter pace you get to see the goalie's depth, change your mind and still execute.
- **It hands the goalie their easiest read.** A shooter flying in on a straight line has told them exactly where they will be in half a second, which is precisely what a retreating goalie needs.
- **A deke needs room to happen in.** You cannot pull the puck across your body and get it back if you are already past the post.

**A change of pace beats raw pace.** Come in hard to the blue line, then ease off through the top of the circles and accelerate again as you make your move. The deceleration pulls the goalie out of their retreat rhythm; the re-acceleration arrives before they have re-set. That single pattern is worth more than any hand skill. **That last ranking is coaching craft rather than a measured finding, and it reaches beyond the sources behind it.** The two coaching sources that teach the change of pace — the IIHF's coach-development manual and Cutting Edge Ice Academy — describe beating a *defender* one-on-one, and neither measures it; no source held for this document measures a change of pace against a goaltender, or against hand skill. See [Time and Space](../hockey-iq/time_and_space.md).

![](diagram:shooting-breakaway-approach)

### Deception — take their eyes off the puck

```facts
Action: Look where you are not going — head and eyes to one corner, puck to the other
Action: Sell a shot to open the five-hole; sell a deke to get the shot away into the space they open
Technique: Move the puck's position, not just your body — a shoulder dip alone does not force a re-read
Read: Head and shoulder fakes are the cheapest thing to sell and the first thing a good goalie discounts
Never: Decide your move at the blue line, before you can see what the goalie is actually giving you
```

What the goalie has been told to watch is a genuine coaching split (see [Goaltender](../positions/goaltender.md#breakaways)): one school says watch the puck and treat everything above it as theatre; another says watch the shooter's chest and hips rather than the stick or the eyes. Either way, **head and shoulder fakes are the cheapest thing you can sell and the first thing a good goalie discounts.** Make the deception something they cannot dismiss — the puck's position, or your hips:

- **Look where you are not going.** Head and eyes to one corner, puck to the other. Cheap, and it still works at every level below professional.
- **Sell a shot to open the five-hole.** Drop your bottom hand, load the shaft, and watch what the pads do. A goalie who respects the shot gets bigger and stops moving — which is the moment a deke is on.
- **Sell a deke to get the shot.** Pull the puck wide to your forehand with your head up and they will start the lateral push. Shoot into the space they have just opened.
- **Change the puck's position, not just your body's.** Moving the puck from in front of you to your hip, or from forehand to backhand and back, forces a genuine re-read. A shoulder dip does not.
- **Do not decide before you can see them.** Deciding at the blue line is the commonest breakaway error, because you are then executing a plan against a goalie position you never actually looked at.

### The classic moves, and what each one is for

```facts
Read: Goalie aggressive and out of their crease → forehand–backhand, you have room to go around them
Read: Goalie deep in the net and squared up → backhand–forehand, the shorter move finishes before they slide
Read: Goalie retreating and not yet square → shoot early, off the wrong foot
Read: Goalie on their feet, big and pushing across → five-hole, because the hole opens mid-push
Read: Goalie stabbing with the stick or over-committing forward → toe drag inside the poke check, then shoot
Read: Goalie patient, deep and giving you nothing → delay, almost stop, and force them to move
Key: One personal go-to executed without thinking beats five you half-know
```

None of these is a trick. Each solves a specific goalie behaviour, and picking the one that matches what you are seeing is the whole skill.

| Move | What it is | Use it when the goalie is |
|---|---|---|
| **Forehand–backhand (the "deke")** | Pull the puck across your body to the backhand and lift it | Aggressive and out of their crease — you have room to go around them |
| **Backhand–forehand** | The reverse, finishing on your stronger side | Deep in their net and squared up — the shorter move gets it done before they slide |
| **Shoot off the wrong foot / early release** | Fire it a stride before they expect it | Retreating and still setting — they are not square yet |
| **Five-hole** | Sell high, shoot low through the legs | On their feet, big, and pushing across (the hole opens mid-push) |
| **Toe drag** | Pull the puck back inside their poke check, then shoot | Stabbing with the stick or over-committing forward |
| **Delay / slow it right down** | Almost stop, force them to move | Patient, deep and giving you nothing |

**One personal go-to, executed without thinking, beats five you half-know.** Pick one move as your default and drill it until it needs no decision, then add a second that goes the other way.

### Where to shoot

```facts
Targets: Blocker side high — the hardest place for a retreating goalie to get a hand to
Targets: Five-hole while they are pushing sideways, open during the push and not before or after
Targets: Short side high only if they have over-committed to the middle; far side low if they are still sliding
Never: Shoot at the middle of their chest — that is where the puck goes when you shoot without looking
Action: Look at the goalie, not the puck, in the last two strides; you cannot aim at a hole you have not seen
```

The targets are not the same as on a normal shot, because the goalie is moving and often deep.

- **Blocker side, high** is the classic breakaway target: the hardest place for a goalie to get a hand to while retreating.
- **Five-hole while they are pushing sideways** — the hole is open during the push, not before it and not after.
- **Short side high** when they have over-committed to taking away the middle. Small window; it is there or it is not.
- **Far side low** if you have cut across the middle and they are still sliding.
- **Not the middle of their chest.** That is where the puck goes when you shoot without looking.

**Look at the goalie, not at the puck, in the last two strides.** You cannot aim at a hole you have not seen.

### Shoot or deke?

```facts
Read: Goalie out at the top of the crease and coming at you → deke; they cannot recover laterally from there
Read: Goalie deep, square and patient → shoot; a deke just walks you into the post
Read: Goalie retreating and not yet set → shoot early, before you reach your "spot"
Read: Being caught from behind → shoot now, because a backchecker's stick arrives sooner than you think
Convention: At rec level, shoot more than you deke — rec goalies are usually deep, flat-footed and square, which is a coaching observation rather than a counted one
```

The honest answer is that it depends on one read: **how deep is the goalie, and are they moving?**

- **Goalie out at the top of the crease or beyond, coming at you → deke.** They have given you the room and they cannot recover laterally from out there.
- **Goalie deep in the net, square and patient → shoot.** There is no room to go around, and a deke against a deep goalie just walks you into the post.
- **Goalie retreating and not yet set → shoot early.** The best moment on any breakaway is often the one before you get to your "spot".
- **You are being caught from behind → shoot now.** A backchecker's stick arrives sooner than you think. See [Puck Handling](puck_handling.md) on why hands are the smallest contributor to beating a defender: if you have a step, use it rather than spending it on a move.

**At rec level, shoot more than you deke.** Rec goalies are far more likely to be deep, flat-footed and square than out and aggressive, and a deke against a deep goalie has almost nowhere to finish. **That comparison between levels is a coaching observation rather than a counted one** — no source held for this document counts how goalies at any level play their depth.

### The rules you must know before you take one

```facts
Rule: On a penalty shot or shootout attempt the puck must be kept moving towards the goal line — no stopping, circling back or curling away to reset (NHL Rule 24)
Rule: On a penalty shot or shootout attempt no goal counts on a rebound of any kind, except post or crossbar, then off the goalkeeper, then in (NHL Rule 24)
Rule: A completed 360° on the approach — the spin-o-rama — stops the shot and kills the goal under the NHL, IIHF and PWHL (NHL 24.2), but Hockey Canada allows it if the puck keeps its overall motion to the goal line (4.11(b), Interp 5, Note 1)
Rule: Do not read USA Hockey’s and CARHA’s silence on the spin-o-rama — a completed 360° on the approach — as a permission: neither writes a provision, but both still require the puck be kept in motion towards the opponent’s goal line (USA Hockey 406(c), CARHA 35(a))
Rule: The lacrosse-style move is permitted on a penalty shot or shootout under the NHL, IIHF, PWHL and Hockey Canada, the puck kept below your shoulders and released below the crossbar (NHL 24.2; Hockey Canada 4.11(b), Interp 6)
Rule: On a lacrosse-style move keep the puck below your shoulders in every book — USA Hockey 621(d) and CARHA 62(d) stop play for a puck above them, as Hockey Canada stops the shot — and release it below the crossbar
Rule: If the goalie leaves the crease before you touch the puck on a penalty shot or shootout attempt and you do not score, you shoot again (NHL Rule 24)
Rule: Under NHL Rule 84.4: three shooters then sudden death, nobody shoots twice until everyone eligible has — check your own book, because British rule sets allow repeat shooters
Rule: Shootout length depends on which British competition you are in — the In-House Rules give NIHL and SNL three shooters and let the same player go again, NIHL 1 and 2 only after the first three, while the Elite League runs the IIHF's five (In-House Rules 2026-27 Appendix 1; EIHL Casebook 84.4)
Rule: Neither the penalty-shot rule nor the shootout rule sets a time limit on the approach, so any "you have N seconds" is folklore (NHL Rules 24 and 84.4)
Convention: Rec and youth shootouts often run house rules — ask first, a shorter approach pushes the read toward shooting
```

Verified against the *NHL Official Rules 2025-2026*, **Rule 24 (Penalty Shot)** and **Rule 84.4 (Shootout)**, which applies Rule 24 to shootout attempts:

- **The puck must be kept in motion towards the opponent's goal line.** You cannot stop, circle back or curl away to reset. Once you shoot, **the play is complete.**
- **No goal can be scored on a rebound of any kind** — with one exception: puck off the post or crossbar, then off the goalkeeper, then directly in. So there is no second chance; the first touch has to be the one.
- **The shot is over the moment the puck crosses the goal line or comes to a complete stop.**
- **The spin-o-rama — a completed 360° turn on the approach — is where the books split, so find out which one you are under.** Under the NHL, the IIHF and the PWHL it *"shall not be permitted"*: the referee stops the shot and it is no goal. **Hockey Canada permits it** — Note 1 to Interpretation 5 of Rule 4.11(b) calls it legal *"if the puck continues its overall motion towards the opponent’s goal line"*. **USA Hockey and CARHA write no spin-o-rama provision, and that silence is not a permission:** both still require the puck to keep moving towards the opponent’s goal line, and USA Hockey’s *Casebook* (Rule 406, Situations 13 and 14) says that once the shooter touches the puck *"they must skate directly toward the opposing goal"*.
- **The lacrosse-style move is permitted** — picking the puck up on the blade and whipping it — *provided the puck is never raised above shoulder height and is not carried higher than the crossbar when released.* The NHL, the IIHF and the PWHL write both limbs into the penalty-shot rule, and Hockey Canada writes both at Interpretation 6 to Rule 4.11(b): above the shoulders *"the shot will be stopped immediately by the Referee"*, and above the crossbar *"no goal will be allowed."* **USA Hockey and CARHA never name the move, but both reach the act:** USA Hockey 621(d) stops play when *"the puck is played above the height of the shoulders with the stick"* and allows no goal from it, its *Casebook* treating the whole stick as high once any part of it is (Rule 621, Situation 7); CARHA 62(d) stops play too, with *"No penalty"*. **So the shoulder-height cap binds you in every book**; what those two lack is a crossbar condition and a penalty-shot procedure of their own.
- **The goalie must stay in their crease until you touch the puck.** If they leave early and you do not score, you shoot again.
- **In a shootout** under the NHL each team gets three shooters, then sudden death; **no player may shoot twice until everyone eligible has shot**, the opposing goalie cannot be replaced unless injured, and a substitute gets no warm-up shots. **⚠️ Under the IIHF — and so in Britain — the goalie rule is the opposite:** Rule 84.4(VI), *"The goalkeepers from each team may be changed after each shot."* 🇬🇧 **Do not carry the IIHF's five shooters into Britain:** Rule 84.4(II) is scoped to IIHF Championship play, and the In-House Rules set **three** for NIHL National, NIHL 1 and 2, and the SNL. ⚠️ **On repeat shooters, name the season, because this changed for 2026-27.** Under the 2026-27 In-House Rules every competition covered lets the same player go again — NIHL National freely, the SNL after each full round of three, and NIHL 1 and 2 only *"AFTER the initial 3 shots have been taken"*. The superseded 2025-26 edition had NIHL 1 and 2 the other way round, so last season's rule sheet will tell you the opposite. **"Everyone eligible shoots before anyone shoots twice" is the NHL's rule, not Britain's.** 🇬🇧 **And the coin toss is the IIHF's:** under 84.4(IV) its winner *"will have the choice whether their team will shoot first or second"*, where NHL 84.4 gives that choice to the home team with no toss. See [UK and England Rules](../foundation/uk_rules.md), and [Game Management](../systems/game_management.md) for the formats.
- **There is no clock.** Neither Rule 24 nor Rule 84.4 sets a time limit on the approach, so any "you have N seconds" you hear is folklore. Take the time — but keep the puck moving while you do, because a complete stop ends the shot.

> **Rec and youth leagues vary.** Many run shootouts under house rules — different numbers of shooters, sometimes a start from the blue line or the red line rather than centre. Ask before the game, because a shorter approach changes your move: less run-up means less time to change pace, which pushes the read toward shooting.

### How to practise it

```facts
Action: Take breakaways at the end of every practice, against a real goalie, keeping score
Action: Drill one go-to move until it is automatic, then a second that goes the other way
Action: Start from your own blue line so you rehearse the change of pace, not only the last ten feet
Action: Alternate shoot, deke, shoot, deke, so you are not building a habit the goalie can read
```

- **Take breakaways at the end of every practice, against a real goalie, keeping score.** Shooting on an empty net teaches you nothing about this — and see [How to Practise](#how-to-practise) on what not to shoot at a live goaltender, because in stand-up hockey only two of the books here stop play for the hit itself and the rest wait on a referee judging the goalie hurt.
- **Practise your one go-to move until it is automatic, then a second in the opposite direction.**
- **Practise the approach, not just the finish.** Start from your own blue line so you rehearse the change of pace, not only the last ten feet.
- **Alternate: shoot, deke, shoot, deke,** so you are not building a habit the goalie can read.

## Shot Choices in Common Situations

Most shooting decisions come round again and again. These are the common approaches; where coaches disagree the alternative is named, so ask your bench which one your team wants.

- **From a sharp angle below the dots.** The goalie is on their post and the short side is closed. The common approach is a hard, low shot at the far pad, because the rebound kicks out into the slot for a teammate driving the net; the alternative is to carry it behind the net or pass it into the slot instead. If nobody is driving the net, the pass or the carry is usually the better play.
- **A two-on-one, with the puck.** Drive at the net as if you will shoot, so the defender has to choose. If they take the pass away, shoot — low at the far pad is the common choice, because the rebound goes to your partner. If they come to you, pass across early and flat, before the goalie can set. Some coaches teach "always get a shot away on a two-on-one"; others want the pass whenever the defender commits to you.
- **The puck comes back to you at the point off a won offensive-zone faceoff.** Get it through low and quickly, while your forwards are already at the net front and the defence has not yet sorted out who has whom. A full wind-up gives them that time.
- **A rebound lands on your stick in the slot.** Shoot it now, without settling it — the goalie is still recovering. If they are down, lift it over the pad. A rebound you stop and look at is a save you have handed back.
- **An empty net late in a game.** Make sure it hits the net: take the extra strides to get closer rather than shooting from distance, and do not try it with a defender in the lane — a miss hands the puck back with the game still alive.
- **Protecting a lead late in a period.** From the point, a shot into a defender's shins with your partner pinched is the classic way to give up a breakaway. Put it on net low or put it deep, and if there is no lane, move it.

## When NOT to Shoot

```facts
Mindset: Shoot-first means taking the good chance immediately; forcing means shooting because you have the puck
Never: Shoot when there is no lane — a shot into shins is a turnover with extra steps
Priority: A teammate in the slot when you are not gets the pass, every time
Read: Goalie set and square, you outside the dots with no traffic → almost no value unless you have bodies to shoot for
Risk: Shooting as the last player back — a wide shot with nobody covering is how odd-man rushes are born
Read: If your line is cycling and the other team is pinned and tired, another five seconds of possession beats a bad shot
Key: Before you shoot, ask what happens if it does not go in — rebound, tip or recovery means shoot
```

A "shoot-first mentality" is good. Forcing low-percentage shots is not. **Shoot-first** means your default look is the net, you do not need talking into it, and you take the good chance immediately rather than hunting a better one — a genuinely valuable trait most amateurs lack. **Forcing** means you shoot because you have the puck, not because there is a chance; that is a turnover with extra steps.

**Do not shoot when:**

- **There is no lane.** A shot into shins is a turnover. Move the puck and get a lane.
- **A teammate is in the slot and you are not.** This is the big one. Your perimeter shot converts at 2–4%; their slot shot converts at 10–15%. On those ranges the pass is worth roughly **three to seven times** your shot — the same span [Where Shots Are Worth Taking](#where-shots-are-worth-taking) gives, and the same coaching-consensus figures, whose source names no dataset. Make it.
- **The goalie is completely set and square and you are outside the dots with no traffic.** This is close to the highest-percentage save in hockey. (A figure of "97-plus percent" circulates for it, from Clear Sight Analytics via secondary reporting — **unverified**, proprietary, and not checkable against a primary source. The point does not need the number: perimeter shots overall convert at 2–4%, which is sourced, and a *clean, set, unscreened* look from out there is the worst subset of that.) Unless you have net-front bodies to shoot *for*, this shot has almost no value.
- **You are the last player back and a miss goes the other way.** See [Risk Management](../hockey-iq/risk_management.md). A wide shot from the point with nobody covering is how odd-man rushes get born.
- **The shot would end a possession you are winning.** If your line is cycling and the other team is tired and pinned, a bad shot resets everything. Sometimes the right play is another five seconds of possession.

**The test:** before you shoot, ask *what happens if this does not go in?* If the answer is "rebound in the slot for my winger" or "tip chance" or "we recover it in the corner," shoot. If the answer is "it goes off a shin pad and they break out," do not.

## Equipment Factors

```facts
Technique: Roughly right flex feels like the stick loading noticeably in a wrist shot, without feeling like a whip
Risk: Too stiff and you cannot fully load the shaft, so you lose power; too soft and the shaft flaps, wasting energy and accuracy
Options: A deeper curve lifts and helps toe drags; a flatter one gives a better backhand and better low shots
Action: Check your lie — stand naturally in skates and see whether the whole bottom edge of the blade touches
Never: Chase equipment as a substitute for reps — a correct stick removes an obstacle, it does not add a skill
```

Brief, because the detail lives in [Equipment](../off-the-ice/equipment.md).

- **Flex** — the number on the shaft, roughly the force in pounds needed to bend it a set distance. Flex is how much of your shot's power comes from the stick rather than your arms. **Too stiff** and you cannot fully load it, so you get less power and a slower release, not more. **Too soft** and the shaft over-bends, energy is wasted flapping the shaft, and accuracy suffers. Roughly right feels like the stick loading noticeably during a wrist shot without feeling like a whip. The common retail rule of thumb is to start at roughly half your body weight in pounds. Smaller and younger players often end up in sticks too stiff for them — and the commonest way anyone lands on the wrong flex is cutting a stick down, which makes it stiffer than the number printed on it, so buy a lower flex if you will cut more than about three inches off.
- **Curve** — the bend in the blade. A deeper curve lifts the puck more easily and helps with toe drags and quick elevation, at the cost of backhand control and low-shot accuracy. A flatter curve gives you a better backhand and better control of low shots. A **toe curve** favours quick releases and shots off the toe; a **mid curve** is more forgiving and more accurate on a full sweep.
- **Lie** — the angle between the blade and the shaft, which determines whether the blade sits flat on the ice in your natural stance. If the toe or the heel is off the ice when you are in your stance, your blade contact is wrong on every shot and no amount of technique fixes it. Check yours: stand naturally in skates, look at the blade, and see whether the whole bottom edge is touching.

Do not chase equipment as a substitute for reps. A correctly sized stick removes an obstacle; it does not add a skill.

## How to Practise

```facts
Priority: Volume builds the motor pattern, deliberate practice builds the skill you will use — do both
Action: Name a target, call the shot out loud before you take it, then score it and keep a count
Action: Practise the release, not the power. Set a no-gathering rule, puck arrives, shoot
Never: Shoot from a static, pre-set position. Stickhandle and move the puck laterally first instead
Action: Practise the backhand every session, and split forehand and backhand 50/50 for a month
Action: Practise the shots you dislike — off-balance, one-handed, pucks arriving behind you
Key: Tips and rebounds cannot be trained on a garage pad; do those on the ice
Action: Fifteen to twenty minutes a day of focused work beats two hours on a Sunday
Rule: Do not shoot at your own goalie's head in the warm-up or fire pucks from ten feet in a drill — in stand-up hockey only IIHF 9.6 and Hockey Canada 6.18(a) stop play for the hit itself, and both are narrow (a "may" on a "dazed" goalkeeper; "U13 and below")
Rule: Every book stops play for an injured goalkeeper (NHL, IIHF and PWHL 8.1, USA Hockey 206(a), Hockey Canada 2.4(b), CARHA 20(d)) — but each needs a referee to judge them hurt, which is what a cage hides
Rule: Never shoot on the opposition's goalie in the warm-up — both teams are confined to their own half. USA Hockey can penalise a breach (636(c), 502(b)); Hockey Canada can report it (2.2(i)) and penalise it (2.2(j)); the NHL and IIHF (86.6) report a breach rather than call it on the ice
```

Shooting is among the most trainable skills in hockey, for a reason that is not about the skill itself: you can practise it almost entirely off the ice, alone, in a garage. (No ranking of hockey skills by trainability was found in Crossref, OpenAlex or Europe PMC, searched 2 September 2026, so read that as the practical point it is — access, not a ranking.)

**Off-ice setup.** A shooting pad (a slick synthetic sheet, typically around 60 cm by 120 cm, or 2 ft by 4 ft), a net or a target board, and a bucket of pucks. Steel-tip protection or an old stick, because concrete will destroy a blade. That is the whole kit and it is inexpensive.

**Volume versus deliberate practice.** Both matter, and they are not the same:

- **Volume** builds the motor pattern. Hundreds of repetitions of the same shot, until the weight transfer and wrist roll happen without thought. There is no shortcut past this.
- **Deliberate practice** builds the skill you will actually use. That means: a specific target, a specific intention, and feedback on every rep. Ten shots at a named corner, checking each one, beats a hundred shots at the general area of the net.

The failure mode is doing only volume — a thousand comfortable wrist shots from the same spot, at the same speed, at nothing in particular. That makes you very good at one shot you will never take in a game.

**What to actually do:**

- **Targets.** Hang or prop something in the corners and five-hole. Missing must be visible and audible, or you will not correct it.
- **Call the shot before you take it.** "Low blocker." Then take it. Score it. Keep a count.
- **Practise the release, not the power.** Set a rule: no gathering. Puck arrives, shoot. Have someone pass to you, or bounce it off a rebounder.
- **Move first.** Stickhandle two touches, change the puck's position laterally, then shoot. Never shoot from a static, pre-set position — you will not get one in a game.
- **Practise off both feet**, including the "wrong" one.
- **Practise the backhand every session.** If you split forehand and backhand 50/50 for a month, your backhand will stop being a liability. Almost nobody does this, which is exactly why it works.
- **Practise shots you dislike.** Off-balance, one-handed extensions, pucks arriving behind you. Games are full of those; comfortable reps are not.
- **On-ice, practise tips and rebounds.** These cannot be trained on a garage pad and they produce a large share of real goals.

> **The target in a real practice is a person, and no rule book will get a whistle blown in time to protect them.** ⚠️ **Do not shoot at your goalie's head in the warm-up, and do not fire pucks at them from ten feet in a drill.** *(That is a coaching caution rather than a rule of hockey, and no incidence figure was found for it.)* **Every book stops play for an *injured* goalkeeper** — NHL, IIHF and PWHL Rule 8.1, USA Hockey 206(a), Hockey Canada 2.4(b), CARHA 20(d) — but each waits on an official judging the player hurt, and a goalie behind a cage who stays on their feet gives them least to judge. **Only two books stop play for the hit itself in stand-up hockey.** **IIHF Rule 9.6** *may*, if the Referee believes the goalkeeper *"is, or may be, dazed"* and there is *"no immediate scoring chance for the attacking team"*. **Hockey Canada Rule 6.18(a)** *shall*, but only in *"categories of U13 and below"*, adding that officials *"should always allow team staff to evaluate the goaltender during a stoppage under this rule (if necessary) to allow for concussion protocols be applied."* The NHL, CARHA and USA Hockey's stand-up rules have no mask-specific stoppage; USA Hockey's one is in its Sled Hockey rules and does not govern the stand-up game. **So do not count on a whistle.**
>
> **And the pre-game warm-up is inside the rules, so shooting on the opposition's goalie is not a grey area.** Each team warms up on its own half — **Hockey Canada Rule 2.2(i)**, *"players will be restricted to their own half of the ice"*, and **USA Hockey Rule 636(c)**, *"on their half of the rink"*. **USA Hockey Rule 502(b)**: *"Penalties may also be assessed during the warm-up period."* **Hockey Canada** has the Referee report a 2.2(i) breach to the Member or league, and **2.2(j)** lets the Referee penalise it as well, the penalty *"served at the start of the game"*. 🇬🇧 **The IIHF reports it too, and that is not a licence:** **Rule 86.6** confines each team to its own end and says *"any violation of this rule by the Players shall be reported to the Proper Authorities"* — so under a British reader's book it goes past the referee to the people who decide whether you play next week. ⚠️ **Do not stretch IIHF Rule 5.6's *"No penalties are to be assessed"* to cover it.** That sentence follows three provisions about protective equipment, players not on the roster sheet and warming up at a stoppage, and the same book penalises fighting before the drop of the puck at **Rule 46.10**. It is not blanket immunity for warm-up conduct.

**What improvement looks like.** Honest expectations, and these are general coaching guidance rather than measured findings. Treat them as a shape, not a schedule:

- **Weeks 1–4:** mechanics start to feel natural; power improves noticeably, mostly because your weight transfer stops being an afterthought.
- **Months 2–3:** release time drops meaningfully; you stop needing to gather. This is the change that shows up in games.
- **Months 4–6:** accuracy tightens; you can call a target and hit it more often than not; the backhand becomes usable.
- **Beyond:** the remaining gains are decision-making, not mechanics — shot selection, deception, and reading the goalie. Those are trained by playing and by watching, not by shooting at a garage door.

Fifteen to twenty minutes a day of focused work beats two hours on a Sunday. See [Practice and Development](../off-the-ice/practice_and_development.md) for how to build this into a wider training plan.

## Common Mistakes

- **Slapping at the puck instead of sweeping it.** **Pull the puck forward in one sweep and keep it on the blade until it leaves.** If you hear a click, you took a bad snap shot by accident.
- **Winding up to shoulder height in traffic because the NHL permits the follow-through.** **Look before you wind up and use a three-quarter wind-up in a crowd; in a CARHA league, where any backswing past fifteen inches is a slap shot and a penalty, shoot a snap or wrist shot.** ⚠️ **A stick that catches someone above the shoulders is not capped at a minor in any book**, and only the NHL, IIHF and PWHL excuse accidental contact from a normal wind-up or follow-through — never a wild swing at a bouncing puck. [High sticks and the wind-up](#high-sticks-and-the-wind-up--what-each-book-actually-charges) sets out each book.
- **One-timing with a stick or a shoulder inside your swing.** **Take the pass, kill it, and shoot half a second later — a snap shot on target beats a one-timer that catches someone's face.** A one-timer is the wind-up you take closest to other people's heads.
- **Shooting with arms only.** **With a beat of time, shift your weight from back foot to front foot as you shoot; without one, shoot off whichever foot carries your weight — never flat-footed with your arms alone.** Power comes from legs and torso through a bending stick, not from upper-body strength.
- **Hitting the puck directly on a slap shot.** **Strike the ice two to five centimetres behind the puck, not the puck.** Miss that and your slap shot is slower than your wrist shot.
- **Stopping your feet before shooting.** **Shoot in stride instead** — your momentum gives back the power you lose by not planting. Stopped feet are the clearest tell in hockey.
- **Gathering the puck before every shot.** **Shoot off the puck you already have, from where you already are.** Settle-look-plant-shoot costs half a second, which is the whole margin.
- **Shooting at a corner without reading the goalie.** **Shoot where the goalie is not: the far corner low just after a cross-ice pass, low blocker side when a teammate is at the net for the rebound, and the five-hole only while the goalie is moving, reaching or mid-drop.** A set butterfly closes the five-hole. This document follows the coaching school that argues against corners by default; plenty of good coaches teach corners, so find out what yours wants.
- **Shooting into shin pads.** **Look for the lane first; change the angle if there isn't one.** A blocked shot gives up the puck and usually starts a counter-attack.
- **Full wind-ups from the point with traffic in the lane.** **Low snap shot, through, every time.** A full wind-up announces the shot and it is getting blocked.
- **Shooting from above the circles because you have the puck.** **Take two strides toward the middle, or move the puck to someone already there.** Kane and Laine scored under 2% of their goals from above the circles — **two players in one analyst's article, from Sportlogiq tracking that is not public**, not a league-wide rate. The direction is the point.
- **Perimeter shots when a teammate is in the slot.** **Make the pass.** A perimeter shot trades a 10–15% chance for a 2–4% one — a range whose source names no dataset, so trust the gap rather than the decimals.
- **High point shots when your team is screening for a tip.** **Keep it knee-height or lower.** A high one is unusable for a tip.
- **Ducking when a defender moves you off the net front.** **Take the shove head up and chin off your chest, and meet a goal post with an arm or a leg first — never your head.** A screen has you standing still beside a steel frame, watching the shooter, which is exactly when ducking becomes the reflex. A tucked chin straightens the cervical spine, and USA Hockey's *Heads Up Hockey* guide says that head-first collision with the boards **or a goal post** *"can occur at walking speed."* ⚠️ **This applies in non-checking hockey too** — a box-out moves you along the same path in non-check adult rec and in women's hockey.
- **Screening with your feet in the blue paint, on the red line around it, or in a goaltender's path back to the crease.** **Plant outside the paint and off the red line, hold the ice you are standing on, and stay out of the road a scrambling goalie takes back to the net.** ⚠️ **Every book that settles the question puts the red line inside the crease, and a skate inside it puts the goal at risk under every book.** **Moving into** a returning goaltender's road home is interference in most books — usually two minutes, and it can end your game if you hurt them; under USA Hockey any deliberate contact with a goaltender is charging, never a bare minor. Never touch a goaltender to draw a call: you take the penalty instead. [In and around the crease](#in-and-around-the-crease--what-each-book-allows-and-what-it-charges) sets out each book.
- **Arriving into a goaltender who has turned away from you.** **Pull up, and take the ice in front of the net rather than the goaltender — a screen is a place you stand, a charge is a speed you get there at, and at the net front they are one stride apart.** ⚠️ **Contact on a goaltender's back is checking from behind, and no book prices it at a bare two minutes:** the NHL, IIHF and PWHL write no minor for it, Hockey Canada and CARHA put a game misconduct even on the minor, and USA Hockey's minor carries a misconduct. Driving a helpless goaltender into the net is a mandatory match penalty in Hockey Canada and CARHA; under USA Hockey a reckless hit, or one head first into the goal frame, is a mandatory major and game misconduct, and a match if it is reckless with excessive force. A hit that reaches the head is head contact too, which can eject you in every book and does so automatically in some; in Hockey Canada minor and female hockey a third head-contact penalty ejects you as well. A hit meant to injure is a match penalty with no discretion in every book except the IIHF, which has no match penalty but can still eject you. [In and around the crease](#in-and-around-the-crease--what-each-book-allows-and-what-it-charges) prices it book by book.
- **Assuming the crease rule is the same in every book.** **Screen from outside the paint, never start contact with a goaltender anywhere on the ice, and if a defender shoves you into the crease, get your feet out before you play the puck or touch the goalie.** Under USA Hockey and CARHA a skate in the crease takes the goal off your team on position alone, bar narrow exceptions; under Hockey Canada any goal on a play where you start contact with the goaltender is disallowed, inside or outside the crease. ⚠️ **Charging a goaltender is not capped at a minor in any book** — it reaches a major and a game misconduct in every one, mandatory under Hockey Canada for any charge, CARHA in the crease or when it injures, USA Hockey when it is reckless, and the NHL, the PWHL and the Elite League when a major for it injures the face or head. [In and around the crease](#in-and-around-the-crease--what-each-book-allows-and-what-it-charges) prices it book by book, and [Offensive Zone Play](../systems/offensive_zone_play.md) has the full comparison.
- **Shooting at your goalie's head in the warm-up, or firing pucks at them from ten feet in a drill.** **Keep warm-up shots off your goaltender's head and take them from distance, stay on your own half, and never shoot on the other team's goalie.** The first is a coaching caution, not a rule: ⚠️ **do not count on a whistle to protect them** — play stops for an injured goalkeeper only once a referee judges them hurt, which a cage hides. The second is a rule: each team warms up on its own half, and depending on the book a breach can be penalised or reported to the league. [How to Practise](#how-to-practise) sets out each book.
- **Never practising the backhand.** **Put backhands into every session, and use the backhand in tight whenever turning to your forehand would cost the chance.** Goalies read it worst and you meet it most in tight, so skipping it is deciding to be bad at it.
- **Bracing hard on a tip.** **Hold the stick loosely and let it give a little on contact.** Stiff hands fire the puck to the corner; soft hands drop it at your feet for a second chance.
- **Watching your shot instead of following it.** **Follow your shot, and your teammates', to the net with your stick on the ice** — the rebound goes to whoever arrives. Shoot it at once, pull up short of the goaltender, and play the puck rather than them.
- **Blaming the stick.** Get the flex and lie roughly right, then stop thinking about it.

## Key Takeaways

1. **Get the puck away before the goalie is set — release speed beats shot speed.** A goalie stops what they are set for, so a quick release is worth more than any amount of velocity.
2. **Inside the dots, shoot the snap shot; keep the full slap shot for the point with a lane and time.** The snap has nearly slap-shot power with nearly wrist-shot release; a full wind-up in traffic is slow, easy to read and easy to block. Three-quarter it in a crowd, and if a stick or shoulder is inside a one-timer's swing, take the pass and shoot second. ⚠️ **Bring the blade down before it reaches anyone's head: a high stick to a person is not capped at a minor in any book, and only the NHL, IIHF and PWHL excuse accidental contact from a normal wind-up.** In a CARHA league any backswing past fifteen inches is a slap shot — a minor, or a major that ejects you and sits you out the next game if it injures someone — and many rec and youth leagues ban it by house rule, so read your rules sheet. [High sticks and the wind-up](#high-sticks-and-the-wind-up--what-each-book-actually-charges) sets out each book.
3. **Aim the height with your follow-through, not the blade angle you finish at.** Finish low for a low shot, high for a high one. (Measured for the stationary wrist shot only: Michaud-Paquette, Pearsall and Turcotte found the blade's orientation *at puck release* did not predict where the shot went, because the blade is still turning through about 180 ms of contact; how fast it turned predicted the high shots.)
4. **Get to the middle before you shoot — location is most of shot value.** One widely cited breakdown puts slot shots at roughly 10–15% and perimeter shots at 2–4%, but **that source names no dataset**, so trust the gap rather than the decimals. Getting to the middle is worth more than any technical improvement.
5. **Make the goalie move sideways, or take away their sight — then go and get the rebound.** Pass across before you shoot; at the net front, stand in the goalie's eyes with your stick on the ice for the tip, then shoot the rebound the moment it comes. ⚠️ **Screen from outside the blue paint and off the red line around it, stay out of the road a scrambling goalie takes back to the net, pull up rather than arrive, and take a shove head up with your chin off your chest.** Contact you start with a goalie can cost the goal and you a penalty, and a charge or a hit from behind on one can end your game in every book — automatically in some, and driving a helpless goalie into the net from behind is a mandatory match penalty under Hockey Canada, CARHA and USA Hockey. The crease rule differs by book, so find out which one your league runs. Nobody has priced a screen, so any multiplier you hear is folklore. [In and around the crease](#in-and-around-the-crease--what-each-book-allows-and-what-it-charges) sets out each book.
6. **On a penalty shot or a shootout attempt, keep the puck moving forward and plan on one shot.** Pick your move on the way in: under NHL Rule 24 you cannot stop, circle back or curl away, the attempt ends the moment the puck stops or crosses the goal line, and a rebound counts only off the post or crossbar, then the goalkeeper, then directly in. **Leave the spin-o-rama — a completed 360° on the approach — out unless you know your book allows it:** the NHL, IIHF and PWHL stop the shot and give no goal, Hockey Canada permits it while the puck keeps moving towards the goal line, and USA Hockey and CARHA write no rule on it but still require that forward movement. **On a lacrosse-style move, keep the puck below your shoulders — that binds you in every book here — and release it below the crossbar.** 🇬🇧 **In Britain, ask which competition you are in before planning on a number of shooters or on shooting twice** — the NIHL, SNL and Elite League differ, and the repeat-shooter rule changed for 2026-27. [The rules you must know](#the-rules-you-must-know-before-you-take-one) sets out each book, and [Game Management](../systems/game_management.md) the formats.
7. **Before you shoot, find the lane and ask what happens if it does not go in.** A shot into a defender's shin pads is a turnover: change the angle or move the puck. A rebound for a teammate, a tip, or a puck you will recover means shoot. With traffic at the net, a hard low shot at the pads can become a goal, a tip or a rebound; alone with the goalie beaten, pick a target. Think twice as the last player back, where a wide shot starts an odd-man rush, and when your line is cycling a tired team.
8. **Practise the backhand and the release.** Both are volume problems, both beat goalies directly, and **almost nobody trains either** — a coaching observation, not a measured finding: no count of how players split practice between shot types was found in Crossref, OpenAlex or Europe PMC, searched 2 September 2026. ⚠️ **Practise *on* a goaltender, not *at* one: no shots at your goalie's head in the warm-up, and no pucks from ten feet in a drill.** No whistle will come in time to protect them, so the care is yours. **And never shoot on the opposition's goalie in the warm-up** — each team keeps to its own half, and that one is a rule.
9. **Off the rush, shoot in stride and shoot early** — as the defender's stick is closing, not after, or you are shooting into a blocked lane. Release off the foot you don't normally use, which on your off wing is the inside foot. If a trailer is coming late into the high slot, delay a beat and pass back: your shot from the wing becomes their shot from the slot.
10. **Use a stick you can actually bend, with a blade that sits flat in your stance — then stop thinking about equipment.** Start at a flex of roughly half your body weight in pounds, lower if you cut off more than about three inches; too stiff and you lose power and release speed. A toe or heel off the ice spoils your contact on every shot.

*The IIHF’s absent match penalty, and what its ceiling actually costs — read in the rulebook texts on 25 September 2026. **IIHF Official Rule Book 2025/26 (v1.1)**: **Rule 20.4**, where a major listed in the Reference Tables carries an *“Automatic Game Misconduct”* and the Player *“shall be ruled off the ice for the balance of the game, but a substitute shall be permitted to replace the suspended Player after five (5) minutes have elapsed”*; **Rule 23.1**, where a game misconduct penalty *“involves the suspension of a Player for the balance of the game, but a substitute is permitted to replace the Player removed”*; **Rule 43.3**, whose discretion attaches to the major and not to the game misconduct — *“The Referee, at their discretion, shall assess a major penalty and an automatic game misconduct penalty”*; **Rule 43.4**, *“[i]f deemed appropriate, Supplementary Discipline can be applied by the Proper Authorities at their discretion”*, routing to **Rule 28**; and **Appendix IV Table 6**, headed *“Summary of major penalties that result in an automatic game misconduct”*, which lists Checking from Behind at 43.3 — the **2026/27** edition merging it into **Table 5**, where that row ticks **MAJOR + GMP** and leaves **MAJOR ONLY** blank. **That the IIHF writes no match penalty was re-tested rather than assumed:** searches of both editions, across line breaks, for “match penalt”, “gross misconduct”, “expelled” and “expulsion” each return zero. Against it, [NHL Official Rules 2025-2026 (PDF)](https://media.d3.nhle.com/image/private/t_document/prd/slwjuaqwmuvj5bkplixo.pdf) **Rule 21.1** — *“A match penalty involves the suspension of a player for the balance of the game and the offender shall be ordered to the dressing room immediately”* — with **21.2** permitting a substitute *“after five (5) minutes playing time has elapsed”*, so the two books reach the same on-ice outcome by different names. **What sits above that on-ice ceiling was read in the same text on 25 September 2026**: **Rule 60.7**, high-sticking's own *“DISCIPLINARY MEASURES”* clause — *“If deemed appropriate, Supplementary Discipline can be applied by the Proper Authorities at their discretion”*, routing to Rule 28 — **word for word the same in the 2026/27 edition**; **Rule 28.1**, which lets those authorities *“assess additional disciplinary measures for any offense committed during the course of a game… whether or not such offense has been penalized by the Referee(s)”*; **Rule 20.7**, *“[t]he Referee is required to report all major penalties and the surrounding circumstances to the Proper Authorities immediately following the game”*, also identical in the 2026/27 edition; and **Rule 23.2**, where a game misconduct *“shall be reported to the Proper Authorities which shall have full power to impose further disciplinary measures”*. **Rule 23.5** names a STICK INFRACTIONS disciplinary category and says only that *“[t]he respective process is outlined in the IIHF Disciplinary Code.”* ⚠️ **The IIHF Disciplinary Regulations, in effect from 6 October 2025 and read on 15 October 2026, list the sanctions available but price no offence and set out no stick-infraction process, and their suspension rules govern IIHF Events only — so no suspension length or tariff is stated anywhere on this page: what was verified is that the route exists and that the referral is mandatory, not what it costs.***
