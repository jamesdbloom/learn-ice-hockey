## Key focus

**Play with your head up.** A player staring at the puck cannot see the pass, the open ice or the checker coming, and gets hit hard. Build feel for the puck first, so your eyes are free to come up.

**Keep your body between the checker and the puck.** Puck on the side away from the pressure, feet wide, low. A puck on the checker's side means your stick has to beat theirs, and theirs is closer. Coaches rank protection above hands.

**Know your out before you reach the puck.** Who is your outlet, and if nobody is there, where does it go — a rim around the boards, a reverse back against the pressure, or a chip past the checker? Protection buys a second, not a minute.

**On the wall, take the contact along your side, head up.** Skates parallel to the boards, forearm and hip to the wall. Never turn your back to the checker and never duck, however slowly they come: one makes it a hit from behind, the other risks your neck.

**Brace with your free hand high on your own stick — never grab.** Same leverage, no penalty. Brace, don't drive — a driven shaft is a cross-check — and holding, or a hand on the cage, is a penalty in every book.

**Never stickhandle in your own zone, and don't dangle through the middle.** A turnover there is an instant chance against. Pass, rim, reverse, or put it off the glass — never over it: from your own zone that is a minor in the NHL, IIHF and PWHL even by accident, and a penalty everywhere if deliberate.

**Look for the pass before the move.** A pass is faster than any skater, cannot be poked off your blade, and moves the defence further than a deke. Even a pass to a teammate in a worse spot usually beats a move against a well-placed defender.

**When you take a defender on, make them turn.** Attack a shoulder, change speed, and sell one fake with your head and shoulders before you accelerate. If a stick seals your lane, push the puck past it and skate around the other side.

**Don't slow down when the puck arrives.** Decelerating to handle it hands the defence two free seconds. At speed, carry rather than dribble: push it ahead and to the side, take two or three hard strides, catch it, repeat.

## Overview

Puck handling — moving the puck on your stick, skating with it, and keeping it from an opponent — buys the half-second every other decision needs. This document is about spending it well.

**Start with your eyes.** A player staring at the puck cannot use anything they know about the game, because it all arrives through the eyes. Ordering your eyes up fails: you look up, lose the puck and look back down. Build feel instead — the toe, middle and heel of the blade through the shaft, the puck all around your body — and the eyes come up on their own.

**Then keep the puck you have.** Coaches rank protection above every deke. With a checker on you, put the puck on your far side, feet wider than your shoulders, hip and backside into them rather than your chest. In a non-checking game, hold your position and never bump or drive into them; in a CARHA adult league, avoid the contact altogether, because CARHA penalises not avoiding it.

On the wall, skates parallel to the boards, forearm and hip to the wall. Never turn your back to the checker and never duck. [Side-on into the checker](#side-on-into-the-checker) explains why.

**Protection buys a second, so spend it.** Decide your pass or your out while you are still skating to the puck. Shielding for three seconds while a second checker arrives is a slow-motion turnover. In your own end, move it at once; low in the offensive zone, keep it moving around the defender until something opens.

**In open ice, pass first and move second.** When a move is right, win it with speed and body position, not your hands: make the defender turn, give one convincing fake, and accelerate into the lane it opens. Whatever you do, keep your feet going.

## The Grip and the Hands

Everything downstream — soft hands, protection, deception — is decided by where your hands are and how hard you are squeezing.

### The top hand does the work

```facts
Technique: Steer with the top hand — it opens and closes the blade and does most of the stickhandling
Position: Top hand nearest the butt-end — left hand for a left-handed shooter, right for a right-handed one
Technique: Let the shaft slide fairly freely through a loose bottom hand, which guides and stabilises
Never: Let the bottom hand grip and therefore fight the top hand
Action: Tighten the bottom hand to set the angle on a wide reach or hold the puck against a stick
Technique: Off-ice, slide a cut-off bottle or tube over the shaft so the bottom hand cannot cheat
```

Your **top hand** is the hand nearest the butt-end of the stick (left hand for a left-handed shooter, right for a right-handed shooter). It is your control hand. It opens and closes the blade over the puck, it drives the puck side to side, and it does most of the actual stickhandling. Think of the stick as a lever: the top hand is the end you steer with.

Your **bottom hand** is a guide and a stabiliser. When you shoot or pass it becomes the power hand; while you are stickhandling it does much less, and the shaft should slide fairly freely through a loose grip. **It is not doing nothing, though** — it steadies the blade, it sets the angle on a wide reach, and it is what you tighten on when you need to hold the puck against a stick. The fault to avoid is a bottom hand that *grips* and therefore fights the top hand, not a bottom hand that participates: see [Grip pressure](#grip-pressure--hold-it-like-a-bird) below, where a tight bottom hand is named as the first thing to check when your hands have gone dead.

**Test it off the ice:** slide a cut-off water bottle or a paper-towel tube over the shaft and hold *that* with your bottom hand instead of gripping the stick. The shaft slides freely, your bottom hand cannot cheat, and your top hand is forced to learn the job. Ten minutes of this teaches more than an hour of ordinary reps.

### Grip pressure — hold it like a bird

```facts
Technique: Hold the stick firmly enough that it cannot escape, softly enough that you do not crush it
Never: White-knuckle the stick — Better Hockey's coaching guide calls it the number-one thing that kills soft hands, and names the bottom hand as the first place to look
Key: A squeezed hand cannot feel, and if you cannot feel the puck you cannot play with your head up
Read: The bottom hand is where to look first for excess grip pressure — one coach's order of checking, not a count
```

Firmly enough that it cannot escape, softly enough that you do not crush it. A squeezed hand cannot feel anything, and if you cannot feel the puck you cannot play with your head up. The bottom hand is the first place to look, and that is one coach's order of checking rather than a count: [Better Hockey](https://www.betterhockey.com/blogs/stickhandling/how-to-develop-soft-hands)'s head coach calls a white-knuckle grip, *"especially in the bottom hand"*, *"the number-one thing that kills soft hands"*, and says the bottom-hand grip is the first thing he fixes on a player whose hands have gone dead.

That ranks grip as the top killer of *feel*, not as the reason pucks bounce off blades — on that, the same guide leads with cushioning and blade angle, which are [Soft hands](#soft-hands) and [Cup the blade](#cup-the-blade) below. Neither is a measurement.

### Hands away from the body

```facts
Position: Top hand out in front of your chest, roughly a fist's distance off your torso, elbow slightly bent
Never: Hip lock — top hand glued to the hip, puck rattling in a narrow band directly in front of your skates
Goal: Reach the full arc around your body — far left, far right, out front, and behind your heels
Key: Hands pinned to the hips leave no far side, so the puck is always in front of the checker too
Risk: With tight hands, every stick check that arrives will get a piece of the puck
```

Watch a new player and you will usually see **"hip lock"** — the top hand glued to the hip, the puck rattling back and forth in a narrow band directly in front of the skates. It feels safe. It is the opposite of safe.

- Pull the top hand **out in front of your chest**, roughly a fist's distance off your torso, with a slight bend in the elbow.
- From there, your blade can reach the full arc around your body: far left, far right, out front, and behind your heels.

**Why a player with tight hands cannot protect anything.** Puck protection means putting your body between the checker and the puck. If your hands are pinned to your hips, the puck can only ever be directly in front of you — which is directly in front of the checker too. You have no far side to move it to. Hands tight to the body is not a stylistic flaw; it is a structural inability to shield the puck, and every stick check that arrives will get a piece of it.

### The one-handed extension

```facts
Technique: Release the bottom hand and reach one-handed — it adds most of a forearm's length to your reach
Options: Pull in a puck outside your normal radius, stretch it away from a checker, or reach around a sealing stick
Action: Practise it deliberately — reach wide, release, control the puck, recover the grip
Key: The one-handed extension should not feel like an emergency
```

Letting go with the bottom hand and reaching one-handed adds most of a forearm's length to your reach. Use it to:

- pull a puck in from outside your normal radius,
- stretch the puck away from a checker while your body stays between them and it,
- reach around a stick that is sealing your normal handling lane.

Practise it deliberately — reach as far as you can to one side, release the bottom hand, control the puck, recover the grip. It should not feel like an emergency.

## Basic Stickhandling

### Soft hands

```facts
Technique: Let the blade give as the puck arrives — loose grip, active wrists, a slight draw-back on contact
Goal: Deaden the puck rather than let it rebound off the blade
Key: Catch it like an egg
Never: Handle with stiff wrists — stiff wrists mean dead hands
```

"Soft hands" means the blade **gives** when the puck arrives instead of repelling it. Loose grip, active wrists, and a slight draw-back of the blade on contact so the puck deadens rather than rebounds. The coaching cue is *catch it like an egg*. Stiff wrists mean dead hands.

### Cup the blade

```facts
Technique: Angle the blade so it leans over the top of the puck rather than standing vertical
Never: Cup the forehand only — an uncupped backhand lets the puck roll up the blade and flip over
Goal: Keep the puck sitting in the pocket of the blade
Technique: Roll the top-hand wrist to change the cup from forehand to backhand — the heart of stickhandling
Read: A puck that keeps jumping off your stick sends you to the blade cup and the grip pressure first — an order to check them in, not a count of causes
Read: One guide used here goes further and says a too-tight bottom hand is almost always the cause of a puck bouncing off the blade — an impression, not a count
```

**Cupping** means angling the blade so it leans over the top of the puck rather than standing vertical. Cup on the forehand and cup on the backhand — the puck sits in the pocket instead of rolling up the blade and flipping over. When the puck keeps jumping off your stick, check the blade cup and your grip first — an order to check them in, not a measured share of the cause. One guide goes further: Hockey's Arsenal writes that when pucks bounce wildly off a player's stick as they receive it, *"it's almost always driven by a bottom hand that grips the stick too tight."* That is an impression, not a count, but it is the same bottom hand [Grip pressure](#grip-pressure--hold-it-like-a-bird) sends you to first.

Rolling the top-hand wrist is what changes the cup from forehand to backhand. That wrist roll is the mechanical heart of stickhandling.

### Wrists, not arms

```facts
Technique: Move the puck with your wrists and hands, not by swinging your shoulders
Never: Use big arm sweeps — slow, visible from twenty feet, and they drag your upper body off balance
Key: You can abort a wrist move halfway through; you cannot abort an arm swing
Action: Drill soft touch — puck or ball back and forth as fast as you can with minimal lower-arm movement
```

The puck should move because your wrists and hands moved, not because your shoulders swung. Big arm sweeps are slow, visible from twenty feet away, and they drag your whole upper body off balance. Quick wrists are fast, quiet, and reversible — you can abort halfway through a wrist move, and you cannot abort halfway through an arm swing.

A useful off-ice drill (**soft touch**): move a ball or puck back and forth as fast as you can with as little lower-arm movement as possible. Only the hands and the top forearm should be working.

### Wide dribbles and tight dribbles

```facts
Options: Wide — big sweeping pulls outside your feet, for open ice, setting up a deke, or crossing a defender's stick
Options: Tight — small quick touches near your skates, for traffic, the boards, the slot and protecting
Risk: Wide handling exposes the puck to a poke check — an opponent jabbing their blade out along the ice
Risk: Tight handling limits your reach, and you get pinned if you never widen
Never: Own only one of the two — wide-only players get poke-checked, tight-only players never move a defender
```

These are two different tools, and you need both. Two terms first. A **poke check** is an opponent jabbing their stick blade straight out along the ice to knock the puck off yours. It is the check most of this section defends against, because coaches treat it as the commonest way a puck is taken — a judgement, not a count. And the **slot** is the ice directly in front of the net, between the two faceoff circles, running from the top of the circles down to the goalmouth; [Rink Map](../foundation/rink_map.md) has that and every other named area of the ice.

| | **Wide** | **Tight** |
|---|---|---|
| Looks like | Big sweeping pulls, well outside your feet | Small, quick touches near your skates |
| Use it for | Open ice, setting up a 1-on-1 deke, moving the puck across a defender's stick | Traffic, the boards, the slot, protecting |
| Risk | Exposes the puck to a poke check | Limited reach; easy to get pinned if you never widen |

The mistake is having only one. Players who only handle wide get poke-checked in traffic. Players who only handle tight can never move a defender.

### Front-to-back as well as side-to-side

```facts
Never: Handle side-to-side only — it is a rhythm, and three seconds of it tells a defender where the puck will be
Action: Train the whole reachable circle — in front, both sides, and behind your heels on both sides
Technique: Add quick diagonal transfers, front-left to rear-right and back again
Goal: Front-to-back range is what makes the toe drag possible and pulls the puck back from a side check
```

Almost everyone learns side-to-side and stops. Side-to-side alone is a rhythm, and rhythms get read — a defender who has watched you go left-right-left-right for three seconds knows exactly when the puck will be on your backhand.

Train the whole reachable circle:

- in front of you,
- both sides,
- **behind your heels** on both sides,
- and quick diagonal transfers (front-left to rear-right, rear-right to front-right, and so on).

Front-to-back is also what makes the toe drag possible, and it is what lets you pull a puck back in when a checker's stick arrives from the side.

### Keep the puck moving

```facts
Never: Let a stationary puck sit on a stationary blade — it is the easiest thing in hockey to take
Action: Keep constant small movement on the puck
Goal: Stop a defender committing to a poke check — they never know which touch is the one they can reach
```

A stationary puck on a stationary blade is the easiest thing in hockey to take. Constant small movement — dribbling — keeps a defender from committing to a poke check, because they never know which touch is the one they can reach.

## Head Up

**If you take one habit from this document, take this one.** It is a coaching priority, not a measured ranking, and the reason follows.

### Why it matters more than anything else

```facts
Key: Reading the forecheck, finding support, timing an entry and hitting a moving teammate all need your eyes off the puck
Never: Stare at the puck — it locks you out of every tactical idea in the game, however well you understand it
Risk: A puck-watcher gets hit hard, because they cannot see the checker coming
```

Look at what the rest of this guide asks you to do. Read the [forecheck](../systems/forechecking_systems.md) and pick the right side. Find the [support](../hockey-iq/puck_support_and_spacing.md) option. Time your [zone entry](../systems/zone_entries.md). [Scan](../hockey-iq/scanning_and_anticipation.md) before you receive. Hit a moving teammate with a [pass](passing_and_receiving.md). Every single one of those requires your eyes to be off the puck.

A player who stares at the puck is not a worse hockey player by a little. They are locked out of every tactical idea in the game, no matter how well they understand it, because understanding arrives through the eyes. They also get hit hard, because they cannot see the checker coming.

### How to develop it — feel, don't look

```facts
Never: Simply order yourself to look up — you will look up, lose the puck, and look straight back down
Technique: Learn the blade by touch — toe, middle and heel, forehand and backhand — and name them as you go
Action: Peek, don't stare. Start with eyes up and glance down only when you have genuinely lost the puck
Position: Eyes up and forward, not chin to the ceiling — that puts the puck at the bottom edge of your vision
Action: Widen your handling — feel comes from variety, and a six-inch band teaches one sensation
```

Do not simply order yourself to look up; you will look up, lose the puck, and look back down. Build the **feel** first, and the eyes lift on their own.

1. **Learn the blade by touch.** Move the puck slowly and deliberately and pay attention to how it feels on the **toe**, in the **middle**, and on the **heel** — and on the forehand and backhand of each. Different spots feel genuinely different through the shaft. Name them in your head as you go.
2. **Peek, don't stare.** Start with your eyes up and glance down only when you have genuinely lost the puck's position. Then make the glances shorter and rarer. The goal is knowing where the puck is without looking, not heroically refusing to look.
3. **Keep the puck in your peripheral vision.** Head up does not mean chin to the ceiling. Eyes up and forward puts the puck at the bottom edge of your vision, which is enough.
4. **Widen your handling.** Feel comes from variety. A player who handles only in a six-inch band has learned one sensation; a player who handles around their whole body has learned dozens.

### Drills for head-up handling

```facts
Action: Stickhandle facing numbers, words or a TV and call out what you see — if you cannot read it, your head is down
Action: Mine field — scatter a dozen pucks or balls and handle through them without hitting any
Action: Weave through zigzagged pucks, sticks or cones so your eyes have to plan a route
Action: Play two-player keep-away — nothing exposes a puck-watcher faster
Technique: Use a golf or tennis ball — the unpredictable bounce forces faster hands and better feel
Read: Expect a small step backwards the first time you take off-ice hands onto the ice
```

- **Read something.** Stickhandle off-ice facing a wall with numbers, words or a TV, and call out what you see. If you cannot read it, your head is down.
- **Mine field.** Scatter a dozen pucks or balls on a smooth surface and stickhandle through them without hitting any. It forces your eyes to the obstacles rather than the puck.
- **Weave and obstacle courses.** Set pucks, sticks or cones in a zigzag and move through them while handling. Now your eyes have to plan a route.
- **Two-player keep-away.** Nothing exposes a puck-watcher faster than someone actively trying to take it. You will look up because you must.
- **Use a golf ball or tennis ball.** They bounce and roll unpredictably, which forces faster hands and — perversely — better feel. When you go back to a puck it feels enormous and slow.

> **Honest note:** off-ice work transfers well but not perfectly. The first time you take your off-ice hands onto the ice, expect a small step backwards while you adjust to skates, a longer stick relative to your height, and a puck that slides much more freely. That is normal. Five steps forward, one step back.

## Stickhandling in Tight

Most puck touches in a real game happen in about the space of a phone box, with a stick in your feet and a body on your back. Open-ice hands are the fun part; tight hands are the useful part.

### Small-area control

```facts
Technique: Shorten everything — small touches, close to your skates, blade always cupped
Never: Make a big pull in traffic — it is a gift-wrapped turnover
Position: Handle in the space you own — the small triangle between your two skates and the toe of your blade
Action: Turn away from pressure, rotating the puck behind your body rather than beating the stick on the pressure side
```

- **Shorten everything.** Small touches, close to your skates, blade always cupped.
- **Keep the puck moving through small arcs**, not big pulls. A big pull in traffic is a gift-wrapped turnover.
- **Handle in the space you own**, which in traffic is usually the small triangle between your two skates and the toe of your blade — plus whatever your body is shielding.
- **Turn away, not into.** When pressure arrives from one side, the first instinct should be to rotate the puck behind your body, not to try to beat the stick on the pressure side.

### Your feet and body are part of your handling

**In short:** Shield the puck with your skates and body, pull it straight back to your blade, and never park on it.

```facts
Position: Use your skates as a wall — a puck in your feet is in one of the harder places on the ice to reach
Technique: Trap the puck against the inside of your skate and pull it back out to your blade — then play it; parking on it to force a whistle is a delay-of-game minor, and a penalty shot inside your own crease
Action: Use your hips and backside as the barrier, not your stick — the stick should be busy handling
Never: Believe the space between your feet is legally protected — none of the six books here reserves it
Rule: A stick in your skates is tripping if it brings you down (NHL Rule 57.1), and hooking with no fall at all if it restrains you (Rules 55.1, 56.1)
Rule: Under USA Hockey Rule 639 a loss of balance is enough — but where the player does not fall, its Casebook (639, Situation 1) requires that the impeding was "clearly obvious" AND that "a competitive advantage was gained"; its Standard of Play names the can-opener between the legs
Rule: Do not expect an automatic whistle — USA Hockey's Note to 639(a) withholds the penalty from a player "clearly hook-checking or poke-checking the puck for the purpose of gaining possession", but its Casebook (639, Situation 2) adds that the defender "is still responsible for their stick"
Rule: Kicking the puck is legal in all zones — the puck, never a player — but you cannot score with a distinct kicking motion (NHL Rule 49.2; kicking a player is 49.3)
Rule: Kicking or attempting to kick an opponent is a match penalty under NHL and PWHL 49.3, with no injury needed, USA Hockey 627(b) and Hockey Canada 7.1(c)(iii) — and USA Hockey 627(a) makes even a non-kicking skate push-off a major plus a game misconduct
Rule: CARHA 48(c) makes kicking or attempting to kick an opposing player a Match penalty, in CARHA-affiliated adult leagues only
Rule: A match penalty is the ceiling inside the game and not the end of it — every book here that writes one holds you out until your case has been dealt with (NHL and PWHL 21.2 and 21.3, USA Hockey 405(c), Hockey Canada 4.10(a), CARHA 34(a)) and makes the referee's report of it mandatory
Rule: Britain's book reaches it differently — the IIHF writes no match penalty anywhere, so IIHF 49.3 leaves kicking or attempting to kick an opponent to a major at the referee's discretion, with an automatic game misconduct once they assess it
Rule: A kicked puck that deflects in off a body is no goal; off your own stick it is a good goal — NHL 49.2(i) and 49.2(iii), IIHF 49.2(I) and 49.2(III), PWHL 49.2(i) and 49.2(iii) — while Hockey Canada 6.10(b) and USA Hockey's Casebook (627, Situation 4) both disallow that good-goal limb
Rule: CARHA takes that goal away too, in CARHA-affiliated adult leagues only — its Rule 69 permits kicking the puck in all zones but adds that the action "cannot result in a goal being scored", and its Rule 60(c) disallows a goal where a kicked puck "deflects off any player or stick into the net"
```

- **Use your skates as a wall.** A puck loose in your feet is not lost — it is in one of the harder places on the ice for an opponent to reach. The checker has to reach past your skates, your legs and your turned body, and doing that at speed usually costs them their balance or a penalty. That protection is practical, not legal: no rule keeps their stick out, though one that trips or holds you is a penalty.
- **Trap it, then play it.** Trap the puck against the inside of your skate and pull it straight back out to your blade. Do it fast: while you dig at your feet your head is down, and that is when the checker arrives. ⚠️ **Trap it and play it — do not park on it.** Holding it in your feet to force a whistle is a penalty in every book, and in your own crease a penalty shot. The trap is a half-second move, not a hiding place.
- **Kick it to yourself — at the puck, never at a player.** Pull the puck into your feet, kick it forward, catch it on the blade. Kicking the puck is legal in all zones. Keep the motion small, and if you cannot see the puck, do not swing your blade at where you think it is. ⚠️ **Kicking or trying to kick an opponent is never a two-minute mistake:** five of the six books throw you out and keep you out until your case has been dealt with, and under the IIHF the referee may give a major that throws you out. [Body Contact and Battles](body_contact_and_battles.md) covers this in full.
- **Use your hips and backside as the barrier**, not your stick. Your stick should be busy handling; your body should be doing the blocking.

**What it costs.** **The space between your feet is not reserved.** None of the six books here reserves it, and none even uses the phrase "between the skates", so a checker may put a stick in there. What turns that into a penalty is the *effect*, and the bar is lower than "he knocked me over". It is **tripping** if it brings you down — *NHL Official Rules 2025-2026, Rule 57.1: "A player shall not place the stick, knee, foot, arm, hand or elbow in such a manner that causes his opponent to trip or fall."* It is **hooking**, with no fall at all, if the stick restrains you: Rule 55.1 defines hooking as *"using the stick in a manner that enables a player to restrain an opponent"*, and a stick in your skates dragging you up is that.

**Under USA Hockey the bar is lower again,** ⚠️ **so find out whether your league runs that book — this document has no count of how many do.** Rule 639 defines tripping as causing an opponent to *"lose balance or fall"* — losing balance is enough. **Where the player does not fall, the Casebook sets a higher bar** (Rule 639, Situation 1): *"it must be clearly obvious their progress was impeded **and a competitive advantage was gained** as a result of the tripping action."* That is an interpretation rather than rule text, and it cuts both ways: it is not a licence to put a stick in somebody's feet as long as they stay upright, and not a promise of a whistle when a stick clips you and costs you nothing.

USA Hockey's Standard of Play names the can-opener outright — *"Placing the stick between the legs of the opponent (can opener/corkscrew) that causes a loss of balance **or impedes the progress** of the opponent"* — and a blade laid flat across your path, *"even if on the ice"*, is the same offence where there is *"no effort to legally play the puck"*. **But do not expect an automatic whistle:** 639(a)'s Note withholds the penalty if *"the player was clearly hook-checking or poke-checking the puck for the purpose of gaining possession."*

As the checker, read it the other way. ⚠️ **If you are the one holding that stick, the Note is not a licence.** The Casebook (639, Situation 2) adds that *"the defensive player is still responsible for their stick"*, and a hook between the legs that recklessly endangers is a major plus a game misconduct or a match penalty (Rule 623, Situation 3) — plan on the match, because Rule 602(a) says one *"shall be assessed"* to any player who *"recklessly endangers"* an opponent. It could also be called spearing if the toe of the blade does it.

**Parking on the puck is a minor in every book here.** The NHL names your skates outright: **63.2(i)** is delay of game for any player who *"holds, freezes or plays the puck with his stick, skates or body in such a manner as to deliberately cause a stoppage of play"*, and the IIHF (**63.2(I)**) and PWHL (**65.2(i)**) say the same in their own pronouns. USA Hockey reaches it through **610(a)**, **632(b)** and **614(a)**; Hockey Canada through **10.1** (item i and 10.1(a), whose Interpretation 1 says *"[p]layers must keep the puck in motion wherever possible"*) and **10.2(a)(iv)**; and CARHA through personal minors at **58(a)** and **74(b)** as well as a bench minor at **55(a)**.

Hockey Canada and CARHA excuse a puck held against the boards or goal while *"they are being checked by an opponent"* — a puck tied up under a check, not one frozen on purpose, and Hockey Canada 6.12(b) penalises making the puck unplayable *"in order to gain a stoppage of play"*. ⚠️ **In your own goal crease it stops being a two-minute mistake altogether.** The NHL, IIHF and PWHL bar any defending skater from holding the puck *"when the puck is within the goal crease"*, with no hand condition, so a loose puck held in your skates in your own crease is a **penalty shot**, or an awarded goal with your goalie pulled. All six books get there, and [the next section](#if-the-puck-pops-up-do-not-grab-it) sets out how.

**Kicking an opponent.** ⚠️ **It is a match penalty** — ejection, with no injury needed — under the NHL and PWHL (**49.3**), USA Hockey (**627(b)**), Hockey Canada (**7.1(c)(iii)**, Attempt to Injure) and CARHA (**48(c)**, CARHA-affiliated adult leagues only), and every one of them penalises the attempt as well as the act. USA Hockey's **627(a)** also makes a non-kicking skate push-off a major plus a game misconduct.

The match penalty is not the end of it either. **Every one of those books keeps you out until your case has been dealt with**, and makes the referee's report mandatory (NHL and PWHL **21.2** and **21.3**, USA Hockey **405(c)**, Hockey Canada **4.10(a)**, CARHA **34(a)**). The NHL Commissioner may add supplementary discipline (**49.4**, **Rule 28**). USA Hockey's 405(c) bars you from practices as well as games, and its Standardized Discipline Policy puts a 627(b) kick at **Level 3 — a recommended three-to-five game suspension**, which a hearing may reduce, maintain or increase.

**Britain's book is different.** **The IIHF reaches it by another route, so do not carry "match penalty" into a British game:** it writes no match penalty anywhere, and its **Rule 49.3** reads *"The Referee, at their discretion, may assess a major penalty and an automatic game misconduct penalty if, in their judgment, the Player kicks or attempts to kick an opponent"*. **No book here makes kicking a player a two-minute mistake.**

**The kicked goal.** Kicking the puck is legal in all zones under NHL **Rule 49.2**, but you cannot score with a distinct kicking motion. For a deflection the test is **body versus stick**: off a **body**, including the goalie's, it is no goal; off the stick of any player but the goalie, or off your own stick, it is *"a good goal"*. The IIHF (49.2(II), 49.2(III)) and the PWHL (49.2(ii), 49.2(iii)) write the same clauses. **The scoring half does not travel — find out which book your league plays under before you count on that goal.**

Hockey Canada **6.10(b)** says *"Kicking the puck cannot result in a goal"*, deflection or not; USA Hockey **617(c)(1)** disallows a kicked puck that deflects off any player, and its Casebook asks this exact question at **Rule 627, Situation 4** and answers *"No."* **And the adult rec book most likely to be yours takes it away as well.** CARHA **Rule 69** adds *"This action cannot result in a goal being scored"*, and **60(c)** disallows a kicked puck that *"deflects off any player or stick into the net"* — CARHA-affiliated leagues only. Under those three books, the kick gets the puck back onto your blade; it does not score.

### If the puck pops up, do not grab it

**In short:** Knock it straight down and move it with your stick at once — and in your own crease, do not trap it in your skates.

A puck that pops up off a skate, a stick or the boards invites you to catch it, and catching it is legal. **Keeping hold of it is not** — and doing it as a skater in your own crease is one of the handful of infractions that hands the other team a penalty shot outright. **The crease is a volume, not a patch of ice**, so a puck you pluck out of the air over your own crease is a puck *in* the crease.

```facts
Never: Keep hold of a puck you have caught — NHL, IIHF, PWHL, Hockey Canada and CARHA print the permission to take it out of the air and USA Hockey's casebook supplies it, but no book lets you keep it
Action: Knock the puck straight down in one continuous motion, then move it with your stick at once — in your own crease, never trap it in your skates
Rule: You may catch the puck out of the air but must immediately place or knock it down to the ice (NHL and IIHF Rule 67.2, PWHL 69.2); Hockey Canada 10.2(a)(iii) and CARHA Rule 61(a) both excuse a catch dropped "without gaining or attempting to gain" an advantage
Rule: USA Hockey's playing rules print no permission to catch the puck at all — its Casebook does, at 618 Situation 1: the catch and the drop "must be virtually one continuous motion", and a stride, a throw or a faked drop stops play
Rule: Holding on to the puck is a minor under NHL and IIHF 67.2, PWHL 69.2, Hockey Canada 10.2(a)(iii) if it gains an advantage and 10.2(a)(iv), with no test, if smothered under you; USA Hockey 618(a) stops play for a caught puck held, but picking one up, or covering one by hand (614(a)), is a minor
Rule: The crease is a volume — all six books run it from the ice to the top of the goal frame (NHL, IIHF and PWHL 1.7, USA Hockey's Notes to 618(a) and 625(b), Hockey Canada 1.5(c), CARHA Rule 5(c)), so a puck caught in the air below the crossbar is in the crease and one above the crossbar is not
Rule: A skater who falls on, holds or covers the puck in their own crease concedes a penalty shot in all six books — NHL 67.4, IIHF 63.6, PWHL 69.4, USA Hockey 618(a) and 614(b), CARHA 58(c) and Hockey Canada 4.11(a)(v), 10.2(a)(v), both books needing a deliberate fall; all six exempt the goalie
Rule: A penalty shot turns on where the puck is, not where you are, in NHL and IIHF 63.6, PWHL 65.6, CARHA 58(c), 61(c) and USA Hockey's Casebook (614 Situations 1, 4); under Hockey Canada a crease cover is the shot with the puck in it (4.11(a)(v)), possibly with you in it (10.2(a)(v)) — treat it as one
Rule: A skater who covers the puck in their own crease with their goalie pulled concedes a goal awarded, not a penalty shot (NHL and IIHF 67.5, PWHL 69.5, Hockey Canada 10.2(a)(vi), CARHA 58(c) Note 1) — USA Hockey 614(b) awards it only where it prevented a goal
Rule: Under USA Hockey the crease penalty shot comes with an optional minor penalty as the alternative, and Rule 406(a) gives that choice to the non-offending team, not to you
Rule: Knocking the puck down to a teammate is a hand pass, allowed only inside your own defending zone, and the zone is set by where the puck is rather than where the players are (NHL Rule 79.2)
```

**What you are allowed to do.** **NHL Rule 67.2** lets you *"catch the puck out of the air but [you] must immediately place it or knock it down to the ice."* The IIHF (67.2) and PWHL (69.2) print the same sentence. **Hockey Canada** reaches the same place from the other end: closing your hand on the puck is a minor at **10.2(a)(iii)** only if it gains an advantage, and it excuses a player who catches the puck and *"immediately drops it to the ice without gaining or attempting to gain an advantage."* **CARHA Rule 61(a)** says the same. ⚠️ **USA Hockey's playing rules print no permission at all** — it lives in the Casebook, and it is strict. **Situation 1 to Rule 618** says the catch and the drop *"must be virtually one continuous motion"*; hold it, take a stride with it, throw it away from your stick or fake the drop, and *"play shall be stopped and a last play face-off occurs."*

**What it costs if you hold on.** Under the **NHL, IIHF and PWHL**, closing your hand on the puck is a minor in three cases (NHL and IIHF 67.2, PWHL 69.2): you catch it and skate with it to avoid a check or gain territory, you cover it on the ice to hide it, or you pick it up off the ice while play is in progress. ⚠️ **USA Hockey divides those differently, so settle which book your league runs before you rely on either — this document has no count of how many leagues run USA Hockey's.** Under **618(a)** a caught puck not dropped at once is a stoppage, not a penalty, but picking the puck up off the ice during play is a delay-of-game minor. **Do not cover a loose puck with your hand either:** **614(a)**'s Note says *"the use of hands to make the puck unplayable should be penalized promptly"*, and **614 Situation 1** gives a minor for it outside the crease. **Smothering a loose puck under your body is a minor in every book here** — NHL and IIHF 63.2(v), PWHL 65.2(v), USA Hockey 614(a), CARHA 58(a), and Hockey Canada **10.2(a)(iv)**, which has no advantage test at all — and Hockey Canada's **10.2(a)(v)** escalates both its limbs inside the crease.

**In your own crease it is far worse, and this half is in all six books.** A skater who holds, covers or picks up the puck in their own crease concedes a ⚠️ **penalty shot**. **In five of the six books it is the puck that has to be in the crease, not you.** NHL **Rule 63.6**: *"If the puck is in the crease, penalty shot. If the puck is outside the crease and gathered into the body of a player (other than the goalkeeper) who is inside the crease, minor penalty."* The IIHF (63.6) and PWHL (65.6) print the same test, CARHA's **58(c)** and **61(c)** locate the puck in the crease in their own text, and USA Hockey's **614(b)** fixes the moment: *"the decision as to whether the puck is in the crease is made at the moment the infraction occurs."* USA Hockey's Casebook works the test both ways round at **614 Situations 1 and 4**.

**Hockey Canada writes it both ways round, so play to the strict answer.** Its **4.11(a)(v)** needs the puck in the crease, but **10.2(a)(v)** awards the shot when the infraction *"occurs inside the offending team's goal crease"*, which can be read to reach you standing in the crease with the puck just outside it; its **Interpretation 5** leans the other way without settling it. **So under Hockey Canada, treat a cover made while you stand in your crease as the penalty shot, and with your goalie pulled as a possible goal, since 10.2(a)(vi) uses the same words.**

One more USA Hockey limb is worth knowing. ⚠️ **Its Casebook at Rule 614, Situation 4 says:** *"If the player bats the puck out of the crease and into their body, only a minor penalty would be assessed to the player so doing."* **That is not a technique to practise** — the swipe that misses leaves you on the ice with the puck loose in your own paint — but it is why a low jab at a puck you cannot safely cover beats a hand on it.

**And the crease is** ⚠️ **a volume, not a patch of ice — which is the half of this that catches players out, because the whole section is about a puck in the air.** All six books run the crease from the ice to the top of the goal frame — NHL, IIHF and PWHL **1.7**, Hockey Canada **1.5(c)**, CARHA **5(c)**, and USA Hockey in a Note to **618(a)** itself: *"For the purpose of this rule, the goal crease shall extend from the ice to the top of the crossbar of the goal frame."* So a puck you pluck out of the air over your own crease, below the crossbar, is a puck **in** the crease, and holding on to it there is the penalty shot. [Rink Map](../foundation/rink_map.md) owns the crease's construction and the small difference in how the books word its height.


![](diagram:the-puck-decides-not-you)

**The crease's vertical ceiling is real, though, and it is the crossbar.** The IIHF's Situation Handbook works both sides of that line. At **Situation 67.6** a defender standing in his own crease, goalie pulled, *"grabs the puck out of mid-air (around the height of the waist) and drops the puck outside the crease"* — the answer is *"an Awarded Goal to Team B"*. **Situations 63.15 and 67.4** make a puck taken knee high in the crease a penalty shot. At **Situation 63.27** the catch is *"(above the crossbar)"*, and *"only a minor penalty is warranted on this play."* ⚠️ **Take those as worked applications and not as rule text** — the Handbook's own Foreword says *"in case of any contradictions, the valid IIHF Rulebook is always the basis."*

USA Hockey answers it differently. ⚠️ **Do not carry the IIHF's answers straight into a USA Hockey game.** Its Casebook answers the standing-in-the-crease catch at **614 Situation 3** with *"[e]ither a penalty shot or awarded goal"* — the goal only because that Situation's goalie *"has been removed"* and the cover prevented an obvious goal — and then adds the escape this section is built on: *"if the puck is caught and dropped to the ice immediately, no penalty shall be assessed as no stoppage of play has been caused (Rule 618(a))."* That escape carries no crease condition, so the catch-and-drop works wherever you stand.

**Three things qualify the penalty shot for holding the puck in your own crease.** First, **all six books exempt the goalie** — a goalie covering the puck in their own crease is doing their job. Second, if your goalie has been pulled for an extra attacker, the same infraction is an ⚠️ **awarded goal** rather than a penalty shot — NHL and IIHF **67.5**, PWHL **69.5**, Hockey Canada **10.2(a)(vi)** — though USA Hockey awards it under **618(a)** and **614(b)** only where the action *"has prevented an obvious and imminent goal"*. ⚠️ **CARHA writes it both ways, so play to the strict answer: with your net empty, any cover in your own crease is the goal.** Note 1 to 58(c) says flatly *"[i]f the goalkeeper has been removed from the ice, then a goal is awarded"*, then points to **Rule 36(b)**, which asks for a shot and a cover *"thereby preventing an obvious and imminent goal"*. Third, under USA Hockey the award is a penalty shot or an optional minor, and **Rule 406(a)** gives that choice to the other team. You do not get to choose the cheaper one.

**And knocking the puck down to a teammate is a hand pass**, legal only inside your own defending zone — and again **where the puck is decides, not where the players are** (NHL **Rule 79.2**). See [Passing and Receiving](passing_and_receiving.md).

### The toe drag

```facts
Technique: Push the puck out on the forehand to offer it, roll the toe over its far side, drag it back toward your feet
Technique: Loosen the top hand as you roll — the stick is held almost in the fingertips
Key: Re-tightening and rolling the blade back to catch it is the hard part, and where the move is usually lost
Goal: The toe drag is a protection move as much as a beat-a-guy move — a failed one leaves the puck in your skates
Read: Use it against a reaching stick, a defender with their weight forward, or to pull the puck around net-front traffic
Never: Use the toe drag in a game until you can do it cleanly several times in a row in practice
```

The **toe drag** is a pull using the very tip (toe) of the blade to draw the puck back toward your feet, usually after pushing it out to bait a stick.

**How it works:**

1. Push the puck out on your forehand, offering it.
2. Loosen the top hand and roll the blade over the puck so the toe is on the far side of it. At this moment the stick is held almost in the fingertips.
3. Drag the puck back toward your body with the toe.
4. Re-tighten the grip and roll the blade back to catch it — **this is the hard part**, and it is where the move is usually lost.

**Why it is a protection move as much as a beat-a-guy move.** Dragging the puck *toward your feet* moves it into the safest place you have. Even a failed toe drag often ends with the puck in your skates, where you can kick it back to your stick. That is a much better failure state than most dekes.

**When to use it:** against a stick that is reaching, against a defender who has committed their weight forward, and in front of the net to pull the puck around traffic before you shoot. Do not use it in a game until you can do it cleanly several times in a row in practice.

## Puck Protection

Protection is where possession is actually won and lost. Dekes decide a handful of plays a game; protection decides dozens. **Nothing this document draws on counts either of those**, so read it as why coaches rank protection above hands rather than as a measurement.

The whole idea in one sentence: **put your body between the checker and the puck, get low and wide so you cannot be moved, and know where the puck is going before you get there.**

### Puck on the far side

```facts
Position: Puck on the side of your body away from the pressure, with your torso as the wall between them
Never: Let the puck sit on the pressure side — their stick has a shorter distance to travel than yours
Technique: Handle with the outside hand where you can, out toward the toe of the blade, away from the poke-check lane
Position: Keep your blade on the far side of the puck too, so a poke has to go through the puck and your stick
Key: A far-side puck is only available to a player whose hands can operate wide
```

The foundation. The puck lives on the **side of your body away from the pressure**. If the checker is on your right, the puck is on your left, and your torso is the wall between them.

- If the puck is on the same side as the pressure, you have already lost it — you are relying on your stick to beat their stick, and their stick has a shorter distance to travel.
- Handle it with the outside hand where you can, out toward the toe of the blade, away from the poke-check lane.
- The stick should be on the far side of the puck too, so that a poke has to go through the puck *and* your blade.

![](diagram:puck-on-the-far-side)

This is why hands-away-from-the-body matters so much. A far-side puck is only available to a player whose hands can operate wide.

### Wide, low base

```facts
Position: Feet wider than shoulder width, knees bent and hips sunk, chest up so you can still see
Never: Stand tall and narrow — it is the reason players get knocked off pucks
Key: Your base is the variable you actually control — feet wide, knees bent, hips sunk
Convention: Leverage often beats strength in a board battle — but not always, and not against a large enough strength gap. Coaching consensus, not a measured law: nothing found in the ice hockey biomechanics literature quantifies it
Action: Keep the feet moving with small choppy steps — a stationary player gets pinned
```

Standing tall and narrow is the reason players get knocked off pucks. Get:

- **feet wider than shoulder width**,
- **knees bent, hips sunk**, weight low,
- **chest up** so you can still see.

Low and wide is hard to move, because leverage often beats strength in a board battle — a stronger player standing upright will frequently lose to a weaker player who is properly down in their base. Not always, and a large enough strength gap wins anyway; the point is that base is the variable you actually control. **It is coaching consensus, not a measured law** — nothing found in the ice hockey biomechanics literature quantifies it, and [Body Contact and Battles](body_contact_and_battles.md) sets out the nearest evidence, from rugby scrummaging.

**Keep your feet moving.** A stationary player gets pinned; a player with momentum can escape along the wall or spin out to the middle. Small choppy steps, not a planted stance.

### Side-on into the checker

**In short:** Where checking is allowed, turn side-on, hip and backside into the pressure, puck on your far side; never turn your back or duck.

```facts
Position: Side-on, outside of your hip and backside into the pressure, not your chest
Key: Your hip and rear end are the strongest, widest surface you own, and cannot be stripped through the front
Goal: Put shoulder, arm, hip and leg on the pressure side at once — three or four barriers instead of one
Never: Turn to face pressure the instant you receive the puck — that is how players get pinned to the boards
Action: With a checker on you in a low-speed battle, go side-on, hip and backside to them, puck on your far side; absorb the contact and spin off — in a non-checking game, hold position; under CARHA, avert it
Priority: Never turn your back to the checker by facing the wall with them behind you, and never duck — no battle outranks either. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the wall, head up and chin off your chest
Risk: USA Hockey warns your checker: "Even a light hit from behind could inflict a severe head or neck injury". Ducking puts your head into the boards first, and your neck can break at walking speed
Action: Already facing the wall and unable to turn — spread the load, stick and gloves up on the glass, arms out, no head contact at all — damage limitation, not a position to aim for
Rule: In a CARHA-affiliated adult league, shield with position and feet and avert the contact — its Rule 49(a) prices intentionally bodying or pushing an opponent, or not averting body contact, at a minor or a major, mandatory if intentional contact injures, and a major ejects you (30(a))
Rule: In Hockey Canada's non-checking divisions — U13 and below, female hockey, and any other division a Member approves — 7.3(a) makes intentionally bumping, shoving or pushing an opponent a minor; 7.3(b) may make it a major plus game misconduct if violent, and must if it injures
Rule: In USA Hockey non-checking games (12U and below, girls' and women's, non-check adult), hold position, never drive your hip in: overt hip, shoulder, forearm or torso action is a minor (604(c)); if reckless, a major and game misconduct (604(d)) or a match (604(e)) — plan on a match (602(a))
```

Turn side-on so your **backside and the outside of your hip** are into the pressure, not your chest. Reasons:

- Your hip and rear end are the strongest, widest surface you own.
- Turned side-on, you cannot be stripped through the front.
- It puts your shoulder, arm, hip and leg all on the pressure side simultaneously — three or four barriers instead of one.

Turning to face pressure the instant you receive the puck is how players get pinned to the boards. Take the puck side-on — hip and backside to the checker, puck on your far side — absorb the contact, *then* spin off. That is for a game with checking: in a non-checking game, hold your position and let them come to you rather than lean in, and in a CARHA adult league, avert the contact rather than absorb it.

**Where this stops being puck protection and becomes a safety problem.** Everything above is about a checker who is already on you in a low-speed battle, **not** a puck race to the wall with a checker arriving at speed. But this override applies at every speed, and it beats everything here: **never turn your back to the checker — never face the wall with them behind you — and never duck.** Get your **skates parallel to the wall**, **forearm and hip against it**, head up and chin off your chest — **never the point of your shoulder, and not the whole shoulder if you can help it.**

If you are already facing the wall and cannot turn, that is damage limitation, not a position to aim for: USA Hockey says to *"distribute the force of the impact over as wide a surface as possible, including putting your stick and gloves on the glass to cushion the blow"*, arms out as a shock absorber, with no head contact at all.

**The two prohibitions guard against two different injuries.** A turned back makes the collision a hit from behind, which you cannot see or brace for — USA Hockey warns that *"Even a light hit from behind could inflict a severe head or neck injury."* Ducking is the mechanism USA Hockey's *Heads Up Hockey* guide puts behind almost all on-ice cervical spine injuries in hockey: a chin tucked to the chest straightens the cervical spine, and of a head-first collision with the boards or a goal post in that position it says a player *"doesn't have to be going at full speed for this to happen — it can occur at walking speed."* That finding is about the **injury** from a head-down collision, not about the turned back, and USA Hockey credits it to research by Charles H. Tator and colleagues. Both prohibitions are absolute, and puck protection is never worth either trade.

**On the shoulder, USA Hockey's wording varies.** It rules the tip out flatly — *"Never hit the boards or glass with the tip of your shoulder – it can cause injury"* — but asks only for *"No shoulder contact on the boards, if possible"*. **So the tip never, and not the whole shoulder if you can help it — USA Hockey's own hedge, not a softening introduced here.** [Body Contact and Battles](body_contact_and_battles.md) sets out its three sentences in full.

**What it costs.** **In a CARHA-affiliated adult league, shield the puck with position and feet, and avert the contact — never lean or back into the checker.** That book's Rule 49(a) prices a player who *"intentionally bodies, pushes, shoves, stands in front of an opponent for the purpose of making contact, and/or does not avert body contact with an opponent"* at a minor or a major, and the major is mandatory when intentional body contact injures — under its Rule 30(a) a major ends your game. If the infraction puts them into the boards, 49(b) doubles the minor, and that double minor counts as two of the three penalties that eject you under Rule 32(a).

**The same goes in Hockey Canada's non-checking divisions — U13 and below, female hockey, and any other division a Hockey Canada Member approves.** Its Rule 7.3(a) gives a minor to a player who *"intentionally body-checks, bumps, shoves, or pushes any opposing player"* there, and 7.3(b) makes it a major plus a game misconduct if the opponent is injured, or at the referee's discretion for a violent one, and 7.3(c) makes a deliberate attempt to injure, or a deliberate injury, by body checking a match penalty. Contact the referee judges *"incidental"* draws no penalty.

**In USA Hockey's non-checking games — 12U and below, every girls' and women's classification, and non-check adult — hold your position with your hip, but do not drive it into them.** USA Hockey's Rule 604 defines an illegal body check as *"intentional physical contact with the opponent using overt hip, shoulder, forearm or torso action"*, prohibits it at those levels under 604(a), and makes it a minor under 604(c). If it recklessly endangers your opponent, 604(d) makes it a major plus a game misconduct, and 604(e) adds that *"[a] match penalty for reckless endangerment may also be assessed for body checking"* at those levels — so plan on a match on top: USA Hockey's general Rule 602(a) says one *"shall be assessed"* to any player who *"recklessly endangers or attempts to injure any opposing player"*. The Note to 604(c) keeps the contact that comes from *"simply maintaining established body position"* — so holding a position you already have with your hip is fine, and driving it into them is not.

### The free hand and the arm bar — what is actually legal

**In short:** Brace with your free hand high on your own stick; never grab an opponent, their stick or their cage.

```facts
Rule: The free hand may fend off an opponent or their stick but never hold either — NHL and IIHF 56.1 and PWHL 57.1; USA Hockey's Rule 622 is silent and its Casebook writes the permission instead, on conditions; Hockey Canada 8.1 permits blocking a stick but not holding it; CARHA writes no permission
Rule: USA Hockey writes the fend-off permission in its Casebook only, and conditions it — control the puck (Standard of Play Situation 6) or be reaching for it, engaged in a corner or in front of the goal (Situation 7), hold a position you "legally established", and never push off to create space
Rule: Holding starts as a minor everywhere, stays one in NHL and IIHF 54 and PWHL 55; injury makes it an ejecting major in Hockey Canada 8.1(b) and CARHA 63(b), and CARHA 63(a) lets the referee give one anyway (30(a)); USA Hockey 622(b) ejects for grabbing a facemask, a match if reckless (602(a); 622(c))
Rule: Keep the free hand off an opponent’s cage — every book here prices grabbing it and four put it above a minor: Hockey Canada 7.1 a match if violent or used for advantage, to punish or injure; CARHA 48(d) if so used; PWHL 51.3 with 51.5; USA Hockey 622(b) and (c); NHL and IIHF 75.2 the lenient pair
Rule: In CARHA-affiliated adult leagues, 63(a) leaves a major to the referee for any hold — wider than Hockey Canada 8.1(b), whose major needs an injury — and 63(b) makes it mandatory if it injures; a major ejects you (30(a)) and brings at least a one-game suspension (32(d))
Rule: NHL 21.1 reaches a match penalty for deliberately injuring or trying to injure an opponent "in any manner"; IIHF Rule 21 is a different rule — Dangerous Actions, a major plus game misconduct, confined to conduct "not covered by the playing rules"
Rule: You may use your arm in a strength move to block, with body position and no holding or cross-check — NHL and IIHF 54.2, PWHL 55.2, and Hockey Canada 8.1 — but 7.3 bars a push in its non-checking divisions; not USA Hockey, whose 622 makes a free arm that restrains or impedes holding, nor CARHA
Technique: Keep the free hand high on your own shaft and use the bar of forearm and shaft as a brace, never a push — driven into an opponent it is a cross-check, and CARHA's definition needs no force
Never: Hang onto an opponent's stick after fending it off
Key: The infraction is holding, not interference — interference concerns a player who does not have the puck
Rule: The IIHF's free-hand wording is substantively identical to the NHL's; USA Hockey's Rule 622 names using a free hand to clutch, grab or hold the stick as holding and writes no fend-off permission — but its Casebook does, for the opponent's body, never their stick
```

**The play: when a checker is on your hip or a stick is across your hands, keep the free hand on your own stick, high on the shaft, and use the bar of forearm and shaft as your brace.** It is the safest habit. You get the same physical benefit with none of the officiating risk, and two hands are ready to move the puck the instant the pressure releases.

**The free hand may fend off an opponent or their stick, and may never hold either.** The legal moves and the penalised versions of them, side by side:

| The move | Legal | Penalty |
|---|---|---|
| **The free hand on your own stick** | Free hand up on your **own** shaft, using the stick and forearm as a bar — braced, not driven | Grabbing, tugging or pulling the opponent or their stick — **holding**, at least a two-minute minor |
| **The stiff arm as a block** | A stiff arm across as a **block**, when you have body position — **NHL and IIHF 54.2, PWHL 55.2, Hockey Canada 8.1 — but 7.3 bars a push in its non-checking divisions; not USA Hockey or CARHA** | Hooking your arm around them, or clamping onto their stick |
| **Fending off their stick** | Momentarily fending it off with the free hand — **NHL and IIHF 56.1, PWHL 57.1, and Hockey Canada 8.1**, which permits blocking a stick with the hands | Hanging onto the stick after fending it off |
| **The bar as a brace** | Leaning the forearm-and-shaft bar in to hold the ground you have — in a Hockey Canada or USA Hockey non-checking game hold it there rather than lean; under CARHA keep it off them | Driving the shaft between your two hands into them — **a cross-check**; CARHA requires no force, and Hockey Canada leaves the push-or-strike test to the referee |

**The bar is a brace, not a shove — and that is where this move ends and a cross-check starts.** Lean it in to hold the ground you have; drive it into them and ⚠️ **the shaft between your two hands has checked an opponent — a cross-check in every book here.**

**What the NHL rulebook says (2025-26):**

- **Rule 56.1, "Free Hand":** *"When a free hand is used to hold, pull, tug, grab or physically restrain an opponent from moving freely, this must be penalized as holding. The free hand may be used by a player to 'fend off' an opponent or his stick, but may not be used to hold an opponent's stick or body."*
- **Rule 54.2, Holding:** *"A player is permitted to use his arm in a strength move, by blocking his opponent, provided he has body position and is not using his hands in a holding manner, when doing so."*

The infraction here is **holding**, not interference — interference concerns a player who does *not* have the puck, which is not you when you are carrying it. Some coaches say "that's interference"; the correct word is holding.

**The IIHF's definition is substantively identical to the NHL's** — same test, same permitted use: *"The 'free hand' may be used by a Player to 'fend off' an opponent or their stick but may not be used to hold an opponent's stick or body."* **The PWHL prints the same sentences at its Rule 57.1.** ⚠️ **USA Hockey reaches the same prohibition and writes its permission somewhere else.** Its **Rule 622 – Holding an Opponent** makes it a minor to use *"a free hand to clutch, grab or hold the stick, jersey or body on the opponent"*, and carries no fend-off permission. Its **Casebook** grants one, on conditions (Standard of Play, **Situations 6 and 7**): you must be in control of the puck, or reaching for it while physically engaged *"in the corner or in front of the goal"*; you may only *"maintain body position that they legally established"*; and *"if they grab the stick, sweater or arm of the opponent, or use the free arm to push off and create space, a penalty should be assessed."* It covers fending off **the opponent, not their stick** — Rule 622 still makes a hand on their stick holding. Under USA Hockey, the brace on your own stick is the habit that needs no conditions met.

**Fending off an opponent's stick is permitted by the NHL, the IIHF, the PWHL and Hockey Canada,** ⚠️ **and USA Hockey's version of that permission is narrower** — it covers the opponent, not their stick, and only on the conditions set out below. **CARHA writes no such permission**: its glossary defines holding as any action *"that impedes the progress of an opposing player whether or not in possession of the puck"*, so in a CARHA-affiliated adult league keep the free hand on your own stick.

The NHL, the IIHF and the PWHL put force in the definition — NHL **Rule 59.1** reads *"forcefully check an opponent"*, and IIHF 59.1 and PWHL 60.1 carry the same phrase — as does USA Hockey's Note to Rule 609. ⚠️ **Hockey Canada and CARHA write no force into their cross-checking definitions, so the margin is thinner under them.** Hockey Canada's Rule 9.2 reads simply *"when a player uses the shaft of the stick, between their two hands, to check an opponent"*, though its Interpretation 1 to Rule 9.2(a) hands the force test back to the referee: *"[i]f a pushing motion is used, a penalty may not be necessary. However, a striking motion must be penalized."* CARHA's definition reaches a check *"at any height"* with no such note. [Body Contact and Battles](body_contact_and_battles.md#the-cross-check-problem) sets out what a cross-check costs.

**What it costs.** **Holding is not always a minor.** ⚠️ **Hockey Canada 8.1(b) makes a major plus a game misconduct mandatory where holding injures an opponent.** CARHA goes further: its **63(a)** leaves a major to the referee for holding *"with the hands, stick or in any other manner"*, injury or not, **63(b)** makes one mandatory where it injures, and under **30(a)** a major means the rest of the game (CARHA-affiliated adult leagues only).

**The cage is priced far above ordinary holding — keep the free hand off it.** ⚠️ **Every book here prices grabbing it, and four of the six put it above a minor.** **USA Hockey 622(b)** makes a major plus a game misconduct mandatory for a player who *"uses their hand to rub, grab or hold the facemask of an opponent"*, and **622(c)** adds that a match penalty for reckless endangerment may also be assessed — plan on it where the grab is reckless, because **Rule 602(a)** says one *"shall be assessed"* to any player who *"recklessly endangers or attempts to injure"* an opponent. The **PWHL's 51.3** makes even a grab *"with a minimal degree of violence"* a major, and **51.5** adds a game misconduct, so the mildest grab still ejects you.

**Hockey Canada 7.1** starts at a double minor, leaves a major and game misconduct to the referee by degree of violence (compulsory if it injures), and makes it a match penalty where the grab is violent or used *"to gain an advantage or to inflict punishment or injury"*. **CARHA 48(d)** is a double minor at its floor and a match penalty where the grab is used *"as an advantage or to inflict punishment or injury"* (CARHA-affiliated adult leagues). **The NHL and the IIHF are the lenient pair** — an unsportsmanlike-conduct minor at **75.2(ii)** for *"grabbing hold of a face mask"*, though the NHL allows Rule 21 – Match Penalties *"specifically when injury results"*, and the IIHF its own Rule 21 – Dangerous Actions where the offence is *"serious and severe, based on the degree of force"*.

**Hockey Canada writes the permission too, in other words** — *"fend"* and *"free hand"* appear nowhere in that book, but **Rule 8.1** reads *"While players are permitted to block an opponent's stick with their hands, arms, or body, they are not permitted to hold an opponent's stick."* Its strength-move sentence matches NHL and IIHF 54.2. **In Hockey Canada's non-checking divisions — U13 and below, female hockey and any other division a Member approves — 7.3 penalises any intentional push, so keep the arm as a brace there rather than extend it.**

### Spinning off pressure

```facts
Key: Protection buys time, not a place to live — once it has bought you that time, get out
Technique: From side-on with your weight low, spin away from the checker, pushing off the outside edge of the outside skate
Technique: The outside skate is the one further from the side you are turning toward; its outside edge is the little-toe side
Options: Or take a tight turn across their path, so your body stays between them and the puck throughout
Read: Spin the moment you feel them lean in — spin before they commit and they simply follow you
Key: The spin-off and the tight turn are both edge-work moves before they are hand moves
```

Protection is a way to buy time, not a place to live. Once it has bought you that time, get out.

- **The spin-off / pivot.** From side-on, hip into the checker and weight low, rotate away from them and take the puck with you. The push comes off the **outside edge of the outside skate** — for **skates and edges**, "outside" is defined relative to the *turn* rather than to the checker or the boards ("outside **shoulder**", later in this document, is the different, boards-relative sense): as in [Skating](skating.md#the-two-foot-hockey-stop), the **outside skate is the one further from the side you are turning toward**, and its **outside edge** is the little-toe side. Turning left, that is the little-toe side of your right blade, and driving against it throws your body left. Their momentum is going into where you were; you are already leaving.
- **The tight turn.** Cut across the checker's path rather than away from it, so that your body stays between them and the puck through the whole turn.
- **Time it against their commitment.** Spin the moment you feel them lean in. If you spin before they commit, they simply follow you.

Both moves are edge-work moves before they are hand moves. See [Skating](skating.md).

### Using the boards

**In short:** Put hip and forearm to the wall, keep your feet moving toward an escape, and never turn your back or duck.

```facts
Position: Hip and forearm against the wall, so the checker can only come from one direction
Never: Take a check on your back at the wall, and never duck to avoid one. Get your skates parallel to the wall instead, forearm and hip against it, head up and chin off your chest
Technique: Forearm and hip meet the wall, not the shoulder — never the point of your shoulder, and not the whole shoulder if you can help it
Never: Get square to the boards with your feet planted — that is how you get pinned and lose it to the second forechecker
Action: Angle yourself so you always have an escape along the wall, and keep the feet moving so you can take it
Options: The wall is an outlet too — a rim onward around the boards or a reverse back against the flow both win the battle
Rule: Being pinned on the wall is not always your own error — USA Hockey Rule 622's Note makes it holding to wrap "one or both arms around the opponent along the boards in a manner that pins them against the boards", a minor under 622(a)
Rule: Women's hockey under the IIHF bars pinning along the boards in terms, as an illegal hit rather than as holding — Rule 101.1 stops players competing for the puck using the boards to "push her into the boards, or pin her along the boards", a minor or a major plus an automatic game misconduct
```

The boards are a free extra defender, on your side.

- Put your **hip and forearm** against the wall and the checker can only come from one direction. You have halved the problem. **Never turn your back to the checker, and never duck.** The override earlier in this section governs here too: skates parallel to the wall, forearm and hip against it — **not the point of your shoulder, and not the whole shoulder if you can help it** — head up and chin off your chest ([Body Contact and Battles](body_contact_and_battles.md), which covers this).
- **Do not get square to the boards and stop.** Facing directly into the wall with your feet planted is how you get pinned and lose the puck to the second forechecker.
- Angle yourself so you always have an escape along the wall, and keep the feet moving so you can take it.
- Remember the wall is also an outlet, not just a shield: a **rim** (a hard puck sent onward around the curve of the boards) or a **reverse** (a puck sent back against the direction the pressure is flowing) both count as winning the battle. See [Breakouts](../systems/breakouts.md).

**And being pinned there is not automatically your own error.** The opponent has a limit too. Of the six books, two name pinning on the boards: **USA Hockey**, for every player — its Note to **Rule 622** makes it holding, a minor, to wrap *"one or both arms around the opponent along the boards in a manner that pins them against the boards and prevents them from playing the puck or skating"* — and ⚠️ **the IIHF, for women's hockey only**: its **Rule 101.1** bars players competing for the puck from using the boards to *"pin her along the boards"*, a minor, or a major plus an automatic game misconduct. The IIHF's holding rule says nothing about the boards. ⚠️ **Do not read that across to the PWHL, which is women's hockey and does not write it** — its boarding rule, **41.1**, needs the opponent to *"hit or impact the boards violently or dangerously"*, which a slow pin on the wall does not reach. The NHL, IIHF, PWHL, Hockey Canada and CARHA all reach a pin through their general holding words — restraining or impeding an opponent, holding *"in any manner"* — so the act can be called in all six books. Play as though the call may not come.

### Know your out before you get there

```facts
Key: Protection is not a destination — every second you hold the puck, a second checker is arriving
Never: Plan to shield it until something happens — what happens is a turnover
Action: Decide your outlet while still skating to the puck — usually the low support player or the next man arriving
Options: With nobody there, your out is a rim, a reverse, a spin to the middle, or a chip off the wall past the checker
Read: Know where the pressure is coming from, and therefore which side the puck must be on when you arrive
Convention: How long you are expected to hold the puck on the wall is a system decision, not a law. Ask your coach
```

This is the idea that separates players who protect the puck from players who merely survive contact.

Protection is not a destination. Every second you hold the puck under pressure, a second checker is arriving. If your plan is "shield it until something happens," what happens is a turnover.

**Decide while you are still skating to the puck:**

- Who is my outlet? (Usually the low support player or the next man arriving.)
- If nobody is there, is my out a rim, a reverse, a spin to the middle, or a chip off the wall past the checker?
- Where is the pressure coming from, and therefore which side does the puck need to be on when I arrive?

Answering those three before your stick touches the puck is what [scanning](../hockey-iq/scanning_and_anticipation.md) is for. A player who scans arrives with a plan and holds the puck for half a second. A player who does not arrives with no plan, protects beautifully for three seconds, and loses it.

![](diagram:scan-before-a-wall-reception)

> **This is a good place to name a coaching choice.** How long you are *expected* to hold the puck on the wall is a system decision, not a law. Some teams want a quick chip or rim out immediately to avoid any risk; others want you to hang on, draw two checkers, and wait for support to arrive so the exit is with possession. These are genuinely different jobs. Find out which one your coach wants.

### Protecting it low in the offensive zone

```facts
Action: Below the goal line with a defender on you, keep your feet moving and the puck on your far side — a carrier who stops down low gets pinned
Options: Cut back up the wall you came down — a defender committed toward the corner cannot turn as fast as you
Options: Pass it back and down to the teammate arriving behind you (low-to-low), then move to a new spot rather than watching
Options: Walk out — if the defender turns their back or over-commits to the wall, turn into the gap and take it to the net
Read: Look for a teammate at the net front before any of those — a quick pass there beats all three
Never: Duck when the check comes on the wall down here — take it with skates parallel to the boards, forearm and hip to the wall, head up, chin off your chest
Convention: How long a team keeps the puck low before attacking the net is a system choice. Ask your bench what yours plays
```

Holding the puck down low in the other team's end is protection with a different purpose. In your own end you protect to get the puck out; here you protect to make the defence move, and every second you keep it is a second they spend chasing rather than covering.

- **Keep your feet moving and the puck on your far side.** A carrier who stops behind the net or in the corner gives the defender a stationary target to pin. Moving, you can turn either way.
- **The common approach is to cycle it.** Cut back up the wall you came down — a defender committed toward the corner cannot change direction as fast as you can — or pass it back and down to the teammate arriving behind you — low-to-low — and get away to a new spot. [Offensive Zone Play](../systems/offensive_zone_play.md#4-cycling) covers the cycle in full, including when to rim and when to reverse.
- **The alternative is to walk out.** When the defender turns their back to the play or over-commits to the wall side, turn off the wall into the space they gave up and take it to the net. That is the moment a cycle exists to create, and it does not last.
- **Before any of that, look at the net front.** A teammate there who has beaten their check is a better option than anything you can do on the wall.

It is still a battle on the wall, so the posture rule above holds here unchanged: skates parallel, forearm and hip to the wall, never your back turned to the checker and never your head down. How long your team wants the puck kept low before it goes to the net or the point is a system decision — some teams want it at the net at the first chance, others want the defence worn down first. Ask your bench what yours plays.

## Carrying at Speed

*[Skating](skating.md) covers the stride itself. Read that one for what a good stride is, and this one for what the puck does to it.*

### Why so many players slow down the moment they get the puck

```facts
Never: Decelerate to a comfortable handling speed on receiving the puck — it hands the defence a free two seconds
Read: What degrades when you pick the puck up is your arms driving the stride, your head position, and the width of your feet
Risk: A dropped head pulls your weight forward off the balls of your feet and shortens your extension
Key: Speed is the most valuable thing you have, and good hands are meant to cost you none of it
```

Handling and skating compete for attention, and the stride is what gets dropped. Hence the most common sight in amateur hockey: a player receives a pass in stride, immediately decelerates to a comfortable handling speed, and hands the defence a free two seconds. Specifically, what degrades is that your arms stop driving the stride because they are working the stick, your head drops (which pulls your weight forward off the balls of your feet and shortens your extension), and your feet narrow, which weakens your edges. **Speed is the most valuable thing you have, and the whole point of good hands is that they cost you none of it.**

### Carry, don't dribble, when you are moving

```facts
Never: Stickhandle every step at full stride
Position: Puck slightly ahead of you and slightly to the side, usually on the forehand where your blade sits
Technique: Push the puck out, take two or three hard strides, catch up to it, push it out again
Action: Touch it just enough to keep it honest — a small dribble every few strides so a poke cannot be timed
Key: A few big pushes and powerful strides beat continuous fussy handling every time
```

At full stride you should not be stickhandling every step. Instead:

- **Put the puck slightly ahead of you and slightly to the side**, usually on the forehand, out where your blade naturally sits during a stride. Ahead means you are skating toward it, not dragging it; to the side means it is not in the way of your feet.
- **Take real strides.** Push the puck out, take two or three hard strides, catch up to it, push it out again. A few big pushes and powerful strides will beat continuous fussy handling every time.
- **Touch it just enough to keep it honest** — a small dribble every few strides so a defender cannot time a poke, then back to skating.

### Pushing it into space and skating onto it

```facts
Key: You and the puck do not have to travel together
Action: Push the puck into the space you are going to be in, then skate onto it
Options: Chip it off the boards past a defender, or slip it past their skates and take the other route around
Goal: Beat a stick that is sealing your handling lane — the puck goes past it, and so do you, separately
Key: You do not need hands to beat a defender if the puck can go one side and you can go the other
```

The advanced version, and the one that keeps your speed highest: **you and the puck do not have to travel together.**

- Push the puck into the space you are going to be in, then skate onto it. This is how you beat a stick that is sealing your handling lane — the puck goes past the stick, and so do you, separately.
- Chip it off the boards past a defender and skate around them to collect it. The wall makes the pass for you.
- Slip it between or past a defender's skates and take the other route around them.

This is also the honest answer to a lot of 1-on-1s: you do not need hands to beat the defender if the puck can go one side and you can go the other.

### Head up while carrying

```facts
Key: Carrying is cheap in attention — a stride spent looking at the puck is a stride not spent reading the defence
Risk: If you can only look up when you slow down, you will only make decisions when you are slow
Action: Practise one-handed carries — top hand only for a stride or two, which frees the other arm to drive
Action: Do your crossover, transition and tight-turn drills with a puck at least half the time
Goal: Speed across the blue line forces defenders to retreat; a carrier who decelerates at the line is easy to defend
```

The reason to carry rather than dribble at speed is that carrying is cheap in attention. Every stride you spend looking at the puck is a stride you did not spend reading the defence. If you can only look up when you slow down, you will only make decisions when you are slow.

Two habits that pay here: **practise one-handed carries** — top hand only for a stride or two, which frees the other arm to drive — and the one instruction that belongs on the skating side of the seam, **do your crossover, transition and tight-turn drills with a puck at least half the time** ([Skating](skating.md)), because that is how you will use them. Where all of this matters most is on [zone entries](../systems/zone_entries.md): speed across the blue line forces defenders to retreat, and a carrier who decelerates at the line makes the entry easy to defend.

## Deception and Dekes

### The principle: a deke works on what you show, not what you do

```facts
Key: A deke is a false signal — defenders commit on your hips, shoulders, head, eyes, weight and blade angle
Action: Commit fully — the fake has to look exactly like the real thing right up to the last instant
Never: Fake with your hands alone — a neutral body gives the defender no false information
Action: Accelerate out of it — the move creates the gap, your legs collect it
```

A defender cannot wait to see what you actually do — by then it is too late for them. So they read your **signals**: your hips, your shoulders, your head, your eyes, your weight, your blade angle. They commit based on those signals.

That is the whole mechanism. **A deke is a false signal.** You show one thing, they commit to it, and you do the other. A defender who commits the wrong way is already beaten, and everything after that is just skating.

Three consequences follow, and they matter more than any individual move:

1. **Commitment sells it.** A half-hearted head turn or a lazy shoulder dip fools nobody. The fake has to look exactly like the real thing right up to the last instant. If you would not have believed it, neither will they.
2. **A fake with only your hands is not a fake.** Moving the puck left while your body stays neutral gives the defender no false information — they read your body, and your body said nothing. Your head, shoulders, hips and feet have to join in.
3. **You must accelerate out of it.** A deke that is not followed by a burst just gives the defender time to recover. The move creates the gap; your legs collect it.

### The four channels of deception

```facts
Options: Head and eyes — a sharp, committed head turn, or a look-off at an option you will not use
Options: Shoulders and hips — a weight shift or shoulder dip, the most convincing because they carry real weight
Options: Stick and puck — blade angle and windup, faking a shot or a pass
Options: Feet and edges — change of pace, stutter steps, a crossover started and abandoned
Key: A change of pace fakes a defender's timing rather than their direction — the half of the job they are least drilled for
```

- **Head and eyes.** A sharp, committed head turn. Look-offs — staring at a passing option you are not going to use.
- **Shoulders and hips.** A weight shift or shoulder dip. These carry real physical weight, so they are the most convincing.
- **Stick and puck.** Blade angle and windup — faking a shot or a pass.
- **Feet and edges.** Change of pace, stutter steps, a crossover started and abandoned. A change of pace fakes a defender's *timing* rather than their direction, which is the half of the job they are least drilled for — see [Change speed, not just direction](#change-speed-not-just-direction).

### The moves

**In short:** Choose the deke that beats what the defender is doing; if you learn only one, learn the head and shoulder fake.

```facts
Options: Fake shot — load exactly as for a real shot, then pull the puck around or pass; only works if your shot is a threat. In a CARHA league load a wrist shot — a slap-shot wind-up can be a penalty there
Options: Toe drag — offer the puck to a reaching stick and pull it back inside the reach, best against an early poker
Options: Inside-out — threaten the middle so they shift to protect it, then cut back to the outside
Options: Outside-in — drive wide so they open their hips to the boards, then cut back underneath into the slot
Options: The slip — push the puck past them on one side, skate the other, and collect it behind them
Risk: Slip forward, not out of trouble — a failed slip in your own zone leaves a loose puck in front of your net
Options: Between the legs — worth it only when your legs are the route to the far side, or the puck must come from behind you
Key: If you learn one move from this section, learn the head and shoulder fake — it costs nothing, it cannot be poke-checked, and it works at every level
```

**The fake shot.** Load into a shooting position — weight transfer, blade drawn back, hands and shoulders exactly where they go for a real shot — then don't shoot. Defenders drop into a block or turn their hips; goalies commit to a save. Then pull the puck around them or pass. It works because the shot is genuinely threatened, so it only works if your actual shot is a threat. **In a CARHA league, sell a wrist or snap shot, never a slap-shot wind-up:** that book makes bringing the stick back *"more than fifteen inches"* behind the puck a slap shot and a minor, and a major if it injures an opponent — a major that throws you out and sits you out at least the next game. A fake slap shot used to intimidate is a minor too. See [Shooting](shooting.md).

**The toe drag.** Covered under [Stickhandling in Tight](#stickhandling-in-tight). As a deke, it works by offering the puck to a reaching stick and pulling it back inside the reach. Best against a defender who pokes early.

**Inside-out.** Attack toward the middle of the ice, get the defender to shift their weight to that side to protect the dangerous area, then cut back to the outside. Effective because defenders are coached to protect the middle first, so the inside threat is always believed.

**Outside-in.** The reverse: drive hard wide as though you are going around them, get them to open their hips and turn toward the boards, then cut back underneath them into the middle. What it buys you is where it finishes — in the slot, rather than out along the wall with the net behind you. Nothing here ranks the moves against each other by the chances they create.

**The slip (or skate-through).** You and the puck take different routes. Push the puck past the defender on one side — through their feet, under their stick, or off the boards — skate around the other side, and pick it up behind them. It defeats a defender who is focused on the puck rather than on you, and it costs you almost no speed. A failed slip usually becomes a loose puck rather than a clean strip — **but where that loose puck ends up is the whole risk assessment, so qualify it by zone.** In the offensive zone or the neutral zone, a loose puck behind a defender is often still a race you can win, and the play is cheap. In **your own zone** it is not cheap at all: a slip that fails there leaves a loose puck in front of your net with you on the wrong side of the defender, which is a scoring chance. Slip forward; do not slip out of trouble.

**Between the legs.** Moving the puck through your **own** legs from front to back or side to side. Genuinely useful in two situations: when a checker is on one side and your legs are the only route to the far side, and when your back is to the pressure and the puck needs to get from behind you to in front. Outside those two cases it is a low-percentage flourish. Judge it honestly: if you would not attempt it in practice with someone checking you, do not attempt it in a game.

**The head and shoulder fake.** Sharp head turn one way, shoulder dips with it, weight visibly shifts — then push off the loaded edge and go the other way. It costs nothing, it cannot be poke-checked, and it works on defenders at every level. If you learn one move from this section, learn this one.

### Over-deking

```facts
Never: Use four fancy fakes where one convincing one would do
Risk: Every extra move is another moment the puck spends exposed and another chance for a stick to arrive
Read: If you find yourself on your third move, the play has already gone wrong
```

More moves is not better. Every extra move is another moment the puck spends exposed and another chance for a stick to arrive. The best deceptive players use one convincing fake and accelerate, not four fancy ones. If you find yourself on your third move, the play has already gone wrong.

## Beating a Defender 1-on-1

### Attack a shoulder, not the chest — and know which shoulder you are choosing

**In short:** Skate at a shoulder, never the chest, so they must turn, then take the lane their turn opens.

```facts
Never: Skate straight at their chest — they stay square, keep both lanes closed and stand you up
Goal: Make them turn — the moment their hips turn, one lane exists that did not before
Options: The outside shoulder — the one nearer the boards — opens the lane back underneath them, but it is where a well-coached defenceman wants you
Options: The inside shoulder forces them to defend the dangerous ice, but you are attacking into help
Read: Outside when you have a speed advantage; inside when you do not, or when they have already shaded you wide
Convention: Which shoulder is not settled coaching doctrine. Ask your coach which they want from your position
```

The one thing every source agrees on is the negative: **do not skate straight at their chest.** A defender you attack head-on stays square, keeps both lanes closed and simply stands you up. Skate at a shoulder, so they have to turn — and the moment their hips turn, one lane exists that did not before.

**Which shoulder is a genuine choice, not a settled rule**, and the two options do different things:

- **Attacking the outside shoulder** (the one nearer the boards) forces them to open their hips toward the wall, which opens the lane back underneath them into the middle. It also puts you on an angle where a change of direction is a small movement for you and a large one for them. The catch is that **this is where a well-coached defenceman wants you.** The standard defensive instruction for a 1-on-1 on the wing is to line up so that *"your outside shoulder should more or less line up with his inside shoulder. This encourages him to try to beat you to the outside, where he's less of a threat"* ([Hockey Player Tips](https://hockeyplayertips.blogspot.com/2008/04/how-to-play-defence-one-onone.html)). Against a defender doing that properly, driving the outside shoulder is taking the option they offered you.
- **Attacking the inside shoulder** — driving at the middle of the ice — forces them to defend the dangerous ice first, which is what they are coached to protect above all else, so the threat is always believed. That is the same mechanism as the **inside-out** move described under [Deception and Dekes](#deception-and-dekes) above. The cost is that you are attacking into help: the middle is where the other defender and the backchecker are.

**How to choose.** Take the outside shoulder when you have a speed advantage and want to beat them wide or cut back underneath. Take the inside shoulder when you do not have the speed to win a wide race, when you have support arriving in the middle, or when the defender has already committed to shading you outside. Either way, the point is to make them turn — and then to take the lane their turn opened, not the one you planned before the rush started.

*Note: neither choice is settled coaching doctrine, and the published material is stronger on the defensive side of this matchup than the attacking side. Ask your coach which they want from your position.*

### Change speed, not just direction

```facts
Key: Defenders defend direction well and timing badly
Action: Slow down to bait, then explode — a defender who has just slowed cannot re-accelerate backwards quickly
Read: Attack while they are transitioning from backwards to forwards, or moving laterally
Never: Use straight-line speed every rush — it leaves the defender only one problem to solve
```

Defenders defend direction well. They are coached to it, they practise it, and their backwards skating is built for it. What they defend badly is **timing**.

- **Slow down to bait, then explode.** A defender maintaining a gap has to match your speed. If you decelerate, they must decelerate or run into you — and a defender who has just slowed down cannot re-accelerate backwards quickly. Go the instant they settle.
- **Attack while they are transitioning.** The moment they pivot from backwards to forwards, or move laterally, they are momentarily unable to change direction again.
- **Vary it.** Straight-line speed on every rush is the easiest thing in the world to defend, because the defender only has to solve one problem.

### Use their momentum

```facts
Key: A defender who has committed weight in one direction cannot recall it
Read: If they lunge or reach with the stick, their weight is forward — go around them, not through them
Read: If they turn their hips to the wall, the middle is open behind them
Action: Against a hard backwards skater, a sudden stop leaves them retreating into space you no longer occupy
```

A defender who has committed weight in one direction cannot recall it. Attack *into* the space their commitment created:

- If they lunge or reach with the stick, their weight is forward — go around them, not through them.
- If they turn their hips to the wall, the middle is open behind them.
- If they are skating backwards hard to maintain gap, a sudden stop leaves them retreating into space you no longer occupy.

### The honest point

```facts
Key: Win 1-on-1s with a change of speed and body position more than with your hands
Action: If you have a step, just skate — skating right at the defenceman to try a move is how you get stood up
Goal: Creating a chance, not beating the defender — a deke leaving you no speed and no angle has achieved nothing
Read: A drive wide that beats nobody but draws two defenders and opens a pass to the middle has achieved a great deal
```

**Win 1-on-1s with a change of speed and body position, not with your hands.**

Watch closely and you will see the same three shapes over and over: a defender flat-footed and beaten wide by pure pace, a defender caught in transition, or a puck chipped into space with the attacker skating around to it. **Nobody has counted how the beats divide between them**, so take that as something to look for rather than a distribution — but notice what all three have in common: not one of them is a defender beaten by stickhandling.

Two practical consequences:

- **If you have a step, just skate.** Coaches see this constantly — a player with a clean breakaway lane who skates *right at* the defenceman to try a move, and gets stood up. If you have speed, use it. (What to do once you are clear and it is only you and the goalie — approach speed, deception, the moves, and shoot-or-deke — is in [Shooting](shooting.md), "Breakaways and Shootouts".)
- **Beating one defender is not the goal; creating a chance is.** A deke that beats the defender but leaves you with no speed and no angle has achieved nothing. A simple drive wide that beats nobody but draws two defenders and opens a pass to the middle has achieved a great deal.

## When *Not* to Stickhandle

Good hands are a tool for when there is no better option. Reaching for them when there *is* a better option is how you lose pucks you never had to risk.

### In your own defensive zone

```facts
Never: Stickhandle in your own defensive zone — a turnover here is a scoring chance immediately, with no recovery time
Action: Get the puck moving — pass it, rim it, reverse it, or put it off the glass and out, never over it
Rule: Over the glass from your own zone is a delay-of-game minor under NHL and IIHF 63.2(iii) and PWHL 65.2(iii), unless deflected or where there is no glass; put out deliberately, it is penalised in every book here
Never: Turn your back to the checker on a rim or a reverse, and never duck — skates parallel to the wall instead, forearm and hip against it, head up and chin off your chest
Key: Nobody has ever been criticised for a clean, boring defensive-zone exit
```

A turnover here becomes a scoring chance immediately, and there is no recovery time. Get the puck moving: pass it, rim it, reverse it, or if nothing is on, put it off the glass and out. **Off it, never over it:** a puck you shoot or bat directly over the glass from your own zone is a delay-of-game minor under the NHL, IIHF and PWHL even when you did not mean it, and one put out of play on purpose is penalised in every book here. Nobody has ever been criticised for a clean, boring defensive-zone exit. ⚠️ **A rim and a reverse are both played on the wall with a forechecker arriving on you, and the posture you take that contact in is not a preference: never turn your back to the checker, and never duck.** Get your **skates parallel to the wall** instead, **forearm and hip against it** — not the point of your shoulder, and not the whole shoulder if you can help it — head up and chin off your chest ([Body Contact and Battles](body_contact_and_battles.md), which covers this). See [Breakouts](../systems/breakouts.md) and [Risk Management](../hockey-iq/risk_management.md).

### Through the middle of the ice

```facts
Never: Dangle through the middle of your own end or the neutral zone — high cost, low reward
Risk: The middle holds the most defenders, a lost puck goes straight to the slot, and no boards help you
Options: Carry through the middle when it is genuinely open — carrying is not stickhandling
```

The middle is where the most defenders are, where a lost puck goes straight to the slot, and where you have no boards to help you. Dangling through the middle of your own end or the neutral zone is a high-cost, low-reward bet. Carry through the middle when it is genuinely open; do not *stickhandle* through it.

### When a simple pass is available

```facts
Never: Dangle when a pass was on — the pass is faster than you can skate, cannot be poked off your blade, and moves the defence further
Key: A pass moves the puck faster than any player can skate and does not expose it to a stick check
Action: Look for the pass first, and only then consider the move
Priority: A pass to a teammate in a worse position usually still beats a move against a defender in a good one
```

**If you break one habit from this section, break this one: dangling when a pass was on.** A pass moves the puck faster than any player can skate, it does not leave the puck sitting on your blade for a defender to poke at, and it moves the defence more than a deke does. (Nothing this document draws on counts how often that choice gets made, so take it as a priority rather than as a statistic.) If a teammate is open, the pass is almost always the better play — and a pass to a teammate in a worse position is still usually better than a move against a defender in a good one.

The habit to build: **look for the pass first, and only then consider the move.** Not the other way around.

### Against multiple defenders

```facts
Never: Try to beat two players — pretending otherwise is how a 2-on-2 becomes a rush the other way
Options: Pass to the teammate the second defender's arrival has left open
Options: Chip the puck into the space behind them
Options: Protect it, take the contact, and wait for support
```

You can beat one player. You cannot beat two, and pretending otherwise is how a 2-on-2 becomes a rush the other way. When a second defender is arriving:

- pass to the teammate their arrival has left open,
- chip the puck into space behind them,
- or protect it, take the contact, and wait for support.


![](diagram:you-cannot-beat-two)

### When you are the last player back

```facts
Never: Try a move when losing the puck means an odd-man rush against you — that prohibition has no exceptions
Convention: How much individual carrying a team wants varies enormously — some coaches want the first available pass, every time
Convention: Some want wingers attacking 1-on-1 on the wall; others want everything chipped behind the defence for a forecheck
Convention: The default assumed here is a possession-oriented breakout with a 2-1-2 forecheck — ask your coach
```

If losing the puck means an odd-man rush against you, the move is off. Always.

![](diagram:the-last-player-back)

> **A coaching choice, named:** how much individual puck carrying a team wants varies enormously. Some coaches want defencemen to skate the puck out of the zone whenever the lane is there; others want the first available pass, every time, with no exceptions. Some want wingers to attack 1-on-1 on the wall; others want everything chipped behind the defence for a forecheck. The default assumption here is a possession-oriented breakout with a **2-1-2 forecheck**, but the honest answer is that this is your coach's call. Ask.

## Handling Bad Pucks and Playing Your Position

Most pucks you receive in a real game are imperfect, and handling them well contributes more to your possession numbers than any deke. **The full treatment of receiving — giving a target, cushioning the puck, killing a bounce, taking a puck out of your feet, and the first touch — belongs to [Passing and Receiving](passing_and_receiving.md), which covers that material.** Read it there rather than here.

The three points that are specifically puck-*control* rather than receiving, and that are worth repeating on this side:

- **A bad puck needs a cupped blade and a soft draw-back — bouncing, on edge or flat, the cup is the same.** It is the *open* blade that a puck ski-jumps over. What changes is the touch count: **let a bouncing or rolling puck settle before you do anything with it** — one extra beat to flatten it is faster than chasing your own deflection, and a puck still standing on its edge will not leave your blade where you aimed it.
- **A puck in your skates is an asset, not a loss.** Trap it against the inside of a skate blade and pull it out to your stick, or kick it forward — though never park it in your own crease — legal in all zones, with the limits on scoring **and the one on contact: at the puck, never at a player**, as set out under [Stickhandling in Tight](#stickhandling-in-tight) above. Practise it off-ice: puck behind you, pull it into your feet, kick it forward, collect it in front. It costs nothing to drill, and it turns a puck you had lost into a puck back on your blade.
- **A backhand puck under pressure is often the *right* puck.** If the checker is on your forehand side, the backhand is the far side, and the far side is where the puck belongs. Cup the backhand as hard as you cup the forehand — an open backhand blade lets the puck roll straight up and over — and remember that backhand control is mostly the same top-hand wrist roll you already trained.

### What each position actually needs

```facts
Key: Every position needs the same underlying handling skills; the situations that demand them differ
Technique: Defencemen — backwards and lateral handling, head-up handling while walking the line laterally along the blue line, retrievals with your back to pressure
Priority: For a defenceman, protection beats deception — beating a forechecker in your own end has a catastrophic failure mode
Technique: Wingers — wall protection above all, coaching craft rather than measurement: the 50-50 puck on the half-wall level with the faceoff dot, carrying at speed, tight hands in the slot
Technique: Centres — hold the puck just long enough to move a defender, then release it; you almost always have options
Never: Dangle as a centre — centres who dangle are usually centres who forgot they had three teammates
```

The underlying skills are the same; the situations that demand them are not, and the full job descriptions live in the position documents.

- **Defencemen** need backwards and lateral handling, tight head-up handling while **walking the line** (skating laterally along the offensive blue line to change the shooting angle and pull the defence out of shape), and above all **retrievals with your back to pressure** — going back for a dumped puck with a **forechecker** (an opponent pressuring you in your own end) bearing down. Protection beats deception here: beating a forechecker 1-on-1 in your own end is a spectacular play with a catastrophic failure mode. See [Defender](../positions/defender.md).
- **Wingers** need wall protection above everything — winning the **50-50 puck** (a loose puck neither team controls, that either could win) on the **half-wall** (the boards level with the faceoff dot, between the corner and the point) is the defining physical skill of the position, **a coaching ranking rather than a measured one** — plus carrying at speed without slowing, the 1-on-1 on the outside, and tight hands in the slot. See [Winger](../positions/winger.md).
- **Centres** need handling *with support around them*: you almost always have options, so the job is to hold the puck just long enough to move a defender and then release it. Centres who dangle are usually centres who forgot they had three teammates. See [Centre](../positions/center.md).

## How to Practise

Puck handling is the one hockey skill you can meaningfully improve without ice. Take the opportunity.

### Kit

```facts
Action: Get a smooth surface first — a shooting pad or plastic tiles; without one the puck will not slide and none of the drills work
Options: A synthetic off-ice puck is the best general-purpose choice, designed to slide on rough surfaces
Options: A golf or tennis ball is free, bouncy, brutal, and builds fast hands
Never: Train exclusively with a weighted puck or stick — you build a stroke that only works with weight
Read: A stick feels long in shoes and a puck feels heavier off-ice, which is why off-ice pucks are slightly lighter
```

Very little: **a smooth surface** (a shooting pad or plastic tiles) and **something to handle**. Get the surface first — without one a puck will not slide and none of the drills below work. That is an ordering *within off-ice handling kit*; for what to buy as a hockey player, and in what order, see [Equipment](../off-the-ice/equipment.md). In rough order of realism: a real puck on a slick pad; a synthetic off-ice puck or biscuit, which is the best general-purpose choice because it is designed to slide on rough surfaces; a weighted stickhandling ball for driveways; or a golf or tennis ball, which is free, bouncy, brutal, and builds fast hands. A weighted puck or stick is optional for strength work — never train exclusively with it, or you build a stroke that only works with weight.

> **Off-ice adjustments.** On skates you are a couple of inches taller, so your stick feels long in shoes, and a standard puck feels heavier off-ice because of the friction — which is why purpose-made off-ice pucks are slightly lighter. See [Equipment](../off-the-ice/equipment.md).

### Little and often beats occasionally and long

```facts
Convention: Ten to fifteen focused minutes every day builds better hands than two hours once a week — one publisher's coaching guidance, not a consensus
Key: No controlled comparison of the two schedules is cited here, and the other stickhandling guides this document draws on prescribe no session length at all
Action: Make it frictionless — leave the pad and a ball where you walk past them
Key: The session you actually do beats the session you planned
```

**Ten to fifteen focused minutes every day builds better hands than two hours once a week.** That is one publisher's coaching guidance (Better Hockey's), not a consensus — the other stickhandling guides here set no session length — and no controlled comparison of the two schedules has been found. It fits how motor skills are generally understood to consolidate, which is why it is worth following. [Skating](skating.md) gives the same advice about edge work, hedged the same way.

Make it frictionless: leave the pad and a ball where you walk past them. The session you actually do beats the session you planned.

### Progressive difficulty

```facts
Action: Climb the ladder rather than repeating the bottom rung
Convention: The order of the rungs is coaching craft — nothing here compares them, and the point is that you keep moving up
Technique: Stationary slow eyes down, then eyes up, then fast eyes up, then moving, then unstable, then constrained
Technique: Then weighted, then back to normal for the contrast, then contested keep-away against another person
Key: Nothing but contested keep-away against another person exposes what you cannot really do
```

Every drill has a ladder. Climb it rather than repeat the bottom rung:

1. **Stationary, slow, eyes down** — learn the shape.
2. **Stationary, slow, eyes up** — learn the feel.
3. **Stationary, fast, eyes up.**
4. **Moving** — walking, then running on the spot, then around the pad.
5. **Unstable** — one leg, a balance board.
6. **Constrained** — through obstacles, inside a taped box, around your own feet.
7. **Weighted**, then back to normal for the contrast.
8. **Contested** — keep-away against another person. Nothing else exposes what you cannot really do.

The order is coaching craft, not a measured progression; what matters is that you keep moving up.

### A starter set of drills

```facts
Action: Soft touch — fast, tiny back-and-forth with minimal arm movement, cupping both ways
Action: Wide reach — puck as far to one side as you can, then the other, releasing the bottom hand to extend
Action: Around the body — front, both sides, behind the heels, then quick diagonal transfers
Action: Figure 8 around two objects two to six feet apart; also upright, to train pushing the puck away and pulling it back
Action: The yo-yo — push out on the forehand, pull back with the toe, push out to the side, pull back again
Action: Human figure 8 — move the puck around and between your own feet
Action: Kick-pass — puck behind you, pull it into your feet, kick it forward, collect it in front
Key: Do every one of those drills with your eyes somewhere else — that is the only version that counts
```

- **Soft touch** — fast, tiny back-and-forth with minimal arm movement, cupping both ways.
- **Wide reach** — puck as far to one side as you can, then the other; release the bottom hand to extend further.
- **Around the body** — front, both sides, behind the heels, then quick diagonal transfers between those zones.
- **Figure 8** — around two objects two to six feet apart; also try it upright (objects in front of you rather than beside) to train moving the puck away and pulling it back in.
- **The yo-yo** — push the puck out on the forehand, pull it back with the toe, push it out to the side, pull it back again. This is the toe drag and the pull-in habit in one drill.
- **Human figure 8** — move the puck around and between your own feet. Trains the in-tight and in-your-feet control described under [Stickhandling in Tight](#stickhandling-in-tight).
- **Kick-pass** — puck behind you, pull it into your feet, kick it forward, collect it in front.

Plus the head-up drills already listed under [Head Up](#head-up) — the mine field, the weave, reading text off a wall, and two-player keep-away. Those are not a separate set; they are these same movements done with your eyes somewhere else, which is the only version that counts.

### Bridge it to the ice

```facts
Key: Off-ice hands are not on-ice hands until you have used them under pressure
Action: In every practice, deliberately spend time doing the same movements on skates
Action: Get into small-area games — keep-away, cross-ice, anything confined
Goal: Small areas force the tight handling and protection that matter, at game speed, against real sticks
```

Off-ice hands are not on-ice hands until you have used them under pressure. In every practice, deliberately spend some time doing the same movements on skates, and get into **small-area games** — keep-away, cross-ice, anything confined. Small areas force the tight handling and protection that matter, at game speed, against real sticks. See [Practice and Development](../off-the-ice/practice_and_development.md).

## Common Mistakes

- **Hip lock.** Get the top hand off your hip and out in front of your chest. A hand welded to the hip caps your reach, parks the puck directly in front of the checker, and makes real puck protection physically impossible.
- **Squeezing the stick.** Hold it loosely, the bottom hand most of all. A hand that squeezes cannot feel, so you cannot play head up. Better Hockey's coaching guide ranks a white-knuckle grip, especially in the bottom hand, as the number-one thing that kills soft hands.
- **An uncupped blade.** Close the blade over the puck, on the forehand *and* the backhand. Open, it lets the puck roll up and flip over.
- **Staring at the puck.** Build feel first and the eyes follow. Staring locks you out of every tactical idea in hockey and gets you hit.
- **Side-to-side only.** Handle front-to-back as well. Side-to-side alone is a rhythm a defender can read, and with no front-to-back range you have no toe drag, no pull-in and no protection behind the body.
- **Only handling in front of you.** Use the full arc around your body — the far side is where protection happens.
- **Standing tall in a battle.** Get wide and get low. Narrow feet and a high centre of gravity are why players get knocked off pucks.
- **Turning to face pressure on the wall.** With a checker already on you in a low-speed battle, stay side-on, hip and backside between them and the puck, absorb it, then spin off — facing up gets you pinned. In a non-checking game, hold your position and never bump, drive or back into them; in a CARHA adult league, avert the contact too. **Side-on is not back-on: never turn your back to the checker, and never duck — at any speed.** Skates parallel to the wall, forearm and hip against it, never the point of your shoulder, head up. Already facing the wall and unable to turn? Stick and gloves up on the glass — damage limitation, not a position to aim for. See [Body Contact and Battles](body_contact_and_battles.md).
- **Standing square to the wall with your feet planted.** Hip and forearm to the wall, feet moving, and an escape angle along the boards — square and planted is how you get pinned and lose the puck to the second forechecker. A pin can be called in all six books, but play as though the call may not come.
- **Protecting with no plan.** Know your out before you get there. Shielding beautifully for three seconds while a second checker arrives is a slow-motion turnover.
- **Grabbing with the free hand.** Keep it high on your own shaft and brace with forearm and shaft — braced, never driven, because the shaft driven into an opponent is a cross-check. Fend off only to keep position you already have, never to push off for space; CARHA writes no fend-off permission at all. Never hold: it is at least a minor everywhere, and Hockey Canada and CARHA can make it a major that ends your game. A hand on the cage is priced above a minor in four of the six books.
- **Slowing down the instant you get the puck.** Carry it ahead and to the side and keep striding; the whole point of good hands is that they cost no speed.
- **Half-hearted fakes.** Commit fully, or do not bother. A fake that would not have fooled you will not fool them.
- **Hand-only dekes.** Sell the move with your head, shoulders and feet. Moving the puck with your body neutral gives the defender no false information at all.
- **Over-deking.** Use one move where one will do. Every extra move is another second the puck is exposed.
- **Dangling when a pass was on.** Look for the pass first. A dangle risks a poke check, slows the puck down and moves the defence less than a pass would.
- **Dangling in your own end or through the middle.** Move the puck there instead — pass, rim, reverse or off the glass. It is the highest-cost, lowest-reward place on the ice to dangle.
- **Neglecting the backhand.** Train it as hard as the forehand. It is half the game, and under pressure it is usually the *correct* side, because it is the far side.
- **Practising in long, rare sessions.** Practise little and often. Better Hockey asks for *"ten to fifteen focused minutes a day"* and says that *"builds softer hands than one long grind once a week."* That is one publisher's coaching advice, not a measured comparison; the other guides here set no session length.
- **Waiting for a whistle with the puck in your feet.** Trap it against your skate and pull it straight back out to your blade. No book reserves the space between your feet — your skates and body protect it, not a rule — and parking on the puck to force a whistle is penalised in every book, and is a penalty shot in your own crease. When you are the checker, play the puck and keep your stick out from between their legs: it is tripping if it brings them down and hooking if it holds them up. USA Hockey penalises the can-opener for a loss of balance alone, and for a reckless one, plan on a match penalty.
- **Kicking at a player instead of at the puck.** Keep the motion small and at the puck, and if you cannot see the puck, do not swing. Kicking or trying to kick an opponent: five of the six books here throw you out and keep you out until a hearing, and under the IIHF — Britain's book — the referee may give a major that throws you out.
- **Grabbing a puck that pops up in your own crease.** Knock it straight down and move it with your stick at once — do not close your hand on it, and do not trap it in your skates there. A catch dropped at once is allowed; keeping hold of it is a penalty shot against you, or in most books a goal awarded if your goalie has been pulled. The crease runs up to the top of the goal frame in all six books, so a puck caught over it, below the crossbar, is in it.
- **Treating "attack the outside shoulder" as a law.** Choose the shoulder deliberately. Outside is one approach, and it is the one a well-coached defenceman is trying to steer you into.

## Key Takeaways

1. **Whenever the puck is on your stick, get your hands off your body — top hand out in front of your chest, bottom hand loose.** A hand on your hip leaves no far side for the puck, and a squeezing hand cannot feel it. Handle all around your body, front-to-back too.
2. **Every time you have the puck, play with your head up.** A player staring at the puck cannot see the checker coming, and gets hit hard. Get there by feel — the toe, middle and heel of the blade by touch — not by forcing your eyes up.
3. **With a checker on you, puck on your far side, low and wide, hip and backside into them — not your chest.** In a non-checking game, hold your position — bumping or driving into them is a penalty. A violent one can end your game; one that injures does under Hockey Canada and CARHA; a reckless one does under USA Hockey, so plan on a match penalty. Under CARHA, avert the contact.
4. **On the wall, take the contact along your side — skates parallel to the boards, forearm and hip to the wall, head up.** ⚠️ **Never turn your back to the checker and never duck, however slowly they are coming**: a turned back makes it a hit from behind, and a head-down collision can injure your neck at walking speed.
5. **Before you reach a puck under pressure, know your out.** Protection buys a second, not a minute. Decide who you are passing to — or where you are rimming, reversing or chipping it — before your stick touches the puck.
6. **Low in the offensive zone with a defender on you, keep your feet and the puck moving.** Look for a teammate at the net front first; otherwise cut back up the wall or pass back to the teammate behind you, and if the defender turns or over-commits, walk out to the net. How long you keep it low is your team's system.
7. **When the puck comes loose into your feet, trap it against your skate and pull it back to your blade — never park on it.** Freezing it to force a whistle is a penalty in every book, and a penalty shot in your own crease. As the checker, keep your stick out from between their legs.
8. **Kick the puck to yourself — small, and at the puck, never at a player.** ⚠️ **Kicking or trying to kick an opponent is never a two-minute mistake**: five of the six books here throw you out and keep you out until a hearing, and under the IIHF the referee may give a major that throws you out.
9. **If a puck pops up over your own crease, knock it straight down and move it with your stick — never close your hand on it or trap it in your skates.** Holding it there is a penalty shot, or in most books a goal if your goalie is pulled — and the crease runs up to the crossbar.
10. **When a stick is across your hands, brace with your free hand high on your own shaft — never drive it, or it is a cross-check.** Never hold — under Hockey Canada and CARHA it can end your game. Fend off only to keep your position, and never under CARHA. Off the cage too: a grab can throw you out, and under USA Hockey and the PWHL always does.
11. **When the puck arrives in stride, keep striding.** Push it slightly ahead and to the side, take real strides, and let the puck and your body take separate routes when a stick is in the way.
12. **Against a defender in open ice, look for the pass first; if you take them on, make them turn.** A pass is faster than you can skate. Win with a change of speed, one committed fake with your head and shoulders, and a burst out of it. Which shoulder to attack is your coach's call.
13. **In your own end or through the middle, do not stickhandle — and if losing the puck means an odd-man rush against you, the move is off.** Pass, rim, reverse, or put it off the glass — never over it, which from your own zone is a minor in the NHL, IIHF and PWHL even by accident.
