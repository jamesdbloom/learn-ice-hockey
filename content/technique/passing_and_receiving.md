## Key focus

**Move it, then move yourself.** A pass makes the defence re-read the ice; a carry lets them skate alongside you. Think *who is open* first. Carry when nobody is, when a defender's gap is poor, or to buy time for support.

**Look before the puck arrives.** Check both shoulders while the pass travels, so you know your next play when it lands. Look later and the checker — here, any opponent after the puck or just coming at you — is already on you.

**Pass to where your teammate is going, away from the checker.** Read their feet and lead them; a pass behind cannot be skated onto. Away from the checker beats their forehand. ⚠️ **In traffic the lead is a safety duty:** a puck in their feet puts their head down as a checker arrives, which is how players get driven head-first into the boards.

**Get open for the passer.** Move into a clear lane, arrive on time rather than early, and show a target: blade flat on the ice, out in front, held still. Call for it. Most bad passes are bad pairs.

**Cushion the puck, and take your first touch into your next play.** Loose grip, give with it — a blocked pass bounces a foot away, all a checker needs. Touch it away from pressure, into your shot or into your stride, so you can pass or shoot at once.

**Receive with your body open to the ice.** Arrive on an angle, hips turned up ice, knowing which way you are going. A closed body puts the pressure on your back — worst on the half-wall and on a breakout.

**Under pressure, make the pass you can complete.** A rim, a bank, a give-and-go, a chip into space or a reset to your defence all keep the puck. Decide your out before you reach the puck, not after you are pinned.

**Have a backhand pass.** A forehand-only passer gets the forehand taken away and is forced into the turn that kills the play. It need not be pretty; give it a cleaner lane, because it floats.

**Never pass across the front of your own goal.** No blind pass up the middle, no defence-to-defence pass through the slot, no cross-ice pass in your own end; D-to-D goes behind the net. These are common defaults, not laws — ask what exceptions your coach has designed.

## Overview

**Passing beats a defence you cannot out-skate.** Every pass makes four or five opponents re-read the ice and re-pick their man. So moving the puck, and then moving yourself, is the default.

**A good pass starts before the puck reaches you.** Players who scan early already know their next play, and players who lead a teammate keep the rush alive. [Passing Into Space vs Onto the Tape](#passing-into-space-vs-onto-the-tape) covers how far to lead, and [Passing Mechanics That Matter](#passing-mechanics-that-matter) which side to pass to.

**Receiving loses more pucks than passing, and almost nobody practises it.** A good receiver finds a lane, shows a target, cushions the puck and arrives open to the ice. On the wall a closed body turns pressure into a hit from behind; [receiving open to the ice](#receive-with-your-body-open-to-the-ice) covers how to take that contact.

**The first touch and the decision under pressure settle most plays.** A touch into your next action buys half a second. The calm players under pressure did not think faster — they decided earlier.

**Your own end has a stricter standard.** A pass picked off at the far blue line costs possession; one picked off in front of your net is a chance against. [The "never" list](#the-never-list) sets the defaults. This document assumes a **low zone collapse** defensive-zone coverage and a **2-1-2 forecheck**, which is one choice among several, so ask your coach what your team plays.

**Two words do constant work below.** The blade's **heel** is the end nearest the shaft and its **toe** the far end. The **slot** is the dangerous ice in front of the net, between the two faceoff circles. [Language and Glossary](../foundation/language_and_glossary.md) defines the rest and [Rink Map](../foundation/rink_map.md) draws the ice.

**One word runs both ways.** ⚠️ **Here, the *checker* is any opponent after the puck or just coming at you**; elsewhere in this guide, and in most coaching, *"your check"* is the opponent **you** are covering. Take the direction from the sentence.

## Why Passing Beats Carrying

```facts
Key: The puck moves faster than any skater can — that single fact is the whole argument
Goal: Force four or five opponents to re-read the ice and re-choose their assignment
Action: Move it, then move yourself — your first thought should be "who is open", not "where can I skate"
Options: Carry when nobody is open, when the defender's gap control is poor, or when you need to buy time for support
Risk: Carrying concentrates the risk on one player; a completed pass makes the checker's work irrelevant
Never: Turn the shot-speed and skating-speed records into a ratio for passing — NHL EDGE publishes no pass speed, and neither does the one NHL passing study read for this document
```

**The puck moves faster than any skater.** So a pass makes four or five opponents re-read the ice, where a carry lets them skate alongside you. A team that moves the puck well beats a team of better skaters: you cannot out-skate five people, but you can out-pass them. And a carry puts all the risk on one player, while a completed pass makes the checker's work irrelevant.

*(No figure is given for pass speed, because nobody publishes one. NHL EDGE tracks shot speed and skating speed but not pass speed, and the one published study of NHL passing from tracking data — Radke et al., LINHAC 2023 — reports none either. Its *"a puck traveling at speeds between 30 and 100 MPH"* is an example about tracking accuracy, not a measurement of passes. The published extremes — the hardest tracked shot, **104.69 mph** (Tage Thompson, 26 October 2024), and the fastest skater, **24.82 mph** (Miles Wood, 10 April 2025) — make no ratio for passing, because a pass is much slower than a shot. Be sceptical of anyone who quotes you one.)*

**None of this means never carry.** Carry when nobody is open, when a defender's gap is poor, or to buy time for support — see [Puck Handling](puck_handling.md). But the default is *move it, then move yourself.*

## The Pass Types

Each type below gets the same treatment: how to do it, when to use it, and what it costs you when it goes wrong.

### Flat / sweep pass — the fundamental

```facts
Technique: Start the puck near the heel with your bottom hand mid-shaft, and cup the blade over it
Technique: Sweep, do not slap — the puck rolls heel to toe and leaves off the toe
Technique: Transfer your weight from the back leg through your hips toward your front foot
Technique: Follow through with the blade pointing at the target, finishing low around knee height
Read: Use it as the default whenever the lane is clear and the receiver can be reached along the ice
Risk: Low, and the risk is lane selection rather than technique — a clear lane is a precondition
```

**Mechanics.**

- **Start the puck near the heel** of the blade, with your bottom hand roughly mid-shaft for leverage.
- **Cup the blade** over the puck. A cupped blade keeps the puck flat and stops it skipping or bouncing off the face; an open blade sends it up on edge.
- **Sweep, do not slap.** The bottom hand pushes the puck along the ice in one motion, and the puck rolls **heel to toe** and leaves off the toe. John Hamre, an assistant coach with the U.S. National Team Development Program, put it plainly in a two-page 2002 column in USA Hockey's magazine *American Hockey*: *"Your bottom hand 'sweeps' the ice. Do not slap at the puck."* And on where the puck sits: *"pass the puck from the middle or heel of the blade — the player and the stick blade are stronger — the passes are flat, crisp, and the puck can be spun flat from heel to toe."* That column is one coach's advice on mechanics, not a USA Hockey rule or curriculum, and it ranks nothing. A slap makes contact a single instant instead of a guided path; check it first when your passes go wide.

- **Transfer your weight** from the back leg through your hips toward your front foot as you release. Power comes from your body, not your arms.
- **Follow through pointing the blade at the target**, finishing low — about knee height. The follow-through is a cue, not the mechanism: the puck leaves along the line the blade face points at separation, and finishing at the target is how you reliably get that angle. [Shooting](shooting.md) explains the same physics, and it is why, on a one-touch pass, "the blade face at the moment of contact *is* the pass".

**When to use it.** As the default. Any time the lane is clear and the receiver can be reached along the ice, this is the pass. It is the easiest pass to receive, which is the point.

**Risk.** Low, and the risk is almost entirely lane selection rather than technique — a flat pass through a stick or a skate is a turnover, so a clear lane is a precondition.

### Saucer pass — over the obstacle, flat on landing

```facts
Technique: Open the face of the blade slightly, like a wedge
Technique: Sweep heel to toe, rolling your wrists upward and drawing the blade toward your body to put spin on it
Technique: Land it a few feet short of the receiver's blade so it settles and slides the last stretch
Read: Use it when it is the only option — a stick lying in the lane, or a pass that must cross a body
Never: Saucer when a flat pass is available — air is a difficulty you are adding on purpose
Risk: Moderate to high — one that lands on edge is worse than no pass, because the receiver commits and then loses it
```

**Mechanics.**

- **Open the face of the blade** slightly, like a wedge.
- **Start on the heel and sweep across to the toe**, rolling your wrists upward and releasing off the toe.
- **The release generates spin.** Spin is what stops the puck wobbling and what makes it land flat rather than tumbling on edge. Drawing the blade slightly toward your body as the puck rolls heel-to-toe is what puts the spin on.
- **Land it a few feet short of the receiver's blade.** A saucer that is still airborne when it reaches the stick is nearly uncatchable. Aim for it to settle and slide the last stretch.
- Height scales with the obstacle. Over a stick in tight it needs only a few inches of air; over a long distance it can travel far higher.

**When to use it.** When it is the *only* option — a defender's stick is lying in the lane, or the pass must cross a body. The classic uses are cross-slot passes in the offensive zone and passes across a defender's stick during a two-on-one rush.

**Risk.** Moderate to high. Too much lift and it flies over the receiver's blade; too little and it is a rolling puck for the checker. A saucer pass that lands on edge is worse than no pass at all, because the receiver commits to it and then loses it. **Do not use a saucer when a flat pass is available.** Air is a difficulty you are adding on purpose.

### Snap / hard pass — when it needs to be there now

```facts
Technique: A shortened sweep — less backswing, a sharp snap of the wrists, blade still cupped, still following through at the target
Goal: Trade a little accuracy for a lot of speed to beat a window that is closing
Read: Use it into a seam a backchecker is about to fill, to a teammate about to be checked, or into the slot for a one-timer
Never: Snap one at a stationary teammate in a low-pressure spot
Risk: A hard pass your teammate cannot handle is your turnover, not theirs
```

**Mechanics.** A shortened, more violent version of the sweep. Less backswing, a sharp snap of the wrists, blade still cupped, still following through toward the target. You are trading a little accuracy for a lot of speed.

**When to use it.** When the lane is open but closing — a seam through the middle that a backchecker is about to fill, or a pass to a teammate who is about to be checked and needs the puck early. Also the right pass into the slot for a one-timer, where puck speed is part of the shot.

**Risk.** Hard passes are harder to receive. If you snap one at a stationary teammate in a low-pressure spot, you have made their life difficult for no reason. Pace is a tool for beating a closing window, not a display of strength. A hard pass that a teammate cannot handle is your turnover, not theirs.

### Backhand pass — the half of the game most players skip

```facts
Technique: Start the puck at the middle or heel on your backhand side and roll it heel-to-toe, exactly as on the forehand
Technique: Pull with the bottom hand and sweep across your body, top hand rotating to keep the face closed
Technique: Drive through with the lower shoulder — the power comes from the body turning, not the arms alone
Read: Use it whenever the play is on your backhand side and turning to your forehand would cost you the window
Risk: Backhand passes are slower and float more, so the lane needs to be a little cleaner
Key: A checker who knows you cannot pass backhand takes the forehand side away and forces the turn
```

**Mechanics.**

- The puck starts near the **middle or heel** of the blade on your backhand side, drawn back past your rear foot, and rolls heel-to-toe on the sweep exactly as it does on the forehand — that roll is what sends the puck out flat instead of on edge. Hamre's column, quoted above, is explicit that this is not a forehand-only rule: *"(The same principles apply to backhand passes.)"*
- **Pull with the bottom hand** and sweep across your body, keeping the blade cupped over the puck the whole way. The top hand rotates to keep the face closed.
- Drive through with the lower shoulder — backhand power comes from the body turning, not from the arms alone.
- Follow through toward the target, exactly as on the forehand.

**When to use it.** Whenever the play is on your backhand side and turning to your forehand would cost you the passing window. Common cases: a defenceman moving the puck to their partner on the backhand side, a winger on their off wing feeding across the slot, and any escape from wall pressure where you have your back to the ice. That last one is a posture to get out of rather than one to settle into, and it carries a safety rule of its own — see [Receive with your body open to the ice](#receive-with-your-body-open-to-the-ice).

**Risk.** Backhand passes are slower and float more, so the lane needs to be a little cleaner. But the real risk is not throwing them.

**Why neglecting it halves your options.** If you can only pass off one side, you have to turn your body to make half your plays, and you rarely have time to turn. Defenders learn it fast: they take the forehand side away and force the turn, knowing it kills the play. A backhand pass does not need to be pretty; it needs to exist, so the checker cannot cheat to one side.

### Bank / board pass — the boards as a teammate

```facts
Technique: Play the angle, not the target. Aim at the spot on the wall that reflects toward your teammate
Technique: Keep it flat and firm, and hit the boards low, where the bounce is truest
Read: Use it when a stick or body sits in the direct lane but the boards run behind them
Action: Call it, and test a bank in warm-up — board liveliness genuinely varies rink to rink
Risk: Low risk, moderate reliability — the puck never enters the lane the checker is defending, but the bounce is not fully predictable
```

**Mechanics.**

- **Play the angle, not the target.** The puck comes off the boards at roughly the mirror of the angle it goes in at, so aim at the spot on the wall that reflects toward your teammate, not at your teammate.
- **Keep it flat and firm.** A puck that hits the boards on edge or at a soft pace dies against the wall or pops out unpredictably.
- **Hit the boards low.** Contact near the bottom of the boards gives the truest bounce. Higher up you may catch the glass stanchion or a change in the board material and lose the angle entirely.
- **Learn the building.** Board liveliness genuinely varies rink to rink. Test a bank pass in warm-up.

![](diagram:bank-pass-angle)

**When to use it.** When a defender's stick or body sits in the direct lane but the boards run behind them — most often to beat a forechecker along the wall, to move the puck around the back of the net, or to get the puck past a defender pinching down the wall at your offensive blue line.

**Risk.** Low risk, moderate reliability — and those are two different things, so keep them apart.

- **Why it is safe:** the puck never travels through the lane the checker is defending. It goes behind them, off a wall they cannot cover, so there is nothing for a stick or a skate to intercept. That, and only that, is the safety argument.
- **Why it is only moderately reliable:** the bounce is not fully predictable, and boards vary rink to rink. Unpredictability is the *cost* of the play, not the reason it works — an unpredictable puck is one your teammate may also fail to read. Compare the [rim](#rim-vs-reverse--two-opposite-plays-keep-them-straight) below, where the same logic applies and the same caveat holds: a puck your teammate is not expecting is a turnover regardless of how well it beat the checker.

**It is underused** because it does not feel like a "real" pass — but a bank a teammate collects is a completed pass, and beating a stick with geometry costs you nothing. Call it, so the reliability problem is the boards' and not your teammate's.

### Rim vs reverse — two opposite plays, keep them straight

**In short:** Rim it when the checker still has you pinned; reverse it when they have skated past you, and only to a teammate who is there.

```facts
Options: Rim goes onward around the boards; reverse goes back the way the puck came, against the flow
Technique: Rim hard, flat and low on the boards, following the curve of the rink
Technique: Reverse short and firm, usually on the backhand, into the space behind you
Read: Choose by the checker's momentum, not their position. Rim if they still have you pinned, reverse if they have skated past you
Action: Call your intention on the rim; one a teammate is not expecting is a turnover to their opposite number
Never: Reverse on hope — it is a support-dependent play, and a reverse into nobody is a turnover you cannot chase, usually deep in your own end
Never: Square up to the wall, head down to watch the puck. Rim with skates parallel to the wall, forearm and hip to it, never the point of your shoulder; head up and chin off your chest, even going slowly
```

These two are constantly conflated. They go in **opposite directions** and they beat **different kinds of pressure**.

| | **Rim** | **Reverse** |
|---|---|---|
| **Direction** | *Onward* around the boards, continuing the way the play is already flowing | *Back* the way the puck came, against the flow of pressure |
| **Mechanics** | Hard, flat, low on the boards, following the curve of the rink | Short, firm, usually on the backhand, into the space behind you |
| **Beats** | A checker sealing you against the wall — the puck goes *past* them | A checker who has over-committed and skated past you — the puck goes back into the space they just left |
| **Typical use** | Defensive-zone exit under pressure; clearing to the far corner | Puck battle on the wall or behind the net with a teammate arriving late |

**How to choose.** Look at the checker's momentum, not their position. If they are still coming and have you pinned, rim it. If they have already gone by you and their feet are pointed the wrong way, reverse it.

![](diagram:winger-dz-rim)

![](diagram:winger-dz-reverse)

**The two pictures above come from [Winger](../positions/winger.md)**, so their captions speak about a winger in their own end. They draw the two halves of the choice: in the rim the checker still has you pinned, in the reverse the checker has skated past you up the wall. The read carries to any wall or behind-the-net battle. ⚠️ **So does the wall-contact warning both captions carry**, which [Body Contact and Battles](body_contact_and_battles.md) owns.

**Rim in the right posture, because the checker is sealing you against the wall.** The natural shape — square to the boards, head down, watching the puck onto your blade — is the wrong one. ⚠️ **Rim with your skates parallel to the wall**, your **forearm and hip** to it, never the point of your shoulder, and your **head up and chin off your chest** — even going slowly. Find the boards by feel. Head down on the wall, digging at a puck, is exactly the picture the checking-from-behind rules describe.

**Risk on the rim.** A rim that a teammate is not expecting is a turnover to their opposite number, and a soft rim dies in the corner. Rims also need a called intention — see [On-Ice Communication](../foundation/on_ice_communication.md).

**Risk on the reverse.** A reverse into a teammate who is not there is a turnover you cannot chase — the puck goes one way and your momentum the other — and a reverse is usually made deep in your own end, where a loose puck becomes a chance against you. Reverses are a *support-dependent* play: never reverse on hope. Cross-reference [Puck Support and Spacing](../hockey-iq/puck_support_and_spacing.md).

**What a hit from behind on the wall costs the checker.** NHL, IIHF and PWHL Rule 43.1 define checking from behind as a check on a player *"not aware of the impending hit, therefore unable to protect or defend"* themselves, where *"contact is made on the back part of the body"*. USA Hockey 608(b) is a major and a game misconduct for a check from behind that *"recklessly endangers an opponent, or causes them to go head first into the boards or goal frame"*. Its Casebook (608, Situation 1) gives a major and game misconduct or a match penalty for any forceful check on a player standing along the boards with their back to the middle of the ice, and 608(c) makes it a match penalty when a reckless check from behind catches a defenceless player.

**Hockey Canada names this exact picture.** Its Rule 7.5 tells referees to *"strictly enforce"* the rule *"[w]here a player along the boards, with their head down looking for the puck or 'digging the puck out,' is hit from behind and driven head-first into the boards"*. That costs a minor and a game misconduct at least, a major and game misconduct if you are hurt, and a **match** if you could not protect yourself — or head contact (7.6; 7.7 in Junior and Senior) where it reaches your head, whichever costs more. **CARHA 53(b)** makes a hit from behind into the boards or the net, on a player who cannot protect themselves, a mandatory match penalty, and a match keeps you out until the Chairman has dealt with your case (34(a)). **None of that protects you; it names a hit you are exposed to.** [Body Contact and Battles](body_contact_and_battles.md) owns the wording and the evidence.

### Area pass / chip into space — passing to a place, not a person

```facts
Goal: Aim at a patch of ice your teammate will reach before an opponent does, not at a blade
Technique: Flat, with enough pace to clear the nearest defender's stick but not so much that it runs to the far boards
Read: Use it when no teammate is open but one is about to be, or in the neutral zone when a direct pass would thread traffic
Key: It cannot be intercepted the way a direct pass can, because it is not aimed at a body — it becomes a race
Risk: Only a good play if your teammate has a genuine speed or angle advantage; otherwise you are giving the puck away politely
```

**Mechanics.** You are not aiming at a blade; you are aiming at a patch of ice that a teammate will reach before an opponent does. Put it flat and with enough pace to get past the nearest defender's stick, but not so much that it runs to the far boards ahead of your teammate. Chipping it off the boards past a defender into open ice is the same idea.

**When to use it.** When no teammate is currently open but one is about to be — the classic case is chipping a puck past a defender who is standing you up at the blue line, into the space behind them where your winger is already skating. Also the safe option in the neutral zone when a direct pass would have to thread traffic.

![](diagram:area-pass-into-space)

**Risk.** Low, and this is the point: an area pass cannot be intercepted in the way a direct pass can, because it is not aimed at a body. It becomes a **race** rather than a turnover. The risk is only that you lose that race — so it is only a good play if your teammate has a genuine speed or angle advantage. An area pass into a defender's feet with nobody chasing it is just giving the puck away politely.

### Drop pass — and why it fails so often

```facts
Technique: Leave the puck flat and dead behind you — a puck pushed backwards is nearly impossible for a forward-skating trailer to receive
Action: Keep skating hard and take a defender with you, or nobody has been removed from the play
Read: Almost exclusively on the rush, entering the offensive zone with speed, with a trailer at a clear head of steam
Never: Drop it if you are not sure the trailer is there
Risk: High — the trailer is often not there or is level with you rather than behind, and a failed drop at the blue line becomes a counter-attack against committed forwards
Convention: Some zone entry systems build one in deliberately — that is a coaching choice, not a general rule, so find out
```

**Mechanics.** You carry the puck forward, leave it stationary or nearly stationary behind you, and keep skating so that the defender follows you rather than the puck. A trailing teammate picks it up in open ice.

**Two things make it work, and both are usually missing:**

1. **The puck must be left flat and dead**, not passed backwards. A puck pushed backwards is moving away from the trailer, who is skating forwards — the closing speed makes it nearly impossible to receive cleanly.
2. **You must keep skating hard and take a defender with you.** A drop pass where you slow down is a free puck for the defence, because nobody has been removed from the play.

**When to use it.** Almost exclusively on the rush, entering the offensive zone with speed, when you have a trailer with a clear head of steam. It also appears deliberately in some **zone entry systems** — see [Zone Entries](../systems/zone_entries.md) — but whether your team uses one is a coaching choice, not a general rule; find out.

**Risk. High.** It fails because:

- The trailer is not actually there, or is level with you rather than behind you.
- Neither player has said anything, so the drop is a surprise to both.
- It is done blind, so it goes to a spot rather than to a teammate.
- It happens at the offensive blue line, where a failed drop puts the defence straight into a counter-attack against a forward group that has all committed forward.

Default position: if you are not sure the trailer is there, do not drop it.

### Stretch pass — the risk/reward play

**In short:** Stretch it only when a teammate is genuinely behind their forwards, and hard; a miss usually starts an odd-man rush against you.

```facts
Technique: A long, hard pass needing a full weight transfer and a long follow-through — most misses are underpowered, not misaimed
Options: Flat if the lane is clear, saucered if it must cross a stick
Read: Use it when the opposition has over-committed to the forecheck and there is genuinely a man behind them — best on a turnover
Rule: A pass may cross both blue lines and the centre red line — none of the NHL, IIHF, PWHL, USA Hockey, Hockey Canada or CARHA books keeps a two-line pass restriction, and offside is the only rule limiting how far forward the receiver may be (NHL Rule 83; USA Hockey 630; Hockey Canada 6.11)
Rule: The receiver is offside when both skates completely cross the leading edge of the blue line before the puck does (NHL Rule 83.1; IIHF Rule 83.1 in the same words)
Rule: A trail skate lifted in the air keeps the receiver onside under the NHL, IIHF and PWHL, but USA Hockey Rule 630(a) and Hockey Canada Rule 6.11 require the blade to be in contact with the ice; CARHA never defines its "own side", so a blade on the ice at or behind the line is onside in every book
Rule: Offside is not the only rule a long pass can break — icing, NHL Rule 81, is a different rule doing a different job, and it limits sending the puck from your own half of the ice past the opposing goal line
Risk: High reward, high cost — a miss is usually an immediate change of possession with your own forwards caught up ice
Key: Ask whether a teammate is actually behind their forwards right now, or whether you are hoping
```

**Mechanics.** A long, hard pass, usually from a defenceman in or near their own zone to a forward who has broken behind the other team's forwards. Flat if the lane is clear, saucered if it must cross a stick. It needs a full weight transfer and a long follow-through; most missed stretch passes are underpowered, not misaimed.

![](diagram:breakout-stretch-pass)

**When to use it.** When the other team has over-committed to the forecheck and a teammate is genuinely behind them. Best on a turnover, while their forwards' feet are still going the wrong way.

**Risk. High reward, high cost.** If it connects you have a breakaway or a two-on-one. If it misses, you usually lose the puck with your own forwards caught up ice, which is how odd-man rushes against start. Ask: *is a teammate actually behind their forwards right now, or am I hoping?* Hope is not a passing lane. See [Risk Management](../hockey-iq/risk_management.md).

**Rules: the pass is legal; the receiver's feet decide it.** No book here keeps a two-line pass rule: the NHL, IIHF, PWHL, USA Hockey, Hockey Canada and CARHA all let a pass cross both blue lines and the red line. Offside is the only limit on how far forward the receiver may be (NHL Rule 83). Icing (NHL Rule 81) is a different rule, limiting a puck sent from your own half past the far goal line. The PWHL, USA Hockey and Hockey Canada books have no passes rule at all. **CARHA still prints the old heading** — *"Passes and Off-side Passes"* — and an old *"RED LINE OFF-SIDE PASS"* signal, but its Rule 73(d) switches it off: *"For the purpose of 2-line passes, the red line will not be in play. It will still be in effect for icing."*

The NHL dropped the two-line pass by a playing-rule change for 2005-06, adopted alongside the new Collective Bargaining Agreement rather than by it; the IIHF had dropped it in 1998. ⚠️ **Both dates are received history, not rulebook text.** Neither appears in the NHL, IIHF, USA Hockey or Hockey Canada books held here, which proves nothing, because a current book states the rules as they stand. Take the dates as commonly given and unchecked.

**The receiver must stay onside, and the books split on one point.** Under **NHL Rule 83.1** a player is offside *"when both skates are completely over the leading edge of the blue line involved in the play"*, and onside if either skate touches the line or is on their own side of it as the puck crosses. The rule's "his own side" is a vertical **plane**, so a trail skate held in the air behind the line keeps you onside. IIHF 83.1 (both editions) and PWHL 85.1 say the same, so the British answer is the NHL's.

**Two books disagree.** ⚠️ **USA Hockey and Hockey Canada rule a lifted trail skate offside.** USA Hockey 630(a) needs *"skate contact with any part of the Neutral Zone or the blue line"*, and its Casebook (630, Situation 7) rules this exact play offside. Hockey Canada 6.11 counts only skates *"in physical contact with the ice surface"*, and Interpretation 3 to 6.11(a) rules the same case OFF-SIDE. CARHA 72(c) Note 1 borrows "own side" without defining it and publishes no ruling, so it settles the point neither way. **Keep your trail blade on the ice, touching the line or behind it, and you are onside in every book.** [Zone Entries](../systems/zone_entries.md) works the rule through.

## Passing Into Space vs Onto the Tape

```facts
Targets: Onto the tape means the puck arrives at the receiver's blade where it is now — correct when they are standing still or about to shoot
Targets: Into space means the puck arrives ahead of the receiver, timed so they skate onto it without breaking stride — correct in almost every other case
Key: A pass to a moving teammate's current position is already behind them when it arrives, and reaching back or slowing down kills the speed that made the play dangerous
Risk: A puck arriving behind a teammate or in their feet in traffic puts their head down to find it, and head down on the boards is how players get driven head-first into them
```

This is the concept that separates players who complete passes from players who complete *useful* passes.

**Onto the tape** means the puck arrives exactly at the receiver's current blade position. Correct when the receiver is stationary or when they are about to shoot.

**Into space** means the puck arrives at a point ahead of the receiver, timed so that they skate onto it without breaking stride. Correct in almost every other case, and especially in transition.

**Why it matters.** A pass to a moving teammate's current position is behind them by the time it gets there. They have to reach back, slow down, or turn — and every one of those kills the speed that made the play dangerous. Receiving **in stride** is the whole point: the receiver keeps their speed, keeps their head up, and keeps the defence retreating.

**And in traffic the cost is not only the rush.** A puck that arrives behind a teammate or in their feet makes them put their head down to find it, at the moment a checker is arriving — and head down is the posture that gets a player driven head-first into the boards. [Body Contact and Battles](body_contact_and_battles.md) owns the wording and the evidence. ⚠️ **Where you put the puck decides where your teammate's head is**, so leading them properly is a duty you owe them and not only a way of keeping the play alive.

### How to judge the lead

![](diagram:lead-the-receiver)

```facts
Read: Read the feet, not the chest — their skates tell you where they are actually going and how fast
Technique: Pick a catch-point ahead of them and pass to that spot, rather than tracking a moving blade with your eyes
Read: Faster receiver, longer lead; harder pass, shorter lead — the two variables trade against each other
Targets: Lead them toward the play they want to make — a winger driving the net wants it ahead and to their forehand
Key: When in doubt, lead them slightly — a pass ahead can be skated onto, a pass behind cannot
```

- **Read the feet, not the chest.** A player's chest can be facing anywhere. Their skates tell you where they are actually going and how fast.
- **Pick a catch-point, then pass to it.** Do not try to track a moving blade with your eyes. Choose the spot where their stick will be when the puck arrives, and pass to that spot. You are aiming at a place in the future.
- **Faster receiver, longer lead; harder pass, shorter lead.** The two variables trade against each other. A slow floater to a sprinting winger needs a large lead; a snap pass to the same winger needs much less.
- **Lead them toward the play they want to make**, not just into empty ice. A winger driving the net wants the puck slightly ahead and to their forehand; a defenceman stepping into a shot wants it slightly ahead and *toward* their shooting side.
- **When in doubt, lead them slightly.** A pass marginally ahead can be skated onto. A pass behind cannot be skated backwards onto.

## Passing Mechanics That Matter

```facts
Action: Head up, always — if you have to look at the puck to pass it, your passing is limited by your stickhandling
Action: Take your look over both shoulders while the puck is travelling to you, not after it arrives
Technique: Move your weight from the back leg to the front leg through the pass — arm-only passes float, and floaters get intercepted
Technique: Finish with the blade pointing where you want the puck, low to the ice
Targets: Put the puck on the receiver's forehand, away from the checker, and slightly in front of them
Priority: When those three preferences conflict, away from the checker wins
```

Beyond the pass types, five habits determine whether your passes arrive.

**1. Head up, always.** Keeping your head up lets you see the lane, see the checker, and avoid the hit. If you have to look at the puck to pass it, your passing is limited by your stickhandling — go work on [Puck Handling](puck_handling.md) until the puck is handled by feel.

**2. See the whole ice *before* you receive.** The best passers have already decided where the puck is going before it reaches them. Take your look over both shoulders while the puck is travelling to you, not after it arrives — by then a checker is on you and the information is out of date. This is the core habit in [Scanning and Anticipation](../hockey-iq/scanning_and_anticipation.md).

**3. Weight transfer.** Power comes from moving your body weight from the back leg to the front leg through the pass. Arm-only passes float, and floaters get intercepted. This is the same power chain as a wrist shot — see [Shooting](shooting.md).

**4. Follow through at the target.** Finish with the blade pointing where you want the puck, low to the ice. A pass that misses left or right was almost always sent that way by the blade face, and the follow-through is the cue that controls it — see the note under the flat pass above, and [Shooting](shooting.md), which covers the face-versus-follow-through account. *(That "almost always" is coaching observation, not a counted failure analysis.)*

**5. Pass to the correct side of the receiver.** This is the most commonly ignored mechanic in the list. Given a choice, put the puck:

- **On their forehand**, because the forehand gives them more control and more immediate options than the backhand.
- **Away from the checker.** If a defender is closing from their left, put the puck on their right — you are passing them a shield as well as a puck.
- **Slightly in front of them**, so their first touch is forward.

Those three preferences can conflict. When they do, **away from the checker wins** — a backhand reception with time beats a forehand reception with a stick in your ribs.

## Receiving — The Neglected Half

Passing is drilled; receiving is assumed. That is backwards, because a pass is only as good as the touch that ends it. Everything below is a skill you can practise deliberately.

### Give a target

```facts
Technique: Stick blade flat on the ice, out in front of your body, angled toward the passer, and held still
Key: That blade is the passer's aiming point — off the ice or waving, and the passer has to guess
Action: Add your voice as well as your stick; a called name plus a target blade removes all ambiguity
Goal: A still blade says "I am open and I want it now" without a word
```

Put your **stick blade flat on the ice**, out in front of your body, angled toward the passer, and hold it still.

That blade is the passer's aiming point. If your stick is off the ice, waving around, or tucked in beside your skates, the passer has to guess, and guessing is how passes end up in feet. A still blade on the ice is a promise, and it is also a communication — it says *I am open and I want it now* without a word.

Add your voice as well as your stick. A called name plus a target blade removes all ambiguity. See [On-Ice Communication](../foundation/on_ice_communication.md).

### Cushion the puck — give with it, do not block it

```facts
Technique: Meet the puck out in front, bottom hand pushing the stick away from your body
Technique: As the puck makes contact, draw the blade back toward your body, letting it travel with the puck for a few inches
Technique: Soft hands means a loose grip — a tight grip makes the blade rigid, and a rigid blade is a wall
Key: A blocked pass rebounds a foot or two off your blade, and that foot is all a checker needs
```

This is the central receiving skill.

- **Meet the puck out in front**, with your bottom hand pushing the stick away from your body.
- **As the puck makes contact, draw the blade back toward your body** — the bottom hand comes in. You are absorbing the pass, letting the blade travel with the puck for a few inches rather than stopping it dead.
- **Soft hands means loose grip.** A tight grip makes the blade rigid, and a rigid blade is a wall. A puck that hits a wall bounces off it.

A blocked pass rebounds a foot or two off your blade. That foot is all a checker needs. A cushioned pass stops dead on your blade, under control, ready to play.

### Receive on your forehand where you can

```facts
Technique: Adjust your body angle before the puck arrives so the pass comes to your forehand
Goal: A cupped blade, more surface area under control, and immediate access to a shot or a pass
Priority: Getting your blade to the puck matters more than which side it is on — do not let the preference make you late
Action: Practise backhand receptions specifically, because in traffic you will not get to choose
```

Your forehand gives you a cupped blade, more surface area under control, and immediate access to a shot or a pass. Where you have the freedom to choose — and you usually do, by adjusting your body angle before the puck arrives — turn so the pass comes to your forehand.

But do not let this become a rule that makes you late. **Getting your blade to the puck matters more than which side it is on.** Practise backhand receptions specifically, because in traffic you will not get to choose.

### Angle the blade to kill a bounce

**In short:** Cup the blade over any bouncing puck, budget a second touch for a hopping one, and play a high puck down at chest height.

```facts
Technique: Cup the blade over the top of the puck — hopping, on edge or flat, the angle does not change — and keep your top hand off your hip, or a ski-jump effect sends the puck over the blade
Read: A flat puck can be played in one touch; a hopping or on-edge puck costs you a second, so budget the extra beat
Technique: Take it on the middle of the blade, not the toe — the toe gives you the least surface and the weakest part of the blade
Action: Angle the face away from the direction you do not want the puck to go, so a squirt goes somewhere safe
Action: Play a high puck down at chest height, not above your shoulders. Keep skating until you hear the whistle — in every book but CARHA it waits to see who gains possession, so it may come late
Rule: Batting the puck above the normal height of the shoulders with a stick is prohibited in all six books (NHL and IIHF 80.1, PWHL 82.1, USA Hockey 621(d), Hockey Canada 6.9(a), CARHA 62(d)) — and Hockey Canada's Note 1 measures that height standing fully upright, so crouching does not lower it
Rule: USA Hockey 621(d) reads harsher than it works. NHL 80.1, IIHF 80.1 and Hockey Canada 6.9(a) stop play on the offending team's possession and control; 621(d) says immediately, but USA Hockey's Casebook (Rule 621, Situations 6 and 15) waits for the same thing, or for the puck to go dead
Rule: A high stick on a player: NHL and IIHF 60, PWHL 61, a minor, double minor if it injures; NHL and PWHL a match if deliberate or tried, IIHF may eject with a major if reckless; USA Hockey 621 (620 in a check); Hockey Canada minor and female 7.6: at least a minor, major and game misconduct if hurt
Rule: CARHA has no minor rung above the shoulders: 62(b) makes contact there a Major "whether or not injury results", its Note ejects you unless accidental and injury-free, and 32(d) adds at least a one-game ban unless accidental; deliberate injury, or an attempt, is a Match, 62(c). CARHA leagues only
Rule: USA Hockey goes past a game misconduct: 620(b) is a major plus game misconduct for reckless, intentional or careless head contact. Plan on a match if reckless (620(c), 602(a)); Casebook Situation 4 makes the match mandatory for excessive force to intimidate or punish
Rule: Hockey Canada Junior and Senior play 9.5, routed to 7.7 — no head-contact count there, but three stick-infraction minors in a game, 9.5 included, still eject under 4.8(b)(i); 7.7(c) also reaches a match penalty for a hit the opponent cannot protect or defend against
Rule: Accidental contact above the shoulders on a normal shooting follow-through is excused only in the NHL, IIHF (60.1) and PWHL (61.1), never for a wild swing at a bouncing puck; USA Hockey has no such excuse, Hockey Canada penalises the contact (9.5 Interp. 1, 7.6(a)) and CARHA 62(b) makes it a major
Rule: Catch a puck out of the air and put it straight down: NHL and IIHF 67.2, PWHL 69.2, Hockey Canada 10.2(a)(iii) and CARHA 61(a) permit that and make holding it a minor. USA Hockey's Casebook permits it (Rule 618, Situation 1); holding it stops play under 618(a)
Rule: A skater covering the puck in your crease gives up a penalty shot (NHL and IIHF 67.4, PWHL 69.4, Hockey Canada 10.2(a)(v), CARHA 58(c)); USA Hockey 614(b) and 618(a): shot or minor, other team's choice. With your goalie pulled it is a goal, in USA Hockey only if it stopped an obvious, imminent goal
```

Pucks arrive rolling, on edge, or hopping — from a bad pass, a bouncing rim, or a rut in the ice.

**Cup the blade for all of them.** The blade angle does not change with the type of bad puck; only the number of touches does.

- **Cup the blade over the top of the puck**, top edge tilted forward, whether it is hopping, on edge or flat. The cup traps it on the ice, and it comes from keeping your top hand off your hip. Hamre's column (see [Flat / sweep pass](#flat--sweep-pass--the-fundamental)) describes the open blade a puck rides over: *"If the top hand is on the hip, a 'ski jump' effect is created for the puck by the stick blade, and the puck will jump over the blade."*
- **A flat puck takes one touch; a hopping or on-edge puck usually takes two.** Cup it, let the first touch knock it flat, and play it on the second. Budget the extra beat rather than making one touch do both jobs.
- **Take it on the middle of the blade, not the toe.** The toe gives you the least surface and the weakest part of the blade. Hamre says the middle or heel is stronger when *giving* a pass — *"the player and the stick blade are stronger"* — and applying that to receiving is this document's inference, not his instruction.
- **Angle the face slightly away from the direction you do not want the puck to go**, so a puck that squirts off your blade goes somewhere safe rather than into the slot.
- **Play a high puck down at chest height, and keep playing until you hear the whistle.** You may catch it with your hand if you put it straight down. Do not plan around the rules below; plan around chest height. A blade that reaches an opponent's head is a penalty in every book.

**What it costs: the stick on the puck.** Every book bans batting the puck above shoulder height with your stick. **NHL Rule 80.1**: *"Batting the puck above the normal height of the shoulders with a stick is prohibited."* The IIHF (80.1), PWHL (82.1), USA Hockey (621(d)), Hockey Canada (6.9(a)) and CARHA (62(d)) set the same line. Hockey Canada adds the useful part: *"The “normal” height of the shoulders refers to when a player is standing fully upright"* (6.9(a), Note 1). **Crouching does not lower your ceiling.** Whether a high-sticked puck can count as a goal, and the crossbar limit on tips, are in [Shooting](shooting.md).

**The whistle is not automatic.** The NHL stops play only *"[w]hen a puck is struck with a high stick and subsequently comes into the possession and control of a player from the offending team"*; bat it to an opponent and play goes on. The IIHF and Hockey Canada are the same. USA Hockey 621(d) says play stops *"immediately"*, but its Casebook (Rule 621, Situations 6, 8, 10 and 15) waits for the same thing — *"an offending team player must be the first to gain possession of the puck"* — or for the puck to go dead. ⚠️ **CARHA has no possession test:** Rule 62(d) stops play *"immediately"*, with the draw in *"the offending team’s defensive zone"* every time (CARHA-affiliated leagues only).

**If both teams leave it alone, play stops, and the draw differs.** The NHL's **Rule 72.3** stops play where a teammate *"elects not to play the puck to avoid the stoppage of play, and the opposing team also abstains"*, with the draw *"in the zone… nearest to where the play was stopped."* IIHF 72.3 and PWHL 74.3 say the same, and Hockey Canada gets there by Interpretation 2 to 6.9(a)(i). **USA Hockey is the outlier:** its Casebook (621, Situation 12) says *"[t]he non-offending team has no obligation to play the puck in this instance"* and puts the draw *"in the Defending Zone of the offending team."* So declining a high-sticked puck wins you the zone under USA Hockey and costs you it under the others.

**The hand.** **NHL Rule 67.2**: *"A player shall be permitted to catch the puck out of the air but must immediately place it or knock it down to the ice."* Closing your hand on it to skate with it or hide it is a minor penalty in the NHL, the IIHF (67.2) and the PWHL (69.2). Hockey Canada (10.2(a)(iii)) excuses an honest catch dropped at once, but a stride while holding it is a minor (Interpretation 4); CARHA 61(a) is the same, and one stride is a minor. USA Hockey stops play instead (618(a)) and keeps its delay-of-game minor for picking the puck up off the ice; its Casebook (618, Situation 1) says the catch and the drop *"must be virtually one continuous motion"*. Knocking it to a teammate is a hand pass — see [Receiving a pass in your feet](#receiving-a-pass-in-your-feet).

**In your own crease.** ⚠️ **A skater never covers the puck there — knock it straight down.** Covering, holding or picking it up there gives up a penalty shot: NHL 67.4 and PWHL 69.4; the IIHF through 63.6, if the puck is in the crease at that instant; Hockey Canada 10.2(a)(v), if the infraction happens inside your crease (scooping it out and then covering it is a minor, Interpretation 5); and CARHA 58(c). USA Hockey gives a penalty shot or a minor (614(b), 618(a)), and the choice belongs to *"the non-offending team"* (406(a)), so never plan on the minor. With your goalie pulled it becomes an awarded goal — NHL and IIHF 67.5, PWHL 69.5, Hockey Canada 10.2(a)(vi), CARHA 58(c) Note 1 (its Rule 36(b) adds conditions; play to the flat answer) — and under USA Hockey 614(b) where the cover *"prevented an obvious and imminent goal"*.

**The stick on a player.** ⚠️ **Once your blade reaches an opponent above the shoulders, it is not a puck rule in any book.** Every book penalises it, and every book has a route from there to an ejection.

- **NHL and PWHL.** NHL Rule 60.1 excuses accidental contact on a normal shooting windup or follow-through, then withdraws that for this act: *"A wild swing at a bouncing puck would not be considered a normal windup or follow through"*. Rule 60.2 is a minor, 60.3 a double minor if it injures, and 60.4 a **match penalty** for trying to injure or injuring. PWHL 61.1 to 61.4 are the same.
- **IIHF.** 60.1 has the same excuse and carve-out, and 60.2 the same minor. 60.4 writes no match penalty: the referee *"may assess"* a major and an *"automatic game misconduct penalty"* for reckless high-sticking. ⚠️ **The 2026/27 edition widens 60.1 and 60.3.** The 2025/26 book needs a stick *carried* above the shoulders; the 2026/27 book needs only contact, so a stick you never carried above your own shoulders can still cost you four minutes.
- **USA Hockey, high sticking.** No follow-through excuse, and no contact needed: its Casebook (621, Situation 2) says high sticking *"must be called"* when a stick is raised above the shoulders to intimidate or carelessly. Rule 621 runs from a minor (621(a)) to a major and a game misconduct for reckless endangerment (621(b)) to a possible match penalty (621(c)). The Casebook (621, Situation 3) says *"[t]he player must be assessed a major plus game misconduct penalty or a match penalty"* whenever the stick recklessly endangers, even from a player falling after being checked. Any USA Hockey game misconduct also costs your next game, and a second major in one game at least two (404(b)).
- **USA Hockey, head contact.** Stick contact to the head during a check is called under Rule 620 instead (621, Situation 1). 620(a) is a minor and a misconduct or a major and a game misconduct; 620(b) a major and a game misconduct for reckless, intentional or careless contact; 620(c) a possible match. The Casebook makes one of the two top options mandatory for intentional or reckless contact (620, Situation 3), and the match itself mandatory for a check using excessive force to intimidate or punish (Situation 4). ⚠️ **Plan on a match:** **602(a)** says one *"shall be assessed"* to any player who *"recklessly endangers or attempts to injure any opposing player"*, and 608(c) makes one compulsory for a reckless hit from behind on a defenceless player. A match also brings a likely multi-game suspension.
- **Hockey Canada, minor and female divisions.** No follow-through excuse. Rule 6.9(c) sends stick contact made while high-sticking the puck to Rule 7.6, Head Contact, which *"supersedes any other rules, with the exception of Rule 7.10 – Fighting"*. Its ladder: a minor for accidental contact, a double minor for intentional contact, and ejection after three 7.6(a) penalties in a game. A major and a game misconduct is at the referee's discretion, and **mandatory** if the opponent is injured or for any deliberate cross-check above the shoulders. A **match penalty** is for a hit the opponent cannot protect against, or for trying to injure or deliberately injuring.
- **Hockey Canada, Junior and Senior.** Rule 9.5 applies instead, routed to 7.7. It *"may be assessed regardless of whether or not contact occurs"*, and Interpretation 1 to 9.5(a) makes a follow-through that hits above the shoulder a minor. There is no head-contact count, but three stick-infraction minors in a game, high-sticking included, eject you under Rule 4.8(b)(i).
- **CARHA has no minor rung above the shoulders** (CARHA-affiliated leagues only). Rule 62(b) makes contact there a **major**, *"accidentally or intentionally"* and *"whether or not injury results"*. A CARHA major puts you out of the game (30(a)); the only exception is an accidental high stick that injures nobody (30(a) Note, 62(b) Note). An intentional one also brings at least a one-game suspension (32(d)), and every game misconduct is reported *"for further action"* (32(b)). Trying to injure or injuring is a **match penalty** (62(c)), and you sit out every game until the Chairman deals with it (34(a)).
- **CARHA below the shoulders.** Rule 62(a) is a minor for checking or intimidating an opponent *"while carrying their stick above the waist height of their opponent"* — no contact needed. Its situation book rules a player who *"swings at puck with their stick above waist height of opponent – making contact with the player"* a **Minor penalty**, and one who strikes an opponent *"above the normal height of the player's shoulder"* a **Major penalty**: height, not whether you were checking, moves you up. Cross-checking (54(a)), hooking (64(a)) and slashing (80(a), and 80(c) for a swing that misses or a wild swing at the puck meant to intimidate) are priced too, and Rule 50 sends stick contact to the head to Rule 62.

### Receiving a pass in your feet

**In short:** Kick it forward to your stick or open your hips and let it run, sending the skate at the puck, never at the man.

```facts
Technique: Kick it forward to your stick with the inside of your skate — one deliberate, controlled kick at the puck, never at the man, and not a panicked scuff
Options: Or open your hips and let it run through, then collect it behind your stride
Never: Reach backwards with your stick while continuing to skate forwards
Rule: Kicking the puck is permitted in all zones and you cannot score with a distinct kicking motion (NHL 49.2) — but that permission is for the puck, and NHL 49.3 makes kicking or attempting to kick an opponent a match penalty whether or not an injury occurs
Rule: Kicking or trying to kick an opponent is a match penalty: NHL and PWHL 49.3, USA Hockey 627(b) (reckless kicking too), Hockey Canada 7.1(c)(iii) and CARHA 48(c) (any attempt, or a deliberate kick). IIHF 49.3: the referee may give a major plus automatic game misconduct. Kick the puck, never the man
Rule: Under USA Hockey a skate does not need a kicking motion to be an offence — 627(a) is a major plus a game misconduct for using the skate to "push off" an opponent, which its Note 2 defines as skate contact with an opponent "in a non-kicking motion"
Rule: A USA Hockey game misconduct is not only this game — 404(b) also suspends you for your team's next game, and a second major in the same game costs at least the next two
Rule: A kicked puck that deflects in off a body is no goal; off your own stick it is a good goal (NHL, IIHF and PWHL 49.2(i) and (iii)) — that good-goal limb is those three books' alone, and Hockey Canada 6.10(b) and USA Hockey's Casebook (627, Situation 4) both disallow it
Rule: In CARHA-affiliated adult leagues a kicked puck deflecting in off your own stick is no goal: CARHA Rule 69 permits kicking the puck in all zones and adds "This action cannot result in a goal being scored", and Rule 60(c) bars a goal where a kicked puck "deflects off any player or stick"
Rule: The kick-shot — kicking your own stick blade at the puck — costs you the goal under NHL, IIHF and PWHL 49.2 and Hockey Canada 6.10(c), and an immediate whistle under USA Hockey 627(c), the book calling it "a very dangerous shot"
Rule: CARHA Rule 68 applies in CARHA-affiliated adult rec leagues only, and not to the goalie: "A Minor penalty shall be assessed to any player, except a goalkeeper, who uses a kick shot during the game" — its glossary defines the act as propelling the puck by kicking your own stick
Rule: A hand pass stops play everywhere except your own defending zone; under the NHL, IIHF and PWHL the puck's location at either contact decides the zone (79.2; PWHL 81.2), but CARHA Rule 61(d) locates the teammate instead, so keep your receiver inside your own blue line too
Rule: USA Hockey 618(b) needs the pass "initiated and completed in their defending zone" and Hockey Canada 10.2(a)(i) reads as though it locates the receiver — but its Note 1 to Interpretation 1 corrects that, and those two books decide it by the puck like the NHL and the IIHF, never by your skates
Rule: The IIHF Situation Handbook 2025/26 rules the blue-line case three ways at Situations 79.2, 79.3 and 79.5, all by the puck; USA Hockey Casebook 618 Situation 3 agrees and adds that "the blue line is considered to be part of the Defending Zone in this instance"
```

It will happen, and it is a skill, not an accident to be complained about.

- **Kick it forward to your stick with the inside of your skate.** One deliberate, controlled kick to a spot in front of you, not a panicked scuff. ⚠️ **Send the skate at the puck, never at the player.** A puck in your feet usually has an opponent's feet beside it, and a kick at them can be an ejection for a reflex — the costs are below.
- **Or open your hips and let it run through**, then collect it behind your stride. Turning your body slightly is usually faster than reaching.
- **Do not reach backwards with your stick while continuing to skate forwards.** That is how you end up bent over, off balance, and hit.

**What it costs: kicking.** ⚠️ **Kicking the puck is legal; kicking a player is at the top of every book's scale.** **NHL Rule 49.2**: *"Kicking the puck shall be permitted in all zones."* But **NHL 49.3**: *"A match penalty shall be imposed on any player who kicks or attempts to kick another player"*, with the five-minute penalty served *"[w]hether or not an injury occurs"*. PWHL 49.3 is the same sentence. USA Hockey 627(b) (*"kicks, attempts to kick or recklessly endangers an opponent by kicking"*), Hockey Canada 7.1(c)(iii) and CARHA 48(c) make a deliberate kick, or an attempt, a **match penalty** too. **The IIHF — Britain's book at every level — writes no match penalty**: under its 49.3, in both editions, the referee *"may assess a major penalty and an automatic game misconduct penalty"*.

**Under USA Hockey a skate needs no kicking motion.** ⚠️ **Its 627(a) is a major and a game misconduct** for any player who *"uses their skate to 'push off' an opponent"*, and Note 2 defines that as skate contact *"in a non-kicking motion"*. **Keep the kick small, keep it deliberate, and send it at the puck.**

**Scoring off a kick.** Under NHL 49.2 you cannot score with *"a distinct kicking motion"*, or with a kicked puck that deflects in off any player's body. But a kicked puck that deflects in off a skater's stick counts — including your own: *"A goal will be allowed when an attacking player kicks the puck and the puck deflects off his own stick and then into the net."* The IIHF (49.2(III)) and PWHL (49.2(iii)) say the same.

**Three books take that goal away.** ⚠️ **Hockey Canada, USA Hockey and CARHA disallow it.** Hockey Canada 6.10(b): *"no goal can be scored as the result of a kicking motion by an attacking player."* USA Hockey's Casebook (627, Situation 4) puts this exact play and answers *"No"*. CARHA Rule 69 allows kicking the puck but adds *"This action cannot result in a goal being scored"*, and its 60(c) bars a kicked puck that *"deflects off any player or stick into the net"*. **So control it and shoot it.** USA Hockey's Casebook would allow the goal had the player *"intentionally directed the puck into the goal with their stick"*; Hockey Canada (Interpretation 1 to 6.10(b) and (c)) disallows a puck kicked to a teammate unless it is *"clearly shot"*; CARHA does not say, so shoot it clearly there too. This document has no count of which leagues run which book, so find out yours before you count on that goal.

**The kick-shot** — kicking your own stick blade into the puck — never scores. The NHL, IIHF and PWHL (49.2) and Hockey Canada (6.10(c)) disallow the goal, and USA Hockey 627(c) stops play, calling it *"a very dangerous shot"*. ⚠️ **CARHA makes it a penalty:** Rule 68, *"A Minor penalty shall be assessed to any player, except a goalkeeper, who uses a kick shot during the game"*, its glossary defining the act as *"placing the blade of the stick behind the puck and then propelling the puck by kicking the stick."* CARHA rules apply in CARHA-affiliated leagues only.

**What it costs: the hand pass.** A hand pass is legal only in your own defending zone, and the test is where the **puck** is, not your skates. **NHL Rule 79.2**: *"The location of the puck when contacted by either the player making the hand pass or the player receiving the hand pass shall determine the zone it is in."* IIHF 79.2 and PWHL 81.2 say the same. USA Hockey 618(b) needs the pass *"initiated and completed in their defending zone"*. Hockey Canada 10.2(a)(i) reads as if it locates the receiver, but its Note 1 to Interpretation 1 corrects that: *"the position of the puck and not that of the player's skates determines which zone the player is in when the hand pass is completed."* The IIHF Situation Handbook (79.2, 79.3 and 79.5) and USA Hockey's Casebook (618, Situation 3) also go by the puck, and the Casebook adds that *"the blue line is considered to be part of the Defending Zone in this instance."*

**So keep the puck inside your own blue line at both touches** — legal everywhere. Hockey Canada tests only the second touch (its Interpretation 1 lets a hand pass back into your zone stand), but both touches inside is legal there too. ⚠️ **CARHA locates the players instead:** Rule 61(d) allows the open-hand play *"unless the player directs the puck to a teammate in the neutral or attacking zone"*, and that book publishes no correction. In a CARHA league, keep the receiver inside your blue line as well.

### Receiving a bad pass

```facts
Action: Get your body in front of a bad pass — shin pads, skates and chest are legal tools, and a puck stopped by your body is still your puck
Key: Putting your body in front of a bad pass is not blocking a shot. Stepping in front of a shot is a separate skill with its own technique and its own list of when not to try it
Priority: Kill it first, look second — stop the puck, then play it
Never: Try to receive a difficult puck and make a play in the same motion
Mindset: Accept a lost half-second and take the safe next option; forcing the original plan through a bad reception is the actual mistake
```

The measure of a good receiver is what they do with a bad pass.

- **Get your body in front of it.** Your shin pads, your skates and your chest are all legal, legitimate tools for stopping a puck. A puck stopped by your body is still your puck. **This is a pass, not a shot.** Stepping in front of a *shot* is a separate skill with its own technique and its own list of situations where you should not attempt it at all — it is treated in [Body Contact and Battles](body_contact_and_battles.md), and nothing in this section transfers to it.
- **Kill it first, look second.** Do not try to receive a difficult puck and make a play in the same motion. Stop it, then play it. Trying to do both is how bad passes turn into turnovers.
- **Accept a lost half-second.** If a bad pass costs you the play you wanted, take the safe next option. Forcing the original plan through a bad reception is the actual mistake.

### Receive with your body open to the ice

```facts
Position: Hips and shoulders turned so you can see up ice and across the ice as the puck arrives, not square to the passer
Action: Arrive at the puck on an angle rather than straight at the passer, curling into the reception
Goal: Your next option should already be visible when the puck lands on your blade
Key: Decide which way you are going before the puck arrives, and be pointing that way when it does
Risk: A closed body invites the pressure to arrive on your back — worst on the offensive half-wall and on breakout receptions
Never: Turn your back to anyone coming at you, or duck. Keep your head up and chin off your chest even going slowly, and never take the wall on the point of your shoulder
Technique: If you are already facing the wall and cannot turn, get your stick and gloves up on the glass and spread the impact as wide as you can, no head contact — damage limitation, not a position to aim for
```

**Open** means your hips and shoulders are turned so you can see up ice and across the ice at the moment the puck arrives — rather than square to the passer with your back to everything you want to do next.

- Arrive at the puck **on an angle**, not straight at the passer. Curl into the reception so that your momentum and your sightline already point where you want to go.
- Your next option should already be visible when the puck lands on your blade. If you have to turn to find it, you have given the forechecker the time they needed.
- The general term for this is *checking your shoulder and opening up*; the practical version is: **decide which way you are going before the puck arrives, and be pointing that way when it does.**

This is especially valuable on the half-wall in the offensive zone and on any breakout reception, where a closed body invites the pressure to arrive on your back.

**On the wall, pressure on your back is a hit from behind into the boards.** Opening up lets you see the checker coming instead of feeling them arrive. Your posture at the moment of contact is not a preference: ⚠️ **never turn your back to anyone coming at you, and never duck** — keep your head up and your chin off your chest, even going slowly, and never take the wall on the point of your shoulder. Caught facing the wall and unable to turn, put your stick and gloves up on the glass to spread the impact — damage limitation, not a position to aim for. [Body Contact and Battles](body_contact_and_battles.md) covers how to take the hit, and the evidence behind it.

### Receiving while moving

```facts
Technique: Reach the blade out ahead of your skates, not beside them — receiving beside your feet leaves you no room to cushion
Action: Keep your feet moving through the reception; gliding to catch a pass costs you a stride every time
Never: Let your stick pull your body off line. Turn to the puck with your feet instead, then use your hands
Action: Adjust your speed to the pass, not the pass to your speed — a small glide or an extra stride makes a difficult pass easy
Risk: A stationary receiver is a target
```

Almost all real receptions happen at speed.

- **Reach the blade out ahead of your skates**, not beside them. Receiving beside your feet leaves you no room to cushion.
- **Keep your feet moving through the reception.** Gliding to catch a pass is a habit that costs you a stride every single time, and a stationary receiver is a target.
- **Do not let your stick pull your body off line.** Turn to the puck with your feet, then use your hands. Reaching with the arms while your skates go elsewhere puts you off balance — see [Skating](skating.md).
- **Adjust your speed to the pass, not the pass to your speed.** A small glide or a hard extra stride to meet the puck at the right moment is invisible to everyone else and makes a difficult pass into an easy one.

## The First Touch Determines Everything

```facts
Key: Receive the puck into the position it needs to be in for your next action, not the position it happens to arrive in
Action: Take the touch away from pressure — if a checker is closing from your left, cushion it slightly to your right
Action: Take the touch into your shooting position in the slot, beside your back foot and ready to release
Action: Take the touch forward in transition, so your next stride is a stride and not a stop
Action: Take the touch into open ice in the middle with nothing on. Do not stop it under your feet and then look up
Read: The test — after your first touch, could you pass or shoot immediately? If not, that touch was wasted
```

A good first touch buys you half a second. A bad one costs you the puck. That is why this section puts it first.

Half a second does not sound like much. On the ice it is the difference between a shot and a blocked shot, between a clean exit and a puck battle in your own corner, between finding the open man and throwing it away.

**The principle: receive the puck into the position it needs to be in for your next action, not into the position it happens to arrive in.**

Practically, that means:

- **Take the touch away from pressure.** If a checker is closing from your left, cushion the puck slightly to your right as you receive it. The reception and the escape are one movement, not two.
- **Take the touch into your shooting position** if you are receiving in the slot. The puck should end up beside your back foot, ready to be released, not in your feet.
- **Take the touch forward** if you are receiving in transition, so your next stride is a stride, not a stop.
- **Take the touch into open ice** if you are receiving in the middle with nothing on. Do not just stop it under your feet and then look up — a puck under your feet is a puck you have to move before you can play it.

The test to apply: **after your first touch, could you pass or shoot immediately?** If the answer is no, that touch was a wasted one, and every wasted touch is time donated to the defence.

## One-Touch Passing

```facts
Goal: Move the puck faster than a defence can rotate, so it lands in a space that was covered when the pass started
Read: A one-touch is on when the pass is flat and catchable, your next target is already open and known, and the lane will still exist
Read: A one-touch is off on a bouncing puck, a target you have not located, or with a defender close enough that a miss is an instant turnover
Technique: Identify the next target while the puck is still travelling — this is a decision made early, not a reaction made late
Technique: Angle your blade before contact; the blade face at the moment of contact is the pass, with no follow-through to correct it
Technique: Soft on receive, firm on release — even a one-touch has a tiny cushion in it
Technique: Set your feet, because you need a stable base to redirect a puck accurately at speed
```

A one-touch pass — sometimes called a first-time pass, and the basis of a *tic-tac-toe* sequence of quick passes — is a pass you redirect on contact, without controlling it first.

**Why it beats coverage.** Defenders and goalies are reacting to where the puck is. Every touch you take gives them time to re-set. A one-touch pass moves the puck faster than a defence can rotate, so the puck arrives in a space that was covered when the pass started and is not covered when it lands. This is exactly why the best power plays are built on one-touch sequences — see [Special Teams](../systems/special_teams.md).

**When it is on:**

- The pass to you is flat, accurate, and at a pace you can redirect.
- Your next target is already open *and you already know it*.
- The receiving lane you are creating will still exist a moment later.

**When it is not on:** a bouncing puck, a target you have not yet located, or a defender close enough that a missed one-touch is an immediate turnover. A one-touch attempt that fails usually fails badly, because you have made no attempt to secure the puck.

**How to prepare before the puck arrives:**

1. **Identify the next target while the puck is still travelling.** One-touch passing is a decision made early, not a reaction made late.
2. **Angle your blade before contact.** The blade face at the moment of contact *is* the pass — there is no follow-through to correct it. Set the angle in advance.
3. **Soft on receive, firm on release.** Even a one-touch has a tiny cushion in it. Pure deflection sends the puck wherever physics wants.
4. **Set your feet.** You need a stable base to redirect a puck accurately at speed.

## Timing and Support — A Pass Is a Two-Player Action

```facts
Key: The receiver creates the pass as much as the passer does — most "bad passes" are actually bad pairs
Action: As the receiver, move into a lane rather than just moving. Find the open line between you and the puck
Read: As the receiver, time your arrival; reaching the open space too soon means a defender arrives with you
Action: As the receiver, give a target blade and a voice, and have a next option — a reception with no exit is a trap
Action: As the passer, deliver it so it can be received in stride, on the side away from pressure, the moment the window opens
Priority: If your team cannot complete passes, look at the players without the puck before you look at the passer
```

Most "bad passes" are actually bad *pairs*.

The receiver creates the pass as much as the passer does. A passer with the puck and their head up is scanning for someone who is open, in a clean lane, and available *now*. If nobody satisfies all three, there is no pass — and the blame for the resulting turnover usually lands on the player holding the puck.

What the receiver owes the passer:

- **Movement into a lane, not just movement.** Skating hard through an area a defender's stick covers does not help. Find the open line between you and the puck.
- **Timing, not earliness.** Arriving in the open space too soon means a defender arrives with you. Support is about *when* as much as *where*.
- **A target blade and a voice**, as above.
- **A next option.** If your reception has no exit, you are not support, you are a trap.

## Deception

```facts
Technique: Look off a defender — fix your eyes on one option, let them shift toward it, then pass to the other
Technique: Turn your shoulders and hips toward one side to pull the defender's weight that way
Options: A genuine shoulder-check doubles as deception, and a short delay before the pass often beats a fake because there is nothing to read
Read: A no-look is justified only when you have already seen a target unlikely to move, the defender is reading your eyes, and the pass is short
Never: Use a no-look as a default, or at all in your own end — every no-look bets that the ice has not changed
Key: Deception is a supplement, not a substitute — selling a look is worthless if the pass that follows is inaccurate
```

Defenders and goalies read your eyes, your shoulders and your blade. Anything you can do with those that does not match where the puck is going buys you a lane.

**Looking off a defender.** Fix your eyes on one option, let the defender shift toward it, then pass to the other. This is the highest-value and lowest-risk deception in hockey, because your eyes cost you nothing — you have already scanned, so you already know where both options are.

**Shoulders and hips.** Turning your upper body toward one side pulls a defender's weight that way. A defender who has committed their weight cannot recover in time to take away a pass to the other side.

**Shoulder-check as deception.** A genuine look over your shoulder is information for you and misinformation for them. It also makes your eventual pass look prepared, which discourages the aggressive stick check.

**Delay and change of pace.** Holding the puck a beat longer than expected freezes a defender who is timing your release. A short delay before the pass is often more effective than a fake, because there is nothing to read.

**No-look passes — and when they are justified.** A genuine no-look pass is justified when:

- You have already seen the target and it is unlikely to move (a player parked at the back door, a defenceman stationary at the point).
- The defender you are beating is actively reading your eyes.
- The pass is short enough that your spatial memory is reliable.

It is **not** justified as a default, and it is a bad habit in your own end. The failure mode is that the target has moved since you last looked, and you have thrown the puck to a memory. Every no-look is a bet that the ice has not changed. In the defensive zone, do not take that bet.

**Deception is a supplement, not a substitute.** Selling a look is worthless if the pass that follows is inaccurate. Get the mechanics reliable first.

## Passing in Common Situations

The same passing decisions come round every game. These are the common approaches; where coaches differ the alternative is named, so ask your bench which one your team plays.

- **Breaking out, a defenceman with the puck behind your own net.** The common first option is a firm, flat pass to the strong-side winger on the wall, to their forehand or off the boards to them. If that winger is covered, reverse it behind the net to your partner or to the centre swinging low; if everything is covered, skate it or rim it hard. Never answer pressure there with a pass through the slot. [Breakouts](../systems/breakouts.md) sets out the patterns teams run.
- **A two-on-one, with the puck.** Make the defender choose: drive at the net under control, as if you will shoot, and pass only when their stick leaves the lane. Pass early and flat, to your partner's stick ahead of them rather than to their skates, so they can shoot before the goalie arrives. If the defender takes the pass away, shoot — then pull up short of the goalie, head up and chin off your chest, because the post can hurt you the way the boards can.
- **From behind the opponent's net.** Wait for a teammate to arrive in the slot and pass to the spot they are skating into, flat and hard. A pass out front that nobody is arriving for is a turnover in the most dangerous place to have one at the other end.
- **From the half-wall up to the point.** Keep it hard, flat and on the defenceman's forehand. A soft pass there is the one a winger jumps for a breakaway; if the lane is not clean, cycle it down low instead.
- **The entry is closed.** Regroup — pass it back to your defence and come again with speed rather than forcing a pass into a crowd at the blue line. A regroup is a completed play.
- **Protecting a lead late in a period.** Take no chances with the puck in your own half: no pass across the middle of your own zone, and no soft pass through the middle at your own blue line. Get it out off the boards or deep into their end, and make them come the length of the ice to score.

## Passing Under Pressure

Under pressure, three things change: you have less time to look, the lanes are shorter-lived, and the cost of a mistake is higher because opponents are already close to the puck.

**What to do differently:**

- **Simplify.** The pass you can definitely complete beats the pass you would like to complete. A rim, a bank off the boards, a short **give-and-go** — you pass to a teammate, immediately skate into open space, and take it straight back — or an area chip all keep possession moving without threading a needle.
- **Decide before you get there.** Know your out while you are still skating to the puck, not after you are pinned. This is the single biggest difference between players who look calm under pressure and players who do not — the calm ones decided earlier, they did not think faster.
- **Use the boards and use the glass.** They are always available and they cannot be intercepted.
- **Take the extra half-second by moving your feet**, not by holding still. A stationary player under pressure has one option and it shrinks. See [Body Contact and Battles](body_contact_and_battles.md) for protecting the puck while you buy that time.
- **Accept the reset.** Sending the puck back to your defenceman, or off the glass and out, is a completed play — not a failure. It is only a failure if you had a better option and did not see it.

### The "never" list

```facts
Convention: The three own-zone passing prohibitions in this block are risk-management defaults rather than laws of hockey. Treat them as always-on unless a coach has built a system around the exception
Never: Make a blind pass up the middle in your own zone — if you cannot see the receiver, the pass does not exist
Never: Make a D-to-D pass through the slot; it goes behind the net, or above the tops of the circles if your team accepts that
Never: Pass cross-ice in your own end — picked off, it leaves your whole structure on the wrong side of the puck
Key: In your own zone the passing standard is certainty; in the offensive zone it is value
```

These three are **risk-management defaults**, not laws of hockey: each one's failure is a high-danger chance against. Treat them as always-on unless your coach has built a system around the exception.

- **Never make a blind pass up the middle in your own zone.** The middle of your own defensive zone is directly in front of your net. An interception there is a chance against with your goalie unset. If you cannot see the receiver, the pass does not exist.
- **Never make a D-to-D pass through the slot.** Defence-to-defence passes are normal and useful — but they go *behind the net* — or *above the tops of the circles*, where your team accepts that — not across the front of your own goalmouth. A blocked or deflected slot pass is a tap-in.
- **Never pass cross-ice in your own end.** A cross-ice pass in your defensive zone crosses in front of the danger area and, if it is picked off, leaves your whole structure on the wrong side of the puck. Some teams accept one above the tops of the circles, where an interception is not a slot chance; below them, almost nobody does.

**The unifying principle:** in your own zone, mistakes are punished immediately, so the passing standard is *certainty*. In the offensive zone, mistakes cost you possession 180 feet from your net, so the standard is *value*. Adjust your risk tolerance by where you are on the ice. This is the core of [Risk Management](../hockey-iq/risk_management.md).

**The exception.** Some structured breakouts include a controlled defence-to-defence exchange behind the goal line — a designed play with eyes up, support set and an escape route, not an improvised cross-ice pass under pressure. This document assumes a **low zone collapse defensive-zone coverage** (everyone sinks toward the net and defends space rather than chasing a man) and a **2-1-2 forecheck** (two forwards pressuring in the other team's end, one behind them, both defencemen at the blue line); yours may differ. Find out which passes your team sanctions — see [Breakouts](../systems/breakouts.md) and [Defensive Zone Coverage](../systems/defensive_zone_coverage.md).

## How to Practise

Passing improves faster than almost any other hockey skill, because most of it can be practised off the ice on a smooth surface with no equipment beyond a stick and a puck.

### Wall passing (solo)

```facts
Action: Face a wall, rebounder or board and pass into it repeatedly — cup, sweep, follow through at a spot
Key: Receive every return properly — cushion, control, reset; half the value of the drill is on the receiving side
Action: Alternate forehand and backhand equally; the backhand improves fastest because it starts furthest behind
Action: Progress to one-touch returns and keep a count
Action: Mark a small target on the wall and aim at it — accuracy without a target is just repetition
Action: Pass and receive while shuffling laterally, so the reception happens with your body moving
```

Face a wall, a rebounder, or a board and pass into it repeatedly.

- Start stationary at a comfortable distance. Cup, sweep, follow through at a spot on the wall.
- **Receive every return properly** — do not just stop it. Cushion it, control it, reset. Half the value of this drill is on the receiving side.
- **Alternate forehand and backhand.** Force yourself to spend equal time on each; the backhand is the side that improves fastest because it starts furthest behind.
- **Progress to one-touch.** Return the puck without stopping it, and keep a count.
- **Add a target.** Mark a small spot on the wall and aim at it. Accuracy without a target is just repetition.
- **Move.** Pass and receive while shuffling laterally, so the reception happens with your body moving.

### Partner drills

```facts
Action: Stationary tape-to-tape at increasing distance, both players giving a target blade every single rep
Action: Skate parallel down the ice passing back and forth, deliberately leading each other into stride
Action: Give-and-go — pass, burst into space, receive it back
Action: Saucer over a stick laid flat on the ice, judged by whether it lands flat, not by whether it clears
Action: Bad-pass practice — have your partner send you rolling, bouncing and behind-you passes deliberately
Action: Pressure passing with a third player closing on the passer, stick on the puck and no contact; clean passing in a quiet drill does not transfer on its own
```

- **Stationary tape-to-tape**, gradually increasing distance. Both players give a target blade every single rep.
- **Moving passes down the ice** — two players skating parallel, passing back and forth, deliberately leading each other into stride. This is the best available drill for learning the lead.
- **Give-and-go**: pass, burst into space, receive it back. This trains the two-player timing that makes a pass work.
- **Saucer practice over an obstacle** — a stick laid flat on the ice between you. Judge success by whether it lands *flat*, not by whether it clears the stick.
- **Bad-pass practice.** Have your partner deliberately send you rolling, bouncing and behind-you passes. Nobody does this, and it is the fastest way to improve your reception of the passes you will actually get in games.
- **Pressure passing.** Add a third player closing on the passer, with their stick on the puck and no body contact. This is what turns technique into a game skill; clean passing in a quiet drill does not transfer on its own.

### Weighted puck training

```facts
Action: Use a weighted puck for strength and mechanics in short doses — the extra mass forces a full weight transfer and a complete follow-through
Action: Switch back to a regular puck in the same session, to carry the improved mechanics into normal weight
Never: Do all your work with a weighted puck — it changes the timing of your release, so it supplements normal-weight repetition
Key: The measured benefit is for shooting, not passing — no measurement of what a weighted puck does to passing was found for this document, which is not proof that none exists
Risk: Treat volume with care and stop if your wrists or elbows complain
```

A weighted puck (heavier than a standard puck) is a common off-ice training tool.

- **Use a weighted puck for strength and mechanics, in short doses.** The extra mass forces a full weight transfer and a complete follow-through; you cannot flick a weighted puck with your wrists alone.
- **Switch back to a regular puck in the same session.** A standard puck feels light and quick immediately afterwards, and the point is to carry the improved mechanics into normal weight.
- **Do not do all your work with a weighted puck.** Weighted-puck practice changes the timing of your release. It is a supplement to normal-weight repetition, not a replacement for it. Treat volume with care and stop if your wrists or elbows complain — see [Conditioning and Recovery](../off-the-ice/conditioning_and_recovery.md).
- **The measured benefit is for shooting, not passing.** One trial (Novak et al., 2022) put sixteen male youth players, mean age 13.6, through twelve weeks: six with a light puck and six with a heavy 260 g one, in a cross-over design where **every player did both** (**the paper gives the light puck as 60 g in its abstract and 90 g in its Methods**). Over the programme shooting speed rose **7.4–13%**, accuracy **14%** and bottom-hand grip strength **8.7%**, and the heavy puck did more for puck speed than the light one.

  **Three limits.** There was **no untrained control group** — everyone shot for twelve weeks — so the gains measure the whole programme, not the weighted puck; only heavy *versus* light is a clean comparison. The players were thirteen. And no measurement of a weighted puck's effect on *passing* was found for this document: read that as "none found here", not proof that none exists. Treat the numbers as belonging to your shot, not your pass.

Structuring this into an actual improvement plan is covered in [Practice and Development](../off-the-ice/practice_and_development.md).

## Common Mistakes

- **Slapping at the puck.** **Sweep it:** the bottom hand pushes the puck along the ice, heel to toe and off the toe. A slap is the first thing to check when your passes go wide.
- **An open blade, passing or receiving.** **Cup it.** An open face flips a pass onto its edge and lets a reception ski-jump over your stick. The only deliberate open face is the saucer's.
- **Passing with your arms.** **Move your weight from back leg to front leg through the pass.** Arm passes float, and floaters get picked off.
- **Following through high.** **Finish low, blade pointed at the target.** A high finish sends pucks over sticks and into shins.
- **Passing to where a moving teammate is now.** **Pass to where they are going, so they skate onto it.** ⚠️ **In traffic a puck behind them or in their feet puts their head down** — the posture that gets players driven head-first into the boards.
- **Passing to the checker's side.** **Put it on the side away from the checker**, even if that is their backhand.
- **No target.** **Blade flat on the ice, out in front, held still, and call for it.** A waving stick makes the passer guess.
- **Blocking the pass.** **Loose grip, and give with it as it lands.** A blocked pass bounces a foot away — the gap a checker needs.
- **Standing still to receive.** **Keep your feet moving and adjust your speed to the pass.** A stationary receiver loses a stride and is easy to hit.
- **Receiving with your back to the ice.** **Open your body before the puck arrives.** On the wall, pressure on your back is a hit from behind. ⚠️ **If the contact comes anyway: head up, chin off your chest even going slowly — never duck, and never the point of your shoulder.**
- **Two touches when one would do.** **Pick your target while the puck travels, and move it first time when it is on.**
- **A drop pass on hope.** **Drop it only to a trailer you know is there, leave it dead, and keep skating.** Not sure? Keep it.
- **Saucering when a flat pass was on.** **Lift it only over a stick or a body.**
- **A forehand-only passer.** **Practise the backhand until it exists.** Defenders take your forehand away within a shift.
- **Looking only once the puck arrives.** **Scan while it is travelling.**
- **Blind passes in your own zone.** **If you cannot see the receiver, the pass does not exist.** A pick-off there is a chance against with your goalie unset.
- **Kicking at the man instead of the puck.** **Keep the kick small and aimed at the puck.** ⚠️ **Kicking or trying to kick an opponent is a match penalty in five books, and under the IIHF it can be a major with an automatic ejection.** In every book a kick means a deliberate one — USA Hockey adds a reckless one — and trying is enough. Under USA Hockey even pushing off an opponent with your skate is a major and an ejection.
- **Timing a stretch pass by the wrong offside rule.** **As the receiver, keep your trail skate on the ice at or behind the blue line until the puck crosses** — onside in every book. A lifted trail skate is onside in the NHL, IIHF and PWHL but offside under USA Hockey and Hockey Canada.
- **Expecting a goal off your own kick.** **Kick it to your stick, then clearly shoot it.** A kick deflecting in off your own stick counts in the NHL, IIHF and PWHL, but not under Hockey Canada, USA Hockey or CARHA.
- **Treating a high stick as just a stoppage.** **Play the high puck down at chest height, and play on until the whistle.** ⚠️ **If your blade reaches an opponent above the shoulders, every book penalises it, and every book can eject you for it.** Only the NHL, IIHF and PWHL excuse a normal shooting follow-through, never a wild swing at a bouncing puck. Under CARHA any contact up there is a major that ends your game unless it was accidental and hurt nobody, and an intentional one costs you at least your next game too.

## Key Takeaways

1. **With the puck, look for who is open before where to skate.** The puck outruns any skater, so you can out-pass five opponents but not out-skate them. Carry when nobody is open, as the exception you choose.
2. **Sweep, do not slap. Cup the blade. Follow through low at the target.** Those three fix most inaccurate passing.
3. **Pass to where your teammate is going, on the side away from the checker.** Read their feet and lead them so they keep their stride; away from the checker beats their forehand. ⚠️ **In traffic the lead is a safety duty:** a puck in their feet puts their head down as a checker arrives.
4. **Look over both shoulders while the pass is travelling to you.** Look after it lands and the checker is already on you.
5. **Cushion the puck.** Meet it out front with a loose grip and give with it. A blocked pass bounces a foot away, and that is all a checker needs.
6. **Have a backhand pass.** A forehand-only passer gets their forehand taken away. Give the backhand a cleaner lane, because it is slower.
7. **Take your first touch into your next play** — away from pressure, into your shot or into your stride. Afterwards you should be able to pass or shoot at once.
8. **Rim past a checker who has you pinned; reverse behind one who has skated past.** Read their momentum, not their position.
9. **When your next target is already open, move it first time.** A one-touch pass beats the defence's rotation — if you chose the target before the puck arrived.
10. **Get open for the passer.** Find a clear lane, arrive on time, and show a still blade flat on the ice. When a team cannot complete passes, look at the players without the puck first.
11. **Never pass across the front of your own goal.** No blind pass up the middle, no D-to-D through the slot, no cross-ice pass in your own end. These are defaults, not laws — some breakouts build in a D-to-D behind the net — so follow them unless your coach has designed the exception.
12. **Stretch and drop passes are reads, not hopes.** Stretch it only when a teammate is truly behind their forwards. Drop it only to a trailer you know is there, and keep skating hard.
13. **Under pressure, make the pass you can complete, and decide your out before you get the puck.** Rim, bank, chip or reset; never thread a needle with a checker on you.
14. **On the wall and on a breakout, receive open to the ice**, so the pressure is not on your back — on the wall, that is a hit from behind. If it comes anyway, keep your head up and chin off your chest even going slowly, never duck, and never take the wall on the point of your shoulder.
15. **Kick the puck out of your feet — never the man.** Kicking or trying to kick an opponent is a **match penalty** under the NHL, PWHL, USA Hockey, Hockey Canada and CARHA. Under the IIHF — Britain's book — it can be a major with an automatic ejection.
16. **Play the high puck down at chest height, and play on until the whistle.** ⚠️ **If your blade reaches an opponent's head, face or neck, every book penalises it and every book has a route to an ejection.** Under CARHA it is a major that ends your game unless accidental and injury-free, plus at least a one-game suspension if intentional.

Together with the book's two situations on Rule 62 — quoted above, the first ruling a **Minor penalty** where a player *"swings at puck with their stick above waist height of opponent – making contact with the player"* and the second a **Major penalty** where the swing strikes an opponent *"above the normal height of the player's shoulder"* — and the routing note under **Rule 50, *Head Contact*** — *"If a stick is used on contact this infraction falls under Rule 62 – High Sticks"*. ⚠️ **Rule 62 is not that book's only rule reaching a stick below the shoulders**, so the following were read too: **Rule 54(a)** (cross-checking, *"A Minor penalty or, at the discretion of the Referee, a Major Penalty and a Game Misconduct"*), **Rule 64(a)** (hooking, a minor for impeding an opponent *"by hooking with their stick"*), and **Rule 80(a)** and **80(c)** (slashing — *"A Minor or Major penalty, at the discretion of the Referee"* for impeding by slashing, and a slashing penalty for a player who swings at an opponent *"(whether in or out of range) without actually striking them"* or who, *"on the pretext of playing the puck, makes a wild swing at the puck with the object of intimidating an opponent"*). **Rule 48(f)** and **48(g)** (butt-ending and spearing) were read and are not relied on above. And **Rule 30, *Major Penalties***, read for **30(a)** — *"A player, including a goalkeeper, assessed a Major penalty shall be ruled off the ice for the remainder of the game (Major penalty plus Game Misconduct)"* — and for its Note, *"The only exception to this rule is when a Major penalty has been assessed for an ACCIDENTAL high stick, in which case the Game Misconduct shall not apply"*, a carve-out the Note to 62(b) withdraws *"when injury results"* (all read 25 September 2026). And **Rule 72(c), Note 1** (Off-sides), read 26 September 2026: *"A player is on-side when either of their skates are in physical contact with, or on their own side of, the blue line at the instant the puck completely crosses the edge of that blue line, regardless of the position of their stick."* ⚠️ **CARHA borrows "own side" from the NHL wording without ever defining it as a vertical plane, and publishes no interpretation of the airborne skate**, so it settles that point neither way. *Unpublished, and therefore not quoted anywhere above: **pass speed and pass-completion rate.** NHL EDGE publishes shot speed and skating speed, both quoted above, but no pass-speed statistic. The LINHAC 2023 paper — the obvious place to look, and the one already in this list — reports passing-lane and opponents-overtaken metrics, not speeds, and its only mention of puck velocity is an illustrative "between 30 and 100 MPH" used to argue that the tracking system's three-inch positional error is tolerable. That is not a measurement of passes and is not quoted as one. On completion rates, Sportlogiq computes them for clubs but publishes no league-wide figure, and the same LINHAC paper says of **itself** — not of the feed — that *"this paper focuses specifically on completed pass events"* and that its authors *"are only able to analyze completed passes"*. (The feed is described the other way round: SportsMEDIA Technology "derive event-level data (**including** completed passes)". The restriction is the authors' analytical scope, not a limit of the tracking data.) With no record of the incomplete passes in their analysis, no completion rate can be taken from it. **The weighted-puck effect is sourced** — Novak et al. (2022), cited above — but it measured shooting, not passing, it had **no untrained control group** (all sixteen players trained with both pucks in a cross-over design), and it is quoted only for what it actually covers. The 2005 CBA and 1998 IIHF dates for the removal of the two-line pass are historical background from secondary sources. Neither date is in any rulebook consulted here — the NHL, IIHF, USA Hockey and Hockey Canada books were searched for both on 31 August 2026, with nil returns throughout — but that is what a current rulebook would be expected to look like either way, since it states the rules as they stand rather than their history. The current rulebook simply contains no two-line provision, which is the point that matters on the ice.*
