===== SCRIPT START =====

This episode is about the breakout. The breakout is how your team gets the puck out of your own end, with a plan, once you have won it back. For each part, I will tell you what to do and also why it works. If you know why something works, you can still make the right play when the picture in front of you looks a little different.

Before any instructions, a few words you will hear all the way through.

Your own end. That is the zone around your own net, from the end boards out to your blue line.

The slot. This is the ice right in front of your net, between the two faceoff circles, from the tops of the circles down toward the goal. It is where most goals are scored from.

The point. This is the area just inside the blue line, near the boards, where defencemen usually stand. It is an area, not the line itself.

The wall. Coaches often say the wall when they mean the boards.

The hash marks. These are the short red lines on the outside of each faceoff circle, level with the dot. Between the hash marks and the goal line means a low spot on the boards, roughly the bottom third of the way out to your blue line.

Strong side and weak side. The strong side is the side of the ice the puck is on. The weak side is the other side.

The forecheck. This is the other team's players coming into your end to win the puck back. The players doing it are forecheckers.

The checker. In this episode, the checker means any player from the other team coming at you, whether they are after the puck or just coming at you.

That is the vocabulary. Now the job.

Most breakouts at amateur level don't fail because somebody can't skate or pass. They fail because five players ran five different plays. So your first job happens before the game. Find out which breakout your team runs, what its calls mean, and which part of it is yours.

I will start with four big ideas, because they matter most. If you only hear the first part of this episode, take those four with you. After that come the five jobs, the plays, and the special moments.

First idea. Never move the puck across the front of your own net. Not with a pass, and not by skating it.

Here is why. If you lose the puck in front of your net, the other team has a shot from point-blank range.

So go behind your own net instead. Say you have the puck in one corner and your partner is on the far side. Pass it to your partner behind the net, not in front of it. If that pass gets picked off, the puck is behind your goal line. Nobody can shoot from there. It becomes a battle for the puck, not a chance against you.

Some teams pass between their defencemen high in the zone instead, above the tops of the faceoff circles, outside the slot. Their reason is that a pass picked off up there is not a shot from the slot. It still leaves a loose puck at the top of your slot, though. It is a different route, not an exception. Ask which one your team uses.

One play does cross the front of your net on purpose. It is a pass right across the ice to the far side, and some teams call it the over. It is a high-risk play. Use it only when the other team has piled onto one side and the middle is empty. And if you can go behind the net instead, do that.

Second idea. Leave your end with the puck. Clear it only when you have read that you should.

There are only two ways out of your end. A controlled exit means the puck leaves on a teammate's stick, carried out or passed out. Your team keeps it. A clear means you just get the puck out, off the glass or the boards, with no teammate in mind. You give it away, and getting it back is a coin flip at best.

Here is why that matters. One large study tracked a huge number of exits in NHL games. Carrying or passing the puck out led to an attack the other way almost nine times in ten. A clear that nobody on your team got back did it about one time in five. The study's own conclusion is that keeping the puck is what matters. A clear your own team picks up does about as well.

So keep the puck by default. Many amateur teams have this backwards. They clear out of habit, and try to make a play only when the ice is already empty.

Then clear when the situation tells you to. There are four times. You are outnumbered low, with nobody to pass to. Or the only other choice is losing the puck in your slot. Or you badly need a line change. Or it is late in a game you are winning, and simple is right.

The second one is never close. A clear costs you the puck. A turnover in your slot can cost you a goal.

When you do clear it, put it high off the glass, in the corner or up the side boards. Not flat up the middle. Here is why. A flat clear up the middle is the one that gets picked off, and the one that gets called for icing. A glass clear slows the puck down and keeps it wide, so it is rarely picked off and rarely ices.

Icing means shooting the puck from your own half of the ice all the way across the other team's goal line, untouched. Play stops, and the faceoff comes back to your end. Some younger age groups play without icing at all, so ask how your games do it.

Off the glass, not over it. If the puck goes straight over the glass and out of the rink from your own end, that is a two-minute penalty if you meant it. In many leagues, Britain's included, it is two minutes even if you didn't. If you flip it out on purpose from anywhere on the ice, that is two minutes too.

And if you clear it to get a tired line off, make sure it doesn't ice. In many leagues, Britain's included, a team that ices the puck can't change before the faceoff.

Third idea. Read the forecheck before the puck reaches you.

Every breakout works by putting the puck where the forecheckers are not. So you need to know where they are before you touch the puck.

If you are the defenceman going back for the puck, glance over your inside shoulder on the way. Here is why. Once you get to the puck and turn to face the boards, that look is gone. You can't get it back, and from then on you are guessing.

Then count the forecheckers. One forechecker means you have options, and probably time to skate it round the net yourself. Two means the corners are gone. Then you are playing it up the boards, or reversing it, or rimming it. I will explain those two plays soon. Three deep means somebody on your team up the ice is free, though it may also mean you have nobody low.

Watch their feet, not their sticks. A forechecker who is gliding is steering you, holding you in. One who is striding hard is coming for you. If you feel yourself being pushed one way, go the other way while you still can.

And decide early. Time is a short window, not something you get to keep. By the time you feel the pressure, the pass you wanted has usually closed.

Now the retrieval itself, because this is where players get hurt.

Skate in at an angle, not straight at the end boards. Curl in, so your first touch already has your body opening up to the ice. The angle is a safety point first and a tactic second. Going into the boards at an angle makes it much less likely that you hit them helmet first.

Two things override everything else at the boards. Never turn your back to the checker. And never duck.

Both protect your head and neck. If you turn your back, you can't see the hit coming or brace for it. If you duck, your head meets the boards first. That holds at any speed, so a slow checker doesn't make it safe.

So here is the position. Skates parallel to the boards, side-on, knees bent and back straight, so you are low. Forearm and hip to the boards. Never the point of your shoulder, and not the whole shoulder if you can help it. Head up. Chin off your chest.

If you are already facing the wall and can't turn, that is damage limitation, not a choice. Get your stick and gloves up and spread wide on the glass, to cushion the hit. And never duck. Keep your head up and off the glass.

From here on I will use one short version of that position, the same words every time, so you can learn it like a line. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the checker, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly.

The rules punish a hit from behind after it happens. They don't stop it. Your angle and your position are the part you control.

Then take the puck to where you can make a play, behind your net or up the boards. Don't stop on it. Here is why. The defenceman standing still on the puck is the one who gets hit.

Fourth idea. The four players without the puck make the carrier's options.

A winger who arrives on the boards moving can take the pass and go. A winger standing still has to start from zero, with a defenceman closing. A centre low in the middle is the one pass that still works when the boards are sealed. And the far defenceman can see what the carrier can't, and can call it.

So without the puck, be somewhere useful, moving, at the right moment. The commonest breakdown you will see on a weekend is a centre who has left early, in a system that wanted them low. Then nobody is underneath the puck at all.

Let me pull those four ideas together, because they are the core.

One. Never across the front of your own net. Go behind it.

Two. Keep the puck by default. Clear it only on a read, and then off the glass, never over it.

Three. Look over your shoulder and count the forecheckers before you get to the puck. Going back for it, angle in. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the checker, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly.

Four. Without the puck, be ready and moving, and know which play your team is running.

Everything else adds detail to those four. Now, the five jobs.

Fifth idea. Five jobs, and know which one is yours.

Whatever play is called, the same five jobs exist. The defenceman who goes back for the puck. Their partner. The winger on the puck side. The winger on the far side. And the centre. Knowing which one is yours before the puck is picked up is most of the battle.

What follows is the most common shape, called the swing breakout. It is a coaching choice, not a law, and some teams set up differently. But the swing is the place to start, and the rest of this episode assumes it.

The first job is the defenceman going back for the puck, and you have already heard it.

The second job is the partner. Stand where a pass behind the net reaches you cleanly. That is usually on the other side of the net, level with the goal line or a little above it.

Why there? Because you are the second wave. If the first option fails, the puck comes back to you.

And you make most of the calls, a word or two, shouted, that names a play or warns of pressure. Why you? You can see the forecheck arriving behind your partner's back, and your partner can't.

Make your calls early and loud. A call that arrives with the forechecker is worse than no call, because it makes your partner hesitate.

Time means nobody is near, so turn and make a play. Only say time when you are sure. A wrong time sends a teammate into a hit they weren't ready for.

The third job is the winger on the puck side, the strong side. You are the first target.

While the other team still has the puck, you are up high, covering their defenceman at the point. That is your job in your own end, and it is not a mistake to be there. The moment your team wins the puck, leave the point and come down the boards to between the hash marks and the goal line.

Why that low? Low enough that the pass from behind or beside the net is short and safe. High enough that you are moving up the ice when you get it. If you sit up near the blue line instead, the pass is longer and flatter. It gets picked off, and it comes into your back.

So the trip from the point down to that spot, and the timing of it, is the job.

Now the mistake wingers make most. Arriving there and then standing still, flat against the boards, waiting.

It feels like you are doing your job, but you are useless there. You have no speed, so the pass arrives and you start from zero with a defenceman closing. And flat against the boards, your only way out is along them, the lane they are defending.

So arrive. Don't wait. Better a little late than a little early. Here is why. It is easy to speed up, and hard to stop and start again.

If your defenceman is fighting for the puck, come back toward them, to shorten the pass. If your defenceman has the puck and time, take a small loop away and come back into the lane at speed. Either way, keep your feet moving. The player standing still is the player who gets hit.

Then show a target. Stick on the ice, blade flat, angled at the passer. Check over your shoulder before it arrives, so you already know what you will do with it.

And don't take the pass with your chest to the boards. Open your hips to the ice, a step or two off the wall. Here is why. Facing the glass, all you can do is chip it blind. Open to the ice, you can skate, pass to the centre, pass back, or cut to the middle.

Once it is on your stick, never give it away at your own blue line. You have four options. Skate it up the ice. Pass to your centre. Pass back to your defenceman. Or, if nothing is open, protect it on the boards until help comes, or chip it off the glass and out. That last one is never a failure. A blind turnover is.

Which comes first is your coach's choice, and the two common answers point opposite ways. Some coaches teach skate first. Why? A winger skating up the ice with the puck can't throw it away, so it is the safer choice when your team's passing is shaky. Others teach pass first. Your first look is up the ice, before your feet commit. Why? In the two seconds after your team wins the puck, the other team is the least organised it will be. Both agree on one thing. Check your shoulder before the puck arrives. So ask which one your coach wants.

Protecting it means keeping the puck moving on your stick, with your body between the checker and the puck. Shield it. Don't pin it. Holding the puck against the boards to get a whistle is a delay-of-game penalty. And while you shield it, the same position. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the checker, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly.

Not every team plays the winger this way. In a flat or spread breakout, both wingers hold higher, near the blue line, and the team gets out with longer passes. In a stretch system, one winger leaves early up the ice. So ask which one you are in. A winger at the hash marks in a stretch system is as wrong as one holding high in a swing breakout.

The fourth job is the winger on the far side, the weak side. This job depends on the system more than any other.

In the swing breakout, you come across or up the middle as the second option. You are also the target for a pass across the ice. Then you get out ahead of the puck.

Some teams use a flying winger instead, sometimes called the slash. You leave early through the middle, or up the ice. Why would a team want that? It stretches the other team's defence and makes space behind you. The cost is that you are less able to help in your own end. And in a team that rims the puck a lot, you stay low on your own boards to collect the rims.

So ask which your team runs. Here is why it matters. A far winger who leaves early in a system that wanted them low is the reason the breakout has no second option.

The fifth job is the centre. You stay low.

Swing back down into your own end, underneath the puck, and offer a short, safe pass in the middle. Where exactly? Inside the faceoff dots and below the hash marks, in the inside half of the circle on the puck side.

There is a reason for each of those two edges. Below the hash marks, the pass is short, and you take it facing up the ice. Above them, you are level with their forwards, the lane closes, and the puck comes into your back.

And inside the dots, because the boards are your winger's ice. If you drift out there, one opponent covers the pair of you. If you can touch the glass, you are in the wrong job.

You are the bail-out. When the boards are sealed and your defenceman can't skate it round the net, the low centre keeps the breakout alive. Without you, your defenceman is down to a rim, a reverse to nobody, or a turnover.

The common mistake is leaving early for the rush. Then your defenceman is down to the boards or a clear, and the other team knows it.

So, better a little late than a little early. Late, you speed up onto the puck. Early, you have to stop and start again, and the play is already dead.

And swing so you leave with speed. Move away from the puck for a moment, then turn back into it, so you cross your blue line at full pace.

Some teams run a centre fly instead. The centre leaves early up the middle to stretch the ice, and the far winger drops in to take the low job. Both are legitimate. What never works is a centre flying while the wingers hold high near the blue line. Then nobody is underneath the puck at all. That is the breakdown you see most weekends. So ask your coach which one you run.

Let me pull the five jobs together. The partner, behind the net where the pass reaches, calling early. The strong-side winger, high until the puck is won, then down the boards, moving and open to the ice. The far winger, doing whatever your system asks. And the centre, low inside the dots, leaving with speed.

Sixth idea. The plays, and what tells you to pick each one.

There are nine plays to know. Teams call them by different names, so hear them as shapes, and then ask what your team calls them.

Up. A direct pass from the defenceman to the winger on the boards. When the lane is open, it is the quickest way out, and the right one. Take it when the forecheck has gone behind the net or into the middle, leaving the boards open.

Pass it firmly to the winger's stick, not into the boards behind them. Head up first, and aim where the winger will be, not where they were. The risk is a forechecker sealing the boards. Picked off at the hash marks, it is a shot from the top of the circle.

Wheel. The defenceman skates the puck themselves, behind the net and up the far side. You need two things, speed and space. A step on the first forechecker, and nobody waiting on the far side.

Cut tight to the net, so the net itself screens the forechecker off. If you cut wide, you give them a straight line to you. Come out of the turn with your head up and speed built. The risk is getting caught below your own goal line with the puck. If you are caught mid-wheel, the answer is a reverse.

Some teams have the partner stand in front of the net during a wheel, so a chasing forechecker has to go round the partner. Whether you can do that at all depends on your league. In some it is allowed. In others it is a penalty just for setting it, and in some you are expected to give the forechecker the ice. So check before your team builds a wheel around it.

Wherever it is allowed, get there early and stand still. Stick in tight and upright, and hands to yourself. No bump, no lean and no shove, and never anything into the forechecker's back. Never your stick across them. A stick held out sideways across the slot is a penalty, even if your feet never moved. A crash in front of the net can hurt even going slowly.

And wherever you play, never slide across into the forechecker's path. That is interference, and two minutes is only the start. If you step into a forechecker who never saw you coming, it can be a major penalty, and it can get you thrown out of the game and suspended.

Reverse. You send the puck back the way it came, usually a hard backhand off the boards behind you, into the space a forechecker has just left. Use it when the forechecker has over-committed and skated past you. Here is why it works. They can't stop, so the puck goes where they have just been.

Bank it hard and low, because a soft one dies in the corner and becomes a turnover. And the teammate arriving behind you shouts reverse, because the carrier is looking the other way. The risk is that you are putting the puck into space you can't see, behind your own net. If nobody is there, the other team has it, with your defence facing the wrong way.

Rim. A hard pass along the boards that follows the curve of the rink round to a teammate further along, usually the far winger. Use it when a forechecker has you sealed against the boards and there is no pass, or when you have no time. Here is why it works. A forechecker sealing you has committed to that wall, so the rim goes past them.

Low, flat, and hard enough to go all the way round. Aim it where a teammate can meet it, not just round. A rim gives the puck away more often than anything except a clear. That is coaching judgement, not a counted figure.

So a rim and a reverse are opposites. A forechecker sealing you, rim it onward. One who has skated past you, reverse it back. Reading which one you are facing is a core skill for a defenceman.

Both are made facing the wall with a forechecker coming in behind you. That is exactly the picture where players get hit from behind into the boards. So turn side-on to take it. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the checker, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly. And if you are caught square to the wall, get your stick and gloves up and spread on the glass, with your head off it.

In some adult recreational leagues, a big backswing turns your rim into a slap shot, which is a penalty there. If it hurts someone, it gets you thrown out of the game. So keep the backswing short.

D-to-D. A pass across to your partner. Here is why it works. A forecheck built to pressure one side has to travel across the ice, and while they travel, everything they had covered opens up. Use it when your side is crowded and the other isn't.

Pass it quickly. If you skate it across, you drag the pressure with you and hand your partner your problem. And always behind the net, never through the slot. Done that way it is one of the safest plays in hockey. Through the slot, it is one of the most dangerous.

Over. The word is used for two completely different plays. Some teams mean the risky pass across the front of your net. Most goalies mean the safe pass behind the net to your partner. If two players mean different things by it, one of them will put the puck through your own slot. So agree one meaning before the game. If over is your pass across the ice, your goalie says D or partner for the pass behind the net.

The cross-ice over is the gamble from the first idea. Against a forecheck piled onto one side, one firm, flat pass beats all three forecheckers. Picked off, it is in the middle of the ice, facing your goal. So where the route behind the net is open, take that instead.

Quick-up. In the two or three seconds after your team wins the puck, the other team is the least organised it will ever be. So look up the ice before you look down. If a teammate is open up there, move it on your first touch. A quick pass now beats a better pass five seconds later. The danger is the habit, forcing that pass into a defence that has already formed.

Stretch. A long pass to a forward who has left early and is waiting up the ice, near the far blue line. Look for it when all three of their forwards have come deep and their defencemen have stepped up. Why then? That shape leaves a lane, and leaves your winger behind their coverage.

But a picked-off stretch is a full-speed rush the other way, with your winger a whole zone out of the play. And few amateur players can make a flat pass that long. So in most amateur hockey it is not a good default.

The receiver has to stay onside. One skate touching the blue line, or behind it, keeps them onside. They are only offside if both skates are completely over before the puck completely crosses. So keep that skate on the ice. In some leagues a skate held up in the air over the line still counts, and in others it doesn't.

Off the glass and out. It gives the puck away, but it gets you out of trouble. It is always there, and never wrong when you are in trouble. Off the glass, not over it.

Seventh idea. Pick the play from the forecheck in front of you.

The right breakout isn't your favourite play. It is the one the forecheck in front of you leaves open. Forechecks are named by how many players are in each line, front to back. Which one a team runs is their coach's choice, and nobody has counted which is most common.

A one-two-two sends one forechecker to steer you, with two forwards behind. It takes away the middle, and often the pass to your partner. And its two forwards sit right where a pass up the boards wants to go.

So against it, you will usually have time. Use it. Skate at the first forechecker until they pick a side, and then the other side is open. Your low centre is the key, because their two middle forwards sit fairly high, and the space under them is real. Don't rim into it. A rim hands the puck to the forwards waiting on the boards.

Against the most patient version, the trap, be patient and regroup, rather than skate into their wall of players. Keep the puck moving while you do, because a referee can penalise a team for delaying the game.

A two-one-two sends two forwards in deep, one each side, with one in the middle. It takes away your time and both corners. So play fast and simple. A rushed pass across the ice is exactly the mistake it is built to force.

Reverse and rim come into their own. Why? Two forwards coming in hard have momentum, and momentum is what the reverse punishes. Make both plays side-on, as always. The wheel is dangerous here, because there is a second forechecker on the far side. And off the glass is a fair answer.

A one-three-one puts one forechecker on you and stretches three players across the middle of the ice, with one behind. It is less a forecheck than a wall. Every long pass is dead.

So skate it out. One forechecker can't stop a defenceman carrying with speed. And making the carrier a threat is what forces their wall to break shape.

Use short passes up the boards, not through the middle, because the boards are the softest part of their shape. That puts a receiver on the boards with pressure coming from behind, so the same position. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the checker, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly.

Chipping it past them and racing for it is not a defeat. Against this shape it is often your best option, because forcing a pass into three players gives them a rush the other way.

A full-ice press is all five of them pushing up, usually late when they are chasing a goal. One clean pass through the first layer beats it. But a turnover now is a chance against.

So beat the first forechecker with a short, safe play. Don't hunt the killer long pass. Off the glass is completely fair, and even icing it costs you less than a turnover. An icing costs you a faceoff in your end, often with the same tired legs. A turnover against a press can cost you a goal.

Sometimes they don't come at you at all. They wait in the middle of the ice, set and patient. If you chip it out, they have it. If you skate into them, they have it. So you need a set pattern, called a control breakout.

It works on two things. The four players without the puck all move, with speed. And the carrier knows where each of them will be, and picks whichever one the trap hasn't covered.

One coaching manual sets out three of them, and says to pick one and drill it until nobody has to think. Why? A team trying to hold all three runs none of them well. So learn your team's one, and where you stand in it.

In some patterns a winger is posted up, standing still on the boards on purpose. Even then, body open to the ice and head up. If contact comes, the same position. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the checker, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly.

And keep the puck moving behind your net while the pattern starts. In some adult leagues, sitting on it there with nobody pressuring you stops play for a faceoff in your end.

Let me pull the plays and the forechecks together. Up when the boards are open, wheel with a step, rim when sealed, reverse when they have gone past, D-to-D behind the net. Against a one-two-two, use the time and make the first forechecker commit. Against a two-one-two, fast and simple. Against a one-three-one, skate it out and go short up the boards. Against a press, beat the first layer first. And against a team waiting in the middle, run your set pattern, with everyone moving.

Eighth idea. Faceoffs in your own end.

Coaches call a faceoff in your own end the most set-up moment in hockey, so it is the best chance for a real set play.

If you win the draw, the classic play is a wheel off the faceoff. The centre pulls the puck back to a defenceman, who wheels behind the net. The far winger goes up the boards as the main target, and the centre curls low.

If the forecheck arrives at once, the defenceman reverses it to the centre swinging low. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the checker, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly. The reverse is why the centre swings low after a draw rather than sprinting up the ice.

Whatever your team runs, decide it before the puck drops. Look at your winger. Look at your partner. Know what happens next.

If you lose the draw, your first job is defence, not the breakout. Get to your coverage straight away. In the coverage this episode assumes, wingers go to the points. Why? A lost draw in your end usually goes back to the point for a shot. Never chase the puck as a group. That is exactly how the goal gets scored. And when you win it back, treat it as a quick-up. They have just committed to attack, and their shape is at its weakest.

Ninth idea. When the breakout breaks down.

Turned over on the boards or behind your net, the breakout is over. Defend first.

The nearest player pressures the puck straight away, so it can't be walked into the slot. Pressure means your stick on the puck, not a hit. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call. And if you can see their numbers, pull up and play the puck.

Everyone else gets back between the player they are covering and your net, with the slot filled. Which coverage you fall into is your team's call, so ask.

Why defend first? Trying the same exit again while three of them are still in your end is how one turnover becomes two. And when you win it back, go before they reset. They have just sent players low. Look up the ice before you look down.

Tenth idea. Your goalie's calls, and where you aim a rim.

Your goalie faces the play, with the clearest view of everything behind your defenceman's back. That view is worth nothing unless the words are agreed in advance and the calls come early.

Here are the usual ones. Time means nobody is near you, turn and make a play. Only say time when you are sure. A wrong time sends a teammate into a hit they weren't ready for.

Man on means pressure is arriving now, so don't turn into it. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the checker, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly.

Leave it means the goalie has stopped a rim behind the net and left it for you. Up, wheel and reverse name those plays. And over, to most goalies, means the safe pass behind the net. Agree your own words before the game.

Now where you aim a rim, and this one depends on your league. In the NHL, and under the rules hockey in Britain uses, your goalie may only play the puck behind the net inside a marked shape. It is called the trapezoid, narrow at the goal line and wider at the end boards. Outside it is a two-minute penalty. In Britain it isn't clear that every league enforces it, so play as though it counts, and ask your league.

Where it counts, your goalie can't go and get a rim that dies in the corner. A defenceman has to, under pressure, with the forecheck arriving.

There are two good rims. Hard and all the way round, to where your winger meets it in stride. Or tight and short, close enough behind the net for your goalie to stop it inside the trapezoid and leave it for you. The bad one is in between, dying in the corner where nobody can get it safely. Agree which of the two good ones your team wants.

And whoever goes into that corner goes in at an angle. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the checker, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly.

In leagues with no trapezoid, your goalie becomes a third puck-handler, who can play a rim anywhere behind the net and pass it to your defenceman. But once your goalie leaves the crease to get it, they can't sit on the puck to get a whistle. That is a penalty too. So don't aim a rim expecting your goalie to hold it for you. Look behind your net before your first shift to see whether the lines are painted, and ask if you aren't sure.

Let me pull those set moments together. Off a draw, decide the play before the puck drops. If you lose it, defend first. When the breakout breaks down, defend first, with your stick on the puck. Agree your goalie's calls. And aim your rims hard and all the way round, or short for your goalie, never into the corner.

Eleventh idea. When the numbers are uneven.

On the power play, when one of theirs is in the penalty box, it is the same breakout. You still have five skaters and five jobs. It is their forecheck that is a player short.

Power-play units often play four forwards, so one of the two defence jobs may be a forward's. Settle whose it is before the puck is behind your net, not while a forechecker is arriving.

Whether you use the extra time is your coach's choice. One way is to take the beat and leave with control, because a controlled exit gives you a controlled entry. The other is to go the instant you win it. Why? Four penalty killers falling back are at their most disorganised in the first two seconds. Ask which your coach wants on the power play specifically.

What changes is the clear. The team killing the penalty is often allowed to ice the puck. A man up, you never are. So don't fire it the length of the ice. Get it out off the glass and short of the red line, so it dies in the middle with a teammate skating onto it. Off the glass, not over it. Over the glass can be a penalty, and on a five-on-four that doesn't shorten your advantage. It takes it away.

With your goalie pulled for an extra skater, late in a game you are losing, it is a different problem. Now a mistake ends the game. Icing still applies to you, because six against five is not short. The controlled exit is the only right one.

Use the sixth skater low, as a short, safe pass, not standing in front of their net. The long clear is off the table, but the glass clear isn't. Aim it to die in the middle of the ice with a teammate skating onto it.

Twelfth idea. When an official's arm goes up.

A delayed penalty against the other team means the referee's arm is up. Play stops when they get control of the puck. So keep the puck. Don't clear it. The moment they control it, your free spell is over.

Make the safe play every time. D-to-D behind the net, back to the point, protect it in the corner, puck moving on your stick. If your goalie comes off for an extra skater, buy them time to reach the bench. And never make a blind pass back through your own crease with the net empty behind it. Putting it in your own net is the only way you can lose from there.

Pulling the goalie here is a coaching choice, not automatic. Plenty of recreational and youth teams don't, and often shouldn't. Why not? A slow goalie, a far bench, or a team that can't keep the puck for four seconds can turn a free attacker into an own goal. With no set routine, just keep the puck and let the whistle come.

On a delayed offside, the linesperson's arm is up because one of their players went into your end ahead of the puck. The raised arm is not a stoppage. The forecheck is still coming, so keep your head up and expect to be hit. Some leagues stop play if an attacker is about to hit you while you still have the puck. Others don't. And even where they do, the protection ends the instant you pass. A whistle stops the play, not a body already moving. So play it as an ordinary breakout under pressure. Take the extra look, with your head up. Don't stop and wait for a whistle. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the checker, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly.

Let me pull the special moments together. A man up, any clear goes off the glass, short of the red line. Goalie pulled, keep the puck. Arm up for a penalty, keep the puck and never pass blind through your crease. Arm up for offside, play on with your head up.

Let me pull it all together.

Never move the puck across the front of your own net. Go behind it. Keep the puck by default, and when you clear it, off the glass, never over it.

Look before you have the puck. Count the forecheckers, watch their feet, and decide early. Going back for the puck, angle in. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the checker, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly.

Know your job. Partner, be where the pass behind the net reaches you, and make the calls. Winger, come down the boards moving, open to the ice, and never give it away at your blue line. Centre, stay low inside the dots, and leave with speed. Then pick the play the forecheck leaves open, and aim your rims where your goalie can help.

Three questions for your coach. Which breakout do we run, and where do I stand in it? What does over mean on our bench? Do we pull the goalie on a delayed penalty? And two for your league. Does the trapezoid count here? And can our partner stand in front of the net on a wheel?

Go and ask. Then all five of you can run the same play, and now you know why each part of it works.
