===== SCRIPT START =====

This episode is about passing and receiving the puck. It takes its time. For each piece, I will tell you what to do and also why it works. If you know why something works, you can still make the right play when the ice looks a little different from the picture in your head.

Before any instructions, a few words you will hear all the way through. I will explain them now, so they don't get in the way later.

Forehand and backhand. Your forehand is the front of your stick blade, the curved side that cups the puck. Your backhand is the back of the blade.

Heel and toe. The heel of the blade is the end nearest the shaft. The toe is the far end, the tip.

The slot. This is the dangerous ice in front of the net, between the two faceoff circles.

The point. This is the area just inside the blue line where defencemen usually stand. It is an area, not the line itself.

The half-wall. This is the stretch of boards roughly level with the faceoff dot, between the corner and the point.

A lane. A passing lane is a clear line along the ice between the puck and a teammate, with no stick or skate in it.

The checker. In this episode, the checker means an opponent coming at you, whether they are after the puck or just coming at you. Coaches also say "your check" for the player you are covering. Here, the checker is always the one coming at you.

That is the vocabulary. Now the job.

A pass takes two players. One sends it, and one receives it. Most of this episode comes back to one of those two jobs.

I will start with four big ideas, because they matter most. If you only hear the first part of this episode, take those four with you. After that we will go deeper. First receiving, then the passes themselves, then the rink, one end at a time.

First idea. Move it, then move yourself.

The puck moves faster than any skater can. That one fact is the whole argument for passing.

When you pass, four or five opponents have to look again. Each one has to pick a new player to cover. When you carry the puck, they just skate alongside you.

That is why a team that moves the puck well can beat a team of better skaters. You can't out-skate five people. But you can out-pass them.

There is a second reason. When you carry, all the risk sits on one player, and that player is you. A completed pass makes the checker's work count for nothing.

So when the puck comes to you, your first thought is who is open. Not where can I skate.

That doesn't mean never carry. Carry when nobody is open. Carry when the defender in front of you has misjudged the space between you. Or carry to buy time while a teammate gets to you. Those are choices you make on purpose. The rest of the time, pass first, then skate into space.

Second idea. Look before the puck gets to you.

While a pass is travelling to you, look over both shoulders. Take the look then, not after the puck lands.

Here is why. When the puck lands, you already know where it is going next. If you wait until it arrives to look, a checker is already on you. And what you see is already out of date.

The best passers have decided where the puck is going before it reaches them. Players who look calm under pressure didn't think faster. They decided earlier.

The same goes for your head when you pass. Keep it up. Your eyes need to see the lane, the checker, and any hit that is coming. If you have to look down at the puck to pass it, your passing is only as good as your stickhandling. So work on handling the puck by feel, and keep your eyes for the ice.

Third idea. Pass to where your teammate is going, on the side away from the checker.

Here is the problem with passing to where a moving teammate is now. By the time the puck gets there, they have moved on. So it arrives behind them. They have to reach back, slow down, or turn. Every one of those kills the speed that made the play dangerous.

So lead them. Put the puck ahead, timed so they skate onto it without breaking stride. That is called passing into space. A pass right onto their blade, where it is now, is for a teammate who is standing still or about to shoot.

How do you judge the lead? Read their feet, not their chest. A player's chest can face anywhere. Their skates tell you where they are really going, and how fast.

Then pick a spot ahead of them, where their stick will be when the puck arrives. Pass to that spot. Don't try to follow a moving blade with your eyes. You are aiming at a place in the future.

The faster they skate, the more you lead them. The harder you pass, the less you lead them. And when in doubt, lead them a little. A pass slightly ahead can be skated onto. A pass behind can't.

Now the side. Given a choice, put the puck on their forehand. Put it away from the checker. And put it slightly in front, so their first touch goes forward.

If a defender is closing from their left, put the puck on their right. Then your teammate's body sits between the puck and the defender. You are passing them a shield as well as a puck.

Sometimes those three clash. When they do, away from the checker wins, even if that means their backhand. A backhand catch with time beats a forehand catch with a stick in your ribs.

And here is why leading matters for more than the play. In traffic, a puck behind your teammate or in their feet makes them look down to find it. That happens right as a checker arrives. Head down near the boards is how players get driven head first into them. So where you put the puck decides where your teammate's head is. Put it where they can take it with their head up.

Fourth idea. Never pass the puck across the front of your own goal.

In your own end, make only the passes you are sure will get there. Here is why. A pass picked off at their blue line costs you the puck. The same pass picked off in front of your own net is a scoring chance against you, and your goalie may not be set for it.

So there are three passes you don't make in your own end.

No blind pass up the middle. The middle of your own end is right in front of your net. If you can't see the receiver, the pass doesn't exist.

No pass from one defenceman to the other through the slot. That pass goes behind the net instead. A blocked or deflected pass in the slot is an easy goal against. Some teams also allow one higher up, above the tops of the faceoff circles.

No pass across the ice in your own end. If it is picked off, your whole team is on the wrong side of the puck. Some teams accept one above the tops of the circles, where a pick-off isn't a chance from the slot. Below them, almost nobody does.

These three are safe defaults, not laws of hockey. Some breakouts build in a planned pass between the defencemen, behind the net. That is a designed play, with eyes up, support set and a way out. It isn't a panicked pass across the ice under pressure. So treat the three as always on, unless your coach has designed the exception. Ask what passes your team allows in your own end.

One idea ties them together. In your own end, the standard is certainty. In their end, the standard is value. A mistake in front of your net is punished straight away. A mistake at the far end costs you the puck a long way from your goal. So the closer you are to your own net, the less risk you take.

Let me pull those four ideas together, because they are the core.

One. Move it, then move yourself. Pass first, and carry when nobody is open.

Two. Look over both shoulders while the puck is on its way to you.

Three. Lead your teammate, on the side away from the checker, because a puck in their feet puts their head down.

Four. Never pass across the front of your own goal. In your own end, make only the pass you are sure of.

Everything else in this episode adds detail to those four. We start with the other half of passing, the catch.

Fifth idea. Get open for the passer.

Passing gets practised. Receiving mostly gets assumed. That is backwards, because a pass is only as good as the touch that ends it. More pucks are lost receiving than passing.

When a pass goes wrong, people blame the passer. But most bad passes are really bad pairs. The receiver makes the pass as much as the passer does.

Think about what the passer is looking for. Someone who is open, in a clear lane, and ready now. If nobody is all three, there is no pass. And the player holding the puck usually gets the blame.

So, as the receiver, you owe the passer four things.

First, move into a lane, don't just move. Skating hard through ice that a defender's stick covers doesn't help. A player can have nobody near them and still not be an option. One stick lying across the only line to them is enough. Being unmarked and being available are different things, and the passer is looking for available.

Second, timing. If you get to the open space too early, a defender arrives with you. When you arrive matters as much as where.

Third, a target. Put your stick blade flat on the ice, out in front of your body, angled toward the passer. And hold it still. That blade is what the passer aims at. If it is off the ice or waving around, the passer has to guess, and guesses end up in your feet. A still blade on the ice says I am open and I want it now.

Then add your voice. Call for it. A called name and a still blade leave no doubt.

Fourth, a next option. Know where you are going once you have the puck. A catch with nowhere to go next isn't support. It is a trap.

And the passer owes the receiver three things back. A pass they can take in stride. A pass on the side away from pressure. And the pass at the moment the gap opens, not two seconds later, once the receiver has drifted into coverage.

So if your team can't complete passes, look at the players without the puck first. Then look at the passer.

Sixth idea. Cushion the puck.

This is the central receiving skill.

Meet the puck out in front of you, with your bottom hand pushing the stick away from your body. As the puck touches the blade, draw the blade back toward you. Let it travel with the puck for a few inches. You are soaking up the pass, not stopping it dead.

Hold the stick with a loose grip. That is what people mean by soft hands. A tight grip makes the blade stiff, and a stiff blade is a wall. A puck that hits a wall bounces off it.

Here is why it matters. A blocked pass bounces a foot or two off your blade. That foot is all a checker needs. A cushioned pass stops on your blade, under control and ready to play.

Where you can, take it on your forehand. Your forehand gives you a cupped blade, more of the blade under the puck, and a shot or a pass straight away. Usually you can choose, by turning your body a little before the puck arrives.

But don't let that make you late. Getting your blade to the puck matters more than which side it is on. And practise backhand catches on purpose, because in traffic you won't get to choose.

Seventh idea. Your first touch decides the play.

A good first touch buys you half a second. A bad one costs you the puck. Half a second sounds like nothing. On the ice, it is the difference between a shot and a blocked shot. It is the difference between a clean exit and a battle in your own corner.

The rule is simple. Take the puck where your next play needs it, not where it happens to land.

Here is what that looks like.

If a checker is closing from your left, cushion it slightly to your right. The catch and the escape become one movement, not two.

If you are in the slot, take it to beside your back foot, ready to shoot. Not into your feet.

If you are skating up the ice, take the touch forward. Then your next stride is a stride, not a stop.

And with nothing on in the middle, take it into open ice. Don't stop it under your feet and then look up. A puck under your feet has to be moved before you can play it.

Here is the test. After your first touch, could you pass or shoot straight away? If not, that touch was wasted. Every wasted touch is time you have handed to the defence.

Eighth idea. Receive on the move, with your body open to the ice.

Almost every real catch happens at speed.

Keep your feet moving through the catch. Gliding to catch a pass costs you a stride every single time. And a player standing still is a target.

Change your speed to meet the pass, rather than asking the pass to meet your speed. A small glide, or a hard extra stride, turns a difficult pass into an easy one. Nobody else even notices you did it.

Reach your blade out ahead of your skates, not beside them. If the puck reaches you beside your feet, you have no room to cushion it.

And turn to the puck with your feet first, then your hands. Don't let your stick pull your body off line. Reaching with your arms while your skates go somewhere else puts you off balance.

Now your body. Receive with your body open to the ice. Open means your hips and shoulders are turned so you can see up the ice and across it as the puck arrives. Not square to the passer, with your back to everything you want to do next.

Come to the puck on an angle, not straight at the passer. Curl into the catch, so your speed and your eyes already point where you want to go. Your next option should be in sight when the puck lands. If you have to turn to find it, you have given the checker the time they needed.

So decide which way you are going before the puck arrives, and be pointing that way when it does.

This matters most in two places. One is the half-wall in their end. The other is any breakout pass in your own end. There, a closed body lets the pressure arrive on your back.

And on the boards, pressure on your back is a hit from behind into the boards. Opening up is your first answer, because you see the checker coming instead of feeling them arrive.

But there is a second answer, for the moment of contact, and that one isn't a choice.

Never turn your back to the checker. And never duck. Keep your head up and your chin off your chest.

Both protect your head and neck. With your back turned, you can't see the hit coming or brace for it. If you duck, your head meets the boards first. That is true even going slowly, so a slow checker doesn't make it safe.

So here is the position you aim for. Skates parallel to the boards. Forearm and hip to the boards. Never the point of your shoulder, and not the whole shoulder if you can help it. Head up, chin off your chest.

If you are already facing the wall and can't turn, get your stick and gloves up on the glass, spread wide, with your head off it. That is only for when things have gone wrong. It isn't a position to aim for.

From here on I will use one short version of that position, the same words every time. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the checker, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly.

Ninth idea. Bad passes, and pucks in your feet.

The test of a good receiver is what they do with a bad pass.

First, get your body in front of it. Shin pads, skates and chest are all legal ways to stop a puck. A puck stopped by your body is still your puck. But this is a pass, not a shot. Stepping in front of a shot is a different skill, with its own technique and its own times not to try it. Nothing here carries over to it.

Second, stop it first, then look. Don't try to catch a difficult puck and make a play in the same motion. Stop it, then play it. Trying to do both is how bad passes become turnovers.

Third, accept losing half a second. If a bad pass costs you the play you wanted, take the safe next option. Forcing the first plan through a bad catch is the real mistake.

For a bouncing puck, or one on its edge, cup the blade over it. That means the top edge of the blade tilts forward over the puck. Use the same angle every time, hopping, on edge or flat. The cup comes from keeping your top hand off your hip. With your top hand on your hip, the blade opens, and the puck jumps right over it.

A flat puck takes one touch. A bouncing one usually takes two, one to knock it flat and one to play it. Plan for the extra beat.

Take it on the middle of the blade, not the toe. The toe has the least surface. And angle the blade so that if the puck squirts off, it goes somewhere safe, not into the slot.

A pass in your feet will happen too. Handling it is a skill, not bad luck.

Kick it forward to your stick with the inside of your skate. One deliberate, controlled kick to a spot in front of you, not a panicked scuff. Or open your hips, let it run through, and pick it up behind your stride. Turning your body a little is usually faster than reaching.

Don't reach back with your stick while you keep skating forward. That is how you end up bent over, off balance, and hit.

Send your skate at the puck, never at a player. A puck in your feet usually has an opponent's feet right beside it.

Kicking the puck is allowed anywhere on the ice. Kicking an opponent, or even trying to, is something else. In most leagues it is a match penalty, which throws you out of the game. Everywhere else, the referee can still throw you out.

And never use your skate to push a player either, even without a kick. In at least one league that alone is a major penalty, and you are thrown out of the game. So keep the kick small, deliberate, and at the puck.

One more thing about kicking. A puck you kick in off your own stick counts as a goal in some leagues and not in others. So kick it to your stick, get control, and then shoot it clearly. And never kick your own stick blade into the puck to shoot. That is called a kick shot. It won't count as a goal, and in at least one league it is a penalty too.

Tenth idea. Pucks in the air, and your hand.

Play a high puck down at chest height, never above your shoulders. Hitting the puck with your stick above shoulder height isn't allowed in any league. In at least one league, shoulder height is measured with you standing up straight. So crouching doesn't make a higher puck fair.

Keep playing until you hear the whistle. In most leagues the whistle waits to see which team gets the puck, so it can come late.

Now the part that matters most here. If your stick reaches an opponent above the shoulders, it isn't a puck rule any more. It is a penalty in every league. Every league has a way to throw you out of the game for it, and for a reckless one, some leagues always do.

Some leagues excuse an accident on a normal shooting follow-through. None excuses a wild swing at a bouncing puck, and some give no excuse at all. In at least one league there is no small penalty up there. Any contact above the shoulders is a major, and it ends your game unless it was an accident and nobody was hurt.

And you don't even have to touch anyone. In some leagues, carrying your stick up high near an opponent can be a penalty on its own. So play the puck at chest height, and keep your stick down near other players.

Now your hand. You can catch a puck out of the air, but put it straight down on the ice. If you close your hand on it and skate, even one stride, in most leagues that is a minor penalty.

If you knock the puck to a teammate with your hand, that is a hand pass. It is only allowed in your own end, inside your own blue line. Anywhere else, play stops. So keep the puck inside your blue line when you touch it and when your teammate gets it. Keep your teammate inside that line too. Then it is fine in every league.

In your own crease, the painted area in front of your net, never cover the puck, hold it or pick it up. Knock it straight down. Covering it there can give the other team a penalty shot, a free shot alone against your goalie. With your goalie pulled for an extra skater, it can give them a goal.

Let me pull receiving together. Get open, in a clear lane, on time. Show a still blade flat on the ice, and call for it. Cushion the puck with a loose grip. Take your first touch into your next play. Keep your feet moving, and come to the puck open to the ice.

If contact comes on the boards anyway, use the boards position. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the checker, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly. Kill a bad pass first, then play it. Kick the puck, never a player. And play a high puck down at chest height.

Now the passes themselves, one at a time. For each one, how to make it, when to use it, and what it costs when it goes wrong.

Eleventh idea. The flat pass, and why it is the default.

The flat pass, or sweep pass, is the default. Use it any time the lane is clear and your teammate can be reached along the ice. It is the easiest pass to catch, and that is the point.

Start the puck near the heel of the blade, with your bottom hand about halfway down the shaft. Cup the blade over the puck, so it stays flat. An open blade sends the puck up on its edge.

Sweep it, don't slap it. Your bottom hand pushes the puck along the ice in one motion. The puck rolls from heel to toe and leaves off the toe. A slap touches the puck for an instant, while a sweep guides it. So if your passes go wide, check first whether you are slapping.

Move your weight from your back leg, through your hips, to your front foot. The power comes from your body, not your arms. Passes made with just the arms float, and floaters get picked off.

Finish low, about knee height, with the blade pointing at your target. The puck leaves along the line the blade face points. Finishing at the target is how you get that line right. A high finish sends pucks over sticks and into shins.

The risk with a flat pass is almost never the technique. It is the lane you pick. A flat pass into a stick or a skate is a turnover, so a clear lane comes first.

Twelfth idea. The other passes, and when each one earns its place.

The saucer pass goes through the air, over something, and lands flat. Open the face of the blade slightly, like a wedge. Sweep from heel to toe, and roll your wrists up as it leaves. Drawing the blade slightly toward you puts spin on it. The spin is what makes it land flat.

Land it a few feet short of your teammate's stick, so it settles and slides the last bit. A saucer that is still in the air when it reaches the stick is nearly impossible to catch.

Use it only when it is the only option. A stick lying across the lane is one. A pass that has to cross a body is another. Never saucer when a flat pass is on, because air is a difficulty you are adding on purpose. And one that lands on its edge is worse than no pass, because your teammate commits to it and then loses it.

The hard pass, or snap pass, is a shorter sweep with a sharp snap of the wrists. You trade a little accuracy for a lot of speed. Use it when a lane is open but closing. One example is a gap a backchecker is about to fill. A backchecker is a forward racing back to defend. Another is a teammate about to be checked, who needs the puck early. It is also the pass into the slot for a one-timer, a shot taken straight off the pass.

Don't fire one at a teammate standing still with nobody near them. A hard pass your teammate can't handle is your turnover, not theirs. Pace is a tool for beating a closing gap, not a show of strength.

Then the backhand pass. Start the puck at the middle or heel of the blade on your backhand side, and roll it heel to toe, just as on the forehand. Pull with your bottom hand and sweep across your body. Drive through with your lower shoulder. The power comes from your body turning, not just your arms.

Here is why you need one. If you can only pass off your forehand, you have to turn to make half your plays, and you rarely have time to turn. Defenders learn it fast. They take your forehand away and force the turn, because the turn kills the play. A backhand pass doesn't need to look good. It needs to exist, so the checker can't cheat to one side.

A backhand is slower and floats more, so give it a slightly cleaner lane.

Thirteenth idea. Using the boards, with the bank, the rim and the reverse.

The boards can be a teammate. A bank pass goes off the boards to someone. Play the angle, not your teammate. The puck comes off the boards at about the mirror of the angle it goes in. So aim at the spot on the wall that bounces toward them.

Keep it flat and firm, and hit the boards low, where the bounce is truest. Boards bounce differently in every rink, so test a bank in the warm-up.

Use it when a stick or a body is in the direct lane, but the boards run behind them.

Here is why it is safe. The puck never goes through the lane the checker is guarding. It goes behind them, off a wall they can't cover. But safe isn't the same as reliable. The bounce isn't fully predictable, and your teammate might misread it too. So call it.

A rim and a reverse are opposite plays, and players mix them up.

A rim goes onward around the boards, the way the play is already going. It is hard, flat and low, following the curve of the rink. It beats a checker who has you sealed against the wall, because it goes past them.

A reverse goes back the way the puck came. It is short and firm, usually on your backhand, into the space behind you. It beats a checker who came in too hard and skated past you, because it goes into the space they just left.

How do you choose? Watch which way the checker is moving, not where they are standing. If they are still coming and have you pinned, rim it. If they have already gone by you and their feet point the wrong way, reverse it.

Both can go wrong. Call your rim, because a rim your teammate isn't expecting goes to the opponent beside them. And a soft rim dies in the corner.

Never reverse on hope. A reverse needs a teammate there. A reverse into nobody is a turnover you can't chase, because the puck goes one way and you go the other. And reverses usually happen deep in your own end, where a loose puck is a chance against you.

Rim or reverse, you are on the wall with a checker on you, so how you stand is part of the play. The natural way is square to the wall, head down, watching the puck onto your blade. Don't do it. Head down on the wall, digging for the puck, is exactly the picture of a hit from behind. And when you are the one arriving, play the puck, and never hit or push a player from behind.

Rim it in the boards position. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the checker, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly. Find the boards by feel, not by looking. And if you are caught facing the wall, get your stick and gloves up on the glass, spread wide, head off it. That is only for when things have gone wrong.

Fourteenth idea. The chip, the drop and the stretch. You make these passes on a read, never on a hope.

The chip, or area pass, aims at a patch of ice, not at a stick. Your teammate has to get there before an opponent does. Put it flat, hard enough to get past the nearest stick, but not so hard that it runs to the far boards.

The classic one is at their blue line. A defender is standing you up there. You chip it past them, into the space behind, where your winger is already skating.

Here is why it works. It can't be picked off like a normal pass, because it isn't aimed at anyone. It turns into a race. So only make it when your teammate can win that race, with more speed or a better angle. A chip into a defender's feet with nobody chasing just gives the puck away politely.

The drop pass is when you leave the puck behind you for a teammate following you. That teammate is called the trailer. The drop fails a lot, and it is worth knowing why.

Two things make it work, and both are usually missing. One. Leave the puck flat and dead. Don't push it back. A puck moving back at a trailer skating forward is nearly impossible to catch. Two. Keep skating hard, so you take a defender with you. If you slow down, nobody has been taken out of the play.

It also fails when the trailer isn't really there, or is level with you instead of behind. It fails when nobody said anything, so it surprises you both. And at their blue line, a failed drop sends them straight back at you, with all your forwards going the wrong way.

So if you are not sure the trailer is there, don't drop it. Keep it.

Some teams build a drop pass into how they enter the zone, on purpose. That is a coaching choice, so ask whether yours does.

The stretch pass is a long, hard pass, usually from a defenceman in or near their own end. It goes to a forward who has got behind the other team's forwards. It needs a full weight shift and a long follow-through. Most missed stretch passes are too soft, not off target.

It works best right after your team wins the puck back, while their forwards are still skating the wrong way.

If it connects, it is a breakaway or a two-on-one. If it misses, you usually lose the puck with your own forwards caught up the ice. That is how odd-man rushes against you start, where more of them attack than you have back.

So ask yourself honestly. Is a teammate actually behind their forwards right now, or am I hoping? Hope isn't a passing lane.

If you are the one catching the stretch pass, you have one job at their blue line. A pass can cross both blue lines and the red line at centre. What limits how far forward you can be is offside. Icing is a different rule, about sending the puck from your own half past their goal line.

You are offside if both your skates are completely over their blue line before the puck crosses it. So keep the blade of your back skate on the ice, touching the line or behind it, until the puck is over. That is onside in every league.

Let me pull the passes together. Flat is the default, and the lane comes first. A saucer only when something is in the way. A hard pass to beat a closing gap. Have a backhand. Bank it when a stick is in the lane, and call it. Rim past a checker who has you pinned, and reverse behind one who has skated past. Chip, drop and stretch only on a read. And at their blue line, keep your back skate on the ice.

Now the rink, one end at a time. These are the common approaches. Where coaches differ, I will say so, so you can ask which way your team plays.

Fifteenth idea. Your own end.

Breaking out first. Say you are a defenceman with the puck behind your own net. The common first option is a firm, flat pass to the winger on the puck side, up the boards. Put it on their forehand, or off the boards to them.

If that winger is covered, reverse it behind the net to your partner, or to the centre swinging low. If everything is covered, skate it or rim it hard. And never answer pressure there with a pass through the slot. Teams run different breakout patterns, so ask where your team wants the first pass.

If you are the winger taking that pass on the boards, it is the catch you already know. Come to it open to the ice, and decide your way out before it gets to you. Whoever has the puck on the boards, the defenceman rimming it or the winger taking it, if the hit comes, use the boards position. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the checker, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly.

Under pressure in your own end, make the pass you can complete. The pass you can definitely complete beats the pass you would like to complete.

Under pressure you have less time to look, the lanes close faster, and a mistake costs more. So keep it simple. You have a handful of safe tools. A rim. A bank. A give-and-go, where you pass to a teammate, skate straight into open space, and get it straight back. A chip into space. Or a reset back to your defence. All of them keep the puck moving without threading a needle.

Use the boards and the glass. They are always there, and nobody can intercept them.

A reset isn't a failure. Sending the puck back to your defenceman, or off the glass and out of your end, is a completed play. It is only a failure if you had a better option and didn't see it.

Buy extra time by moving your feet, not by standing still. A player standing still under pressure has one option, and it keeps shrinking.

And decide your way out while you are still skating to the puck, not after you are pinned.

Protecting a lead late in a period, take no chances with the puck in your own half. No pass across the middle of your own end. No soft pass through the middle at your own blue line. Get it out off the boards, or deep into their end. Make them come the whole length of the ice to score.

Sixteenth idea, and it is short. The middle of the ice.

The neutral zone is the middle of the rink, between the two blue lines. There, when a direct pass would have to thread through traffic, the chip is often the safe choice. Chip it into space for a teammate who can win the race.

When the way into their zone is closed, regroup. Pass it back to your defence and come again with speed. Don't force a pass into a crowd at their blue line. A regroup is a completed play.

Seventeenth idea. Their end.

A two-on-one with the puck, two of you against one defender. Make the defender choose. Drive at the net as if you are going to shoot. Pass only when the defender's stick leaves the lane.

Pass early and flat, to your partner's stick ahead of them, not at their skates. Then they can shoot before the goalie gets across. If the defender takes the pass away, shoot.

Drive in under control. Near the net, keep your head up and your chin off your chest, because the post can hurt you the same way the boards can. And never go into the goalie. A goalie is watching the puck, and one who is down or turned can't see you coming. Contact can hurt them, it can get you thrown out of the game, and it can cost your team the goal.

From behind their net, wait for a teammate to arrive in the slot. Pass to the spot they are skating into, flat and hard. A pass out front with nobody arriving is a turnover in the most dangerous place to have one.

From the half-wall up to the point, keep it hard, flat, and on the defenceman's forehand. A soft pass there is the one an opponent jumps on for a breakaway. If the lane isn't clean, cycle it down low instead, along the boards.

Then one-touch passing. A one-touch pass is one you send straight on, without stopping the puck first. A quick run of them is sometimes called tic-tac-toe.

Here is why it beats a defence. Defenders and goalies react to where the puck is. Every touch you take gives them time to reset. A one-touch moves the puck faster than a defence can shift. So it lands in a space that was covered when the pass started, and isn't covered when it arrives. That is why the best power plays are built on one-touch passing.

It is on when three things are true. The pass to you is flat and catchable. Your next target is already open, and you already know where. And the lane will still be there a moment later.

It is off for a bouncing puck, a target you haven't found yet, or a defender close enough that a miss is an instant turnover. A one-touch that fails usually fails badly, because you never secured the puck.

Get ready before the puck arrives. Pick your target while the puck is still coming. Set the angle of your blade before contact, because the blade face at contact is the pass. Keep a tiny cushion even then, soft on the catch and firm on the release. And set your feet, so you have a stable base.

Last in their end, fooling the defence. Defenders and goalies read your eyes, your shoulders and your blade.

Look at one teammate, let the defender shift toward them, then pass to the other. That is the safest fake there is. You have already looked, so you know where both teammates are, and moving your eyes costs nothing.

Turning your shoulders and hips one way pulls a defender's weight that way. Once a defender has committed their weight, they can't recover in time to stop a pass the other way.

Sometimes holding the puck a beat longer works better than any fake. A defender timing your release freezes, and there is nothing to read.

A no-look pass only makes sense when three things are true. You have already seen a teammate who isn't going to move. The defender is reading your eyes. And the pass is short. Never use one as a habit, and never in your own end. Your teammate may have moved since you looked, and then you have passed to a memory.

And a fake only helps if the pass after it is accurate. Get the passing right first.

Let me pull the rink together. In your own end, make the safe pass, decide it early, and never go through the slot. A reset is a completed play. In the middle, chip it when the lane is crowded, and regroup when the way in is closed. In their end, make the defender choose on a two-on-one. Drive the net under control, head up, and never into the goalie. Pass hard and flat to the point. One-touch it when the target is already open. And look off a defender before you pass.

Eighteenth idea, and it is short. How to practise.

Passing improves faster than almost any other skill. Most of it can be practised off the ice, on a smooth surface, with just a stick and a puck.

Pass against a wall or a rebounder. Receive every return properly. Cushion it, control it, reset. Half the value is in the catching. Spend as much time on your backhand as your forehand. The backhand improves fastest, because it starts furthest behind. Mark a small target on the wall and aim at it. Accuracy without a target is just repetition.

With a partner, skate down the ice side by side, leading each other into stride. That is the best drill there is for learning the lead. Have your partner send you bad passes on purpose, rolling, bouncing and behind you. Hardly anybody does it, and it is the fastest way to get better at the passes you will really get.

Then add a third player who pressures the passer, stick on the puck and not a hit. Clean passing in a quiet drill doesn't carry over to a game on its own.

Let me pull it all together.

Move it, then move yourself. Look over both shoulders while the puck is coming. Lead your teammate, on the side away from the checker, because a puck in their feet puts their head down. And never pass across the front of your own goal.

Get open, give a still target, and call for it. Cushion the puck, and take your first touch into your next play. Keep your feet moving, and receive open to the ice. If the hit comes on the boards anyway, use the boards position. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the checker, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly.

Flat is the default. Have a backhand. Rim or reverse by reading the checker. Chip, drop and stretch only on a read. Under pressure, make the pass you can complete, and decide before you get there.

Kick the puck, never a player. Play a high puck down at chest height, and keep your stick down near other players.

Much of this depends on what your team plays. So here are three questions to ask your coach. Which passes do we allow in our own end, and do our defencemen ever pass to each other above the circles? On the breakout, where do you want the first pass to go? And do we use a drop pass when we enter their zone?

Go and ask. Their answers tell you which passes your team wants, and now you know why each one makes sense.

### 7. Departures from the source, the template and the remake

- **Template shape adapted to a skill page.** Vocabulary, four big ideas and a recap, then three parts (receiving, the passes, the rink one end at a time), each with a recap, then practice and the close. "The rink one end at a time" is the fifteenth to seventeenth ideas, built from the source's "Passing in Common Situations" and "Passing Under Pressure".
- **No league questions in the close.** The brief asks for "three questions to ask your coach". The template also had two league questions; this script's only league-dependent instructions are framed so they are safe in every league.
- **Added from BC, not in the source:** "never the point of your shoulder" in the full posture and short form; the slow-speed limb; the net-front head-up limb and the goalie limb on the two-on-one; "drive in under control".
- **Added from the brief, not in any corpus document as worded:** the checker definition ("whether they are after the puck or just coming at you"), "never use your skate to push a player, even without a kick" as a standalone instruction, "stick on the puck and not a hit" in the pressure drill, and the "down or turned" goalie reason.
- **Added for the review (m6), not in the source's rim text:** "never hit or push a player from behind". The source's KT14 :905 and :221 name the hit; this states the instruction to the arriving player.
- **Glosses of mine:** forehand and backhand, backchecker, one-timer, odd-man rush, the neutral zone.
- **Softened:** "Nobody does this" (P :794) became "Hardly anybody does it". "Tap-in" became "an easy goal against".
- **From the remake, removed:** "Stop because you assumed a whistle, and it can be a goal against", which is not in the source. "If you are standing in your crease, treat it the same even when the puck is just outside", which over-reads the IIHF and Hockey Canada conditions. The book-count phrasing for the high stick ("no league caps it at a minor", "an injuring high stick is a major and a game misconduct, accident or not") was cut to one line that is not cheaper.
- **Item 16 scan:** no sentence opens on an act verb followed by ", and" plus a consequence. The remake's "Slow down, and nobody has been taken out of the play" is now "If you slow down…".

### 8. What I found unclear in the source

- **The two-on-one (P :713) sends the carrier to "drive at the net" with no goalie limb and no head-up limb.** The script borrows both from BC. ⚠️ The source should carry them, which is a plan row for the coordinator.
- **The source's checker definition (P :42, "the opponent pressuring you")** is narrower than the brief's item 20 wording. The fixed boards posture at P :541 and :553 relies on it. A reader could take "pressuring you" to mean only a player after the puck.
- **The source's posture (P :195, :542, :553) omits "never the point of your shoulder"** and the slow-speed limb, which BC owns. The script imports them. A plan row could add them to P.
- **The pressure-passing drill (P :787, :795)** has "a third player closing on the passer" with no contact limb.

### 9. Where this script is weakest

- **The gap between the spine recap (20%) and the receiving recap (52%) is about 13 minutes**, longer than the template's roughly eight.
- **The two-on-one limb is the least traceable text in the script.** Its reason is the brief's wording, and its nearest corpus line is about a different act.
- **"Everywhere else, the referee can still throw you out"** for a kick at an opponent rests on one book, the IIHF's discretionary major plus automatic game misconduct. It is true, but "can" carries the weight.
- **"In most leagues that is a minor penalty"** for closing a hand and skating "even one stride": "one stride" is explicit only in Hockey Canada and CARHA.
- **Backward and forward pointers were audited by hand for shape.** The remaining signposts are "From here on I will use one short version", "the catch you already know" (which restates the content) and the recap openers.
- **Median 10 and 50% at ten words or fewer** is plainer than the template. Some passages may sound clipped.
