===== SCRIPT START =====

This episode is about skating, and how you use it in a game. It takes its time. We will build it up one piece at a time, and for each piece I will tell you what to do and also why it works. If you know why something works, you can still do the right thing when the play looks a little different from the picture in your head.

Skating is the skill under every other skill. Passing, shooting and defending all happen while you skate, so how well you skate limits all of them. And skating is a technique. It gets better with practice, at any age.

Before any instructions, a few words you will hear all the way through. I will explain them now, so they don't get in the way later.

Edges. The bottom of a skate blade is not flat. It is ground with a groove down the middle, so each blade has two thin edges. The inside edge is on the big-toe side of your foot. The outside edge is on the little-toe side. Nearly everything you do on skates, you do with the blade tilted onto one of those edges.

The stride. This is your normal skating step, the push and glide that moves you up the ice.

A crossover. This is when you skate round a curve and your outside foot steps over in front of the other one. It is how you build speed on a turn.

A tight turn. This is a sharp turn in a small space. Both skates stay on the ice, with your feet side by side.

The hockey stop. This is the stop where you turn both skates sideways and let the blades scrape the ice.

A pivot. This is switching between skating forwards and skating backwards without stopping.

The boards. This is the wall around the rink.

The checker. In this episode, that means any player from the other team who comes at you near the boards, whether they are after the puck or just coming at you.

That is the vocabulary. Now the job.

When a teammate watches a good skater, they don't notice a pretty stride. They notice a player who gets there first, and arrives ready to do something. Most of that comes from the first few steps, and from refusing to skate at one speed. It does not come from top speed.

I will start with four big ideas, because they matter most. If you only listen to the first part of this episode, take those four with you. After that we will go back through the skills one at a time, and add the detail.

First idea. Go into the boards on an angle, with your head up.

It comes first because it matters more than anything else in this episode. You head into the boards all the time. You chase a puck into a corner, or a checker comes at you along the wall.

Go into the boards on an angle. Never straight at them. Keep your head up and your chin off your chest. Never go in head first.

Here is why the angle matters. On an angle, a leg, your side or an arm takes most of the impact, not your head. You also keep better control of the puck. And you choose your angle several strides before you get there, in the route you pick. So this is the part of a boards battle you control completely, long before anybody reaches you.

Now two things you never do near the boards. Never turn your back to the checker. And never duck.

Here is the reason behind both. You want to see what is coming, and take it on your side, not on your head or neck.

Say you arrive square to the wall with a checker closing. Turning to face the wall feels like protection. It is the opposite. If you turn your back, you can't see the hit coming, and you can't brace for it. And even a light hit from behind can hurt your head or neck. So a slow checker is not the safe case.

Ducking is the other danger. If you duck, your head meets the boards first. Even going slowly, meeting the boards head first can hurt your neck. No speed is slow enough to make it safe.

So both habits hold at every speed. They also hold in every league, whether or not anyone is meant to be hitting you.

If contact is coming anyway, here is the position you aim for. Skates parallel to the boards, which means pointing along them, side-on. Forearm and hip to the boards. Never the point of your shoulder, and not the whole shoulder if you can help it. Head up, chin off your chest.

If you are already facing the wall and can't turn in time, that is the last resort, not a choice. Get your stick and gloves up and spread wide on the glass, with your head off it. That is only for when things have already gone wrong.

From here on I will use one short version of that position, the same words every time, so you can learn it like a line. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the checker, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly.

Everything after the route you pick is about taking contact, not skating. That has its own episode, on body contact and battles.

Second idea. Win the first three steps.

Hockey isn't a sport of long, straight sprints. You are always starting, stopping, turning and starting again. A race to a loose puck or a rebound, a race for a step on a defender, or a race back when you have been turned around. Coaches find that nearly every one of those races is settled within about three strides. That is coaching know-how, not a measurement. But it is enough to act on.

Here is why it matters more than top speed. Game skating comes in short bursts, a second or two at a time. You are rarely at full speed when a race is decided. So your top speed rarely decides it. Your first three steps nearly always do.

Starting is a different technique from your stride, and this catches almost everybody. Your stride is long and uses the whole blade. A start is short, quick and on your toes.

Run the first few strides on the front of your blades, on the inside edges. Turn your feet out a little, so both blades bite. Lean your body forward, so you drive along the ice instead of lifting yourself up. Take short steps first, getting longer with each one. They look short, but your pushing leg still straightens fully every time. In a start, quick feet beat long reach.

The lean is your body only. Your eyes stay up.

Don't hop from a standstill when you start straight ahead. A hop spends your push going up, when you want to go forward.

To start sideways, or to turn back the other way, use a quick crossover start instead. That is the right tool at the start of a backcheck. A backcheck is racing back toward your own end after your team loses the puck.

And a start that chases a puck into a corner ends at the boards. So drive your three steps, then come in knees bent, head up, on an angle, and able to stop. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the checker, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly.

Third idea. Change speed on purpose, and keep your feet moving when the puck arrives.

A skater at one speed is easy to defend. The defender just copies your speed and holds it.

So glide to read the play, then explode out of the glide. Here is why that works. When you glide, the defender has to decide. Do they close in, or ease off? Then you explode, and whatever they chose, you have opened a gap between your speed and theirs. That difference is what gets you free. Not your top speed. That a change of speed beats top speed is coaching know-how, not a measured finding.

It works without the puck too. Drift, let the player marking you relax, then break.

And don't skate flat out the whole shift. If you do, you have no faster gear to change to, and no moment to get your head up.

The second half of this idea is about the puck. A lot of players take a pass and coast. They slow down at the very moment they needed speed. So keep your feet moving as the puck arrives. Skate first, and handle second. Push the puck into the space in front of you and skate onto it.

Fourth idea. When the play turns, stop and go straight back the other way, on either side.

The instant your team loses the puck, stop, and go back. Don't loop round in a big curve. A loop feels faster, but it isn't. It carries you away from the play for a second you don't get back.

That means you need to stop on both sides. Many players can only stop properly one way. Nobody has counted how many, so test your own weak side rather than assume you are fine. A player who can only stop one way loops instead, and ends up further from the puck.

Let me pull those four ideas together, because they are the core of skating in a game.

One. Go into the boards on an angle, head up, chin off your chest. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the checker, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly.

Two. Win the first three steps. Short, quick steps, body forward, eyes up, driving along the ice.

Three. Change speed on purpose, and keep your feet moving when the puck arrives.

Four. When the play turns, stop and go straight back, and be able to do it on either side.

Everything else in this episode adds detail to one of those four. So now let's go back through the skills, starting with your body.

Fifth idea. Stay low.

Bend your knees until they cover your toes. Looking straight down, your knees should hide your skates. Coaches mostly describe that as a knee bend of about a right angle, with your thighs heading toward flat.

Bend at your knees and hips, not at your waist. Chest up, backside back, like the bottom of a squat, not like reaching for your toes. Hunching forward kills your push and blocks your view. The one time you do lean forward on purpose is the hard start from the second idea.

Keep your weight on the balls of your feet, the pads just behind your toes. Not on your heels, because then you are slow to start and easy to knock backwards. And not up on your toes, because that is wobbly.

Here is why low matters so much. Standing too tall costs you four things at once.

Power. Your push comes from straightening a bent leg. A straight leg has nothing left to push with.

Balance. A low player is harder to knock off the puck.

Reaction time. From a deep bend, you can push off in any direction straight away. Standing tall, you have to bend first, and that moment can cost you the puck.

And edges. You can't tilt your blade onto a steep edge unless you bend your knee and ankle.

So stay low through the glide, through the turn and through the stop. Here is a simple check. If your thighs don't burn after a hard shift, you probably weren't low enough.

Sixth idea. Eyes up.

Not just chin up. Eyes up, scanning the ice. Keep them up as you start, as you turn, and when you have the puck.

Here is why. You can't read a play you aren't looking at. And your head pulls your body with it. If you look down at the puck, your weight tips forward off the balls of your feet. So a player skating with their head down is also skating with their weight in the wrong place.

There is a safety reason too. Skating head down is the habit that puts your head down just as you reach the wall. And meeting the boards or a goal post head down, head first, can hurt your neck even at walking speed.

Players drop their eyes most often in a turn, without thinking. Keep them up there too.

To fix it, pick something at the far end of the rink, and skate to it without looking at the puck. Keep the puck at the edge of your vision.

Seventh idea. Train your outside edges.

Your two edges do different jobs. Inside edges make power. Your stride, the first push of a crossover, your stops and your starts all come off them. Outside edges give you control. Turning, holding a curve, cutting back against pressure, protecting the puck and slipping a checker all come off them.

Most players are far better on their inside edges. Those are what you land on when you are upright and nervous. An outside edge asks you to lean your weight out over the skate, and that feels like falling until you trust it. So your outside edges are probably your biggest skating weakness, and your biggest room to improve. That is coaching know-how, not a counted finding, so test your own.

The drill is the outside-edge circle. Skate round a faceoff circle on one foot, on the outside edge of the foot nearer the middle. This is the hard one, and the one people fall on, so build up to it. Start at walking pace, on a wide curve, with your helmet on. Lean more only as the edge holds. Feeling wobbly means you are at the edge of your control, and that is where you improve. Feeling out of control means you have gone past it, so slow down.

Do edge work slowly and cleanly. One coaching source says ten to fifteen focused minutes, done often, beats a long, exhausting session now and then.

Let me pull your body together. Stay low, knees over your toes, chest up, weight on the balls of your feet. Keep your eyes up, not just your chin. And train your outside edges, starting slow, with your helmet on.

Eighth idea. Learn to fall, and learn to get up.

You will fall while you learn outside edges. Everyone does. You will fall long before you can stop, and far more often than you do anything fancy. A fall you have practised costs you a bruise. One you haven't can cost you a wrist or a head. It takes about five minutes to learn.

First, falling to the ice.

As you feel yourself going, get low. Bend your knees and sink, so there is less height to fall.

Go down on your side, onto your hip, your thigh and the side of your backside. Those are padded. Never land on your tailbone, your knees or a straight arm.

Keep your hands in. Never put a hand out to catch yourself on the ice. Here is why. A stiff arm puts all your weight through one wrist.

Slide with the fall, and don't fight it. Sliding spreads the bump out. Fighting the fall turns it into a crash.

Now your chin. This part changes with where you are falling, so listen to both halves.

Going down onto the ice, tuck your chin toward your chest. Do it most of all if you fall backwards. That stops your head snapping back onto the ice.

Sliding toward the boards, do the opposite. Head up, chin off your chest. Turn so your skates or your side meet the boards first. If you can't, get your arms out. Anything but your head first.

Here is why it changes. On the ice, a tucked chin keeps your head off the ice. Going into the boards, a tucked chin puts your neck first in line.

So, onto the ice, chin in. Into the boards, head up.

If you end up facing the boards with no time to turn, put your stick and gloves up on the glass, spread wide. Keep your head off it. That is only for when things have already gone wrong. It is not how you aim to meet the boards.

The idea behind all of it is the same. Spread the bump over as much of your body as you can.

Now getting up.

When you have fallen, stay down for two or three seconds first. Check that you are all right. Getting up fast off a fall you haven't checked can make an injury worse. That is coaching caution, not a medical finding, but do it every time. If you are hurt, stay down, and let play be stopped.

A fall can give you a concussion. A concussion is an injury to the brain, from a knock to the head, or a jolt that snaps the head. You don't have to be knocked out to have one.

If a concussion is suspected, come off straight away, and tell someone. You are off for the rest of that day. And you don't go back on the ice until a medical professional says you can, even if you feel fine. It isn't the player's call, because a concussed player will often say they are fine.

Afterwards, someone stays with them. They don't drive, and they don't go home on their own. If they feel worse, get medical help straight away.

After any knock to the head, treat it as a possible neck injury too. If a player is still down, don't help them up or move them, and leave their helmet on. Phone for an ambulance if anything looks serious, like being knocked out, a fit, neck pain, or numbness or tingling.

If you are all right, here is how to get up. Roll onto your hands and knees. Bring one skate up and plant it flat beside your hands, with your knee bent at about a right angle. That builds a base before you rise. Push down through that skate and your hands, and bring the other skate up beside it. Come up into your low stance, not standing tall, or you will just fall again.

Once you know you are not hurt, and nobody suspects a concussion, be quick. Two seconds on the ice, not six, can be the difference in a scoring chance. Check first, then hurry. Never the other way round.

Let me pull falling together. Onto the ice, get low, go to your side, hands in, chin in. Into the boards, head up, skates or side first. Facing the glass with no time to turn, stick and gloves up and spread wide. Then two or three seconds to check, and if a concussion is suspected, off for the day and not back until a medical professional says so.

Ninth idea. The stride, and where your power leaks out.

Push out to the side and slightly back, never straight back. A blade pushed straight back just slides along the ice. It grips because it is cutting across the way it is travelling.

Finish the push. Most players stop far too early. Straighten the pushing leg until it is done.

Then bring that foot back in under your hips, not down in front of you. A foot that lands in front of you brakes every stride. And bring it back quickly. Here is why. One small study filmed fast and slow skaters, and the fast ones got the foot back to the ice sooner. So speed is not just a bigger push. It is a faster return.

Between pushes, glide on one bent leg. If you straighten up in the glide, you have to bend again before the next push.

Out of a start, the same goes. After your first few steps, get off your toes onto the whole blade and let your stride get longer. A player who stays on their toes is always speeding up and never gliding. They tire, and they end up with a low top speed.

Two things here are genuine disagreements between coaches. The first is the toe flick. One coaching school says finish each push with a snap of the toe, out and back. Another calls the flick a mistake, and wants you to push through the heel and middle of the blade. The second is your arms. The most widely taught way is front to back, like a sprinter. Some coaches teach a side to side swing instead, to match the sideways push of your legs.

Both camps agree on what matters. Keep it rhythmic, in time with your legs, and keep your shoulders square up the ice. So use what your coach teaches. If nobody has taught you, start with front to back, because it is more widely taught, not because it won the argument.

Here is a free test of your stride. Skate from goal line to goal line at a comfortable pace, and count your strides. Then do it again, trying to use fewer. If the count drops a lot, you weren't finishing your push. Do it again every month.

Tenth idea. Cross over when you have room, turn tight when you don't.

These two get mixed up all the time. A crossover builds speed through a wide curve. A tight turn changes direction in the smallest space.

So with room and a need for speed, cross over. With no room, turn tight. Coming off the wall, escaping a checker, or cutting back on the forecheck, there is no room for a wide curve, so a crossover is the wrong tool. The forecheck is the pressure you put on in their end to win the puck back.

A crossover has two pushes, and most players only do one. The outside leg pushes hard on its inside edge, then crosses over in front. Meanwhile the inside leg pushes too. It pulls under your body and out the other side, on its outside edge. That second push is called the under-push, and it is the half most players are missing. If you just lift one foot over the other, you have switched off half your power.

To feel it, skate a few laps of a circle doing only the under-push. Never cross your outside foot over. To go faster round a turn, pull that leg back under you faster. Not wider, not harder. Faster.

Lean your whole body into the curve, from your ankles. Don't lean your upper body while your legs stay upright. Then your edges aren't working, and you are just falling sideways.

For a tight turn, sink lower. You can't make a tight turn standing up. Here is why. How tight you turn depends on how far you lean, and how far you can lean depends on how much you bend your knees and ankles. So sink, keep your chest up and your eyes up, and let your whole body go into the turn, not just your feet.

The turn that shakes off a checker is the cutback. With the puck along the wall, turn tight back against the way you were going. The checker who committed to your first direction is left behind. It is all outside edge. And because you are on the wall, the same line holds. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the checker, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly.

Almost everyone has a stronger crossover side and a weaker one, even professionals. Find out which is yours.

Here is why it matters. You drift toward turns on your strong side without noticing, and that makes you predictable. A winger with a weak side takes a worse route on the backcheck, because turning that way costs speed. A common guide is two laps on your weak side for every one on your strong side, kept up over a season. That is a coaching guide, not a tested best. Give your tight turns the same treatment, because they have a weak side too.

Eleventh idea. The stops, and starting out of them.

The hockey stop first. Turn your hips so both skates go sideways across the way you are moving, and let the blades scrape. Feet about shoulder width apart. Keep your knees bent, and sink lower as you stop, so your legs soak it up. Chest up, head facing forward.

Don't lean back away from the stop. That is the instinct, and it puts you on your backside.

The front skate works on its inside edge, and the back skate on its outside edge. Here is why both should bite. A stop on one edge leaves you off balance for the start out of it, and the start is the point of the stop.

If you can't do a hockey stop yet, use the snowplough. Point your toes in a little, press on both inside edges, and push the blades out. It is slow, and it leaves you out of position, but it always works.

A stop is only worth what comes after it. A good stop leaves you loaded. That means low, on your edges and balanced, so your first step out is immediate.

Now your weak side. Here is what it costs you. You take longer routes to avoid it, without noticing. You turn instead of stopping, which carries you further from the puck. You arrive at battles slowing down instead of set. And you lose the stop and go back race, one of the commonest races in the game.

Then practise the stop and the start as one movement. Stop, three explosive steps the other way, stop, three steps back. That one drill holds most of what hockey asks of your legs. And when you skate on your own, stop at every blue line and goal line, half of them on your weak side.

Let me pull the moving part together. Push out to the side, finish the push, and bring the foot back under you fast. Cross over with room, turn tight without it, and make your crossover use both pushes. Stop on both sides, low, and go straight out of it.

Twelfth idea. Skating backwards, and why it is a defender's skill.

Your gap is the space you keep between you and a puck carrier skating at you. Holding a tight gap is the core defensive skill, and it is a skating problem. Here is why. You can't hold a tight gap against a fast forward unless you can go backwards nearly as fast as they go forwards.

A defenceman who can't do that does one of two things. They leave a huge gap and get walked into their own end. Or they turn early to skate forwards, and get beaten by the first change of direction. Both look like bad decisions. Really, they are skating failures. How big a gap to keep belongs to the episode on defending the rush.

Your main backward push is the C-cut. One foot pushes out and curves back in, carving a C into the ice, while the other skate glides straight back under you. Push one foot at a time, all the way out, with your full weight on the pushing foot. Then bring it back under you, just like the forward stride. Pushing weakly with both feet at once is the slow way most beginners use.

Sit lower than you do going forwards, as if on a stool, with your knees covering your toes and your back straight. But don't tip forward at the waist. Sink your backside down and back, and let your chest come forward to balance it.

Your weight sits over the middle of the blade. Here is the check. If you feel the front of the blade digging in, you have leaned too far forward. And leaning too far forward kills your speed and control going backwards.

C-cuts are your cruising gear. When an attacker speeds up or attacks a lane, C-cuts alone won't keep up. That is what backward crossovers are for. Your outside leg crosses over, and your inside leg pulls under you and pushes out, finishing off the toe. Just like going forwards, that under-pull of the inside leg is the half most players are missing. For a defenceman, it is the difference between holding a gap and getting beaten wide. Keep your shoulders level and your hips open to the play, so you can still see the puck and the attacker.

Forwards need backward skating too. You use it reading the play while retreating through the middle of the ice. And you use it when you cover for a defenceman who pinches, stepping down from their blue line to keep the puck in.

Thirteenth idea. Pivot late, and know which way.

You are skating forwards and need to skate backwards, still facing the play. Stay square, going forwards, as long as you can. Here is why. Every moment you are going forwards is a moment you could still step up, which means moving forward to challenge the carrier.

If you step up, it is your stick on the puck, not a hit. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call, so find out. And if you can see the carrier's numbers, pull up and play the puck.

When you do pivot, open your hips first, not your shoulders. Your shoulders turning first spins your eyes off the carrier. Open your lead skate, put your weight on it, bring the other skate round, and start your C-cuts at once. And keep your knees bent through it. Players who stand up as they pivot are the ones who get beaten.

Then the part that holds for everyone. Pivot late. Once you have turned to skate forwards, you can't react to a change of direction any more. So stay backwards as long as you can.

The other way, from backwards to forwards, is for when the puck turns over and you need to go. Open your hip toward where you are going, plant, and drive out on your inside edge with a short, quick start. Most forwards lose a step here on a rush going the other way, so drill it both directions.

Now which way you pivot. The usual coaching default for a defenceman is toward the boards, not toward the middle.

Here is how it works, and why. The default is about what happens if you fail. If you get beaten in the pivot, you want to be beaten toward the wall. There, the boards act like a second defender and the shooting angle is poor. You don't want to be beaten into the slot, the ice in front of your net between the two faceoff circles. That is where goals come from.

How much the wall helps depends on how wide your rink is. On a wide rink, an attacker forced outside still has room to keep going. But on any rink, being beaten toward the wall is the better failure.

Being beaten toward the wall is not an invitation to ride the attacker into it, push them into it, or trip them into it. Driving a player into the boards is boarding. It is a penalty in every league, and it can get you thrown out of the game.

Pivoting toward the boards is a default, not a law. Some coaches want a pivot to the middle in certain situations. One is when turning away would take your eyes off the puck. Another is when you have help on the inside, and the bigger danger is the attacker cutting behind you. So ask your coach which they want. And drill both ways, because the attacker decides the right direction, not which way you happen to be good at.

Let me pull backwards together. Hold your gap with your feet, C-cuts for cruising and backward crossovers when they speed up. Sit low, weight over the middle of the blade. Open your hips, keep your knees bent, and pivot late. Pivot toward the boards by default, but ask your coach, and drill both ways.

Fourteenth idea. Using your skating in a game.

The same few situations come round every shift, and each one has a skating answer.

The loose puck. The race is the first three steps, so drive them. But how you arrive matters just as much. Come in knees bent, head up, on an angle to the boards, and able to stop. Here is why. A player who wins the race at full stretch has no balance left to protect the puck.

And because it ends at the boards, the same line again. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the checker, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly.

The turnover. When your team loses the puck, stop and go the other way. Don't loop.

The backcheck. Come back hard in your lane and pick up your attacker on the inside. That means goal side, between them and your net, not level with them. Get there with your skating. It is your stick on the puck, not a hit. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call. Never hit a player who can't see you coming, and never from behind. If you can see their numbers, pull up and play the puck.

Who takes which lane on the way back is a team choice. Commonly the centre takes the middle lane and the wingers take the outside. Some teams give the middle to whoever gets back first. Ask which yours plays.

Driving the net. Skate a straight line, stick on the ice, and pull up a skate's width outside the red line round the crease. The crease is the blue paint in front of the net. Here is why you stop there. Rebounds go to the player who has stopped and is ready, not to the one still gliding past the post.

Keep your skates off that red line as well as out of the blue, because some leagues count the line as part of the crease. And while the goalie is in the crease, keep your stick out of it too. In some leagues, a goal scored with a stick or skate in there doesn't count.

Pull up short of the goalie, and never go into the goalie. A goalie watching the puck can't see you coming or brace for you, and contact can hurt them. It can also be a penalty, and in some leagues it can get you thrown out of the game. And it can cost your team the goal.

At the net front, keep your head up and your chin off your chest. A goal post can hurt you the same way the boards can.

Getting open. When you want the puck in their end, find open ice and stop in it. Here is why. A stopped player gives your passer a target. A player circling through the space is open for an instant, and then gone. Keep your eyes up and your feet ready, because a stopped player is also easy to hit.

On a breakout, the play that gets the puck out of your own end, many teams want the opposite. They want you swinging through with speed, to take the pass on the move. Ask your bench which they want, and where.

The forecheck. Skate at where the carrier is going, not where they are. Here is why. If you chase straight at them, they turn away into open ice. Come at an angle that takes away the middle, and you steer them toward the wall, where the play gets smaller.

You steer with your skating and your stick, not your body. Pick your line early, hold it, and add nothing when you arrive. An arm, a shoulder, a hip or a late swerve turns the angle into a check. Stick on the puck, never on their hands or body. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call, so find out first. Never your stick across them, and never into their back. If you can see their numbers, pull up and play the puck. Even going slowly, a crash into the boards can hurt.

Who goes in first, and how hard, is your team's system. Ask what yours plays.

Let me pull the game together. Drive the first three steps to a loose puck, and arrive able to stop. When the puck turns over, stop and go back. Come back goal side on the backcheck, stick on the puck, never a hit on someone who can't see you.

At the net, stop short of the crease, skates off the red line, stick out while the goalie is in, never into the goalie, and head up. Stop in open ice to get the puck, unless your team wants you swinging. And on the forecheck, skate at where the carrier is going, with your stick, not your body.

Fifteenth idea. How to get better.

You can't see yourself skate. Nearly every fault in this episode is invisible from the inside. You honestly don't know whether you are finishing your push, or bringing your foot back under you. So get a coach to watch you. That is what power skating is, coached skating on its own, away from team practice. No coach? Have someone film you from the side and from behind. The faults are obvious on video.

Before you decide you have a technique problem, check you don't have an equipment problem. Boots that are too big, too soft, or not done up properly make a deep knee bend and hard edges impossible, because your foot moves inside the boot. Dull or uneven blades, or a groove you are not used to, change how every edge feels.

If you get thirty minutes of open ice, here is one sensible order. It is a coach's judgement, not a tested ranking, and a coach who has watched you may change it.

Edge work first. Then weak-side crossovers, forwards and backwards. Then stops and starts both ways. Then pivots, forwards to backwards and back again, down the length of the ice. Then full strides from goal line to goal line, counting them. Then all of that again with a puck. Long sprints at top speed aren't on the list. Game skating comes in short bursts, so edges, starts and pivots buy you more.

Why do it with a puck half the time? Because a puck changes your skating until you train it. Your arms stop, your head drops, your feet narrow, and you coast.

If your practice time is short, your position helps you choose. A centre gains most from pivots, turning both ways, and keeping good technique when tired. A winger gains most from the first three steps, straight-line speed, and switching from forechecking to backchecking. A defenceman gains most from backward skating, backward crossovers, pivots both ways, and moving sideways along the blue line. Those priorities assume a common setup, so ask your coach what your team's system asks of your feet. Goalies skate a different way altogether, and should train it with a goalie coach.

How long does it take? Stopping and turning both ways takes a few months of steady practice. Elite edges and fast pivots take years. That is one coaching provider's guidance, not a measurement. You won't get fast this month. But you can get noticeably steadier, lower and better balanced this month, and that alone changes how many battles you win.

Let me pull it all together.

Start at the boards. Go in on an angle, head up, chin off your chest. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the checker, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly.

Win the first three steps. Change speed on purpose, and keep your feet moving when the puck arrives. When the play turns, stop and go straight back, on either side.

Stay low, keep your eyes up, and train your outside edges.

When you fall onto the ice, go to your side, hands in, chin in. Into the boards, head up. Check yourself before you get up. If a concussion is suspected, come off, don't go back that day, and not until a medical professional says you can.

Push out to the side and bring the foot back fast. Cross over with room, turn tight without it. Stop both ways.

On defence, hold your gap with your feet, and pivot late.

In a game, arrive able to stop. Come back goal side. Stop short of the crease and never into the goalie. And steer with your skating and your stick, not your body.

Much of this depends on how your coach teaches and what your team plays. So here are three questions for your coach. Which way do you want me to pivot? When I want the puck, do you want me stopped or swinging? And which is my weak side, for stopping and for crossovers?

Go and ask. Their answers tell you how your team wants you to skate, and now you know why each answer makes sense.

### 7. Departures from the source, the template and the remake

- **The template's "rink one end at a time" is replaced by skills one part at a time** (body, falling, moving, backwards, in a game, getting better). Skating.md is organised by skill, not zone, and its game situations are one section (:814).
- **Fifteen ideas**, four as the spine, against the template's eighteen.
- **Falling has its own part, placed after outside edges** ("the one people fall on", :209), not after pivots as in the source. It follows the approved Basics placement and wording.
- **"The checker" is defined by the brief's wording**, not the remake's "an opponent coming to hit you or take the puck off you".
- **Two additional sources**: CR for the concussion definition, aftercare and neck-injury lines (brief item 10); BC for the four gates and limbs the brief requires and skating.md does not carry. Both are linked from S :7.
- **The goalie-contact reason** is traced to BC passages about backing into your own goalie, as the puck_handling Detailed script did. A strict §9 reader may want an attacker-side line.
- **The crease stick rule** is stated "in some leagues", unscoped from USA Hockey, and applied to everyone as a habit. Stricter, not cheaper.
- **The hockey-stop balance reason** is voiced as a reason; S labels it the document's own reasoning (:702).
- **Pagé's recovery finding** is voiced as "one small study", with no figures.
- **Position priorities** say "a common setup" instead of naming the 2-1-2 forecheck and the low zone collapse (:856).
- **The getting-open alternative has no "why"** because S gives none (decision 6 of the plan); it stops at how it works and "ask".
- **The remake's "two different injuries, and neither one is the lesser"** is replaced by the Basics' one shared reason, per the owner's "aware, not freaked out".

### 8. What I found unclear in the source

- S :823 (facts) scopes the crease stick rule to USA Hockey; S :833 says "some books count the red line as crease" for skates. The two limbs have different scopes and are easy to blur when heard.
- S :702 and :713 give the only reason for a two-edge stop as the document's own reasoning, while :698 states the edge assignment flatly. A listener hears the reason as fact.
- S :41 says what to do once contact is unavoidable "belongs to Body Contact and Battles", yet :137 and :662 teach the posture in full. The script follows the standard and teaches it once.
