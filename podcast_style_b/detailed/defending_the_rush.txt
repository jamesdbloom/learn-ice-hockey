===== SCRIPT START =====

This episode is about defending the rush. It takes its time. We will build the job up one piece at a time, and for each piece I will tell you what to do and also why it works. If you know why something works, you can still get it right when the play looks a little different.

Before any instructions, a few words you will hear all the way through. I will explain them now, so they don't get in the way later.

The rush. A rush is an attack that comes at you before your team is set up. Your goalie is still moving, nobody has picked up an attacker yet, and the attackers get to choose the play.

The carrier. That is the attacker with the puck.

Goal side. Being goal side means being on the line between an attacker and your own net. Some coaches call it the defensive side. It means the same thing.

The gap. Your gap is the distance between you and the carrier as they skate at you. Coaches often measure it in stick lengths.

The slot. This is the ice right in front of your net, between the two faceoff circles. Its top part, from the faceoff dots up to the tops of the circles, is called the high slot.

The three lanes. Picture the ice split lengthways into three strips. Two outside lanes run along the boards. The middle lane runs between the faceoff dots.

The numbers. A two-on-one means two attackers against one defender. The number always says the attackers first.

Backchecking. That is racing back toward your own end when your team loses the puck.

That is the vocabulary. Now the job.

Why does the rush deserve so much attention? Think about a shot in your own end when your team is set up. Your goalie is square to the puck, still, and already tracking it. Five of your players are between the shooter and the net.

On a rush, none of that is true. Your goalie is moving, pushing across or backing in, and a goalie who is moving when the shot comes can't be set. Being set is what makes ordinary saves ordinary. Nobody on your team has an attacker picked up yet. The attackers pick the play. And you are skating backwards while they skate forwards.

That is why coaches and analysts treat rush chances as the most dangerous in hockey, and published tracking backs them up. But a real odd-man rush, with more attackers than defenders, only comes a few times a game. So stopping a rush before it starts beats defending one well.

I will start with four big ideas, because they matter most. If you only listen to the first part of this episode, take those four with you. After that we will go back through the rush one stage at a time, and add the detail.

First idea. Stay goal side of every attacker, all the time.

Be between the puck and your net. Always. Every attacker, all the time.

If an attacker gets between you and your net, it has already gone wrong. Everything after that point is damage control. You are chasing a chance they already have.

Here is why this one idea matters so much. Almost everything else in this episode is the same idea in a different place. Keeping a tight gap is staying goal side while you are close. Backchecking to the inside of an attacker is getting back to goal side. Don't get beaten wide, and don't get beaten to the middle, are the same instruction.

So when a rush goes wrong and you are not sure why, this is nearly always the answer. Somebody lost goal side of somebody.

Second idea. Start skating backwards early, keep the gap closing all the way in, and watch the chest.

A big gap looks safe. It isn't. A big gap hands the carrier three things at once.

First, speed. They can accelerate into the space between you. By the time they reach you, they are moving faster than you are, and you are moving backwards.

Second, time to look. With lots of clear ice in front of them, they can lift their head, find the late attacker, find the far winger, and pick the best of three options. With a stick length of ice, all they can do is handle the puck.

Third, a shooting lane. A shot from distance with a clean lane is a real chance. A shot from distance through your shins is not.

Close the gap, and you take away all three at once. That is why coaches treat gap control as the most valuable habit in defensive hockey. It is one adjustment that removes speed, vision and the shot together.

Now the catch. You can't close a gap from a standstill.

Standing at your blue line waiting for the carrier feels patient. It is the opposite. When they arrive, they are at full speed and you are at zero. While you speed up backwards, they are gaining on you. So the gap opens at exactly the moment you most need it closed. Coaches call this the most common mistake defencemen make.

So start skating backwards early, while the other team is still picking up the puck in their own end. Get to their speed before they arrive, not after. Read how fast the attack is coming, not just where the puck is. A slow regroup lets you skate up and squeeze. A quick pass out of their end means you need to be moving already. And every time they turn back and come again, it is a new gap, so skate up into them again.

The shape you want is a gap that keeps closing the whole way in. A gap that shrinks forces the carrier to decide earlier than they wanted to. A gap that grows as they come at you gives them room to score.

And while you do all that, watch the chest and hips, not the puck.

A good attacker fakes with everything they can move. The puck, the stick, the hands, the shoulders, the head and the eyes can all lie to you. The chest and hips can't. The middle of the body has to point where the body is really going.

So fix your eyes on the numbers on the front of their jersey. You will still see the puck. You just won't be led by it. If the puck goes wide but the chest stays square, they haven't gone anywhere. Watching the puck is how you get beaten clean, because your feet follow your eyes, and your eyes have just been sent the wrong way.

Third idea. Take the middle, and give the carrier the wall.

The middle of the ice is the dangerous ice. Shots from the slot beat goalies. Shots from down along the boards, below the faceoff dots, mostly don't.

So don't line up chest to chest with the carrier. Sit slightly to the inside. Line up the shoulder nearer the boards with the middle of their chest. Now your body is between them and the middle of the ice, and the only way they can see to go is toward the boards. If you get it backwards, with your inside shoulder on their chest, your body slides toward the boards. That opens the middle, the very lane you wanted to take away.

Good rush defence is not taking everything away. It is choosing what to leave the carrier on purpose. You leave them the outside, and you take the middle.

Here is why the wall helps you. A carrier pushed to the boards has no ice on one side, a shrinking angle to the net, and nowhere to cut back. The boards are like a second defender. But they take away the carrier's ice, not the carrier.

So you do it with your route, the path you skate, not with your body. Steering a carrier toward the wall with your route is fine wherever you play. Don't shove them, don't lean on them, and never put your stick across them. Pin the puck, not the player.

Whether you may finish with your body at all is your league's call. Many youth, women's and adult leagues don't allow body checking, so check the game you are in, not just the age group. And even where checking is allowed, driving a carrier into the boards can be boarding, whether or not they saw you coming, and it can end your game.

Steering also turns a carrier round. They shield the puck on the boards, so by the time you arrive, their back is often half turned to you. If you can see the numbers on their back, pull up and play the puck. Never into anyone's back. Never lift anyone off their feet. Even going slowly, a crash into the boards can hurt.

Fourth idea. Two attackers coming at you, and you are the only defender. Take away the pass, and give up the shot. And say it out loud.

Here is why. Your goalie can save the shot. Your goalie can't save the pass.

On the shot, your goalie can see the puck the whole way, is square to the shooter, and is set. That is a save goalies are trained for and expect to make, even a good shot from the wing.

On a pass across the front of the net, your goalie has to push all the way across the crease, the goalie's painted area. The puck travels faster than they can move. They arrive late and off balance. A tap-in into an open half of the net is almost impossible to stop.

So the question is not which chance is better for the attackers. It is which chance your goalie can actually stop. One of them, and only one.

That makes it a deal between you and your goalie. You own the pass. Your goalie owns the shooter, and plays the carrier as if the second attacker isn't there. Neither of you covers both. If your goalie also cheats toward the pass, the shooter gets an open net. If you also drift toward the shooter, the pass is open. Both of you hedging is how a two-on-one becomes a goal. So shout, I've got the pass, and your goalie can commit fully to the shooter.

This is the common way, not the only way. Some teams time a slide across the passing lane instead, and some goalies would rather you pressure the puck. So settle it with your coach and your goalie before the game, not during it.

Let me pull those four ideas together, because they are the core of the job.

One. Stay goal side of every attacker, all the time.

Two. Start skating backwards early, keep the gap closing all the way in, and watch the chest, not the puck.

Three. Take the middle and give the carrier the wall, with your route, not your body. Pin the puck, not the player, and if you see their numbers, pull up.

Four. On a two-on-one, take away the pass, give up the shot, and tell your goalie the pass is yours.

Everything else in this episode adds detail to one of those four. So now let's go back through the rush, starting with the attack coming at you through the middle of the ice.

Fifth idea. How big should the gap be?

Here is a rough guide coaches use. About two to three stick lengths as the attack comes through the neutral zone, the middle of the rink between the two blue lines. About a stick and a half as the carrier crosses the red line at centre ice. And about one stick length at your own blue line. That is close enough to reach the puck with a poke check, a quick jab of your stick at the puck. And it is far enough that one fake doesn't put them past you.

Treat those numbers as a guide, not a law. They change with how fast you skate backwards and how skilled the carrier is, and the coaching sources don't agree on them. What matters is the shape. Closing, never growing.

How tight your team plays the gap is a coaching choice. A team that presses in the neutral zone wants you challenging at your blue line, because a press is about taking the puck back early. A team that plays a trap sits back and clogs the middle. It wants you keeping your distance and steering the carrier wide. So ask which your team wants.

Your blue line helps you here. An attacker can't go into your zone ahead of the puck. So if you squeeze tight at your line, the carrier has to slow down and wait for teammates, or come in alone.

Sixth idea. Your stick.

Your stick lets you take away options without committing your body. Keep the blade flat on the ice, out in front of you, in the lane between the carrier and the teammate they most want to pass to. One hand on the stick gives you reach when you need it. Two hands give you control. Either way, never with the blade in the air.

Only poke check when you can really reach the puck, not as a hope. A missed poke check is something the carrier skates around, and often a hooking or tripping penalty. And hide it until you use it. If the carrier sees it coming, they pull the puck back and go around your reach.

Seventh idea. Turn as late as you can.

At some point you have to turn and skate forwards with the carrier. That turn is called pivoting. Put it off as long as you possibly can. Here is why. Once you have turned to skate forwards, you can't react any more. You are committed to their direction, and a change of direction beats you.

When you do turn, let your hips lead, not your shoulders. Turning your upper body first opens the gate before your feet are ready.

Which way to turn is a coaching choice. The usual default is toward the boards side, not the middle. Here is why. If you are beaten, you want to be beaten toward the wall, not toward the slot. Some coaches want a turn toward the middle on certain reads, such as when you have a teammate inside you. So ask which your coach wants, and practise both ways.

Eighth idea. At your blue line, step up or back off. Never half.

There are two good ways to defend your line, and this is a real trade-off, not a right answer.

The first is stepping up. You stand your ground at or just inside your blue line and challenge the carrier as they try to come in. Here is what it gets you. It denies them the zone. They have to dump the puck in or turn back, and neither of those makes a chance. Here is what it costs. If you miss, there is nothing behind you, and your partner is left facing a two-on-one or worse. And a skilled carrier can chip the puck past you and skate onto it.

Stepping up is a challenge with your stick on the puck, not a hit. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call. And if the carrier turns and you see their numbers, pull up.

The second is backing off. You give ground while keeping your gap tight, and you defend them as they come in. Here is what it gets you. Nothing gets behind you. The worst case is that they enter with the puck, not a breakaway. Here is what it costs. You give up your blue line, so play happens in your end.

So when is each one right? Stepping up suits a slow attack, or a carrier with their head down or already near the boards. It suits a time when you clearly have support behind you, or your forwards have clogged the neutral zone. Backing off suits attackers with speed, a rush where they outnumber you, being the last defender, being unsure, and being tired.

Step up only when you are confident you will win the puck. Confident, not hopeful. How confident is enough is your coach's call, and it usually follows your team's neutral-zone system. A pressing team will accept the odd miss. A trapping team wants you back.

The worst choice of all is half of each. You drift forward, change your mind, and arrive late with a huge gap. That is worse than either. So pick one and go. When in doubt, back off with a tight gap. It has no disaster waiting at the end of it. And never back off with a gap that is growing. A soft retreat hands the carrier speed and the zone together.

A word about that carrier with their head down. It tells you they won't change direction, so your body can get there first. That is all it tells you. It isn't a licence to arrive harder. Building up speed into contact, even with two hard strides, can be charging. Charging counts whether your game allows checking or not, and it can end your game. Take the ice, not the player.

And keep stepping up and pinching apart in your head. Stepping up is challenging in the neutral zone or at your own line, with nothing behind you. Pinching is stepping down from the other team's blue line, with your forwards behind you. The pinch is the safer of the two.

When you do force a dump-in, the job isn't over. That puck still has to be picked up at your own boards, usually with an attacker chasing you. So decide how you will meet the wall before the race starts.

Here is the position. Skates parallel to the boards, so you are side-on. Forearm and hip to the boards. Never the point of your shoulder, and not the whole shoulder if you can help it. Head up. Chin off your chest. Never turn your back to the other player, whether they are after the puck or just coming at you. And never duck. Both protect your head and neck. If you turn your back, you can't see the hit coming or brace for it. If you duck, your head meets the boards first. Keep it up even going slowly, because a head-first knock into the boards can hurt at walking speed.

If you are already facing the wall and can't turn in time, there is a last resort. Get your stick and gloves up and spread on the glass, with your head off it. That is for when things have gone wrong, not a position to aim for.

From here on I will use one short version of that, the same words every time. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the other player, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly.

Let me pull the middle of the ice together. Keep the gap closing. The stick lengths are a guide, and how tight your team plays is your coach's call. Stick flat in the passing lane, and poke check only when you can reach the puck. Turn as late as you can, hips first. At your blue line, step up or back off, never half, and when in doubt, back off with a tight gap. And when you force a dump-in, plan how you meet the wall. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the other player, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly.

Ninth idea. Backchecking, and who owns the middle.

Backchecking is the difference between a rush where they outnumber you and an even one.

Remember the three lanes. The usual setup is the centre in the middle lane, and each winger in the outside lane on their side, picking up the attacker there.

The middle lane is the most dangerous one to leave empty. And the attacker skating hard through the middle without the puck is the one most often missed. Here is why. Nobody looks at a player without the puck, because eyes go to the puck. So that attacker arrives in the slot unmarked, at speed, with a clean look. If you are the centre coming back, that attacker is yours before anything else.

That setup is a coaching choice. Some teams give the middle to whoever gets back first, often when the centre is the one caught deep, and often in recreational hockey where lines are less fixed. Others have the backcheckers pick up man-on-man, starting from the puck and working outward. Nobody has counted which is most common, so ask which yours uses.

Whatever the system, two things hold. Somebody owns the middle, and nobody assumes it is somebody else.

Tenth idea. Skate first, then look, and come back on the inside.

When the puck turns over, the instinct is to turn your head and watch what happens. That costs you two strides you never get back. So turn and sprint first, and look on your third or fourth stride. The picture will still be there when you look up, and you will be several metres further into the play.

Now, where to skate. Chasing from behind an attacker's shoulder isn't backchecking. So take a route that puts you inside them, between them and your net, even if that route is a little longer. Arrive at their inside hip, not their back pocket. From there you are goal side, and your stick reaches the passing lane.

Take the ice, not the player. Get to the spot ahead of them and stay in the lane you picked, rather than veering into them. Keep your stick in the passing lane, not across them. A stick laid across any player is a penalty. And contact on a player without the puck is interference, which can cost far more than two minutes.

And never let the route end in their back. A backchecker arrives from behind, every time. Checking from behind counts whether the player has the puck or not. If you hit a player from behind, plan on being thrown out of the game. Into the boards, it can cost you more games too. Swinging wide to hit them from the side doesn't get you round it either.

If you really are beaten and can only chase, chase the stick, not the body. Get your blade under their stick or onto the puck. Keep it low on their shaft, lift up, not sideways, and let go. Never at their hands, never a chop, and never a two-handed shove. Reaching around their body from behind is a hook or a trip, and near your own net that gets expensive.

Eleventh idea. Your team's backchecking system decides your job.

Say your defenceman is already on the carrier and playing a good gap. Don't sprint in to help. That puts two of you on one attacker and leaves the player you were meant to pick up free. Your job as a backchecker is the player nobody has. Here is why. If each defender trusts the others to win their own one-on-one, a three-on-two turns into three one-on-ones.

Some teams coach the opposite on purpose. The first forward back pressures the carrier all the way in, and the defenceman holds the inside instead of engaging. That pressure is your stick on the puck, not a hit. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call, and if you see their numbers, pull up. Here is that system's reason. There is constant pressure on the puck, and the defenceman is never beaten wide, because they never have to commit.

What goes wrong under both is making it up in the middle of a rush, because nobody else on the ice expects it. So find out which your team plays.

Coaching material names four team backchecking systems, and they are different jobs, not different amounts of effort.

The first is defence early strike. It starts in your own end. Instead of sinking deep, your two defencemen tighten up early and close the ice around the other team's forwards. The ice, not the player. When the other team breaks out, the wide defenceman strikes across at the carrier, and the partner shifts inside to cover. Here is why a team plays it. It takes away the time the other team expected as they left their end. That strike is a challenge with your stick on the puck, not a hit. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call, and if you see their numbers, pull up.

The second is the wide-lane lock. The first forward back takes an outside lane and stays there, deeper than the attacker they are covering. One defenceman slides to the middle and owns it, and the other takes the carrier's side. The ice ends up in three parts, each with an owner.

The third is the midlane backcheck. All three forwards come back through the middle, which frees the defencemen to play the outside. The first forward back stays between the puck and the net, so if a defenceman is beaten inside, somebody is already there.

The fourth is hound the puck, and it is the opposite of the midlane backcheck. The first forward back chases the carrier as hard as they can, and keeps pressuring the puck even after a pass. The defencemen hold the inside and protect the ice between the dots.

Why pick one over another? The lock and the midlane backcheck both put bodies between the puck and your net, and both forgive somebody else's mistake. The price is that they give the carrier time. Hounding takes that time away. But it has a weak spot the others don't. When the forward can't catch the carrier, nobody is sure who has whom.

Good teams fix that with a hand-off rule. For example, the forward hounds the puck as far as the red line. If the forward isn't level with the carrier by then, the defence takes over, and the forward peels off to a lane. If your team hounds and has never said where the hand-off is, raise it at practice.

And hounding never means hitting. You are arriving at speed behind a player skating toward their own end boards, and that is exactly the picture the checking-from-behind rules are written for. Hounding is a challenge with your stick on the puck, not a hit. Take the puck, take the ice, or angle them off it. Never finish into their back. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call, and if you see their numbers, pull up. Either way, hounding is your feet, your stick and your angle, and nothing else.

Let me pull backchecking together. Sprint first, then look. Come back to the inside hip, and take the ice, not the player. Somebody owns the middle. Don't chase a carrier your defenceman already has, unless your team plays that way. Know your team's system and its hand-off. And never let a chase end in a player's back.

Twelfth idea. One-on-one, and one against two.

One-on-one is you against the carrier. It is your gap and your angle put together, and everything else is built from it.

Your target is not a hit, and it is not a turnover. It is a shot from a place your goalie is comfortable with. So steer the carrier to a bad angle, force them wide, and keep forcing. Keep your stick in the lane, on the ice, out front, because even in a one-on-one there are passing options arriving behind the play.

Don't lunge. A lunge is a single bet on their next move. If you guess wrong, you are out of the play and they have the whole net. Contain them, keep your feet, and let them run out of ice. Use the boards as your second defender, with your route. Pin the puck, not the player.

Your challenge is your stick on the puck, not a hit. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call. Even where you may, a body check is a bonus, not a plan, because missing a hit on a rush costs far more than not trying one. No shove, no lean, and never your stick across them. If you see their numbers, pull up. Never lift anyone off their feet. Even going slowly, a crash into the boards can hurt.

One attacker against two of you is the easy case, and teams waste it all the time. The mistake is both defenders going to the carrier. That opens the ice behind you for a late second attacker, and turns a comfortable situation into a scramble.

So one of you takes the puck, normally the one on the carrier's side, and plays it exactly like a one-on-one, with a very tight gap. The other stays back and inside, watching for the second wave. You are not a spectator. You are covering the late attacker who hasn't arrived yet.

The one on the puck can afford to be aggressive with the gap, because a miss only leaves your partner in a one-on-one. But what is safe is the gap, not the contact. Your challenge is your stick on the puck, not a hit. Having a partner behind you is no excuse for charging. Building up speed into contact, even with two hard strides, can be charging. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call, and if you see their numbers, pull up.

And call it. I've got them, from the one taking the puck, so the other one stays back.

Thirteenth idea. The two-on-one, in detail.

You already know the deal. You own the pass, your goalie owns the shooter. Here is how you do your half.

Sit in the passing lane, roughly on the line between the two attackers, a little toward the middle of the ice.

Put your stick flat on the ice on the passing side, blade out in the lane, arm long. Your stick takes the pass away, not your body. That way your feet stay free.

Back up at the speed of the attack, and stay goal side of both attackers. Don't back up so fast that you end up behind your own goal line. And don't stop, or the shooter walks in on top of your goalie.

Near the net the danger turns into a tap-in by the far attacker. So as the pass arrives, lift or press that attacker's stick, low on the shaft, well below their bottom hand, up or down, not sideways, then let go. Never hold their stick, never hook it, and never chop it, puck or no puck. Never at their hands. And never shove with your stick held in both hands. That is a cross-check, whatever your blade is doing.

Don't slide. Not until the pass has gone. A slide is a one-time commitment. If you go down before the pass, you have taken yourself out of the play, and both options are open. If the pass has gone and you can really reach it, sliding into the lane is right. Keep your head up and out of the shot lane, don't take their legs, and ask your coach to show you how to go down.

And don't try to block the shot. That means leaving the pass, and worse, a half block can deflect a puck your goalie was tracking cleanly.

There is one honest exception. If a backchecker has picked up the second attacker, and the shooter is walking into the slot with no real pass left, it isn't a two-on-one any more. Close on the puck, with your stick, not a hit. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call, and if you see their numbers, pull up.

Now the other way to play it, the timed slide. Instead of keeping the lane and living with the shot, you time a slide across the passing lane, to take the pass away completely and force the carrier to shoot. That is a different system, not a mistake.

Notice what doesn't change. Your goalie still owns the shooter, and you still own the pass. What changes is how you take the pass away, and what it costs if you get it wrong. If you go early, or slide past the lane, you are on the ice and out of the play, and you can't fight for the rebound.

If your team slides, your head stays up and out of the shot lane. This system exists to force a shot, so a shot is what you are going down in front of. Don't borrow the way you drop to block a shot. A slide across a passing lane is a different move, in a different direction. How you go down is the part that hurts you, and this episode doesn't teach it. So ask the coach who wants you to slide to show you exactly how.

A slide isn't free, either. If the attacker goes over your body, that is a trip, and touching the puck first won't always save you. A slide that catches them low, on the legs, can be clipping, which costs more. In some leagues a reckless one throws you out of the game even if nobody is hurt.

The worst outcome isn't picking the less popular system. It is you and your goalie having different answers. So ask your coach which one your team plays, and settle it with your goalie before the game.

Let me pull the first numbers together. One-on-one, steer them to a bad angle and let them run out of ice. One against two, one takes the puck and the other stays back. Two-on-one, keep the lane until the pass has gone, unless your team slides, and settle it with your goalie.

Fourteenth idea. The other numbers.

Two-on-two. It should be routine, but coaches say it is the one most often messed up, because even numbers make people relax.

The usual way is man-on-man. Each defender takes an attacker and keeps a tight gap on them. The alternative is zone. The defender on the puck side takes whoever carries the puck into their half, and the other holds the middle. Here is the difference. Zone copes better when the attackers cross. Man-on-man is tighter when they come straight. Find out which your team wants, because mixing them on one rush leaves an attacker free.

Either way, never both drift to the puck. The puck pulls you in. Two of you go to the carrier, they make a simple pass, and their partner has the net. If the attacker you are covering isn't the carrier, stay with them and let your partner do their job. The defender on the far side isn't free. Being ready to help is not the same as leaving your player.

And attackers cross on purpose, to make two defenders swap badly. So either follow your player through the crossing, or call a clean switch, out loud. Never half of each.

Three-on-two. You take the chaos out by agreeing beforehand which of two systems you are playing.

The first is two wide, middle to the backchecker. Each defenceman takes an outside attacker, and nobody chases the carrier. A carrier in the middle belongs to the backchecking centre. A carrier out wide belongs to the defenceman in that lane. If your backchecker really isn't coming, the defenceman on the puck side squeezes the middle and gives up the far attacker. But look before you assume it.

The second is strong side takes the puck, weak side splits. The defenceman on the puck side takes the carrier as a one-on-one, stick on the puck, not a hit. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call, and if you see their numbers, pull up. The other defenceman sits in the passing lane between the middle attacker and the far one, like a two-on-one.

Here is the trade. The first keeps three defenders on three attackers if the backchecker arrives, but gives up the middle if they don't. The second never gives up the middle, but one defender is covering two people. Two defencemen playing different versions on one rush is worse than either, so ask your coach which you play.

Whichever it is, back up together. Both defencemen go back at the same speed, level with each other across the ice, with the middle sealed between you. A staggered retreat opens a diagonal seam straight to the slot. And don't back all the way to the goal line. Collapsing into your own crease gives up the whole slot. Back up under control, and force the shot from outside.

Three-on-one. This is a bad situation. Take the middle, goal side of the puck, with your stick in the most dangerous lane. Your job changes from stopping it to making it take longer, because every extra second brings another backchecker. Back up slowly, and make them make an extra pass. Never commit to one attacker. If you go at the carrier, it turns into two attackers alone on your goalie. Give your goalie the shot from distance. And call for help, loudly.

Fifteenth idea. The late attacker, and two passes to watch for.

The player who arrives second is the one who gets forgotten. The trailer is the attacker following the rush a few metres behind. They arrive in the high slot just as the first wave has pulled everyone low, and they arrive with a clean look.

In the usual setup, the trailer belongs to the backchecking forwards, not the defencemen. Here is why. Your defencemen are busy with the first wave, and if they step up to the trailer, the first wave gets the net. Some teams give the trailer to the weak-side defenceman instead, and drop the third forward to the front of the net. That is tighter on the trailer and thinner in front. Ask which your team runs. A trailer covered by nobody and a trailer covered by two are both goals.

This is why skating first matters. A backchecker who is several metres behind can't catch the trailer. And if you are the last one back and everyone else is covered, the trailer is yours. Don't skate into the pile.

Defencemen, you will see the trailer first, so call it. Trailer, or high.

Now the drop pass. The carrier crosses your blue line, leaves the puck behind for a teammate, and keeps driving at the net, often straight through you. The common answer is that the defenceman on the carrier stays with the player driving the net. The dropped puck belongs to the backchecking forward who has the trailer. Here is why. If you follow the puck back, you leave the player at your net free, and you hand the shooter a screen. So stay goal side of the driver, stick in the lane to the slot, and shout drop. Keep your spot with your feet. No shove, no lean, never your stick across them, and never into their back.

Some teams have the defenceman nearest the dropped puck step to it, while the partner picks up the driver, with the switch called out loud. Ask which yours plays, because both defencemen going to the puck is the version that loses.

And the stretch pass. An attacker leaves early, and a long pass from deep in their end finds them behind your defence. The fix is getting depth before the pass, not speed after it. While they have the puck in their end, look over your shoulder for the forward leaving early, and start giving ground in time to keep them in front. Some teams would rather stand higher and accept the odd long pass, trusting the backcheck and the goalie. How high your defence stands is your coach's call, so ask.

Sixteenth idea. Chasing a breakaway.

A breakaway is an attacker alone with the puck against your goalie. This is your goalie's save, and goalies usually make it. Your job is to do the one useful thing that is legal, and then get out of the way.

The one useful thing is your stick on the puck. A poke at the puck itself, or a lift of their stick as they shoot. Keep a lift low on the shaft, up, not sideways, and let go. Never at their hands, never a chop, and never a two-handed shove. Skating to catch them is fine, and worth doing if you have the speed.

If you get alongside, angle them wide with your route. Turning a breakaway into a shot from a bad angle is a win. But the angle ends where contact begins. Steer with where you skate, and don't add an arm, a shoulder or a hip at the end. No shove, no lean, and never your stick across them. You are arriving from behind, so never into their back. If you see their numbers, pull up. Never lift anyone off their feet. Even going slowly, a crash into the boards or the net can hurt.

What you must not do is a hook, a trip, a hold or a slash from behind that takes away their chance. That gives the other team a penalty shot instead of two minutes, in every league, unless they choose the two minutes instead. In some leagues, a foul from the side and a little behind counts too. If your goalie has been pulled for an extra attacker, the same foul gives them a goal. With your net empty, in some leagues a foul from the front does it too.

Don't let the thought, I got the puck first, talk you into a reach or a slide. Touching the puck first can save you the penalty shot in some leagues, but you still take a tripping penalty there. And in some leagues it never saves a slide that takes someone down. If you catch them low, on the legs, it can be clipping. Sweeping their feet out from under them is slew-footing, and in most leagues that throws you out of the game.

So stay on your feet, and put your stick on the puck and nothing else. If your blade isn't touching the puck or their blade, you are probably committing a penalty. Take your goalie's odds over a penalty shot.

And don't stop and look at the referee. Play goes on until their team loses the puck, so keep defending.

One more desperate moment. The puck is in the air at your own crease. Knock it straight down and play it with your stick. Never close your hand on it, skate with it, or fall on it. Smothering the puck is your goalie's job. Anywhere, closing your hand on it can be a penalty. If the puck is in your crease and you fall on it or hold it, that is a penalty shot. So is throwing your stick at the puck or the carrier in your own end.

Let me pull the rest of the numbers together. Two-on-two, never both to the puck. Three-on-two, know your system and back up level. Three-on-one, delay. Somebody takes the trailer. And chasing a breakaway, stick on the puck.

Seventeenth idea. Talk.

A rush is loud and fast, and it comes at most of your team from behind them. Talking is the only way anyone knows what is happening. Keep it short, early and loud.

Three habits make your calls work. Call early. A call as it happens is information. A call after it happens is only commentary. Call the answer, not the problem. Back door, for an attacker arriving unmarked at the far post, tells a teammate where to go. Watch out doesn't. And everyone talks. Your goalie has the best view of the whole rush, and should be the loudest voice on the ice.

Eighteenth idea. When the rush is stopped, keep working until everyone has a player to cover.

Stopping the rush is only half of it. The seconds straight after are when good teams still give up goals. Everyone relaxes, and nobody picks anyone up.

So here is the order. First, notice when the attack loses its speed. A dump-in picked up in the corner, a shot saved, or a pass back out toward their blue line.

Second, find the front of your net before anything else. During a rush, nobody was assigned there.

Third, switch from lanes to players, from who is in my lane, to who is my player. Do it in about a second.

Fourth, backcheckers go to their spots in your team's own-end system, not to the nearest puck. In one common system, a winger who followed an attacker into the corner lets them go and gets back out toward the blue line. Under man-on-man, the winger stays. Those aren't the only two, so know yours.

Fifth, talk it through. I've got the corner. You take the point. Net front is mine.

A rush isn't over when the shot is saved. It is over when everybody has someone to cover.

Nineteenth idea, and the last. The best rush defence is not creating the rush.

Almost every rush where they outnumber you is made by your own team. Coaches point to five causes. That is what coaches stress, not a counted list, because nobody publishes data on what starts these rushes. But it is the list worth drilling.

One. A pinch that misses. A defenceman steps down at the other team's blue line to keep the puck in, and doesn't get it. The fix isn't, never pinch. It is, pinch on a read. Pinch when you will clearly win the puck, when a forward can cover for you, and when the score and time say it is worth it. A pinch is a challenge with your stick on the puck, not a hit. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call, and if you see their numbers, pull up.

Two. Forwards leaving early, hunting a breakaway pass before your team actually has the puck. When the puck doesn't come out, that forward is no use at either end. The test is whether you can still get back if you are wrong.

Three. Turnovers in the middle of the ice, the most direct cause of all. A turnover there hands the puck to a team already facing the right way. So don't stickhandle through the middle under pressure, and don't force passes across the neutral zone.

Four. A bad line change. Change when the puck is in their end, or heading away from yours. A change with the puck loose at your own blue line is how a three-on-two is born.

Five. Nobody left above the puck in their end, meaning nobody between the puck and the blue line. Nothing looks wrong until the puck turns over. It usually happens when both defencemen go deep at once. Some teams cover the line on purpose with whoever is highest, or with a winger. Unless your team plays that way, one of the two defencemen stays above the puck. So before you go below the puck, know who is above it.

Let me pull it all together.

Stay goal side of every attacker, all the time. When a rush goes wrong, somebody lost goal side of somebody.

Start skating backwards early, and keep the gap closing all the way in. Watch the chest, not the puck.

Take the middle and give the carrier the wall, with your route, not your body. Pin the puck, not the player. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call, and if you see their numbers, pull up.

On a two-on-one, take away the pass, give up the shot, and tell your goalie the pass is yours.

At your blue line, step up or back off, never half. When in doubt, back off with a tight gap.

On a backcheck, sprint first, come back to the inside, and make sure somebody owns the middle. Know your team's system. And never let a chase end in a player's back.

Know your job on every rush before it arrives. Chasing a breakaway, stick on the puck. Talk early. After the save, keep going until everyone has a player. And best of all, don't create the rush.

A lot of this depends on what your team plays. So here are three questions for your coach. Which backchecking system do we play, and where is the hand-off? On a two-on-one, do you want me in the lane or sliding? And on a three-on-two, is it two wide, or strong side on the puck? And one for your league. Is body checking allowed in this game?

Go and ask. Their answers tell you how your team wants you to defend the rush, and now you know why each answer makes sense.

### 7. Consequences checked against the source wording, never cheaper

- **"Driving a carrier into the boards can be boarding … and it can end your game."** No book caps boarding at a minor. Hockey Canada and USA Hockey eject on any major (:482, :500). "Can" is true everywhere.
- **"Charging … can end your game."** No book caps charging at a minor (:864, :889).
- **"A stick laid across any player is a penalty."** This replaces "a stick laid across a player without the puck is a penalty", which tied the ban to possession. A stick across the carrier is holding, hooking or a cross-check too (:569, BC :1448).
- **"Contact on a player without the puck is interference, which can cost far more than two minutes."** USA Hockey: "never only a two-minute risk" (:350). CARHA: a major and game misconduct at discretion (:352).
- **"If you hit a player from behind, plan on being thrown out of the game. Into the boards, it can cost you more games too."** This replaces the earlier "…into the boards, plan on being thrown out of the game, in every league", which the review flagged as an overreach.
  - The source (:442) says a forceful hit into a back at the wall ends your game in five of six books, and under the IIHF "plan on it". The NHL, PWHL and IIHF write no minor at all (:427), and Hockey Canada and CARHA eject at the bottom tier (:448).
  - A light push away from the boards is a minor plus misconduct under the USA Hockey Casebook (:446). "Plan on" is therefore conservative there, not a claim about every book.
  - "More games" is the match penalty: into the boards on a player who cannot protect themselves, it is mandatory under Hockey Canada and CARHA, and a match bars you from further games until the case is dealt with (:431, :450).
- **"A reckless [slide] throws you out of the game even if nobody is hurt, in some leagues."** USA Hockey 639(b) is mandatory; IIHF 44.3 is discretionary (:603, :605). "In some leagues" is accurate.
- **"Slew-footing … in most leagues throws you out."** It ejects at the floor in the NHL, PWHL, IIHF and USA Hockey, which is four of six (:740-741). Hockey Canada and CARHA can eject. "Most" is accurate and not an understatement (brief item 15).
- **"A penalty shot instead of two minutes, in every league, unless they choose the two minutes instead."** This is :42 and :696 together. The USA Hockey option is the fouled team's election, and the script says so.
- **Clipping** is "low, on the legs", never "at or below the knees" (brief item 17).
- **Charging** is "even with two hard strides" (brief item 12).
- **Spoken without any condition**: the stick-across ban, the never-into-a-back limb and the no-shove/no-lean limb. None is tied to possession (brief item 1).

### 8. Departures from the source and the template

- **The boards posture is imported from BC.** DTR points to it (:57, :71) but does not teach it. The remake left it out for that reason. The brief's item 5 requires it wherever a boards act is taught, and the retrieval after a forced dump-in is one.
- **"Never lift anyone off their feet"** is taken from BC :1384, which is about the net front. Brief item 11 says a borrowed limb must fit the new act. The reason BC gives, that a player whose edges are gone cannot brace or choose what meets the boards or post, holds for any contact. It is applied to steering, one-on-one and the breakaway angle, and the slow-speed limb carries the same reason.
- **"Up, not sideways" on the far attacker's stick** comes from BC's stick lift (:343, :358). DTR :569 says only "lift or press … low on the shaft … then let go". The script says "up or down, not sideways" to cover the press.
- **On the breakaway, DTR :706 says "a stick lift on their blade".** The script says "a lift of their stick … low on the shaft", which is BC's form and DTR :569's. It is not a different act, but the wording differs.
- **The ride in a checking league is not taught** (see §4). It is stricter than the source.
- **The item-13 gate**, "stick on the puck, not a hit; your league's call; pull up on the numbers", is added at every challenge. The source gates most of these acts, but not all in the same words or the same paragraph. It now appears ten times, which is the script's most repeated sentence (see §10).
- **"Three questions for your coach" plus one for the league.** The template had five plus two. The brief asks for three, and the league question is the checking gate.
- **The template's eighteen ideas become nineteen here.** The spine is still four ideas.
- **The source's "first man back" and "late man"** become "whoever gets back first" and "the late attacker". "Man-on-man" and "odd-man rush" are kept, per plan decision 12.
- **The source's "the point"** is replaced by "toward the blue line" in two places, because the term is not in the vocabulary block. It survives only in the spoken call "You take the point" (:1015), which is the source's own words.

### 9. What I found unclear in the source

- **KF :26 and :42 say a foul from behind on a breakaway is a penalty shot "in every book".** The carve-outs section (:729-:759) shows puck-first relief turns it into a minor in four books. Both are true, but the Overview sentence reads as unconditional. The script says the penalty shot "in every league", then "touching the puck first can save you the penalty shot in some leagues". That is consistent with the source as a whole.
- **"Pin the puck, not the player" (:16) against "In a checking league ride them wide along the wall" (:480, :495).** The Key focus reads as universal, while the body permits pinning the player in checking leagues. The script follows the Key focus. A reviewer may want the source to say which it means.
- **The source gives no reason for the two zone coverages after a rush** (:1014). It points to `defensive_zone_coverage.md`. The script says how each works and stops, as plan §A.2 requires.

The owner's "aware, not freaked out" may argue for fewer. Merging any two would put a challenge without its gate in the same unit.
- **"Never lift anyone off their feet"** appears three times: third idea, one-on-one and breakaway. On the breakaway the slow-speed sentence now says "a crash into the boards or the net can hurt", because a breakaway angle may end at either.
