===== SCRIPT START =====

This episode is about shooting the puck. It takes its time. We will build it up one piece at a time, and for each piece I will tell you what to do and also why it works. Most of it isn't about how hard you shoot. It's about where you shoot from, how quickly the shot gets away, and what the goalie can see.

Before any instructions, a few words you will hear all the way through. I will explain them now, so they don't get in the way later.

The slot. This is the ice in front of the net, between the two faceoff circles, from the edge of the crease out to the tops of the circles. Its top part is called the high slot.

The perimeter. This is the outside of the attacking zone, along the boards and up by the blue line. It is everything that isn't the middle.

The point. This is the area just inside the blue line where defencemen usually stand. And the half-wall is the stretch of boards roughly level with the faceoff dot.

The crease. This is the goalie's area, the blue paint in front of the net. The red line around its edge counts as part of the crease too.

A screen. That is a player standing in the goalie's line of sight, so the goalie can't see the shot coming.

A tip, or a deflection. That is changing the direction of a teammate's shot as it goes past you.

A rebound. That is a puck that bounces back out off the goalie after a save.

Release speed. That is how long your shooting motion takes, from your first movement until the puck leaves your stick. Shot speed is something else. It is how fast the puck travels after it leaves.

The wind-up, or the backswing. That is lifting your stick back before you bring it down to shoot.

And the lane. That is a clear path from your stick to the net.

That is the vocabulary. Now the job.

I will start with four big ideas, because they matter most. If you only listen to the first part of this episode, take those four with you. After that we will go back through the detail. What to check before you shoot. The shots themselves. The front of the net. And the big moments, like the rush and the breakaway.

First idea. Where you shoot from matters more than how you shoot.

A shot from the slot goes in several times as often as a shot from the perimeter. Not slightly more often. Several times. Coaches agree on that gap. The exact numbers you hear come from a source that doesn't say what data it used. So trust the gap, not the numbers.

That means the most useful part of shooting has nothing to do with your hands. Here is the habit. If a teammate is in the slot and you are not, pass it to them. If two strides toward the middle would turn your shot into a slot shot, take the two strides. One pass or two strides like that are worth more than any shooting technique.

Here is how big the gap is. One analysis looked at two of the best shooters in the professional game. One of them took over a fifth of their shots from above the faceoff circles. Less than one goal in fifty of theirs came from up there. If shooters that good can't score from there, you can't either.

So get to the middle, or get the puck to someone who is already there. That is what the whole attack is built to do.

Second idea. Get the shot away before the goalie is set.

Release speed matters far more than shot speed. Here is why. A goalie doesn't stop the puck by reacting to it. A goalie reads you. They watch your body, your hands, your stick and your weight. Then they get into position before the puck is even on its way.

If your shot arrives after the goalie is set and square to you, it's an easy save, however hard you hit it. If it arrives before they are set, it's a real chance, even if it isn't hard.

So stop gathering the puck. Most players settle the puck, look up, set their feet, and then shoot. Each of those steps is a warning to the goalie. Shoot the puck you already have, from where you already are.

And don't stop your feet. Stopping before you shoot is the clearest warning in hockey. Every goalie reads it, and every defender reads it too. Learn to shoot in stride, while you are still moving. It feels weaker at first, because you can't plant and load. Your momentum gives you the power back. And the goalie gets less warning.

Third idea. Make the goalie move sideways, or stop the goalie seeing the puck.

A goalie who sees the puck the whole way, set and square, saves almost everything. Take away either one, the sight or the set position, and the same shot from the same spot gets much more dangerous. And that difference is entirely under your team's control.

The first way is to make the goalie move. Picture an imaginary line down the middle of the ice, from net to net. Coaches call it the royal road, a name a former professional goalie made popular. When the puck crosses that line just before a shot, the goalie has to push across to get square again. While the goalie is still moving, they can't be set.

So get the puck across the middle before the shot. A pass across the slot. A pass between your two defencemen at the blue line. A pass from the corner out to the far point. Or come out from behind the net on the other side, so the goalie has to turn and find the puck again. Then shoot the moment it arrives.

The second way is to take the goalie's sight away, with a screen. Coaches and goalies agree that both of these work. Nobody has a trustworthy number for how much. So ignore any multiplier you hear. Take the direction, not a number.

Now how to screen, because the front of the net has rules and it has risks. Stand in the goalie's eyes, from outside the crease. Keep both skates out of the blue paint, and off the red line around it as well. Several rulebooks count that line as part of the crease. So off the line is the answer that holds wherever you play. Keep your stick on the ice, ready for a tip.

Take the goalie's eyes, never the goalie's room to move. Never touch the goalie. And never stand in the goalie's road back to the net. If the goalie has come out to play the puck, hold the ice you are already on, and don't move into their path home.

Now the part that keeps you safe there. You are standing still beside a steel goal post. Your eyes are on the shooter, not on the defender trying to move you. So know where the near post is before the shot. Take a shove braced, with a wide base, knees bent and weight low. Keep your head up and your chin off your chest, and never duck. If you do go into a post, meet it with an arm or a leg first, never your head.

That holds even going slowly, because a crash into a post can hurt you at walking speed. And it holds where body checking isn't allowed, because a defender clearing the front of the net moves you along the same path.

Hold your spot with where you stand. Don't lean on anyone or push back to keep it. Never put your stick across anyone, and never go into anyone's back. If the goalie leans into you, give ground straight away, then set the screen again. Never take the goalie braced.

Fourth idea. The second chance. Tip the puck, and follow every shot.

A goalie who has read a shot and got square can't read it again when it changes direction a few feet in front of them. And a goalie who has just made a save is getting back into position, not set. That is why tips and rebounds matter so much. Changing the puck's direction late is worth more than adding speed to it.

To tip a shot, put your blade in the puck's path, flat on the ice or just above it. Don't swing at the puck. Hold the blade at an angle and let the puck bounce off it. Open the blade face a little and the puck lifts. Close it and the puck stays low.

Hold the stick loosely, too. A stiff grip fires the puck off to the corner. A loose grip that gives a little drops it at your feet, so you get another go.

Tip at knee, waist or chest height, and keep the blade below everyone's shoulders. You are tipping in a crowd, so bring the blade down after the tip as well as before it. A stick that catches someone above the shoulders is a penalty in every league, and in every league it can cost more than two minutes.

Then the rebound. Follow every shot to the net, yours and your teammates', with your stick on the ice. Go in with your head up and your chin off your chest, and know where the post is. When the rebound comes, shoot it straight away. If the goalie is down, lift it over the pad. If you stop to look at it, the goalie is set again.

And play the puck, never the goalie. Arrive under control and pull up short. Building up speed into contact, even with two hard strides, can be charging. Any contact with a goalie can be a penalty, it can get you thrown out of the game, and it can cost your team the goal. A goalie who is turned away, or down, can't protect themselves, so contact can hurt them badly. If a defender shoves you toward the goalie, keep trying to avoid them anyway.

Let me pull those four ideas together, because they are the core of shooting.

One. Get to the slot, or pass to someone who is there.

Two. Shoot before the goalie is set. Don't gather the puck, and keep your feet moving.

Three. Get the puck across the middle first, or put a body in the goalie's eyes. Screen from outside the paint and off the red line. Never touch the goalie, and stay out of their road home. Know where the post is, take a shove braced, head up, chin off your chest.

Four. Tip it with soft hands and the blade below everyone's shoulders. Follow every shot, and pull up short of the goalie.

Everything else in this episode adds detail to one of those four. So now let's go back through it, starting with what you check before you shoot.

Fifth idea. Get the puck through, not just hard.

A shot that hits a defender's shin pad is worse than no shot at all. Here is why. You've given the puck away. The goalie hasn't had to do anything. And the block usually starts their attack the other way. That's three costs and nothing gained.

It happens because players decide to shoot before they check for a lane. So the fix is the order you do things in, not a new skill. Look for the lane first. If you can't see the net, or you can't see your teammates' sticks in front of it, there is no lane.

No lane? Change the angle. Don't shoot from the spot where you got the puck. Step sideways along the blue line until the defender blocking you has to move their feet. Their stick and body are set for where you were a second ago. Usually a step or two does it, and it costs you almost nothing.

Lift the puck just a few inches, to get it over sticks. A foot or more off the ice is too high to tip, and easy for the goalie to catch. A few inches is the whole target.

Or fake the shot. Drop a shoulder, and the defender drops into the lane. Then step round them and shoot for real. Sell the fake with your shoulders and eyes, not a big backswing. CARHA is the rulebook many Canadian adult rec leagues play, and in those leagues faking a slap shot can be a penalty on its own.

And if there's still no lane, don't shoot. Move the puck, across to your partner or down to the half-wall and back, until there is one.

If you're a defenceman at the point, your shot is almost never there to score on its own. It's there to make a tip, a rebound or a scramble in front. All of those need the puck to arrive. So shoot low and hard, at knee height or below, with enough pace that a small touch still carries it in. Getting it through beats shooting it hard. A high point shot is no use to a teammate trying to tip it.

If your winger is screening, the lane past them is also the goalie's line of sight they are blocking. So shoot through it on purpose. Say who you're shooting at first, and keep it low. Then your teammate knows it's coming and has their blade on the ice.

Sixth idea. Aim where the goalie isn't.

Almost every young player is told to aim for the corners. There is a coaching school that disagrees, and this episode follows it. Here is its reason. Corners are the smallest targets on the net. The goalie guards them with their quickest limbs. And a miss goes wide and out of the zone. So aim where the goalie is not, and where a miss still does something useful.

Plenty of good coaches still teach corners. So this is a choice between two ways of coaching, not a correction. Find out which one yours wants.

Here are the targets worth having. The order is coaching judgement, not a measurement.

First, the five-hole, the gap between the goalie's legs. Time it. It opens while the goalie is moving, reaching for a post, or dropping down. Against a goalie already down in the butterfly, on their knees with both pads out to the sides, it's mostly closed. That is the whole point of the butterfly. So don't fire at the five-hole when the goalie is already set down there.

Second, the far corner, low, straight after a pass across the ice. The goalie is still moving across, and the side they're moving away from opens up. That chance lasts only an instant.

Third, low on the blocker side, to make a rebound when a teammate is in front. The blocker is the flat pad on the goalie's stick hand. It kicks pucks back out into the slot.

Fourth, the gaps under the arms and over the pads.

And last, the short side high, the near top corner. Only when the goalie has left their post early. Not as your normal shot.

Now the big decision. With teammates in front of the net, shoot to create. Low, hard, on net, at the pads. Here is why. A shot at a corner has one good outcome. A low, hard shot at the pads can end as a goal, a tip or a rebound, in the most dangerous part of the ice. That is three good outcomes against one. But if you're alone in the slot and the goalie is beaten, pick your spot and finish. Be clear with yourself which of the two you are doing.

From a sharp angle below the faceoff dots, the goalie is on the post and the near side is closed. The common choice is a hard, low shot at the far pad, because the rebound kicks out to a teammate driving the net. The other choice is to carry it behind the net or pass it into the slot. If nobody is driving the net, that is usually the better play. Ask which your coach wants.

Seventh idea. Know when not to shoot.

Shooting first is a good habit. It means your first look is the net, and you take the good chance straight away. Forcing a shot is different. That means shooting just because you have the puck. A forced shot is a turnover with extra steps.

So here are the times not to shoot. There is no lane. A teammate is in the slot and you're not. The goalie is set and square, you're outside the faceoff dots, and nobody is in front of the net. You're the last player back, and a miss goes the other way. That is how an odd-man rush starts, more of them coming than you have back. Or your line is keeping the puck against a tired team, and five more seconds of it beats a bad shot.

And here is one test for every shot. Before you shoot, ask what happens if it doesn't go in. If the answer is a rebound for your winger, a tip, or a puck your team wins back in the corner, shoot. If the answer is that it hits a shin pad and they break out, don't.

Let me pull that part together. Look for the lane before you shoot. If there isn't one, step sideways, lift it a few inches, fake it, or move the puck. From the point, low and hard so it gets through. Aim where the goalie isn't. With bodies in front, shoot low at the pads to create. And before every shot, ask what happens if it doesn't go in.

Now the shots themselves. There are six things you can do to send a puck at the net. You will use three of them all the time.

Eighth idea. The wrist shot, the one everything else is built on.

Start the puck behind your back foot, the foot furthest from the net. Why back there? It gives you a runway. If you start it beside your foot, there's nothing to speed up through.

Hold the stick firmly, but not tightly. A white-knuckle grip locks your wrists, and the wrists are the whole shot.

Then pull, don't slap. Drag the puck forward along the ice in one sweep, keeping it on the blade the whole time. As you sweep, move your weight from your back foot to your front foot. The power comes from your legs and body, not your arms.

At the same time, push down with your bottom hand and pull back with your top hand. Pushing and pulling against each other bends the shaft, and that bend stores power. As the puck leaves, roll your wrists over so it spins. A spinning puck flies flat. A wobbling one goes wherever it likes.

Then follow through at the target. Finish low and the puck stays low. Finish high and it rises. You aim the height with where your follow-through ends up, not with your eyes. One study of wrist shots taken standing still found the angle of the blade as the puck leaves it doesn't predict the height. What did predict it was the way the blade turns through the shot. And you can aim a finish. You can't measure a blade angle in the middle of a shot.

Use the wrist shot almost anywhere you have a moment of time. Off the rush, in the slot, off a rebound, from the half-wall. It is your most accurate shot, and it needs the least room.

Ninth idea. The snap shot, which is probably your shot inside the faceoff dots.

The snap shot is quicker. The puck sits beside or just behind your skate, about where it is when you're stickhandling. Your chest faces the net. There's no long sweep and no wind-up. Here is why that helps. You are already in an attacking stance, so nothing about your body tells the goalie a shot is coming.

Move your bottom hand lower than for a wrist shot, to about the middle of the shaft. Drop the blade just behind the puck. Push down with your bottom hand and pull back with your top hand to load the shaft, like cocking a spring. Then release almost straight away. You get most of the power of a slap shot, with most of the quick release of a wrist shot. That is why, at higher levels, it has become the shot players use most.

Coaches disagree on one part. Most teach moving your weight from back foot to front foot, as on the wrist shot. Some teach shooting onto the leg nearest the puck instead, giving up a little power to gain time. So with a moment to spare, go back to front. Without one, don't waste a stride getting onto the right foot. Shoot off whichever foot your weight is already on. Every coach agrees on the thing to avoid. Never shoot flat-footed with your arms alone.

The snap shot won't beat a goalie from the blue line. From twenty feet, it will, again and again.

Tenth idea. Practise the backhand, the shot off the back of your blade.

Almost nobody practises it, and goalies find it harder to stop. Here is why it's hard for them. Goalies face far more forehands, so their timing is tuned to the forehand. A backhand comes off a different point, at a different moment, with less warning. Coaches see this, though nobody has counted it.

To shoot it, keep the puck close to your body. Hands a little wider apart than on the forehand. Sweep it across in an arc, not straight at the net, and follow through at the target. Use it in tight around the net, on breakaways, and on rebounds that land on your backhand side. Anywhere turning to your forehand would cost you the chance.

Eleventh idea. The slap shot, and why it is usually the wrong choice.

The slap shot is the hardest shot in hockey, and the one players use too often. It takes the longest to set up. It's the least accurate. And it's the easiest to read and block.

Here is how it works. Lift the stick back for the wind-up. Hands wide, with your bottom hand at the middle of the shaft or below. Then hit the ice an inch or two behind the puck, not the puck. That sounds wrong, so here is why. Hitting the ice bends the shaft, and the shaft springs the blade into the puck. If you hit the puck directly, you get an arm-powered slap with none of that stored power. That is why a beginner's slap shot is often slower than their wrist shot.

Follow through low, with the blade face square. From the point, a low shot is one a teammate can tip, and it leaves a rebound someone can play.

When is it right? From the point, when you have a lane and time and want the puck to arrive low and hard for a tip. On a set one-timer. That is roughly the list.

The trouble is the warning. A full wind-up is a warning to the goalie, and it tells the defender to drop into the lane. A miss from the point usually goes round the boards and out of the zone. So a three-quarter slap shot on net beats a full one that sails wide. And most of the time, from the point, a low, hard snap shot is the better play. It's nearly as heavy, much quicker, and much more likely to get through.

Now the leagues. Some take the slap shot away. In the Canadian adult rec leagues that play the CARHA rulebook, any slap shot is a penalty. A backswing of more than fifteen inches behind the puck counts as one. So a three-quarter wind-up counts too. If one hurts someone, it becomes a major penalty. That puts you out of the game and at least the next one. And faking a slap shot to scare someone is a penalty there as well.

So in a CARHA league, shoot a snap or a wrist shot, with your backswing under fifteen inches. Lots of other rec and youth leagues ban the slap shot by their own rules, often below a certain age. So read your league's rules before you plan around one.

Twelfth idea. Look before you wind up.

Any wind-up in traffic risks a high stick. That means your stick up above the shoulders. In some leagues that alone is a penalty, and catching someone with it is worse. So look before you wind up. In traffic, shorten it to a three-quarter wind-up, or in a CARHA league, shoot a snap or a wrist shot. Keep your blade below everyone's shoulders, all the way through the follow-through. That is the whole instruction, wherever you play.

Here is why it matters so much. A high stick on a person is a penalty in every league, and in every league it can cost more than two minutes. Only some rulebooks let off an accidental touch from a normal shooting motion. None let off a wild swing at a bouncing puck. In some leagues, one the referee judges reckless puts you out of the game, even if nobody is hurt.

And in a CARHA league, catching someone above the shoulders is a major penalty, even by accident. If you meant it, or it hurts them, you are out of the game. If you meant it, you miss at least the next game as well. So don't lean on any rule to excuse the swing. Keep the blade down.

Thirteenth idea. The one-timer.

A one-timer is shooting a pass straight away, without stopping it first. It's the quickest way to move a shot from one side of the ice to the other. And that is exactly what makes a goalie move sideways.

Set your feet before the pass arrives. That's the whole skill. If you're still skating when the pass gets there, you're receiving it, not one-timing it. Open your body to the passer, so the pass reaches your blade without going through your feet.

Start your backswing as the pass leaves your teammate's stick, not when it reaches you. If you wait to see the puck arrive, you're already late. And keep the backswing short. A half or three-quarter backswing misses far less often than a full one, and a missed one-timer is worse than no shot. In a CARHA league, a backswing over fifteen inches behind the puck makes it a slap shot and a penalty.

Most passes arrive a little wrong, so have a plan. A pass slightly ahead of you, reach out and take it off the end of your blade. A pass behind you, don't reach back and swing at it. Stop it, and take a snap shot half a second later. A bouncing or rolling puck, give up on the one-timer. Cup your blade over the puck and give with it, then shoot second. Swinging at a bouncing puck sends it into the corner. And if that swing catches someone, no rulebook lets you off.

Now the safety point. A one-timer is a wind-up taken in the slot, with bodies all round you. If there's a stick or a shoulder inside your swing, don't one-time it. Take the pass and shoot second. A snap shot on target beats a one-timer that catches someone's face.

Whether you're the one-timer option at all is your coach's call. Some power plays are built around a one-timer from the far side, and others aren't. Ask what your team runs.

So, which shot when. Inside the faceoff dots, the snap shot. With a moment to spare, or from further out, the wrist shot. In tight, or on your backhand side, the backhand. In front of the net, the tip. And the slap shot only from the point, with a lane and time, and never in a CARHA league.

Let me pull the shots together. The wrist shot is a sweep, with your weight moving forward, and your follow-through sets the height. Inside the dots, the snap shot, with no wind-up. Practise the backhand. Keep the slap shot for the point, where your league allows it, and hit the ice behind the puck. Look before you wind up, and keep the blade below everyone's shoulders. On a one-timer, set your feet early and keep the backswing short. If a stick or a shoulder is inside your swing, take the pass and shoot second.

Now back to the front of the net, because there is more to know there.

Fourteenth idea. The crease, in more detail.

A screen is a place you stand, not a shove. Even from outside the crease, if where you stand stops the goalie moving freely in their crease, your team can lose the goal. So leave the goalie room to move, and never crowd them.

Once you are in the crease, where you stand can cost your team the goal, even if you never touch the goalie. In some leagues, even a teammate's goal from outside won't count while your skate is in there. In others it can stand if you do nothing at all. The details vary, so the habit that works everywhere is to stay out.

Any contact you start with a goalie in the crease can cost the goal, even an accident. In some leagues, contact with the goalie costs the goal anywhere on the ice. And never touch a goalie to try to win a penalty. You'll take the penalty instead.

If a defender shoves you into the crease, get your feet out straight away. Keep your head up and your chin off your chest as you go, because the post is right there. Don't play the puck or touch the goalie on your way out. Being pushed in can excuse where you were standing. It doesn't excuse what you do next. And if you make no effort to avoid the goalie, you can still be penalised.

Stay out of the goalie's road home, too. A goalie scrambling back to the net is a player without the puck. Getting in their way is interference. That's usually two minutes, and more if you hurt them. In some leagues, stopping a goalie getting back can be a penalty with no contact at all. You are allowed the ice you are already standing on. Just don't move into their path.

Fifteenth idea. What goalie contact costs.

Never start contact with a goalie, anywhere on the ice. A goalie outside the crease is not fair game either.

Here is why it costs so much. Any contact with a goalie can be a penalty, it can get you thrown out of the game, and it can cost your team the goal. A goalie who is turned away, or down, can't protect themselves, so contact can hurt them badly.

If you charge a goalie, plan on being thrown out of the game. That can happen in every league, and in some it is automatic, even if nobody is hurt. Building up speed into contact, even with two hard strides, can be charging. So pull up early.

If the goalie has turned away from you, contact on their back is checking from behind. No league prices that at a bare two minutes, and in almost every league it puts you out of the game. If you drive a goalie who can't protect themselves into the net, some leagues make it an automatic match penalty. That goes past being thrown out.

If the contact reaches the head, that can put you out of the game in every league, and in some it is automatic. In some leagues even a careless hit does it. And being thrown out often isn't the end of it. In some leagues it also costs you the games after.

Let me pull the front of the net together. Screen from outside the paint and off the red line, and leave the goalie room to move. Never start contact with a goalie, in the crease or out of it. If you're shoved in, get your feet out first, and don't play the puck or touch the goalie on the way. Stay out of the road home. Head up, chin off your chest, and know where the post is. And pull up short of the goalie every time, because even two hard strides into contact can be charging.

Now the big moments. The rush, the two-on-one and the breakaway.

Sixteenth idea. Shooting off the rush.

A rush is when you're attacking with speed, before the defence is set. These are some of the best chances in the game, and most are wasted by shooting a beat too late.

As you carry the puck wide toward the net, the defender is skating backwards with their stick out toward the puck. There's a window, and it closes when that stick arrives. Shoot as the stick is closing, not after. If you wait for a good spot, you're shooting into a blocked lane from a bad angle.

Shoot off the foot you don't normally shoot off. Here is why. It gets the puck away earlier in your stride, at a moment the goalie doesn't expect a shot. It costs a little speed, and it's worth it.

But check which foot that is for you, because it depends on your wing. Coaches often say the outside foot, nearest the boards. That's right on your strong wing, say a left-handed shot on the left wing. On your off wing, your normal shooting foot is already the outside one. So there, the unusual foot is the inside one. If you get the label wrong, you just practise the habit you already have.

Don't stop your feet. The rush shot is the purest case for shooting in stride. On a wide rush, the goalie hugs the near post, so the far side, low, is open. If you cut to the middle, the five-hole opens as the goalie pushes across.

And know what's behind you. A trailer is a teammate arriving late, behind the play. If one is coming into the high slot, hold the puck a moment and pass back to them. That often beats your own shot from the wing. Here is why. It turns an outside shot into a slot shot.

Now the two-on-one, two of you against one defender, with the puck on your stick. Drive at the net as if you'll shoot, head up, so the defender has to choose. If the defender takes the pass away, shoot. The common choice is low at the far pad, because the rebound goes to your partner. If the defender comes at you, pass across early and flat, before the goalie can set.

That is the common approach. Some coaches want a shot every time instead. Others want the pass whenever the defender commits. Ask which yours wants. Either way, follow it in and pull up short of the goalie.

Seventeenth idea. Breakaways.

A breakaway is you, the puck and the goalie, with no defender close enough to stop you. Here is the one thing that decides it. A well-coached goalie's whole plan is to make you move first. They come out to meet you, back in with you to keep the angle, stay on their feet, and watch the puck. Your plan is the mirror image. Make the goalie move first.

But you can't just wait. If nobody commits, you run out of ice and lose. So you need ways to force it.

The first is your speed. Come in slower than you feel like. Skate hard to the blue line, ease off through the tops of the circles, then speed up again into your move. Here is why. At full speed you get about one option, and the goalie gets an easy read on where you'll be. The slow-down pulls the goalie out of their rhythm. The speed-up arrives before they have reset.

A move also needs room, and you can't pull the puck across your body once you are past the post. That change of pace beating raw speed is coaching know-how, not a measured finding.

The second is fooling the goalie with the puck, not just your head. A good goalie ignores head and shoulder fakes first. So change where the puck is, which forces a real re-read. Look at one corner and put the puck in the other. Sell a shot, and if the goalie gets big and stops moving, that is the moment to deke, which means a fake with the puck. And don't decide your move at the blue line. Decide once you can see what the goalie is giving you.

Then match the move to the goalie. Out of the crease and coming at you, deke. Go forehand to backhand, round them, because they can't get back across from out there. Go round them, never through them. If it goes wrong, pull up short of the goalie, head up. Contact with a goalie can get you thrown out of the game.

Deep in the net and square, shoot. A deke against a deep goalie just walks you into the post. Backing in and not yet square, shoot early. Pushing across, go five-hole, because it opens during the push. Stabbing at the puck with their stick, pull it back inside the stick, then shoot. And if you're being caught from behind, shoot now. A backchecker's stick arrives sooner than you think.

Have one move you can do without thinking. Then add a second that goes the other way.

On a breakaway, blocker side high is the classic target. It's the hardest place for a goalie to get a hand to while backing in. Never the middle of the chest. That is where the puck goes when you shoot without looking. So in the last two strides, look at the goalie, not the puck. You can't aim at a hole you haven't seen.

At rec level, shoot more than you deke. Rec goalies are more often deep and square, and a deke against a deep goalie has nowhere to go. That's what coaches see, not a count.

Eighteenth idea. Penalty shots and shootouts.

A penalty shot is a breakaway the referee gives you, from centre ice. A shootout is a series of them, to settle a tied game.

On these, plan on one shot. Keep the puck moving toward the goal the whole way. You can't stop, or circle back to set yourself again. And a rebound almost never counts. So the first shot has to be the one. The main rulebooks set no time limit on the way in, so take your time. Just keep the puck moving.

Leave out the spin-o-rama, a full turn on the way in, unless you know your league allows it. In some leagues it stops the shot and there's no goal. The lacrosse-style move means lifting the puck onto your blade and whipping it in. If you try it, keep the puck below your shoulders, because that limit applies in every league. And release it below the crossbar.

Shootout formats differ between competitions. Some let the same player shoot again, and some don't. Rec and youth leagues often run their own. So ask before the game. If the run-up is shorter, lean toward shooting, because you have less room to change pace.

Let me pull the big moments together. Off the rush, shoot as the defender's stick closes, in stride, off the foot you don't normally use. Know where the trailer is. On a two-on-one, drive the net and make the defender choose. On a breakaway, come in slower, make the goalie move first, and look at the goalie in the last two strides. On a penalty shot, keep the puck moving and plan on one shot.

Last part. Practice and your stick.

Nineteenth idea. How to practise.

You can practise most of shooting alone, off the ice, with a shooting pad, a net and a bucket of pucks. Do two kinds of practice. Lots of repetitions build the motion. Focused practice builds the shot you'll actually use. That means a named target and a reason for every shot.

So pick a target, call the shot out loud, take it, and keep count. Practise the release, not the power. Make a rule, no gathering. The puck arrives, you shoot. Move the puck sideways before you shoot, because you won't get a still puck in a game. Practise off both feet, including the one you don't like. Practise the backhand every session. And practise the shots you don't like, the off-balance ones and the pucks that arrive behind you.

Tips and rebounds can't be practised on a garage pad, so do those on the ice. Take breakaways at the end of practice against a real goalie, and keep score. Start from your own blue line, so you practise the change of pace too. And fifteen to twenty minutes a day of focused work beats two hours on a Sunday.

When you practise against a real goalie, protect them. Don't shoot at your goalie's head in the warm-up, and don't fire pucks at them from ten feet in a drill. That's a coaching caution, not a rule, so the care is yours. And don't count on a whistle to protect them. In most leagues, play doesn't stop straight away for an injured player unless it looks serious. And a cage hides how hurt a goalie is.

And never shoot on the other team's goalie in the warm-up. Each team warms up on its own half of the ice. Breaking that can get you a penalty, or reported to your league.

Twentieth idea, and it is short. Your stick.

If your stick is too stiff to bend on a wrist shot, you lose power and your release gets slower. The flex number printed on the shaft tells you how stiff it is. A common starting point is about half your body weight in pounds. Cutting a stick down makes it stiffer than that number. So if you'll cut more than about three inches off, buy a lower flex. Then stand normally in your skates and check that the whole bottom edge of the blade touches the ice.

Then stop thinking about it. A stick that fits won't make you a better shooter. It just stops the stick holding you back.

Let me pull it all together.

Where you shoot from matters most. Get to the slot, or pass to a teammate who's there. Shoot before the goalie is set, in stride, without gathering. Get the puck across the middle first, or put a body in the goalie's eyes.

At the net, screen from outside the paint and off the red line. Never touch the goalie, and stay out of their road home. Know where the post is, take a shove braced, head up, chin off your chest. Tip with soft hands and the blade below everyone's shoulders, follow every shot, and pull up short of the goalie.

Find a lane before you shoot, and aim where the goalie isn't. Inside the dots, the snap shot. Look before you wind up, keep the blade below everyone's shoulders, and in a CARHA league, no slap shot at all. Off the rush, shoot early and in stride. On a breakaway, make the goalie move first.

Much of this depends on what your team wants. So here are three questions for your coach, and two for your league. Do you want us aiming for the corners, or shooting low to make rebounds? Am I a one-timer option on our power play? On a two-on-one, do you want the shot or the pass? And for the league. Which rules do we play under? And is the slap shot allowed?

Go and ask. Their answers tell you how your team wants you to shoot, and now you know why each answer makes sense.

### 7. Departures from the source, the template and the remake

- **Template shape adapted.** A shooting page has no "one end at a time". The parts after the spine are: before you shoot, the shots, the front of the net, the big moments, and practice and the stick. Each part but the last ends in a recap.
- **Vocabulary wording.** The point, half-wall and high slot use the approved template's definitions; `shooting.md` uses the terms without defining them. The butterfly gloss ("on their knees with both pads out to the sides") and the blocker gloss ("the flat pad on the goalie's stick hand") are the remake's, consistent with SH :398 and :400.
- **Second source.** PWTP supplies "know the near post, take the shove braced, wide base, knees bent, weight low" and "give ground to a goalie who leans in" (:697-698). `shooting.md` carries the head-up, post and walking-speed limbs itself, but not those two.
- **"Never your stick across anyone"** is a generalisation of SH :570 (a cross-check on a goalie) and PWTP :713. It is stated unconditionally per brief item 2, which is the conservative direction.
- **"Coaches agree on that gap"** stands in for SH :445/:447's "every model agrees on the shape… a coaching-level consensus range".
- **The professional shooters are not named.** SH :455 names Kane and Laine; the script says "one analysis looked at two of the best shooters in the professional game" and voices only the Kane figures, as fractions.
- **CARHA is named eight times**, against the standard's "no book names unless the instruction changes by league". The instruction does change in a CARHA league, and the brief makes the CARHA slap-shot rule central. It is voiced at the fake shot, the slap shot, the wind-up, the one-timer, "which shot when" and the close. It is not voiced at the point-shot paragraph (which already says "low and hard… getting it through", a snap-shot instruction) or the breakaway's "sell a shot" (no wind-up is taught there).
- **"Two hard strides can be charging"** is stated generally, per brief item 12. SH :560 gives the two-stride threshold for Hockey Canada; PWTP :740 gives CARHA's "more than two steps or strides".
- **Remake content dropped:** the tip crossbar rule, the empty net, protecting a lead, the faceoff point shot, and the remake's "five minutes" for a CARHA high stick (now "a major penalty", which is what SH :191 says).
- **Remake wording corrected:** every "he/him/his"; the remake's "charge into a goalie and you can be thrown out" (a conditional imperative, item 16) is now "If you charge a goalie, plan on being thrown out"; "a backswing more than fifteen inches behind the puck" was first changed to "a backswing of more than fifteen inches", on the ground that SH :138 and :159 give no "behind the puck". ⚠️ **That was wrong, and review m4 corrected it: the CARHA Rule 79 Note itself reads "bring the stick back behind the puck more than fifteen inches either on or off the ice" (`sources/carha_2026.txt:3490-3491`).** The script now says "behind the puck" again, at the slap shot and the one-timer. The source page omits the phrase, but the rulebook carries it; "no league caps it at two minutes" was considered and rejected for "in every league it can cost more than two minutes", because the first can be heard as "never only two minutes", which is false (NHL 60.2 is a minor).

### 8. What I found unclear in the source

- **CARHA's fifteen inches** is "either on or off the ice" in the Note and "off the ice" in the Glossary (SH :138, :161). The source says play the Note; the script says "a backswing of more than fifteen inches behind the puck counts" and does not voice the conflict. `shooting.md` also drops the Note's own "behind the puck" (`carha_2026.txt:3490-3491`), which is worth a plan row against the source page.
- **SH :118** gives a blade-angle height cue for the snap shot ("roll the wrists up to open the face") and then says the blade-angle account failed for the wrist shot and is untested for the snap. A listener-facing script cannot carry both; it carries neither.
- **The low blocker side** (a rebound target, SH :400) and **blocker side high** (the breakaway target, :709, :718) are opposite heights on the same side. Both are right in their situations; the script keeps them in separate ideas so they don't collide.
- **SH :46** says "six things you can do to a puck". The six are the five shot sections plus the tip; the script repeats the count without listing them.

### 9. Where this script is weakest

- **The fourteenth and fifteenth ideas are the rules-densest stretch** (about 2,700 characters together). They are mostly consequence sentences. They are where a reviewer should start, because each is a summary of a multi-book tariff, and summaries drift.
- **"In almost every league it puts you out of the game"** (checking a goalie from behind, review m5) counts the IIHF's discretionary tier with the mandatory ones. USA Hockey's 608(a) minor-plus-misconduct option is the one that need not eject.
- **"Some leagues make it an automatic match penalty"** (review m6) for driving a helpless goalie into the net: USA Hockey's 608(c) needs reckless, excessive force on a vulnerable or defenceless player (SH :537, :576), which "can't protect themselves" approximates.
- **The breakaway idea is a list of reads** in one long stretch. It meets the sentence targets but may sag when heard.
- **The fourth and fifteenth ideas' goalie sentences** now put the penalty, the ejection and the lost goal on any goalie contact first, and the injury reason second (review m1). That closes the "so" trap the first draft carried.
- **The 1,304-character final request** is short. The close is split across two requests.

### 10. What I could not check

- No rulebook was read. Every consequence is checked against `shooting.md`'s wording only. A `rules-verifier` pass against the primary books is still required (§12).
- No audio was made, so the seams are unheard.
- The script was not chunk-read for antecedents by a second reader. "They" was checked by hand for its role noun in every two-person passage (screen, rush, two-on-one, breakaway).
- Whether "the CARHA rulebook" is heard clearly by the voice (CARHA spoken as a word) is untested.
