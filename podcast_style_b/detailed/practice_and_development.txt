===== SCRIPT START =====

This episode is about practice. Not how many hours you put in, but what you do with them, so that you actually get better. It takes its time. For each idea I will tell you what to do, and also why it works. If you know why something works, you can build your own practice, not just copy someone else's.

Most of us get very little ice. So the real question is how to make every minute count, on the ice and off it.

Before any instructions, a few words you will hear all the way through. I will explain them now, so they don't get in the way later.

A rep. A rep is one go at a drill or a skill. One pass, one stop, one shot.

An outside eye. This is someone or something that watches you from the outside. A coach, a training partner, or a phone on a tripod.

Edge work. Each skate blade has two edges, an inside one and an outside one. Edge work means skating drills that train both edges, in both directions.

Your weak side. This is the side you are worse at. Stopping one way, taking a pass on your backhand, or going into one corner on your bad-hand side.

Stick-and-puck. This is open ice time where people practise on their own, with a stick and a puck, and no game.

A small-area game. This is a live game in a small space, like one zone, a corner, or across the ice, usually with fewer players.

A cold retest. This means trying a skill at the start of a session, a week or more later, before you have warmed up on it.

That is the vocabulary. Now the ideas.

I will start with four big ideas, because they matter most. If you only listen to the first part of this episode, take those four with you. After that we will go through the rest, one part at a time. What to practise, how skills change, practice off the ice, team practice, and keeping yourself healthy.

First idea, and almost everything else comes back to it. Give every rep a goal, and find out whether it worked.

Before each rep, name the one thing you are trying to do. And name how you will know whether you did it. Not work on my shot. Something like this instead. Hit the far post from the top of the circle, on my forehand, off a pass, ten times in a row.

Here is why that matters. You can do something a thousand times and improve once. Repetition is doing the thing again. Deliberate practice is doing it again differently, because you found out what went wrong. Only the second one moves you forward.

So you need feedback, and you need it on this rep, not next month. Without it, you are rehearsing your mistakes, not removing them. And reps with no correction make your faults more permanent, not less. If you can't tell whether a rep was good, fix that first, before you add more reps.

Now the outside eye. You can't see your own stride. Nobody can. So get an outside eye on it. A coach, a training partner who will tell you the truth, or a phone on a tripod. That isn't a luxury. It is the feedback. The research this idea comes from assumed a teacher who could see faults the player couldn't. That is the part amateurs skip, and it is the one that costs most.

And stop counting hours. You may have heard that ten thousand hours of practice makes an expert. That was never a research finding. The number was an average from one study of violinists, and a popular writer turned it into a rule. When that study was run again more carefully, it didn't find the same result.

That doesn't mean practice doesn't matter. It does. It means hours alone don't rank people. What does the work is how the hours are spent.

So I skate three times a week tells you nothing about whether you are improving. Ask a better question. What did I work on, and how did I know whether it worked? An honest twenty minutes with a goal and feedback beats two hours of shooting with no aim. That is a sensible reading of the research, not a tested finding.

So the recipe has four steps. Set a goal for the rep. Get feedback on it. Work at the edge of what you can do. Then correct, and go again. The next idea starts with working at your edge.

Second idea. Practise at game pace, at the edge of what you can do, and take the hard side.

Once you are fit to, do your drill reps at full game speed. A half-speed rep builds a half-speed skill. Skills belong to the speed you practise them at. If you only ever handle the puck at about sixty percent pace, sixty percent is the pace you own it at.

So if a drill is too fast to do cleanly at full speed, that is the drill doing its job. Don't slow it down to make it look tidy.

Work at the edge of what you can do. That means just past what you can already do every time. If you get a rep right every single time, you are performing it, not practising it. The misses are where the learning is.

So take the hard side. Stop on your weak side. Take passes on your backhand. Go into your bad-hand corner. Your strong side doesn't need the extra reps. The reps you need are the ones that make you look bad.

And don't cheat the rep. Curling early. Leaving before the pass arrives. Coasting the last few strides. Taking the shot from closer than the drill wants. Here is why that matters. Every cheat is a rep of what you will do in a game. Do the rep the way the game will ask for it.

Third idea. Keep your head up on every puck-handling rep, and at home above all.

The skill isn't moving the puck. The skill is moving the puck while you look at something else. A player who stickhandles beautifully with their eyes down can't see the check coming. They can't see the open teammate, or the ice ahead.

Here is why home matters most. Thousands of head-down reps in your kitchen make head-down your habit. Under pressure in a game, your habits are what come out. Then you spend months unlearning it, and your handling gets worse while you do. A drill done badly, often enough, is worse than not doing it at all.

So look at the television while you stickhandle. Or put numbers on the wall and call them out. Fifteen minutes with your head up beats an hour with it down.

And pick a spot with room to swing. This episode only talks about what carries over to the ice. It hasn't checked whether your kitchen, garage or driveway is safe to swing a stick in. That is for whoever is there to judge.

Fourth idea. Put an opponent in the rep, even a passive one.

Most drills have one right answer, known in advance. You skate the pattern, pass to where the coach is standing, and shoot. That is fine for learning a movement. But it only trains doing it, not choosing when to do it.

And choosing is half of hockey. A skill used at the wrong moment is worthless, and often worse. A perfect long pass into a covered lane just gives them the puck. A fancy move at your own blue line turns into a two-on-one the other way. That is why the most skilled player on a rec team often isn't the most effective one.

So add a defender. A passive one is enough. Let them take away just one option, and the drill turns into a decision. Now you have to look, read and choose.

If you get any say in how your team spends its ice, ask for small-area games. Here is why. In a full game, even the best players get the puck for very little time. USA Hockey reported that in the men's Olympic gold-medal game of two thousand and two, players averaged about a minute each with the puck. At its twelve-and-under championship, it was under forty seconds. It is the governing body's own report, and it doesn't say how it counted.

So a full-ice drill where you queue for ninety seconds gives you almost nothing. Shrink the space and cut the numbers, and the puck lands on your stick far more often. Coaches put that at several times as often, though nobody has counted it. And every touch comes with a decision.

An opponent in the rep doesn't mean a hit. The defender takes away an option with their stick and where they stand. Take the puck with your stick, not your body. Stick on the puck, never on hands or body. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call. Until you know, don't hit anyone. Even where it's allowed, ask your coach before you hit in a drill.

Never hit a player who can't see you coming, and never from behind. Even a light hit from behind can hurt someone's head or neck. If you can see their numbers, pull up and play the puck.

Small-area games often end up at the boards, so here is the position to know. At the boards, I will call the other player the checker, whether they are after the puck or just coming at you. Never turn your back to the checker, and never duck. Both protect your head and neck. If you turn your back, you can't see the contact coming. If you duck, your head meets the boards first. That is true even going slowly.

So aim for this. Skates parallel to the boards, which means pointing along them. Forearm and hip to the boards. Never the point of your shoulder. Head up, chin off your chest. And if you are already facing the boards and can't turn, get your stick and gloves up on the glass, spread wide, with your head off it. That is only for when things have gone wrong.

From here on I will use one short version, the same words every time. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the checker, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly.

Let me pull those four ideas together, because they are the core.

One. Give every rep a goal, and find out whether it worked. Get an outside eye, because you can't see your own stride. And audit your hours instead of counting them.

Two. Do your reps at game pace, at the edge of what you can do, on the hard side, and don't cheat them.

Three. Head up on every puck-handling rep, at home above all.

Four. Put an opponent in the drill, and ask for small-area games. Stick on the puck, not a hit, and never push or hit anyone from behind. At the boards, skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the checker, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly.

Everything else in this episode adds detail to those four. So now let's go through the rest, starting on the ice.

Fifth idea. Look after your goalie.

In a warm-up or a shooting drill, your goalie faces pucks from closer than a game usually puts them. And unlike everyone else in the drill, their job is not to get out of the way.

So aim at the pads, the glove and the blocker. Never at the head. And shoot from where the drill puts you. Don't walk in and fire from ten feet.

Don't count on the whistle to protect them. Play stops for an injured goalie only once a referee judges the goalie is hurt. Often that is only once the shooters lose the puck. And a goalie behind a cage is the hardest player on the ice to judge. So the safety is in where you aim, not in the whistle.

Development stops when someone is hurt, and that includes your goalie. Protecting them in drills is part of practising well.

Sixth idea. What to spend your practice on.

Spend it on what limits you most, and for most players that is skating. Here is the order this episode uses. Skating first. Then puck handling with your head up. Then passing and receiving. Then shooting.

Here is why skating comes first. You use it on every second of every shift. And you do every other skill while skating. So better skating improves your passing, shooting, checking and defending too. You do them all from a steadier base, with more time. For most amateur players, the things that pay most are knee bend, outside edges, stopping both ways, and backward crossovers.

Second, puck handling, always with your head up. The skill is handling it while you look at something else.

Third, passing, and especially receiving. Receiving is the half people neglect, and it is worth more. A lot of turnovers in amateur hockey aren't bad passes. They are good passes that bounced off a stiff blade. And a pass taken cleanly and moved on in one touch moves the puck faster than defenders can follow. That is what coaches see, not something anyone has counted.

So practise soft hands, which means letting your blade give a little as the puck arrives. Practise taking it on your backhand, taking it with your feet moving, and moving it on in one touch.

Fourth, shooting. It matters, but by a wide margin it comes fourth. You might shoot five times in a game, and you skate the whole game. When you practise it, practise the shots you actually take. Off a pass. In stride. Quick rather than hard. And change where you release it from.

That order is a common coaching default, not a law of hockey. A realistic alternative is to fix whatever is costing you most right now. A good skater who can't take a pass doesn't need edge work. They need receiving. And a goalie's list is different again. So ask your coach what they want you to work on first, and why.

Now notice something about that order. It is close to the reverse of what is fun. Shooting is fun. You see straight away whether it went in, and nobody watches you miss. Edge work burns your legs and makes you look like a beginner.

So, left alone, most players shoot the most and skate the least. Coaches see that all the time. Some fun is fine, because practice you skip is worth nothing.

But whichever order you follow, give edge work the first ten minutes of every practice or stick-and-puck, before the puck comes out. Here is why it goes first. Do the unpleasant work at the start, while you still have the will to do it.

Seventh idea. Change one thing at a time, and expect it to get worse before it gets better.

Getting better isn't a smooth line. It is a staircase, with long flat bits and the odd step down. Most players picture a smooth curve going up. That picture makes them quit at exactly the wrong moment. So judge your progress over months, not over sessions.

And nothing guarantees an outcome. Your body, your coaching, when you started, and luck all play a part. Practise because it moves you forward, not because a number promises you something.

When you change a movement, you get worse straight away, and you stay worse for a while. A wider stride, a different hand position, receiving with your feet moving. Any of them will do it. Here is why. You have swapped an old movement you had done thousands of times for a new one you haven't. That doesn't mean the change is wrong. It is just what learning a change looks like.

And how a session feels is a poor guide to what you learned. Research on learning backs this up. People mix the two up all the time. The session that felt worst is often the one that taught you most.

It usually goes like this. Week one is awful. Week two is less awful, but still worse than before. Then in a game, under pressure, the old way comes back, because it is still quicker for your body to find. So the player decides the change doesn't work for them, and goes back.

Coaches see that happen somewhere between week two and week five. Often it is just before the new way would have paid off. Nobody has measured those weeks, so trust the shape of it, not the exact numbers.

Three things stop you giving up too soon.

First, decide the change is permanent before you start. Set a date to review it a couple of months away, not a couple of skates away.

Second, keep practice and games apart. Use the new way in every drill and every stick-and-puck. In games, for the first few weeks, let yourself use the old way.

Third, judge it on a cold retest, not on how it felt. A week later, at the start of a session, before you warm up on that skill, try it. Can you still do it? That tells you far more than any feeling.

And change only one thing at a time. If you rebuild your stride, your shot and your passing in the same month, all three get worse at once. That is how people quit.

Now say you have stopped getting better. Work out why before you grind harder. There are four usual reasons.

One. The skill is becoming automatic. That sorts itself out.

Two. You have taken your current technique as far as it will go. The only way up is a rebuild, and that will feel like going backwards.

Three. You have stopped working at the edge, and gone back to what you can already do. That is the most common one, and the easiest to fix.

Four. Something else is holding you back, and very often it is skating. A shot that won't improve is often a balance problem, not a hands problem.

Here is why the diagnosis matters. If the real problem is your technique, or your skating, piling on more reps wastes your effort. And it can get you hurt.

Eighth idea. How to think while you practise, and three popular ideas.

First, where to put your attention. Think about what the puck or the ice should do, not about your body. Drive the puck through the far post, rather than rotate my wrists. Push the ice away sideways, rather than straighten my leg. Coaches often say it the body way, because it is easier to say. Turning it round costs you nothing.

The research on this is disputed, though. A recent review found the effect may be small or nothing at all. So treat it as a free, sensible place to start. And if thinking about your body is the only way you can feel a correction, do that instead.

Second, blocks or mixing. You may be told to mix your reps up, rather than taking fifty wrist shots and then fifty snap shots. The idea works well in the lab, and much less well in real sport. So don't treat it as a law. Anyone telling you block practice is useless is overstating it.

Here is a sensible default. Practise a brand-new movement in a block, because you need reps in a row to feel each correction. Then mix it up once it roughly works, so it holds up in a messy game. That is coaching craft, not a proven finding.

Third, learning styles. You may hear that you are a visual learner, or a hands-on learner. A review of classroom research found almost no evidence for that, and nobody has tested it in hockey. So if a coach's explanation doesn't land, ask for a different one. Ask because it was unclear, not because you belong to a type.

Let me pull practice on the ice together. Aim at your goalie's pads and glove, never the head, from where the drill puts you. Spend your practice on what limits you most, which is usually skating, with edge work first. Change one thing at a time, expect it to get worse first, and judge it on a cold retest. If you stop improving, find out why before you add reps. And think about what the puck should do, not your body.

Ninth idea. Most of your practice will happen off the ice, so make it the kind that carries over.

The test is simple. Off-ice practice carries over when it copies the real thing. Not just the movement, but what you look at and react to while you do it. Most of what follows is reasoning from that test plus what coaches agree on. There is very little direct evidence that any off-ice hockey drill makes you better on the ice. And none of it replaces ice time.

Stickhandling off the ice carries over best of all. Your hands do almost exactly what they do on the ice. It costs nothing, and you can do it in the kitchen. Use a smooth floor, tile, a slide board or a shooting pad, not carpet.

A ball rolls and bounces, and a puck slides. A ball is good for soft hands. A slick training puck on a smooth floor is closer to the real thing. So use both. And head up, every time.

Passing against a wall or a rebounder carries over well too, and people underrate it. A rebounder is a frame that sends your pass straight back. You get hundreds of passes to take in ten minutes, and you know straight away whether each one was clean. Here is why that is so good. It has exactly the shape of deliberate practice, lots of reps with instant feedback.

The catch is that a wall sends back a flat, predictable pass on your forehand. Real passes bounce, arrive behind you, and come to your backhand. So change your angle and distance, and take half of them on your backhand.

Shooting off a shooting pad into a net carries over reasonably well. The release, the weight shift and the path of your hands all carry over. Use a pad. Shooting off concrete wrecks your blade and teaches you to chop at the puck. And pick a spot where a missed shot can't hurt anyone or break anything.

But a pad can't teach you to shoot in stride, off a real pass, under pressure, or against a goalie. It trains how to shoot, not when. And a pad grips more than ice, so your shot will feel easier on the ice. Your numbers on the pad mean little.

A slide board trains one thing. The push out to the side, and bringing your leg back under you. It can't give you edges, glide or balance on a blade. It supports your skating. It isn't skating practice.

Synthetic ice, the plastic panels, is the closest thing to real ice off the ice. Edge work, crossovers, tight turns and switching direction all carry over. But it grips more, so each push takes more effort and the glide is shorter. It also blunts your blades much faster. So budget for sharpening, or keep a separate pair of skates for it.

Roller hockey is good for your hands and your reading of the game. Puck control in tight spaces, passing, vision and quick decisions all carry over. The skating doesn't. Wheels grip differently, there is no true edge, and you can't do a hockey stop. Coaches disagree about how much that costs you, and neither side has a study behind it. So play roller for your hands, your head and the fun. Just don't count it as skating practice.

Fitness work off the ice makes you a better athlete on the ice. Strength, sprinting, and work on one leg at a time. But it supports your skating, it doesn't replace it. One small study of a single youth team found off-ice agility work did carry over. Work on the ice probably did more. So do both, mainly because it makes sense.

Tenth idea. What carries over less than people think, and what is worse than nothing.

Some things carry over less than they promise. Agility ladders train small, quick steps. A skating stride is a long push out to the side through an edge. They barely overlap. Balance boards train balancing on a still base. Edge control is pressure through a moving blade. Related, but not the same.

Weaving the puck round cones makes you good at weaving round cones. There is no defender, no time pressure and nothing at stake. Those are the three things that make stickhandling hard in a game.

Shooting from one spot at an empty net builds a shot you can only take when nobody is there. So change your spot, add movement, and shoot off a pass.

Watching highlights shows you how plays end. Watching your own video shows you decisions, and that is more useful.

And wrist rollers, stick weights and grip trainers do very little. The power in a shot comes from your legs, your body and the flex of your stick.

Now the serious warning. Some off-ice practice is worse than nothing. It builds a habit you will later have to remove. And removing a habit is much harder than building one.

Head-down stickhandling is by far the biggest. That is the one from the third idea.

Practising slowly and comfortably is the second. Skills belong to the speed you practise them at. So do some of your puck-handling reps faster than you can quite handle cleanly.

The third is grooving a fault. If you take ten thousand shots with a bad weight shift, you own a very solid bad weight shift. So get your technique checked before you add volume. That is the best reason to book a few coaching sessions early, not late.

The biggest version of this is changing position. Habits that were right in your old job can work against you in the new one. If that is you, the episode on switching positions goes through them.

So before you take up any off-ice drill, ask yourself one question. If I do this five thousand times, what habit will I own? If it is a habit you would have to break, change the drill.

One more warning about off-ice claims. One study found that young players who test fast on land are also fast on the ice. That is useful for testing players. It doesn't prove that getting faster on land makes you faster on the ice. So be careful with anyone who uses a result like that to sell you a programme.

And buying the tool isn't the work. A rebounder improves nothing sitting in the garage. Ten minutes against it, a few times a week, does.

Let me pull practice off the ice together. Do what copies the real thing. Head-up stickhandling and wall passing come first, and take half your passes on your backhand. Use a pad for shooting mechanics, and get your decisions on the ice. Roller is for your hands and your head, not your skating. Fitness supports your skating, it doesn't replace it. And before any drill, ask what habit five thousand reps will give you.

Eleventh idea. Getting the most out of team practice.

Two players can do the same practice, and one gets far more out of it. Coaches find it is mostly habits, not talent. These habits are coaching craft, not research.

Before it starts. Get on the ice early. Ten extra minutes of edge work, twice a week, is more focused skill work than the practice itself contains. And know your one thing for today. Without it, you will just do whatever the drill asks and improve nothing.

One piece of kit, for anyone playing in Britain. Under England Ice Hockey's rules, an approved neck guard is required in all on-ice activities. That means practice, not only games. The Elite League requires the same. Whether that reaches Scotland and Northern Ireland is unsettled, so wear one there as if it does.

During the drill. If you don't know what a drill is for, ask. Otherwise you practise its shape and miss the point. A breakout drill isn't about the skating pattern. It is about the timing of your support, and the first pass.

Do it at game pace. This is the big one. Take the hard side, and don't cheat the rep.

Compete in the battle drills, the one-on-ones and the small-area games. Here is why. They are where your compete level gets trained, because there is something to win. A player who never battles in practice won't battle in a game.

Competing still means the puck, not the player. Stick on the puck, never on hands or body. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call. Until you know, don't hit anyone. Even where it's allowed, ask your coach before you hit in a drill. Never hit a player who can't see you coming, and never from behind. Even a light hit from behind can hurt someone's head or neck. If you can see their numbers, pull up and play the puck.

And when a battle drill goes into the boards, the same line every time. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the checker, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly.

Picture six skaters two ways. In a full-ice drill, they queue for one puck, and one goes while five wait. In a small-area game inside one zone, all six play at once, with a goalie in the net.

Here is what a small-area game trains that a drill can't. Reading a live opponent, and choosing between options with no time. Timing your support, not just knowing where to stand. Making space, in a space that keeps taking it away. Faking someone out, which needs someone to fake. Protecting the puck when someone is really trying to take it. Looking around, because the picture keeps changing. And your compete level, because there is something to win.

To be honest about the evidence, the research is supportive but thin, and mostly not about hockey. That small-area games build decisions is very likely, and nearly every coach believes it. But it hasn't been well tested. Use them anyway.

If you are setting up a small-area game, add rules on purpose. One touch only. Three passes before anyone can score. The puck has to go below the goal line first. Each rule forces a habit without a coach having to shout it.

Keep the numbers low, because two-on-two and three-on-three give far more touches than four-on-four. And keep the shifts short, thirty to forty-five seconds. Here is why. Longer than that, and the quality falls apart.

And if you have no team practice at all, which is true of most rec teams, ask whether your rink runs stick-and-puck or drop-in small-area sessions. A drop-in is a session you just turn up to. They are usually the cheapest and best-value ice you can buy.

While you are standing in line. Coaches reckon about half of a typical team practice is spent queuing. That time is either wasted or used.

Watch the rep in front of you, and say to yourself what you would do differently. That is free practice. Keep your feet moving. Standing still in cold air is how you pull something on your next hard rep.

Don't lean on your stick. That habit follows you into games, where your stick needs to be on the ice. Keep your head up, because practices have loose pucks flying around. And a line standing still against the boards is exactly where they end up. Then picture your next rep, exactly as you want to do it.

Afterwards. Stay on for five minutes and do the thing that went badly, slowly, with no pressure. Then write one line about what you learned. By the next morning you will have forgotten the detail.

Twelfth idea. Train your decisions, not just your hands.

A skill is something you can do. Hockey is a string of choices between the things you can do, under time pressure. And almost nobody trains the choosing. Drills take the read out so the skill can be practised. That is fine, but drills alone build a player with good hands and no idea when to use them.

Picture one rep twice. First, nobody is defending, and you make the pass. Then add one passive defender. Nobody else moves. But the defender's arrival takes the passing lane away, and now you have to choose something else. That is the whole difference between a skill drill and a decision drill.

There are cheap ways to train it. Always have an opponent, even a token one, playing with their stick, not a hit. Add a rule that forces you to look around. For example, shoulder check before every pass you receive. A shoulder check is a quick look over your shoulder to see what is around you. Or never pass back to the player who passed to you.

Shrink the space and shorten the clock. Here is why. Time pressure is what separates a decision you can make from one you can make in a game.

Practise doing nothing. Amateur players tend to always do something. Dump it, force it, shoot it into someone's shins. So practise holding the puck one more beat, or making the boring pass across.

And decide in advance, off the ice. Work out what you will do in the situations that keep coming up. The puck on your backhand on the boards, with a forechecker closing. Taking a pass at the blue line, with a defender stepping up at you. A choice you have already made comes far faster than one you invent on the spot.

Here is a cheap drill for this, and it works off the ice. Play a game clip, and pause it the moment a player gets the puck. Say out loud what you would do, and why. Then play on. Do it twenty times. You are training the same reading of the play the game asks for, at a speed you can think at.

Thirteenth idea. Film yourself, and count things rather than points.

Video is the cheapest way to get the outside eye most amateur players are missing. You can't see your own stride, where your head is, or the four passing options you never looked at.

Film one period from a fixed spot, high up. A phone on a tripod in the stands is enough. You need to see the ice, not just the play.

Watch it with one question, and only one. How many times did I shoulder check before I got the puck? Did I stop, or did I curl? A general watch gets you a general shrug.

Count something, so you have a number to beat next month. And watch your shifts without the puck. Measured at five-on-five, that is nearly all of your ice time, and it is where most of your improvement is.

Expect it to feel bad. Everyone looks slower on video than they feel. That gap is exactly what you need to see. And if you can't film yourself, watch one good player at your position for a full shift. Follow them, not the puck.

Here is why you count things and not points. Goals, assists and plus-minus depend a lot on your linemates, the other team, your ice time and luck. Over a short amateur season, that noise drowns out the signal. You can improve a lot and score less.

So count the things you control. Shoulder checks before you get the puck, per shift. First passes completed out of your own end. Passes you move on in one touch. Times you got beaten wide in a game. Battles won along the boards. Whether you can stop both ways at full speed. That one is a yes or no, and most players can't honestly answer it. And how long a set edge-work pattern takes you, timed once a month.

Pick one or two, check them monthly, and write them down. If you pick more than two, you will end up measuring none.

There are other signs you are improving when the scoresheet doesn't show it. The game feels slower, because you are reading it faster. You are less tired doing the same work, usually because you skate more efficiently. You get the puck more, because teammates pass to players who take it cleanly. And your mistakes get more advanced. Getting caught on a pinch beats not knowing the pinch was there. A pinch is a defenceman stepping in from their blue line to keep the puck in.

Let me pull team practice together. Get on early, and know your one thing. Ask what the drill is for, and do it at game pace. Compete in the battle drills, with your stick on the puck, not a hit, and never push or hit anyone from behind. If a battle goes into the boards, skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the checker, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly.

In the line, watch, keep your feet moving and your head up. Train decisions with an opponent and a clock. And film yourself, and count one or two things you control.

Fourteenth idea. Build a plan you will actually keep.

If you skate once or twice a week, like most adult players, your ice time is probably a game. And a game gives you about a minute with the puck. So your real practice for the week is roughly nothing, unless you make it off the ice.

Here is one example of a workable week, about three to four hours in total. It is a coaching example, not a prescription, so change it to suit you.

Head-up stickhandling, fifteen minutes on three weekday evenings. That is the most carry-over you can get per minute off the ice.

Wall passing, ten minutes twice, half of it on your backhand. That trains receiving, the half of passing most players neglect.

One twenty-minute shooting session off a pad. Few shots, done well, changing where you release from. That is for mechanics only. The decisions come on the ice.

Two strength and mobility sessions of thirty to forty minutes. Work on one leg at a time, your hips, and your inner thigh muscles. That supports your stride, and it helps your groin and hips, though no exercise guarantees you won't get hurt.

Ten minutes of edge work at the start of every practice or stick-and-puck, before the puck comes out. For most adults, that is the only skating practice they get.

On game day, one thing to work on, chosen in advance. That turns the game into a rep.

And twenty minutes a week of video, with one question. That is the feedback that makes everything else deliberate.

The off-ice parts happen wherever you have floor space. Remember, this episode has only judged whether they carry over, not whether that room is safe.

If you can, add one stick-and-puck or drop-in session a month spent only on skating. And one power-skating session a season, which is a class just for skating technique. Neither is exciting, and both do more for you than anything else on the list.

If you get three to five sessions a week, you have enough ice to give each session its own job. One skills session, low volume, full attention, ideally with someone who tells you the truth. One small-area game session, for decisions, competing and touches. One team practice. One or two games. And two strength sessions, kept away from your hardest days on the ice.

But only one thing at a time should be a real technique rebuild. Here is why. Rebuilds make you worse for a while. If you rebuild three things in one month, all three get worse at once, and you quit.

Then the reality check. If your plan needs more than about four hours a week, and you have a job and a family, it is a fantasy. You will drop it, and it will feel like a failure of character, when the real problem was the maths. So build the plan you will keep in your worst week, not your best one. Fifteen minutes of head-up stickhandling four times a week, kept up for a year, beats a big programme kept up for a fortnight.

Fifteenth idea. Don't get hurt while you are trying to get better, because getting better stops when you are hurt.

The most common way a developing amateur player gets hurt isn't contact. It is a sudden jump in how much they train. That is what coaches see, not a measured ranking. And it is no reason to take contact less seriously.

So build up slowly. The classic pattern is four months of nothing, then five sessions in the first week. Raise how much you do, or how hard you do it, but never both at once. And not by much in any one week.

Be most careful coming back from a break, like a summer off or an injury. Your body has lost fitness, but your competitiveness hasn't. You will feel ready before you are. That is when groin and hip problems tend to show up, the classic hockey overuse complaint. So come back at low volume.

Warm up before hard skating. Do it especially in a cold rink, and before a game where your first shift is flat out. Standing in a line and then sprinting is a real risk. That is one more reason to keep your feet moving while you queue.

Treat a technique change as extra load too. A new movement uses your body in ways it isn't used to. So start it at low volume.

You will hear a rule of thumb about soreness and pain. Soreness that eases as you warm up is usually fine. Pain that gets worse is not. Treat that as a rough guide, nothing more. It doesn't come from any medical guideline, and plenty of real injuries feel better once you warm up.

So see a physiotherapist for anything sharp, swollen, on one side only, or lasting more than a couple of weeks. A week off now costs less than a season.

And sleep and protein aren't optional extras. They are how practice turns into skill, and training turns into fitness.

Let me pull the week and your body together. Build the plan you will keep in your worst week. Rebuild only one thing at a time. Build up slowly, never more and harder at once, and come back from a break at low volume. Warm up before you skate hard. And see a physiotherapist for anything sharp, swollen, on one side only, or lasting more than a couple of weeks.

Sixteenth idea, for anyone starting as an adult.

If you haven't found a session to go to yet, start with the getting started episode. This part assumes you have.

You can learn this as an adult. Plenty of people start in their thirties, forties and fifties, and become properly competent. But be honest about both sides.

Harder first. Skating is the big one. Balancing on a thin blade comes easily to children and slowly to adults. Expect it to hold you back for a couple of years.

Fear of falling is the second. Adults fall from higher, weigh more, and have work on Monday. That fear makes you stand tall and lock your legs. And standing tall is the root of most skating faults. So the fear isn't just uncomfortable. It is the actual technical problem.

You also get fewer hours than a child. You need more recovery and get less of it. And being the worst player on the ice is harder to take at forty than at ten. It makes adults avoid the very practice they need.

Easier, too. You can read about a defensive system once and understand it. Good positioning makes you useful before your skills arrive. A well-positioned poor skater beats a badly positioned good one more often than anyone admits.

You can choose what to work on, book ice, and hire a coach. You can put up with a dip that lasts weeks, because you know why it is happening. You can spend money on power skating, a few coaching sessions and gear that fits. And you can learn far more from watching hockey on television than a child watching the puck.

You may also hear that you had to start at four to be any good. The research on sport doesn't support that. On average, the more skilled athletes did not start their sport earlier.

First steps. Learn to fall, and to get up, on purpose, in full gear. Here is why. Losing the fear is what lets you bend your knees.

But don't assume your gear makes a fall harmless. It doesn't protect a wrist you put out to catch yourself. And a fall is one of the ways a concussion happens. That is why you practise the technique, not just falling over.

So here is the technique. Get low first. Go down to the side, onto your hip and thigh. Keep your hands in, rather than out on a locked, straight arm. That is widely taught coaching craft, not a written rule.

Then the chin, and it changes with where you are falling. Falling backwards onto the ice, tuck your chin in. That keeps the back of your head off the ice. Going forwards into the boards, the opposite. Head up, and never duck. A chin on your chest puts your neck first in line. So, falling flat, chin in. Hitting a wall, head up.

After you fall, take two or three seconds to check you aren't hurt. If you are hurt, stay down.

Now the concussion part, kept short. A concussion is an injury to the brain, from a knock to the head or a jolt that snaps it. You don't have to be knocked out to have one.

If a concussion is suspected, come off straight away, and tell someone. You are off for the rest of that day. And you don't play again until a medical professional says you can, even if you feel fine.

Afterwards, someone stays with you, for at least the first few hours. You don't drive, you don't drink alcohol, and you don't go home on your own. If you feel worse, get medical help straight away.

After any knock to the head, treat it as a possible neck injury too. If a player is still down, don't help them up or move them, and leave their helmet on. Phone for an ambulance if anything looks serious, like being knocked out, a fit, neck pain, or numbness or tingling.

Then the rest of the first steps. Bend your knees more than feels natural, because almost every adult skating fault is a straight-legs fault. Get skates that fit. Badly fitting skates make skating harder, in a way a fitter can see and you can't.

Coaches advise two shorter sessions a week rather than one long one. That hasn't been tested, and the research on spacing out practice doesn't all point one way. And work on one skill a month, not five.

You will see confident figures for how long adult beginners take. Every one of them comes from coaching companies and shops, not from research, and people vary hugely. So don't measure yourself against them. Think of it like this. Months for the basics, years for the rest, and the curve is a staircase.

Seventeenth idea, and it is about learning things you read, not things you skate.

Trying to remember an answer teaches you more than reading it again. But only if you then check the answer. Here is the surprise. Straight after studying, reading it again looks better. A week later, the people who tested themselves remember more. In one study, students who read a passage four times remembered about forty percent of it a week later. Students who read it once and tested themselves three times remembered about sixty. So the method looks worse at exactly the moment you would judge it.

The check is not optional. A wrong answer you never correct is worth close to nothing. And a wrong answer you actually thought about helps. A wild guess doesn't.

All of this was tested on things people read, not on physical skills. So use it for systems, reads and rules, not for your shot. When you are learning how your team plays, or reading about a system, try to answer questions about it before you look. Be willing to be wrong. Then go and check. The checking is the part that does the work.

Let me pull it all together.

Give every rep a goal, and find out whether it worked. Get an outside eye, because you can't see your own stride. And audit your hours, don't count them.

Do your reps at game pace, at the edge of what you can do, on the hard side, and don't cheat them. Keep your head up on every puck-handling rep, at home most of all.

Put an opponent in the drill, and ask for small-area games. Compete for the puck with your stick, not a hit, and never push or hit anyone from behind. At the boards, skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the checker, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly.

Aim at your goalie's pads and glove, never the head, from where the drill puts you. Don't count on the whistle.

Skating first, with edge work at the start of the session. Change one thing at a time, expect it to get worse first, and judge it on a cold retest a week later. Off the ice, do what copies the real thing, and ask what habit a drill will give you.

Build up slowly, never more and harder at once, and come back from a break at low volume. When you fall, get low, go onto your hip and thigh, hands in. Falling flat, chin in. Going into the boards, head up, never duck. And if a concussion is suspected, off for the day, and not back until a medical professional says so. Someone stays with you afterwards. And after any knock to the head, don't move a player who is down, and leave their helmet on.

Much of this depends on your coach and your team. So here are three questions to ask your coach. What should I be working on first, and why? What is this drill for? And can someone watch me, or film me, and tell me the truth?

Go and ask. Then go and practise the thing that makes you look bad.

### 7. Departures from the source, the template and the remake

**From the source.**
- **The contact gate, the injury reason and the boards posture are imported from `body_contact_and_battles.md`.** The source sends a reader into battle drills, one-on-ones, small-area games and "puck protection under real contact" (:343, :381, :513) with no gate and no posture on the page, only a pointer at :343. The brief's rule ("any drill that has contact needs its gate") required them. This widens §9's one-source traceability, as the plan's decision 6 allows for a safety limb.
- **"Even where it's allowed, ask your coach before you hit in a drill"** is not in any corpus document. It is the reviewer's optional wording, kept because "your league's call" alone, in a drill, could read as permission to hit in practice wherever the league allows it in games. The untraced "the drill's rules are your coach's" is gone. The gate now leads with the traced instruction "Until you know, don't hit anyone" (BC KF :19).
- **"Pick a spot where a missed shot can't hurt anyone or break anything"** is a gloss on the scope note (:234), added at the reviewer's request. The source deliberately does not assess home safety, and this sentence gives a plain instruction rather than an assessment.
- **"You don't drink alcohol"**: the reviewer sketched "you don't drink". I added "alcohol", because heard alone "don't drink" could mean water.
- **"Small-area games often end up at the boards"** is my framing, to put the posture in the spine (brief item 28). The source says small-area games train "puck protection under real contact" (:343).
- **The concussion aftercare and neck lines are imported from `conditioning_and_recovery.md`**, as brief item 10 requires. The source page carries only removal and clearance (:560).
- **The goalie-stoppage sentence is unqualified ("play stops … only once a referee judges"),** following the body (:408, "every one waits on a referee"), not the Key focus's "in most rulebooks" (:25). See §8.

**From the template.**
- Seventeen ideas against eighteen, with a vocabulary block of seven terms. The template's four-beats-per-system rule is applied to the page's one real "system", the priority order (what it is, why skating first, the alternative, ask your coach). Blocked-then-varied and the weekly plan are given as defaults with the source's reason and "coaching craft" label. The source gives no alternative weekly plan, so none is invented.
- The close asks three coach questions and no league questions. Only one instruction in this script depends on the league, and the gate already says "ask".
- The boards short form is the template's word for word, plus the brief's "Keep it up even going slowly."
