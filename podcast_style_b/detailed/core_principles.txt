===== SCRIPT START =====

This episode is about the handful of ideas that hold in hockey whatever position you play, whatever system your team uses, and whatever league you are in. It takes its time. For each idea I will tell you what to do, and also why it works. If you know why something works, you can still do the right thing when the play looks a little different from the picture in your head.

Before any instructions, a few words you will hear all the way through. I will explain them now, so they don't get in the way later.

The carrier. That is whoever has the puck, on either team.

Goal side. Being goal side means your body is between the attacker you are covering and your own net.

Above the puck. This means between the puck and your own net. When your team loses the puck, getting above it is the first thing you do.

The slot. This is the ice right in front of the net, between the two faceoff circles.

The crease. This is the light blue painted area in front of the goal, where the goalie plays. The line around its edge counts as part of it.

The boards. These are the walls around the rink. Coaches also call them the wall, and the two words mean the same thing.

That is the vocabulary. Now the ideas.

Start with one fact, because it explains everything after it. Researchers used the NHL's own tracking of players and the puck. They found that at five-on-five, which means five skaters a side, a forward has the puck for about forty seconds in every twenty minutes on the ice. Each time, they have it for a little over a second. That is professionals, and the league calls that tracking unofficial. So take the shape, not the exact number.

The shape is clear, though. For almost all of your shift, you don't have the puck. What you do in the rest of the time decides what those few seconds are worth. That is where games are decided.

So most of the ideas in this episode are about the time without the puck. I will start with four big ideas, because they matter most. If you only listen to the first part of this episode, take those four with you. After that we will go back through the rest, and add the detail.

First idea. When you are defending, stay goal side.

Your body goes between the attacker you are covering and your own net. Your stick goes in the passing lane, the line a pass to that player would travel along.

Here is why. If that attacker gets between you and your net, there is nothing left between them and the goal except your goalie. Once they are past you, your goalie is doing your job.

This usually goes wrong in one way. You watch the puck. The puck pulls every eye on the ice. While you watch it, the player you were meant to be covering walks in behind you.

So keep that player in view. Take short looks at the puck, not long ones.

And unless your team has sent you to pressure the puck, don't join the chase. Hold your position, goal side, with your stick in the lane.

Why not help? Because two players on one carrier leave somebody free. The carrier only has to find that free player, and one pass beats both of you.

Second idea. When the puck goes to the net, go with it.

When a teammate shoots, skate to the front of the goal and stop there. Don't stop short. Don't peel away once the shot has gone.

Here is why. A rebound is a puck that bounces back off the goalie. A tip is a shot that changes direction off a stick. Both go to whoever is already standing at the net. A player still on the way is too late.

There is one exception, and it is a team choice. On a shot, your team keeps somebody back as the safety. That is the player who stays out of the attack, so there is somebody to defend if it goes wrong. It is usually a defenceman at the point, the area just inside the blue line. Or it is the high forward at the top of the faceoff circle. If that is your job on that shot, stay there. Ask your coach who it is.

Now where you stop. Stop with your skates and your stick blade just outside the crease, and off its line. Keep your stick on the ice, ready for a tip.

Why just outside, and not in? Because in every league, a screen from just outside the crease that never touches the goalie keeps the goal. Inside the crease, a goal can come off. So a clean screen from outside costs you nothing, and inside can cost you the goal.

Take the ice in front of the net. Never the goalie. Arrive under control, and pull up short of the goalie.

Any contact with a goalie can be a penalty, and it can get you thrown out of the game. Here is why it is priced so high. A goalie who is down, or turned away, can't protect themselves, so contact can hurt them.

The front of the net is also a place to look after yourself. Your eyes are on the puck, so a defender can move you without you seeing it. Keep your head up and your chin off your chest. A goal post can hurt you the same way the boards can, head first, even going slowly.

And if a defender leans on you there, keep your spot with your feet. Don't push back, don't lean, and never put your stick across anyone.

Third idea. Never pass the puck, or skate it, across the front of your own goal.

Here is the why, and it is simple. If you lose the puck in your own slot, it is a shot against you. Losing the puck and facing the shot happen in the same moment. Anywhere else, there is still time, and there are still players, between the turnover and your net.

So take it another way. Behind the net. Or up the wall, which means along the boards. Or through a high exchange, which is a pass between your defencemen above the tops of the faceoff circles.

Teams differ on the route. Some go behind the net. Some exchange it high. Ask which one yours uses.

What does not change is that your own slot is never the route.

Fourth idea. Decide how you will meet the boards before you get there.

This one is not about winning the puck. It is about not getting hurt. It holds in non-checking hockey exactly as in checking hockey, because the boards don't need an opponent to hurt you. And it holds at every speed. No system changes it, and no coach can.

Races for a loose puck along the wall end with somebody meeting the boards. How you arrive decides whether that is nothing or a serious injury to your neck. There is no time to decide when you get there. So decide on the way in.

Go in on an angle, not straight at the wall. When you can't avoid the wall, turn your skates parallel to the boards. Feet apart. Knees bent. Forearm and hip to the boards. Never the point of your shoulder, and not the whole shoulder if you can help it. Head up, and your chin off your chest.

Now two things you never do, at any speed. Here, the checker means any opponent near you at the boards, whether they are after the puck or just coming at you.

Never turn your back to the checker, however protective it feels. And never duck.

Both protect your head and neck. If you turn your back, you can't see the hit coming or brace for it. If you duck, your head meets the boards first. That can hurt you even at walking speed. So no speed is slow enough to make it safe.

Sometimes the wall arrives when you are already facing it, with no time to turn. That is the last resort, not a choice. Get your stick and gloves up and spread wide on the glass, with your arms out. Keep your head off it. That way the impact lands across as much of you as possible. It is for when things have already gone wrong, not a way to aim to arrive.

That position matters more than anything else in this idea. From here on I will use one short version of it, the same words every time, so you can learn it like a line. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the checker, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly.

Let me pull those four ideas together, because they are the core. One. Defending, stay goal side, with your stick in the lane. Watch the player, take short looks at the puck, and don't join the chase unless you were sent. Two. When the puck goes to the net, go with it, unless you are the safety. Stop just outside the crease and off its line, stick on the ice, head up. Take the ice in front of the net, never the goalie.

Three. Never move the puck across the front of your own goal. Behind the net, up the wall, or a high exchange, whichever your team uses.

Four. Decide how you meet the boards on the way in. Go in on an angle. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the checker, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly.

Everything else in this episode adds detail to those four, or tells you what to find out before you play. So now let's go through the whole shift.

Fifth idea. The four jobs without the puck. Off the puck, you are always in one of four situations. Your team has it. The other team has it. It is loose, and nobody has it. Or it has just changed hands. What is right in one is close to the opposite of what is right in another. Most players have a plan for only one of them.

When your team has the puck, get open somewhere the carrier can see you and pass to you. And when the puck goes to the net, go with it, and stop just outside the crease, head up, never the goalie.

When the other team has the puck, the usual approach is that the closest player goes to pressure it. They use their stick and an angle. Angling means skating at the carrier so they can only go the way you have chosen.

That pressure is stick on the puck, not a hit. Never on their hands or body, and never a chop. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call. And if you can see their numbers, pull up and play the puck.

Not every team sends the closest player. Some systems send fewer players at the puck. So find out what your team runs. If you are not the one pressing, stay goal side of the attacker you are covering, with your stick in the passing lane.

When the puck is loose and nobody has it, one player goes hard for it. One supports. Nobody else joins in. Which of you goes, and which supports, is your team's call.

The player who goes is often racing into the boards. How you arrive matters more than getting there first. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the checker, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly. If you're already facing the wall, stick and gloves up on the glass, head off it.

Picture it. The puck is dumped into the corner, and you and an opponent both go for it. You are half a stride behind. The temptation is to put your head down and dig. That is exactly the head-down posture to resist. Take a wider angle instead. Arrive alongside the wall, not into it, with your head up. It costs you a fraction of a second and nothing else.

And when you get there, it is the puck, not the player. Stick on the puck, not a hit. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call. If you can see their numbers, pull up.

When the puck has just changed hands, act before the other team does. If your team just won it, go. If your team just lost it, get back above the puck, between it and your own net.

Why the hurry? Because a turnover you are slow to react to can become a rush against you.

Getting back often means chasing a player from behind. So never hit a player who can't see you coming, and never from behind. Chase the puck, not the player. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call. If you can see their numbers, pull up and play the puck. A hit from behind ends your game in almost every league.

Picture a winger who had a quiet game. They hardly touched the puck. But their two most valuable plays both happened without it. They arrived where the defenceman could pass to them, so a breakout pass had somewhere to go. And they got back above the puck, so a turnover didn't become a rush. Nobody remembers either one. Both of them mattered.

Sixth idea. Look before the puck reaches you.

A glance over your shoulder while a pass is still on its way to you is free. Your eyes have nothing else to do. A glance after the puck arrives costs you time with the puck. And the picture has changed while you looked.

So take three looks. One while your teammate is getting the puck. Another as the passer's head comes up. And a last one just before the puck reaches you. Then take the pass already turning the way you decided to go.

Picture it. You are on the half-wall in your own end. That is the boards about level with the faceoff dot, between the corner and the point. Your defenceman has the puck behind the net. A forechecker is coming. A forechecker is the opponent coming to win the puck back.

Say your first look comes when the puck touches your stick. Then your decision takes about as long as the forechecker needs to reach you. Now say you looked twice while the puck was travelling. The decision is already made. Taking the pass and making your own are one movement.

Here is why this is worth so much. Players who seem to see the whole ice have almost always just looked already. It is the cheapest thing in hockey to get better at. You don't need to be fitter, get more ice time, or have more talent.

This one has been measured, and in hockey. A study looked at professional players in Sweden's top men's and women's leagues. What a player did after taking a pass worked out more often when they had looked first.

Two limits travel with that. The study counted success by its own measure, not by goals. And it only recorded whether a player looked or not. So it can't say whether looking more often is better than looking once. Going from no look to one look is the finding. Advice to look on a fixed clock, every second or two, goes further than the study does.

There is one exception, and it is the goalie. A goalie facing a shot has nothing to gain by looking away. They follow the puck all the way into the pad, the glove or the chest, because losing sight of it is what costs the goal.

Seventh idea. Give the player with the puck two places to put it.

A player with the puck and two options beats a player with the puck and none, whatever the difference in skill. Being one of those options, on purpose, is most of what a teammate is for.

Here is why. Almost every bad pass is bad because there was nothing to pass to. And fixing your own position is faster than fixing somebody else's hands.

Two options only count as two if they are at different angles from the puck. If two teammates stand in one line from the carrier, one defender's stick takes both away. So move until you, the carrier and the third player make a triangle, not a line.

Then check the lane between you and the carrier is really empty, not just short. The test is time, not distance. Would the pass beat the opponent who is closing on your teammate?

The common mistake is skating toward a teammate who is under pressure. Say your defenceman has the puck on the half-wall with a forechecker on them. You skate straight over to help. Now one forechecker's stick covers you both. And you have taken the ice your teammate was going to use.

When a teammate is pinned in a battle, moving away is usually the help. They need somewhere to put the puck, not another body in the pile.

That is coaching reasoning, not a measured result. No governing body has been found to set a support distance. So judge your distance by time, not by a set number of feet.

Defending, it reverses. Stay behind the first defender, rather than joining them on the same puck. And when your team is the one putting on pressure, you take options away on purpose. An angling forechecker tries to leave the carrier one bad pass. A penalty kill, when your team is a player short, keeps its forwards from both drifting to the same side.

Here is one situation where that matters a lot. A two-on-one is two attackers against one defender. The common approach is that the defender takes away the pass and gives up the shot, on purpose.

Why? Because your goalie can save the shot, but not the pass.

The other common approach is a timed slide across the passing lane. This episode doesn't teach that technique. So ask your coach and your goalie which one your team plays.

And two more cases change this. Some teams double-team the puck behind their own net, on purpose. Two of you on the puck is still stick on the puck, not a hit. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call. If you can see their numbers, pull up. And if your team marks players rather than areas, you may have to follow yours out of the supporting spot completely. Ask which your team plays.

Let me pull the whole shift together. Off the puck, know which of the four situations you are in. Your team has it, get open. They have it, the closest player pressures with the stick and an angle, stick on the puck, not a hit, and everyone else stays goal side. It's loose, one goes and one supports. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the checker, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly. It has just changed hands, act first, and if you see their numbers, pull up. Look before the puck reaches you. And give the carrier two places to put it, at different angles.

Eighth idea. Where you are standing decides what a mistake costs. The same play is not worth the same everywhere. A pass across the ice can be brilliant in the corner of their end and a disaster in front of your own net. Nothing about the pass changed. Only the ice under it changed. Here is why that helps. Most bad hockey decisions are good decisions made in the wrong place. If you think about where you are first, a long list of things you must not do turns into one question.

The question is this. If I lose the puck here, how many seconds and how many players stand between that and a shot on my net? In front of your own net, the answer is none and none. Deep in their corner, it is four or five seconds and the whole team.

So change where you are standing before you decide what to do. One stride out of the most dangerous ice makes every option better. And that stride is almost always there.

Picture it. You are behind your own net with the puck, and a forechecker is coming. The pass across to the far winger is on. But it is also the one option where a mistake becomes a goal against. If it is read, losing the puck and facing a shot are the same moment.

Now think about a rim. A rim is a hard pass around the boards, following the curve of the rink. It gains less if it works. But it costs almost nothing if it doesn't. That makes the rim the better bet from behind your own net. And the worse bet at the other end of the ice.

So play simple in your own end, and creative in theirs. A missed chance in their end costs you a chance. A turnover in your own end can cost you a goal.

And the game itself moves this. The score, the clock and an extra player all change how much risk makes sense. Leading by one in the third period, go for the safe option earlier than usual. Trailing late, take more risk.

How the areas of the ice rank by what a turnover costs is coaching reasoning, not a measured result. It is worked out from where dangerous shots come from, and how long it takes to recover. So take the order of the areas, not the exact labels. Coaching reasoning is not a reason to ignore an idea. It just tells you how much has been measured.

Let me pull your own end together. Before you choose a play, ask what losing the puck right here would cost. Take the stride that changes the ice. Never move it across your own slot. Use the route your team uses. And play simple there, because a turnover there can be a goal.

Ninth idea. The front of their net, in more detail.

You already have the main job. Go with the shot, stop just outside the crease, stick on the ice, never the goalie. Here is a little more about why the crease matters.

Standing in the crease can cost your team the goal. Depending on the league, a goal can come off just because you were in the crease, or because you blocked the goalie's view or got in their way. In some leagues, standing in it while your team has the puck also stops play. Then the faceoff moves out to the neutral zone, the middle of the ice.

So whatever league you play in, the same habit works everywhere. Skates and stick blade just outside the crease, and off its line.

If you do end up in the crease and the goalie makes contact to get into position, give ground at once. Holding your spot there can be a penalty, whether a goal is scored or not.

Outside the crease, you still never go into the goalie. Deliberate or unnecessary contact with a goalie, anywhere on the ice, can be a penalty in every league.

Building up speed into contact with a goalie, even with two hard strides, can be charging. Charging a goalie can get you thrown out of the game in every league, and in some it is automatic. So arrive under control, and pull up short.

If you play in goal, know one thing. In some games where body checking is allowed, a goalie who has the puck out near the corners can be checked to win it. So don't treat the corners as safe ice.

As a skater, don't take that as permission. In most leagues, going into a goalie anywhere is a penalty. Take the ice in front of the net, never the goalie.

Let me pull their net together. Go with the shot. Stop just outside the crease, off its line, stick on the ice, head up. If the goalie makes contact to get position, give ground. Never into the goalie, in the crease or out of it. And arrive under control.

Tenth idea. Most of what you are told is your team's system, not a law of hockey.

A system is your coach's plan for where each player goes. Almost every "always do this" in hockey really means "in our system, do this". Who goes in first to win the puck back in their end. Who covers the front of your net. Whether your defence partner follows you to the puck. How high a winger stays in your own end. A coach decides each of these. A different coach decides them differently, and is also right.

Here is how to tell the difference. If an instruction is about where a teammate goes, it is probably a system. If it is about what the referee will do, it is a rule. Rules are written in a book. Systems are written on a whiteboard.

Take "stay high". In your own end, while the other team has the puck, it means level with their defenceman on your side. That stops the moment your team wins the puck. Then you come down the boards to be the outlet, the first pass out of your end.

Why does this matter so much? Because if you treat a coaching choice as a law, you get two things wrong at once. You do the wrong job when you change teams. And you stop asking, because nobody asks about a law. The same instruction can be right on one bench and a serious mistake on the next.

So ask, don't guess. One question opens it up. When the puck is in our corner, what does the far winger do?

"Stays up" or "comes down" is not the answer on its own. In a low zone collapse, the far winger does come down. A low zone collapse is a system where each player covers an area of ice, not an opponent. What tells you the system is what the winger comes down to.

Say the far winger drops into the high slot, then climbs straight back out. The high slot is the top part of the slot, from the faceoff dots up to the tops of the circles. That is a collapse. The winger is covering a piece of ice.

Now say the far winger follows a player who leaves the blue line, into the corner if need be. That is man-on-man, where you follow one opponent, or a hybrid of the two. The winger is covering a player. Your coach may shout the same words in both systems, but they mean different jobs. So ask which yours is.

The forecheck works the same way. Forechecking means pressuring the other team in their own end, to win the puck back. One forecheck is the two-one-two. Two forwards go deep, and one stays high.

Why would a team play it? Because it wins pucks back close to their net, and it wears their defence down. What it gives up is everything behind it. If they break out cleanly, you can face an odd-man rush, more attackers than defenders. And it is the most tiring system to run.

The usual alternative is a one-two-two. One forward steers the carrier toward a wall, and the others hold a shape behind. It gives up time, but it is hardly ever out of position. It is often recommended for adult recreational hockey, because a two-one-two is hard to keep up for a whole game.

Whichever it is, the forward steering the carrier is still stick on the puck, not a hit. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call. If you can see their numbers, pull up. And ask which forecheck your team runs.

One more thing about systems. Sometimes your coach tells you to do something different from what you have heard anywhere else, this episode included. Then do it your coach's way on the ice, and ask about it afterwards. These ideas are for the situations nobody has given you an instruction for.

There are three exceptions, and no coach can change them. How you meet the boards. Staying out of the crease and off the goalie. And whether your league allows body checking. That last one is the next idea.

Eleventh idea. Find out whether your league allows body checking, before your first game. Body checking means deliberately hitting an opponent with your body to separate them from the puck. Whether you may do it, or can have it done to you, is set by your age group, your league and your country.

Here is the catch. Nothing on the ice tells you. A checking game and a non-checking game look exactly the same from the bench. What tells you is paperwork. Which competition you are registered in, which age group you are in, and what that competition's own rules say.

Why does it matter so much? Because this is the difference between a mistake that costs you a shift and one that gets you thrown out of the game, or hurts somebody. And it is the one thing in this episode you cannot work out on the ice. You have to be told.

So before your first game, not after your first hit, ask your coach or your league secretary. Which competition do I play in, and is it a checking one? In Britain, ask for your competition by name, because the answer is in that competition's own rules.

If you can't name your competition, you don't yet know what is legal for you. And ask again every time you change team, league or age group. An adult moving between leagues is more exposed to this than a beginner, not less.

A coach cannot make a body check legal in a non-checking game. This is the one place where "it depends what your coach says" is the wrong answer.

Twelfth idea, and it is short. Holding your spot.

In most leagues you are allowed to hold the ice you are standing on. You don't have to move to let an opponent through. Except in the crease. If the goalie needs the ice, give it at once. That is how you take away a lane without hitting anybody.

Holding your spot is done with your feet and your position. Keep your stick down and your free hand off them. No push, no lean, and never your stick across anyone.

But there is a catch in adult recreational leagues affiliated to CARHA, a body that runs adult recreational hockey. CARHA's rules let a defender stand their ground. The same rules also penalise deliberately bodying or shoving an opponent, standing in front of them to make contact, or not avoiding contact with them.

That can be a minor, or a major that throws you out of the game with at least a one-game suspension. They never say how the two rules fit together. So find out whether your league is affiliated to CARHA before you rely on holding your ground.

Let me pull it all together. You almost never have the puck, so play the rest of the shift. Defending, stay goal side, with your stick in the lane, and don't join the chase unless you were sent. When the puck goes to the net, go with it, unless you are the safety. Stop just outside the crease, off its line, stick on the ice, head up. Take the ice in front of the net, never the goalie. And never move the puck across the front of your own goal.

Decide how you meet the boards on the way in. Go in on an angle. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the checker, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly. If you are already facing the wall with no time to turn, stick and gloves up and spread wide on the glass, head off it.

When you pressure, or chase, or race for a loose puck, it is the puck, not the player. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call. If you can see their numbers, pull up.

Look before the puck arrives. Give the carrier two places to put it, at different angles. Play simple in your own end and creative in theirs.

And settle two things before you play. What system does your team play? And is your game a checking game?

So here are three questions to ask your coach. First. What system do we play in our own end, and what does the far winger do when the puck is in our corner? Second. On a shot, who stays back as the safety, and which way do we take the puck out of our end? Third. Which competition am I registered in, is it a checking one, and if it is adult recreational hockey, is the league affiliated to CARHA?

Go and ask. Their answers tell you how your team wants you to play, and now you know why each answer makes sense.
