===== SCRIPT START =====

This episode is about the map of the ice. It takes its time. We will go round the rink one place at a time, and for each place I will tell you what it is, what to do there, and why. Almost every instruction you get on the ice is a place plus a reason. Get to the slot. Hold the point. Take it behind the net. Once you can picture the places, the instructions start to make sense on their own.

Before any instructions, a few words you will hear all the way through. Most of them are paint on the ice. I will explain them now, so they don't get in the way later.

Picture the rink from above. It is a long rectangle with rounded corners. A wall of boards runs all the way round it, with glass on top. Players call the boards the wall. On an NHL rink the ice is two hundred feet long and eighty-five feet wide.

The goal lines. Near each end, a thin red line runs right across the ice. The net stands on it. A goal only counts when the whole puck goes over that line, between the posts.

The blue lines. These are the two wide blue lines, each about a foot wide. They split the ice into three parts, called zones.

The centre red line. This is the red line across the middle of the ice, halfway between the two blue lines.

The crease. This is the light blue paint in front of each net, with a red line round its edge. It is the goalie's area. Players also call it the paint.

The faceoff circles and dots. A faceoff is the restart where an official drops the puck between two players. In each end there are two big circles, one either side of the net, each with a red dot in the middle. There is one more circle at centre ice. And there are four dots near the blue lines with no circles round them.

The hash marks. These are the short red lines on the outside of each circle in the two ends, roughly level with the dot.

That is the paint. Everything else in this episode is read off it.

I will start with four big ideas, because they matter most. If you only listen to the first part of this episode, take those four with you. After that we will go round the rink, starting in your own end, then the middle, then their end, and add the detail.

First idea. Say our end and their end, and read every place word from your team and the puck.

The two blue lines split the ice into three zones. Your defensive zone is the end with your own net in it. Your attacking zone, often called the offensive zone, is the end with their net in it. The middle part, between the blue lines, is the neutral zone.

Here is the catch. Those names follow your team, not the building. The same patch of ice is your defensive zone and their offensive zone at the same moment. And teams change ends after every period. So the end you defended in the first period is the end you attack in the second.

That is why somebody shouting get it deep can confuse you. You have to know whose end they mean. Usually a teammate means it from your own team's side. If there is any doubt, say our end or their end. Those two words can't be misread.

The other place words move too.

High means toward the blue line you are facing, away from the net. Low means toward the goal line, toward the net. That is true in all three zones. It always means the net that matters right now. So rotate high means climb toward the blue line. And swing low in your own end means come down toward your own goal line.

Strong side means the side of the ice the puck is on. Weak side is the other side. Nothing is painted for this. The split is just the middle of the ice, wherever the puck is now. When the puck crosses the middle, the sides flip.

That happens several times a shift. It is why defence partners talk all the time. One of them is the strong-side defender, on the puck. The other is the weak-side defender, in front of the net. And they keep trading those jobs.

Why does this matter so much? Because a moving word heard as a fixed place sends you to the wrong half of the rink. You end up there doing exactly what you were told. So read high, low, strong and weak from your team and the puck, not from the paint.

Second idea. Win the slot.

The slot is the area right in front of the net, between the two faceoff circles. It runs from the tops of the circles down to the edge of the crease. Nothing marks it. You read it off the circles.

Here is why it matters. It is where most goals are scored from. It is the shortest way to the net, and it gives the widest angle on it. In your own end, everything comes down to who is covering the slot. In their end, everything comes down to getting a puck and a body there at the same time.

The slot has layers, and each layer is a different job.

The high slot is the top layer. It runs from the level of the faceoff dots up to the tops of the circles. This is where shooters set up. A player left alone there gets a clean look, with time to pick a corner. And a pass to them from the boards becomes a one-timer, a shot taken straight off the pass without stopping the puck.

The low slot runs from the dots down toward the net, outside the crease. This is where tips, screens and rebounds happen. A tip is a stick changing the direction of a shot late, so the goalie can't adjust. A screen is a body standing in the goalie's line of sight. Defenders can't let anyone stand there unmarked. It is the ice a net-front player fights for.

The goalmouth is the smallest layer. It is the ice right at the edge of the crease, in front of the net. Tap-ins and jam plays happen there.

The counted goals point the same way. In four hundred NHL games in one recent season, just over half the goals came from within twenty feet of the net. That is top professional hockey, so take the direction, not the number.

When you go to the net to get into that low layer, go under control. Stop short of the goalie. Any contact with a goalie can be a penalty and can get you thrown out. And keep your head up, chin off your chest, because a goal post can hurt you the same way the boards can.

Third idea. Shoot from inside home plate.

Home plate is not painted on the ice. It is a shape that coaches and analysts use. Picture a line across the tops of the two faceoff circles. From each end of it, go straight down to the faceoff dot below. Those two dots are forty-four feet apart. Then angle in from each dot to the goalpost on that side. The posts are six feet apart. It looks like a baseball home plate, square across the top, with its point at the net. Some people call it the house.

Here is why it matters. A shot from inside that shape is worth several times a shot from outside it. The people who draw it each draw it a little differently, so treat it as an idea, not a measurement. But the idea is clear. Take your shots from inside it. And if you are outside it, work the puck inside before you shoot.

Defending, it runs backwards. The shot you can most afford to give up is the one from outside home plate. So protect the inside first.

Fourth idea. Be inside, force them outside.

Inside means toward the middle of the ice. Being on the inside of an opponent means being between them and your net. Outside means toward the boards.

Get to the inside means put your body between the player you are covering and your net. Force them outside is the same idea for the player with the puck. Take away the middle, so their only way forward is toward the boards.

Why? Because the middle of the ice is where goals come from. Wide is safe for you and bad for them. A shot from out by the boards rarely scores. A pass from wide into the middle is the dangerous one. So force the carrier wide, but keep your stick in the passing lane through the middle.

Do it with your feet and your position, not with a hit.

Putting yourself between a player and the net is position. Finishing the play with a hit is a separate act. So keep your stick on the puck, not a hit. A check is legal only on the player who has the puck, and only where your game allows checking. Many youth age groups, a lot of women's hockey and non-check adult leagues don't allow it. So find out what your league allows before you finish anything with a check.

And wherever you play, never hit a player who can't see you coming, and never from behind. Even a light hit from behind can hurt someone's head or neck. If you can see their numbers, pull up and play the puck.

Let me pull those four ideas together, because they are the core of the map.

One. Say our end and their end. Read high, low, strong side and weak side from your team and the puck.

Two. Win the slot. In your own end, know who covers it. In theirs, get a puck and a body there together. Go to the net under control, short of the goalie, head up, chin off your chest.

Three. Shoot from inside home plate, and make them shoot from outside it.

Four. Be inside, force them outside, with your feet and your position. Stick on the puck, not a hit. Never hit anyone who can't see you coming, and if you see their numbers, pull up.

Everything else in this episode adds detail to one of those four. So now let's go round the rink, starting in your own end.

Fifth idea. In your own end, take the puck behind your net, never through your own slot.

Behind the net means the strip of ice between the goal line and the end boards. On an NHL rink it is eleven feet deep. On international ice it is about thirteen.

Here is why you go there. If you lose the puck behind your net, there is no direct shot at your goal. If you lose it in your own slot with the puck on your stick, it is often a goal against. That makes behind the net the safest place in your own end to settle the puck and set up.

That one doesn't change from team to team. The one that does is the pass between defence partners, called D-to-D. The usual instruction is to make it behind your own net too. Some teams make it higher up in the zone instead. Ask which one your team uses.

Sixth idea. Know which layer of the slot is yours.

Mixing up the slot and the goalmouth is one of the most common ways to blow an assignment. That is coaching know-how, not a counted ranking. They are two different jobs, one layer apart.

Who covers each layer is a coaching choice, not a rule of hockey. Here are the setups you are likely to meet, and how each one works.

The first is the low zone collapse. It is a zone system, where each player covers an area rather than a person. The weak-side defenceman owns the goalmouth and does not leave it. The high slot belongs to the centre. When the centre goes into the corner to help, the weak-side winger comes down into the high slot instead. This is the one this episode assumes.

Here is what goes wrong in it. A defenceman who leaves the goalmouth to cover the slot has just left a tap-in unmarked.

The second is man-on-man. Nobody holds an area. You go with your player wherever they go. The goalmouth belongs to whoever's player is standing in it.

The third is a hybrid. You play man-on-man below the faceoff dots and zone above them. Then the goalmouth belongs to whichever of your three low players is matched with their forward at the net front.

The map doesn't tell you which of these your team plays, or why your coach picked it. So ask which system your team plays, and which layer is yours, before the puck drops.

Now the part that keeps you safe in front of your net. It holds whether you are defending your net or attacking theirs.

Keep your head up there, chin off your chest. Your eyes are on the puck, so you get moved by players you feel rather than see. And a goal post can hurt you the same way the boards can.

Hold your spot with your feet. Never shove, never lean, and never put your stick across anyone. Never put your stick or your body into anyone's back, and if you can see their numbers, pull up. Never move a body across your own goalmouth. And keep all of this up even going slowly, because a crash there can hurt at any speed.

Seventh idea. Read depth off the paint.

High and low move with the play. The paint doesn't. So coaches use the painted marks to say exactly how deep they mean.

The hash marks first. Below the hash marks means the band between those marks and the goal line. The dot sits twenty feet out from the goal line, and the blue line sixty-four. So that band is about the bottom third of the zone, not the bottom half. Above the hash marks is everything from there out to the blue line. In your own end, coaches lean on this mark more than any other. That is coaching observation, not a count.

The dots work the same way. Above the dots and below the dots is how coaches split the zone, though not into equal halves. Above the dots runs forty-four feet back to the blue line. Below the dots runs thirty-one feet to the end boards.

Because the dots are painted, once you know which end is meant, above and below them name a fixed line, not a feeling. That is why a hybrid system can say man-on-man below the dots and zone above, and everyone knows where the switch happens.

Inside the dots is the other common use. It means the ice between the two dots, toward the middle rather than out toward the boards. A centre swinging low on a breakout comes through there. The breakout is your team's planned way of getting the puck out of its own end.

Eighth idea. The corners and the wall.

The corners are the rounded ends of the rink, beside and behind the net, outside the faceoff circles. Most loose-puck battles happen there. A puck won in a corner starts nearly every breakout in your end, and nearly every cycle in theirs. A cycle is your forwards keeping the puck in their end by passing it round the corner, the boards and behind the net. That is coaching observation, not a measured share.

The wall is both a weapon and a trap.

As a weapon, it is a passing partner. You can bank a pass off it, chip the puck off it past a player, or rim it. A rim is a hard pass along the boards that follows the curve of the rink round to a teammate.

As a trap, a player pinned against it has no way out on one side. A single opponent can seal them there. So when you hear get off the wall, it means stop battling and move the puck to open ice. In any battle there, stick on the puck, not a hit.

Now, what to do when contact is coming at the boards. I will call the other player the checker, whether they are after the puck or just coming at you.

Go into the boards on an angle. Skates parallel to the boards, feet apart, knees bent. Forearm and hip to the boards. Never the point of your shoulder, and not the whole shoulder if you can help it. Head up, chin off your chest. Never turn your back to the checker. And never duck.

Both of those last two protect your head and neck. If you turn your back, you can't see the hit coming or brace for it. If you duck, your head meets the boards first. Keep it up even going slowly. A slow checker doesn't make it safe.

If you are already facing the wall, try to turn parallel first. Only if you can't, get your stick and gloves up and spread on the glass, arms out, and keep your head off it. That is the last resort, not a choice.

From here on I will use one short version of it, the same words every time, so you can learn it like a line. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the checker, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly.

Ninth idea. Clear it off the glass, not over it.

When you clear the puck out of your end, off the glass and out is a good play. That means banking it off the glass so it comes back down and out of your zone. Over the glass is not. In many leagues, including British hockey, a puck shot straight out of the rink from your own end is a two-minute penalty, even if you didn't mean it. That includes over the glass at the benches. And putting the puck out of play on purpose, from anywhere on the ice, is a penalty everywhere.

Goalies, that goes for you too. In some leagues, a goalie who shoots the puck straight out of the rink with their stick takes a penalty from anywhere on the ice.

If you play in Britain, the glass is a little lower than on an NHL rink. So keep your clears down.

Tenth idea. If you play defence, your stick hand picks your side.

A left-shot player holds the stick with the left hand low, so the puck sits on the left. A right-shot is the opposite. It has nothing to do with which hand you write with.

A left-shot defenceman normally plays left defence, and a right-shot plays right. Here is why.

On your natural side, pucks behind your own net come to your forehand. So you can pass out of the corner straight away, instead of turning your back to the play. Passes to your partner and shots from the point are on your forehand too. So you can move the puck across quickly and take a one-timer. And along your own boards, your body is between the attacker and the middle, with your stick in the passing lane.

Playing your off side, a left-shot on right defence, is common and not a disaster. It gives you a better angle for cutting to the middle. But your retrievals and passes to your partner come to your backhand, and that costs a beat. One study of NHL pairs found a real but small edge for pairs on their natural sides, though some analysts think teams chase it too hard. That is why coaches pair a left-shot with a right-shot when they can. Know before the shift which side you are on.

Forwards have more choice. Plenty of wingers play the off wing on purpose, a left-shot on the right wing. It puts the puck on the inside for cutting to the net, and it opens up the one-timer from the circle.

One limit on any one-timer. In adult leagues that play the CARHA rulebook, keep your backswing under fifteen inches. Past that it counts as a slap shot, which is a penalty there. And if it hurts someone, it ends your game and at least your next one.

One warning about the word off side. Here it means your unnatural side. The same word is used for the offside rule at the blue line, for the weak side of the ice, and for lining up wrong at a faceoff. Two of those are rule calls. So work out which one is meant before you act on it.

Let me pull your own end together. Take the puck behind your net, never through your slot. Know which system your team plays, and which layer of the slot is yours. In front of your net, head up, chin off your chest, hold your spot with your feet, and never shove, lean, or put a stick across anyone.

Read depth off the hash marks and the dots. Use the wall as a partner. If you do go into the boards, here is the line again. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the checker, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly. Stick on the puck, not a hit, and never push or hit anyone from behind. Clear it off the glass, not over it. And if you play defence, know which side your stick hand puts you on.

Eleventh idea. Keep somebody above the puck.

Above the puck means further from the net than the puck is, up toward the blue line. From there you can stop a rush back the other way. Below the puck means closer to the net than the puck is. A player down there has committed, and can't help against a rush.

Here is why it matters. Everyone below the puck is the most reliable way to give up an odd-man rush. That is an attack where more of them are coming at your net than you have defenders back. A two-on-one is one.

So somebody stays above it. In their end, that is usually a defenceman at the point, plus the highest forward. In your own end, it is whoever hasn't gone into the battle.

That is a coaching habit, not a law, and two things about it change.

Who it is changes. In a five-player rotation, where all five skaters keep swapping spots, it is whoever happens to be highest, not always a defenceman.

And sometimes your bench gives it up on purpose. On a full-ice press, on a delayed penalty, or with six skaters and your own goalie pulled, a rush back the other way has stopped costing you anything. So those are the times a team sends everyone. Outside them, assume somebody stays high. And find out what your team calls.

Twelfth idea. The blue line tells you when you can go in.

The blue line is the widest line on the ice, and that width does a job. Offside is judged against the whole line.

So here it is in plain words. On the way into their end, the puck has to go completely over the blue line before both your skates do. You are offside only when both skates are fully over the line before the puck is. One skate on the line keeps you onside.

That gives you a trick that is really just the rule working. If you are going to arrive early, drag your back skate along the line, blade touching it, until the puck has crossed. Your body, stick and speed can be ahead of it. Do that and you are onside wherever you play.

Drag it. Don't lift it. In some leagues a back skate lifted off the ice doesn't count, even if it is still above the line, so you are offside. And even where a lifted skate can count, swinging it forward over the line makes you offside. Only a blade touching the line is safe everywhere.

Thirteenth idea. The centre red line, for icing and for your gap.

Icing is measured from the centre red line. A puck shot from your own half that crosses their goal line, anywhere along it, with nobody touching it, is icing. Some adult leagues use the blue line instead of the red, so ask where your line is.

If you want to send the puck deep without icing it, get it over the red line on your stick first. In some rulebooks a puck that only touches the red line off a skate doesn't count, so use your stick.

The red line is no longer a passing restriction in the NHL. That is why the long stretch pass up the ice exists at all.

Defencemen also use the red line to judge their gap. The gap is the space between you and an attacker skating at you. A common guide is to be about a stick and a half away by the red line. That is a guide, not a law. It changes with how fast you skate backward and how good the attacker is. It is coaching convention, not a measured figure, and one coaching page says two stick lengths instead.

Fourteenth idea. Through the middle, gain ice north-south, and break them east-west.

North-south means up and down the ice, goal to goal. It is direct, fast and low-risk. East-west means across the ice, boards to boards. It is slower and riskier, but it moves the defence.

A north-south player plays direct and straight. Too much east-west is a criticism, because passing sideways lets the defence reset. But east-west movement is also what beats a set defence. So here is the useful version. North-south to gain ice. East-west to break their shape once you are there.

Two more words run through the whole rink, wide and middle. Wide is out near the boards, and middle is the central lane. Beaten wide means an attacker got round you on the outside. Take away the middle means close that lane and push the play wide.

The four neutral-zone dots help you here. They sit just outside each blue line, forty-four feet apart. They mark the usual side-to-side lanes for setting up in the middle. Defencemen defending a rush line up roughly between them. And in the neutral-zone setup called the one-two-two, forwards line up on them.

Fifteenth idea. Where checking isn't allowed, steer, don't bump.

Back to be inside, force them outside, because in a lot of hockey that is the whole of defending. Body checking is barred in young age groups, in much of girls' and women's hockey, and in non-check adult leagues. Local leagues can bar it in more, so your age alone doesn't answer it. Find out what your league allows.

Where it is barred, you still play the same way. Close the gap with your angle and your skating, and keep your stick in the lane. You may steer the carrier toward the boards, and close off the boards to slow them down. But never bump, push or shove them. Never put a hip, shoulder or forearm into them on purpose. That is an illegal check. Never put your stick across them. And ease off near the wall, because even going slowly, a player steered into the boards can still be hurt. Stick on the puck, and if they turn their back to you, pull up.

Never step into the path of a skater who doesn't have the puck. That can be interference.

In some adult rec leagues, you may hold your ground to take away the carrier's lane. But get out of the way of the contact rather than take it. A collision you could have avoided can be a major penalty, and in those leagues a major ends your game and at least the next one.

Women's hockey where some contact is allowed has its own limits. Contact is allowed only while you are clearly playing the puck. Never use the boards to pin a player or push them in. Never step or glide into anyone, and never hit from behind. In the PWHL, the women's professional league, angle a player with your body, but don't finish with a hit.

Sixteenth idea. Know your bench side.

Both teams' benches are on the same side of the ice, opposite the penalty boxes. So one half of the ice is the bench side, and the other is the far side.

That is a real tactical fact, not trivia. Changing is safe on the bench side. On the far side, lines get caught.

If you are stuck on the far side and tired, stay in position and finish the shift. A bad change is worse than a long shift.

When you change, the player coming off has to be at the bench and out of the play before the next one steps on. If not, it is a bench penalty for too many players on the ice. Nothing is painted to show how close counts, and the distance varies. So get all the way to the bench before your teammate jumps on.

Bench side shows up at faceoffs too. There are nine faceoff spots. One at centre ice, four in the neutral zone with no circles, and two with circles in each end. Players name the end-zone ones by end and side, like D-zone draw, bench side, or O-zone draw, far side. Your bench side decides who can change.

The hash marks keep players apart on a draw. Here is the part that catches people out. If a winger creeps over the hash marks before the puck drops, the centre gets thrown out of the faceoff, not the winger.

Let me pull the middle together. Keep somebody above the puck, unless your team has called a time to send everyone. Puck over the blue line before both your skates, and drag your back skate, don't lift it. Get it over the red line on your stick before you dump it in. Gain ice north-south, and break their shape east-west. Where checking is barred, steer with your skating and your stick, never a bump. Change on the bench side, and get all the way to the bench before your teammate jumps on.

Seventeenth idea. In their end, hold the point, and don't mix it up with the half-wall.

Once you are set up in their end, the blue line is where the defencemen live. The point is the area just inside the blue line, near each set of boards. It is an area, not the line itself. There are two, the strong-side point on the puck side, and the weak-side point.

From the point, a defenceman can shoot for tips and rebounds, slide along the line to open a shooting lane, or pass down to either half-wall. A pass from the corner or the half-wall out to the point is called low-to-high. It sets up most point shots.

The half-wall is the boards about level with the faceoff dot, between the corner and the point. You will also hear it called the half-boards. Wingers live there when their team has the puck.

Here is why the half-wall matters so much. From there you can see the point, the slot, the corner and the front of the net, and every one of those passes is open to you. Coaches call it the most important spot in their end that you don't score from. That is coaching observation, not a measured ranking.

Don't mix up the point and the half-wall. They are a long way apart. On an NHL-sized rink the half-wall is a little under forty-four feet from the point. A defenceman told to hold the point stays just inside the blue line. One who drifts down to the half-wall has left the line.

Holding the line means keeping pucks in at the blue line. A puck that slides back out into the neutral zone means the attack has to start again. But don't get caught inside. If you can't hold the line, get out and reset. Holding the line is stick on the puck, not a hit. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call, and if you can see their numbers, pull up.

Eighteenth idea. Defencemen, step down only as far as you can get back from.

For a defenceman joining the play, picture three depths in their end. Just inside the blue line is normal. The top of the circle, the edge of the faceoff circle nearest the blue line, is more active, and you can still get back. Below the circles is a commitment. Down there you are a fourth forward, and you can't get back in time. So a forward must have taken the blue line for you before you went.

Why do coaches say step down to the top of the circle? Because it is a clear, limited instruction. You know exactly how far to go, and you know you can recover from there.

Nineteenth idea. Behind their net, and the far side of their net.

Behind their net is a different place from behind yours. Commentators call it the office. The name comes from Wayne Gretzky, who set up there. The defence can't easily pressure a player behind the net without opening up the slot. And a carrier back there can come out on either side, so the defence has to commit.

The backdoor is the front of the net on the weak side, away from the puck. What makes it dangerous is that a player arriving there is behind the goalie's view. How it ranks against every other open space, nobody has counted.

Twentieth idea. At the net front, live just outside the paint.

Stay out of the paint. But get as close to it as you can, because the ice just outside it is where tips, screens and rebounds happen.

And keep your skates off the red line round the crease, not just out of the blue.

Here is why. Most rulebooks count that red line as part of the crease. The rest don't say it clearly. Off the line is the only ice that is safe wherever you play.

It matters because a skate in the crease can cost your team the goal. In some leagues no goal counts while an attacker is in the crease, whoever scored it. In others the goal only counts if the player in the crease isn't blocking the goalie's view. Blocking the view is exactly what a screen does. So either way, a skate on the line can wipe out your teammate's goal.

So plant yourself just outside the line, not on it. A clean screen is fine in every league. That means clear of the crease and its red line, in the goalie's line of sight, with no contact.

Keep your head up there, chin off your chest. A defender can move you while your eyes are on the puck, and a goal post can hurt you the same way the boards can. If you are driving to the net, come in under control, and stop short of the goalie.

Now the goalie. Any contact with a goalie can be a penalty, and it can get you thrown out of the game. Here is why it is taken so seriously. A goalie who is down or turned can't protect themselves, so contact can hurt them. And contact can cost your team the goal as well.

Deliberate or unnecessary contact with a goalie is a penalty everywhere, in the crease or out of it, goal or no goal. A goalie outside the crease is not fair game. Play the puck, not the goalie, wherever the goalie is. Some leagues let a touch go if it happened while the goalie was playing the puck, it was truly accidental, and you tried hard to avoid it. Others call even an accidental touch. So do everything you can not to touch the goalie at all.

Building up speed into contact, even with two hard strides, can be charging. A charge on a goalie can get you thrown out of the game in every league, and in some it always does. It can mean a ban for games after this one, too.

That holds behind the net and out in the corners too. You can still go for a puck the goalie is playing out there, with your stick, never with your body. So play the puck.

One more thing at the net front. When their goalie has come out to play the puck, clear the way back to the net. Do it even if you never touch them. Skating into the path of a goalie who doesn't have the puck can be interference, even with no contact at all. Usually that is two minutes. A violent or reckless one can cost a major and get you thrown out, and trying to hurt someone gets you thrown out of the game everywhere.

Twenty-first idea. F one, F two and F three are jobs, not people.

A forecheck is when your forwards pressure the other team in its own end, to win the puck back rather than skate back and wait. F one is the first forward to the puck. F one pressures it. F two is the second forward in. F two supports F one and closes off the way out that F one has taken away. F three is the third forward. F three stays higher, as the safety, and covers the rush back.

The order of arrival decides the job, not your position. Say your centre is usually first in, but tonight a winger gets there first. Then the winger is F one, and the centre takes the support job. Nobody swaps positions. You swap jobs. And it can be somebody different every time down the ice.

Here is why it matters. Teams that treat F one as the centre's job end up with two players chasing and nobody high. And if two players both think they are F two, nobody pressures the puck. So take the job your order of arrival gives you.

Third forward high is the same F three job, said from the other direction. It is the forward who stays above the puck. How high F three sits is your coach's choice, so ask.

Pressure means stick on the puck, not a hit. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call. Never hit a player who can't see you coming, and never from behind, because they can't protect themselves. If you can see their numbers, pull up and play the puck. And building up speed into contact, even with two hard strides, can be charging.

Twenty-second idea. Where there is a trapezoid, dump the puck into the corners outside it.

The trapezoid is a shape painted behind each net in some leagues. It is narrow at the goal line and wider at the end boards. On an NHL rink it is twenty-two feet wide at the goal line and twenty-eight at the boards. Where it is used, a goalie who plays the puck behind the goal line outside it takes a two-minute penalty. The one exception is a goalie who keeps a skate touching the crease while playing it.

Not every league has a trapezoid, and this difference changes how you play. The NHL and international hockey have one. USA Hockey, Hockey Canada and the Canadian adult rulebook, CARHA, don't. In the PWHL, the women's professional league, play as if it counts. So find out which rulebook your league plays under. Where there is no trapezoid, expect the other goalie to come out and play your dump-in.

Britain plays the international rulebook, so keep your own goalie inside it. But England Ice Hockey's rules say it isn't currently enforced, and it is unclear how far that reaches. So in Britain, keep your own goalie inside it, and outside the Elite League, expect theirs to come out. Ask your league.

Here is why the trapezoid matters to everyone else. It is why dump-and-chase works. That means shooting the puck deep into their end and chasing it, instead of carrying it in. Where the trapezoid applies, their goalie can't calmly collect a puck rimmed into the corner. A defenceman has to go and get it, with your forechecker arriving. So aim your dump-ins away from the trapezoid, into the corners.

Chase the puck, not the player. Stick on the puck, not a hit, and whether you may hit at all is your league's call. If you can see their numbers, pull up. And if their goalie comes out for it, play the puck, never the goalie. Any contact with a goalie can be a penalty and can get you thrown out of the game.

On defence, the trapezoid turns round. It is your defencemen's problem, not your goalie's. Go into the corner expecting no help from your goalie. Play the puck, not the player. Whether anyone may hit at all is your league's call, and if you can see their numbers, pull up. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the checker, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly.

Two more lines for goalies. No trapezoid doesn't mean a goalie can do anything behind the net. Playing the puck there may be free. Sitting on it is not. Freezing the puck behind the goal line, outside the crease, is a two-minute penalty everywhere, trapezoid or not, even after a save. Play it with your stick. Out in front, coming out to cut down the angle, making the save and then covering the puck is legal in most rulebooks, so ask about yours.

And keep your goalie on their own side of the centre red line. A goalie who joins the play beyond it takes a two-minute penalty. Some rulebooks judge that by the goalie's skates, not by where the puck is. So keep the skates on your own side, even when reaching for a puck that is still on your side.

Let me pull their end together. Hold the point just inside the blue line, and don't drift down to the half-wall. If you can't hold the line, get out. Step down to the top of the circle, and below it only when a forward has your line. At the net front, live outside the paint and off its red line, head up, chin off your chest, and stop short of the goalie. Any contact with a goalie can be a penalty and can get you thrown out. When their goalie comes out, clear the way back. Whoever gets to the puck first is F one, and pressure means stick on the puck, not a hit. Where there is a trapezoid, dump it into the corners outside it.

If you do go into the boards after it, here is the line again. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the checker, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly. Stick on the puck, not a hit, and never push or hit anyone from behind.

Twenty-third idea, and the last. Look at the rink you are actually on.

The measurements in rulebooks describe rinks built to those rulebooks. A lot of community, recreational and older rinks weren't. That is general observation, not a counted figure, but it is enough to tell you not to assume.

In Britain it isn't just a rec-rink problem. The governing body's own rules allow rinks shorter and narrower than the international minimum, at every level. So if you play in Britain, assume your rink is smaller than every diagram you have seen. Playing international rules tells you which penalties get called. It tells you nothing about the size of the ice.

Size changes how you play, so here is how.

On wide international ice, the corners are bigger and further from the net. A rimmed puck is a longer chase and a longer pass back to the middle, so dump-and-chase pays less.

The half-wall is further from the slot too. A pass from the wall to the middle takes longer, and that gives defenders time to close. So play in their end gets more patient and stays more round the outside.

Steering an attacker to the boards no longer finishes the play, because they still have room to keep skating. So your gap and your stick matter more. And getting back across the ice takes longer, so late attackers arrive more freely.

The neutral zone stays about the same length. What changes is the width. So a neutral-zone trap that squeezes the width is much harder to run.

Coming back to narrow ice, it all reverses. You have far less time on the wall, and quick simple plays beat clever slow ones.

Smaller rinks change things too. On a short sheet the neutral zone shrinks, so long passes and regroups mostly stop working. On a narrow sheet everything happens faster and closer.

So on your first shift at a new rink, check three things on purpose. How far it is from the goal line to the end boards. Whether the trapezoid is painted. And how the puck comes off the corner boards on a rim. Those three answers change your breakout.

If the trapezoid isn't painted, that is a strong hint your league isn't enforcing it, but only a hint. The rule turns on where the puck is, not on the paint.

And learn your home rink's dead spots, the places in the boards that kill a rim. They are a real advantage.

Let me pull it all together.

Say our end and their end. Read high, low, strong side and weak side from your team and the puck, not from the paint.

Win the slot. In your own end, know who covers it and which layer is yours. In theirs, get a puck and a body there together, under control, short of the goalie, head up, chin off your chest. Shoot from inside home plate, and make them shoot from outside it.

Be inside, force them outside, with your feet and your position. Stick on the puck, not a hit. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call. Never hit anyone who can't see you coming, and if you see their numbers, pull up.

In your own end, go behind the net, never through the slot, and clear it off the glass, not over it. Keep somebody above the puck. Puck over the blue line before your skates, and drag your back skate, don't lift it.

At the net front, live outside the paint and off its red line. Stay off the goalie, wherever the goalie is, because any contact can get you thrown out. Whoever gets to the puck first is F one, and pressure means stick on the puck, not a hit. Where there is a trapezoid, dump it into the corners outside it, and chase the puck, not the player. And look at the rink before you trust the diagram.

Much of this depends on your team and your league. So here are three questions for your coach. What system do we play in our own end, and who has the slot and the goalmouth? Do we pass D-to-D behind our net, or high in the zone? And when do we give up having somebody above the puck? Then three for your league. Which rulebook do we play under? Can we check? And is the trapezoid enforced here?

Go and ask. The answers tell you where to stand, and now you know why each place matters.

### 7. Departures from the source and the template

- **Shape.** Twenty-three ideas against the template's eighteen, because a map has more places than a position has jobs. Recaps after the spine, own end, middle and their end, then the close; the final "look at the rink" part has no recap of its own.
- **Questions.** "Three for your coach, then three for your league", as in the remake. The template has five and two; the brief names three for the coach.
- **Two added sources** (header). The source teaches no boards posture and no net-front head-up limb, and the brief requires both.
- **"A goalie who is down or turned can't protect themselves"** is the brief's wording (item 24); `rink_map.md` does not say it.
- **"Even a light hit from behind can hurt someone's head or neck"** softens BC :861's "could inflict a severe head or neck injury" for the owner's "aware, not freaked out" rule.
- **"In some leagues no goal counts while an attacker is in the crease, whoever scored it"** drops USA Hockey's two carve-outs. Stricter, not cheaper.
- **"Most rulebooks count that red line as part of the crease. The rest don't say it clearly."** Drops the NHL and PWHL tables' "on the line is outside", a permission.
- **Britain's trapezoid**: "England Ice Hockey's rules say it isn't currently enforced, and it is unclear how far that reaches", not "in its competitions", because 22.3 names no competition (:305).
- **Women's unit** gains "and never hit from behind", from the general gate (BC :31), not from :531.
- **"Coaches call it the most important spot in their end that you don't score from"** rephrases "the most important non-scoring area" (:417).
- **"Five-player rotation, where all five skaters keep swapping spots"** for the source's "five-man rotation" (gender rule). "Man-on-man" and "odd-man rush" are kept (plan, decision 12).
- **The vocabulary block** defines the painted markings rather than lane / point / half-wall: on this page those places are the ideas themselves.

### 8. What I found unclear in the source

- :604 says D-to-D "behind your own net rather than through the slot… one some teams relax high in the zone", while :456 says the pass "is the one some teams take high in the zone instead". Same meaning; the script uses "higher up in the zone".
- :190 "close to about a stick-and-a-half by the red line" does not say whose position is at the red line, the defender's or the attacker's. The script keeps the remake's "by the red line".
- KT11 says freezing behind the goal line is a minor "in every book here", but :297–299 and :582 show USA Hockey's 614(c) turns on the body being entirely outside the crease. The script follows KT11 ("outside the crease").

### 9. Where this script is weakest

- **It is 43 minutes and near the top of the band.** The middle third (ideas twelve to sixteen) is the most rules-dense stretch: offside, icing, the no-check unit, bench changes.
- **The safety limbs repeat a lot.** "Stick on the puck, not a hit" appears in many units by design (items 13, 22, 26, 27). A listener may hear it as a refrain. That is the intent, but it is the least "plain" thing in the script.
- **Idea fifteen names three rulebooks' positions in five short paragraphs** without naming the books (except the PWHL). A listener cannot tell which applies to them beyond "find out".
- **Spine at 9.4 minutes**, later than the standard's eight.
- **Seam three falls mid idea seventeen**, between the point and the half-wall. No limb is split, but it is not an idea boundary.
- Backward and forward pointers audited by hand for shape: "the four big ideas", "From here on I will use one short version" and "here is the line again" are the only callbacks, and each restates its content.
