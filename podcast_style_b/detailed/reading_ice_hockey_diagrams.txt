===== SCRIPT START =====

This episode is about reading hockey diagrams. That means the drawings in this guide, and the ones your coach puts on a whiteboard before a drill. For each mark I will tell you what it means, what it tells you to do, and why.

You can't see a picture while you listen. So I will describe every mark in words.

A diagram is only useful if you can turn it into what you do on your next shift. And if you know why a mark is drawn the way it is, you can still read it when a coach draws it a little differently.

First, a few words you will hear all the way through.

A diagram. This is a drawing of the rink from above, with the players and their movements marked on it.

The caption. This is the writing that goes with the diagram. It says what the picture is for.

The key. This is the list that says what each mark means. Different coaches use different keys.

A route. This is a line drawn on the rink. It shows where a player skates, or where the puck goes.

Forwards and defencemen. The forwards are the centre and the two wingers, the players up front. The two defencemen play behind them. And then there is the goalie.

Forechecking. This means pressuring the other team in their own end, to win the puck back. Backchecking is the opposite job. It means racing back toward your own end to defend, after your team loses the puck.

The slot. This is the ice right in front of the net, between the two faceoff circles.

A coaching choice. This is something teams do differently, where more than one way can be right. A lot of what a diagram shows is a coaching choice.

That is the vocabulary. Now the job.

I will start with four big ideas, because they matter most. If you only listen to the first part of this episode, take those four with you. Read the caption. Find yourself. Follow the routes in order, and see how each one ends. And read each position as a relationship, not a spot. That order is the same for every diagram you will ever meet.

First idea. Read the caption first, and believe it over the picture.

Before you look at a single player, read the words.

Here is why. A diagram looks very certain. Every player sits on an exact spot, and every line goes exactly one way. It is the most certain-looking thing on a page. But it shows one moment, of one system, and there are usually several systems your team could be playing. The caption is where it says so.

So a drawing of a forecheck shows one way to forecheck. It may not be the way your team does it. The picture can't tell you that. Only the caption, and your coach, can.

The caption tells you things the picture can't. It says what the picture is for. It says which details are a coaching choice. And when part of the ice is shaded, it says whether that shading is ice to head for, or ice a pass must never cross.

So if the caption and your eye disagree, the caption wins.

And where a caption says a detail is a coaching choice, take it at its word. Don't treat the drawing as the answer. Ask your coach which version your team runs.

Second idea. Find yourself.

Once you have read the caption, find the player who is you. Every player in a diagram is drawn as a small shape, and each shape carries two separate facts.

The shape is the position. A circle is a forward. A triangle is a defenceman. The goalie is just the letter G, with no shape around it. A circle would say forward, and a triangle would say defenceman, so the goalie gets neither.

The fill is the team. An open shape, just an outline, is your own team. A solid shape, filled in, is the other team.

So the shape tells you what a player is. The fill tells you whose side they are on.

Why keep those two facts apart? Because then you can find yourself in any picture. Neither fact depends on colour. So it works on a screen, on a printed page, or on a black and white photocopy. And it works for a player who can't tell red from green.

The shape also stays with the player when the play turns. A winger is a circle when they are attacking. They are still a circle when they are backchecking. The shape follows the player, not the moment.

One more mark here. An X is not a player at all. It is a pylon, a practice cone.

Inside the shape there is often a letter. That letter is a role. It is not a spot on the ice, and it is not a name.

The main example is the forecheck. The forwards doing it are called F one, F two and F three, and the number is the order they arrive in. It has nothing to do with which forward position they play.

So if you get to the puck first on the forecheck, you are F one. You do F one's job, whichever forward position you play. Don't wait for the centre.

Going to the puck means the puck, not the player. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call. Never hit a player from behind, or one who can't see you coming. If you can see their numbers, pull up and play the puck. Even a light hit from behind can badly hurt someone's head or neck.

Some coaches assign F one by position instead, especially with newer players. So check how yours does it.

Third idea. Follow the routes in order, and read how each one ends.

The lines in a diagram are routes. They show where a player goes, or where the puck goes.

Some routes carry a small number, a short way along the line. That number gives the order things happen in. Route one happens first, then route two.

Why the numbers? Because a still picture can't show what happened first. It can't show whether the pass beat the forechecker, or the forechecker got there before the pass. The numbers can.

The numbers count routes, not players. A number is never a player's name, and it never sits inside a player's shape. That is where the letters go.

So read the routes like steps. Find route one, and follow it to its end. Then find route two, and follow that. Don't take in the whole picture at once, because the order is the play.

Then look at how each route ends, because the end tells you what to do when you get there.

An arrowhead means the move carries on past the end of the line. Keep going.

Two short bars across the end mean you stop there, under control.

A route skating backwards simply ends where it is drawn.

Now the most important ending of all. Some routes finish on an opponent. They end in the same two short bars. Read them as arrive under control.

That is all they say. They say where you stop, and that you arrive under control. They never say whether you may make contact when you get there. A route that ends on an opponent is not an instruction to hit.

Don't push or shove a player either. That can be a penalty in any league. Whether you may body check at all depends on your age group and your league. Many youth, women's and non-check adult leagues ban it outright. And where checking is banned, a hit can get you thrown out of the game. Whether checking is allowed can also change from one game to the next, depending on who you play.

So when you arrive, play the puck with your stick, not a hit. If you can see their numbers, pull up. Find out what your league allows before you play any route that finishes on a player. And until you know, don't hit anyone.

Fourth idea. Keep the relationship, not the spot.

A diagram draws everyone at one instant. So a drawn position is not a spot to skate to. It is where that player is compared with the puck and the other players, at that moment.

If you're a defenceman, look at where you are compared with the puck and the attacker you're covering. If you're a forward supporting a teammate, look at where you are compared with the teammate who has the puck.

That relationship is what you take onto the ice. The puck moves, so you move with it, and you keep the same relationship. If the teammate with the puck moves up the ice, you move up the ice too. If the attacker you are covering moves, you stay in the same place compared with them, not with the drawing.

Why? Because the drawn spot is only where the play was at one moment. A moment later the puck has moved, and the spot is wrong. A player who skates to the drawn mark and stays there is standing where the play used to be.

Let me pull those four ideas together, because they are the core of reading any diagram.

One. Read the caption first, and believe it over the picture. When it names a coaching choice, ask which version your team runs.

Two. Find yourself. Circle is a forward, triangle a defenceman, G the goalie. Open is your team, solid is theirs. The letter is a role, so if you are first to the puck on the forecheck, you are F one. Go for the puck, not the player, and never hit anyone from behind or anyone who can't see you coming. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call, and if you can see their numbers, pull up.

Three. Follow the routes in the order of their numbers. An arrowhead keeps going. Two bars stop there, under control. A route that ends on an opponent means arrive under control and play the puck. It never means you may hit, and you never hit anyone from behind or anyone who can't see you coming. Find out what your league allows, and until you know, don't hit anyone.

Four. Keep the relationship to the puck and the other players, not the spot on the page.

Everything else in this episode adds detail to those four. You don't need to memorise the rest. Come back to it when a mark is unfamiliar.

Fifth idea. Check whose key it is. And on your own team, your coach's whiteboard wins.

Hockey has no single standard way of drawing plays. Different keys draw things differently. Even the keys behind this guide disagree with each other in places.

Here is where this guide's marks come from, so you know what to expect elsewhere. The player shapes come from a handout used on a coaching course. Most of the lines come from the key published by the IIHF, the international federation. And a few marks are this guide's own, and appear in neither. Those are the shading, the dot for the puck, and the numbers on the routes. So most of what you learn here you will meet again, but not all of it, and not always with the same meaning.

So on a diagram from anywhere else, check its key before you read it. If no key is printed, ask whoever drew it, before the drill starts.

And on your own team, your coach's whiteboard wins. Nothing in this guide has been checked against how any particular club or coach draws things. If a mark on the whiteboard is new to you, ask about it before the drill starts.

Sixth idea. The letters, and the jobs they give you.

On the forecheck, the letters tell you the job. F one goes to the puck. F two comes in second, and covers the way out that F one has left open, rather than going for the same puck. F three stays high, and is the first one back if the puck gets out.

The same limits hold for all three. Go for the puck, not the player. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call. Never hit a player from behind, or one who can't see you coming. If you can see their numbers, pull up and play the puck. Even a light hit from behind can badly hurt someone's head or neck.

Why go by who arrives, and not by position? Because the puck won't wait for the right person to get there. And two players doing one job while nobody does the other is a very expensive mistake.

So say which one you are. A quick call, like I've got F one, or I'm high, lets your linemates take the other two jobs.

The letters move with the play, too. If you were F one and you get beaten, don't chase. Take whichever job is now empty. Usually that means dropping in high as the new F three. That is what stops a forecheck from wearing you out.

Now the marks themselves.

Seventh idea. The lines, and what each one tells you to do.

A solid line with an arrowhead is skating forward, without the puck.

A long, smooth wave is skating with the puck. You carry it. Keys give this one different names, like skate and stickhandle, or skating with control of the puck. The drawing is the same, so go by the drawing, not the name.

A dashed line with an arrowhead is a pass. The puck travels along it, not a player.

A double line is a shot.

Skating backwards is a row of small arches that overlap, each one flatter than it is wide. Watch for this one on other diagrams. Some keys draw skating backwards as a tight wave instead.

A sharp zigzag, straight lines and corners rather than curves, is backward crossovers.

Short bars drawn across a line, like a little fence, are lateral crossovers. That means crossover steps going sideways. The published version is just the row of bars, with no line and no direction. This guide adds a line and an arrow, so you know which way you are going.

A line ending in two bars is a sudden stop. A pair of slashes at the end of a line is a different mark. It is a plain stop, and this guide uses that one too.

A bare hook, curling in low and leaving the other way, is a pivot. That means turning from skating forwards to skating backwards, or back again. You will find it in the key, but in no play diagram here. That's because this way of drawing can't show which way a player is facing.

Dropping the puck means leaving it behind for a teammate. It is drawn as a small marker, then a straight line with an arrowhead. The published keys put a wave in front of it, to show the carry that set the drop up. This guide leaves the wave off, so the mark shows only the drop.

The puck itself is a small solid dot, about a third the width of a player. Don't confuse it with the marker on a drop pass. The drop-pass marker sits on a route, not beside a player.

Which team has the puck isn't shown by the player shapes at all. The puck is drawn, and the caption tells you the rest.

Eighth idea. The one mark this guide never uses, and why.

A line that ends in a single bar appears in none of these diagrams. That is on purpose.

On one coaching handout, a single bar means checking pressure, closing on a player. A coaching manual uses the same mark for a screen, a pick or a block. And the IIHF's key has no single-bar mark at all. There, one bar is just half of its two-bar sudden stop.

So picture what happens. A coach draws a single bar to mean pressure. A player who learned the IIHF's key reads someone stopping, where the coach meant someone closing in. Same mark, a different job. That is the clearest reason not to assume a key you meet somewhere else means the same as this one. If your own coach draws a single bar, ask what it means before the drill starts. Don't guess.

So in this guide, a route that closes on an opponent ends in the two bars, the same mark as a sudden stop. It is the one place these diagrams knowingly use one mark for two things. You tell them apart by where the route finishes. If it finishes on open ice, it is a stop. If it finishes on an opponent, it means arrive under control, and the caption tells you what the drill is for.

Why two bars and not an arrowhead? Because an arrowhead reads as carrying on past the end of the line. The bars read as ending there. You arrive, and you stop, under control. Play the puck, not the player. And it still never means you may hit.

Let me pull the marks together.

Solid arrow, skate forward. Long smooth wave, skate with the puck. Dashed arrow, a pass. Double line, a shot. Small overlapping arches, skate backwards. Sharp zigzag, backward crossovers. Bars across a line, crossovers sideways. Two bars at the end, stop there under control.

Where two bars finish on an opponent, arrive under control and play the puck. Never take it as permission to hit, and never hit anyone from behind or anyone who can't see you coming. Until you know your league's answer, don't hit anyone.

And a single bar means different things on different keys, so this guide never uses it.

Ninth idea. Shaded areas, and faint dashed lines.

Many diagrams in this guide wash an area of the ice in a light colour, with a thin dashed edge. Usually its name is written inside it. The slot. The house, which is a wider scoring area in front of the net, shaped like a baseball home plate with its point at the net. Or the passing lane.

The shading is this guide's own. Neither published key has anything like it. Four things are worth knowing about it.

First, the shading is not paint on the ice, unless the caption says it is. The slot and the house are names for areas, not markings. Go and look at a rink, and nothing marks either of them out. You find them from the lines that are painted, like the faceoff circles, the dots and the crease.

The edge of the shading is drawn thin and broken on purpose, so you can't mistake it for a line on the rink.

The one exception is the trapezoid, a shape painted on the ice behind the net, where the game uses one. That one really is paint, and its caption says so.

Second, the shading doesn't tell you whether to go there or stay out. The caption does, and the diagrams use it both ways. In one picture a shaded box is the ice a centre is heading for. In another it is ice a pass must never cross. Read it the wrong way round, and you could send a pass through the one place it must never go. So for every shaded area, ask one question. Is this ice to get to, or ice to keep clear? The caption answers it.

Where an area is a danger, it is tinted red instead of blue. That is the one thing in this way of drawing carried by colour alone. At such a light shade, red and blue look almost the same in black and white, or on a photocopy. So read the caption, because it says in words which one it is.

Third, the same faint dashed mark drawn between two points, with no shading, is a line rather than an area. It has a short name beside it. It shows something you need to see that isn't painted on the ice and isn't anyone's route. Nobody skates it.

One example is the royal road. That is the imaginary line down the middle of the ice, from one net to the other. Here is why it gets drawn. A pass that crosses it just before a shot makes the goalie push hard sideways. So the shot arrives before the goalie is set. When you see the royal road drawn, look for the pass across it.

Another example is the shooting lane a defenceman had a second ago, before someone stepped into it. Lines like these are there so you can see what someone else is set up for.

Fourth, when one area is shaded more heavily than another in the same picture, the strength of the shade is a ranking.

The example in this guide is a map of what a mistake costs. It grades the ice by how expensive it is to lose the puck there. It marks five areas, four in red and one in blue. But the four reds come in only three strengths, because two are the same on purpose. The middle of the neutral zone is shaded just as heavily as your own end. That's because a mistake in either costs about the same. So on the ice, be as careful with the puck in the middle of the rink as you are in your own end.

So don't expect four different reds, and don't try to rank them by eye. Take the order from the caption's words. The shades are close together by design. Black and white printing squeezes them closer still. And across red and blue, the order flips completely, because a light blue prints darker than the faintest red.

Let me pull the shading together. A shaded area is a name for some ice, not paint. The caption tells you whether to go there or keep out. Red means danger, and the caption says so in words. A dashed line with no shading is something to see, not a route. And darker shading is a ranking, which you take from the caption, never by eye.

Tenth idea. The traps on someone else's diagram.

These are the places where another key will catch you out.

The biggest trap is the IIHF's key. It uses the circle and the triangle for the two teams, one shape for the attacking side and the other for the defending side. The position goes inside as a number. One is the goalie, two the right defence, three the left defence, four the right wing, five the centre, and six the left wing. Even the goalie can be a number there, inside a team shape, not a bare G.

So on that key, a right wing on the defending team is a triangle. Same two shapes, a completely different meaning. Read it with this guide's eyes and you would take that winger for a defenceman. So don't carry this guide's reading onto one of its diagrams.

This guide doesn't use those numbers. A number would say F one is the centre. And F one isn't the centre. It is whoever gets to the puck first.

Open and solid can be a trap too. The coaching handout this guide's player shapes come from prints an open and a solid version of each player. But it never says what the difference means. Reading open as your team and solid as theirs is this guide's own choice. On another diagram, open and solid may mean something else.

One letter clashes. On both published keys, a circled C means the coach. In this guide it means a centre. No diagram here draws a coach. But on someone else's diagram, a circled C is the person on the bench.

Some keys also have a mark that means a hit. The IIHF's key has a line ending in a small curled bracket, and it calls that mark a body check. That tells you what the person who drew the drill had in mind. It does not tell you what your league allows. No key settles whether you may hit. Your age group and your league decide that. Until you know, don't hit anyone.

And don't call any of these drawings the international way of drawing hockey. Both published keys have the word international in their titles, and they still disagree. Each meaning is right inside its own key. They are just not the same key.

One last trap, and it is on every diagram. A diagram shows one moment. A movement that only makes sense over time needs two pictures. So where you see a pair, read the caption before the pictures. It tells you whether they are a sequence, one after the other, or two different answers to the same moment.

Let me pull the traps together. On the IIHF's key, the two shapes are the two teams, and the number inside is the position. Open and solid may mean something else on another key. On someone else's diagram, a circled C is the coach. A curled bracket is a body check, and it still doesn't tell you what your league allows. And where you see a pair of pictures, the caption tells you how to read them.

Let me pull it all together.

Read a diagram in this order. The caption first, and believe it over the picture. Then find yourself. Then follow the routes in order, and see how each one ends.

The caption tells you what the picture is for, and which details are a coaching choice. Where it names one, ask which version your team runs.

You are an open shape. A circle is a forward, a triangle a defenceman, a G the goalie, and an X a practice cone. The letter inside is a role, not a spot. If you are first to the puck on the forecheck, you are F one, unless your coach assigns it by position. Go for the puck, not the player, and never hit anyone from behind or anyone who can't see you coming. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call, and if you can see their numbers, pull up.

Numbers on routes give the order. An arrowhead carries on. Two bars stop there, under control. Keep your relationship to the puck and the other players, not the spot on the page.

A route that ends on an opponent means arrive under control and play the puck, not the player. It never tells you whether you may hit. Your age group and your league decide that. Until you know, don't hit anyone.

Shading is a name, not paint. The caption says whether it is ice to go to or ice to keep out of. Darker shading is a ranking, and the order is in the caption's words.

And on any diagram from somewhere else, check the key first. On your own team, your coach's whiteboard wins.

So here are three questions to ask your coach. Do you assign F one by who gets to the puck first, or by position? When a diagram shows a coaching choice, which version do we run? And is there any mark you use that I should know before the drill starts?

And one for your league. Is body checking allowed in my age group and my league?

Go and ask. Their answers tell you how to read what your team draws, and now you know why each mark is drawn the way it is.

### 4. What I left out, and why

⚠️ **Read this first. The omission a listener could be caught by:** the book names, ages and divisions of the no-checking rule. These are USA Hockey 12 and under, all girls' and women's, non-check adult, and any game against such a team (:10–12). They also include Hockey Canada U13 and below, female hockey, and divisions a Member approves (:12–13). The script says "depends on your age group and your league" and "many youth, women's and non-check adult leagues ban body checking outright". It adds "the answer can change from one game to the next, depending on who you play" and "until you know, don't hit anyone". That last line is said in ideas three, eight and ten, in the spine recap, in the marks recap and in the close. **It is survivable because the instruction is stricter, not cheaper.** The residual risk is that a USA Hockey under-twelve or Hockey Canada U13 listener is not told by name that they are in a no-check game. **If one thing goes back in, make it those two thresholds in one sentence.**

Also left out:
- **The tiers of a no-check-game hit**: Hockey Canada 7.3(b) (a major plus game misconduct, mandatory if the player is injured) and 7.3(c) (a match penalty for deliberate injury), and USA Hockey 604(d) (a major plus game misconduct) and 604(e) (a match penalty). These are in the trailer only (:299). After review MJ2, the script now says "where checking is banned, a hit can get you thrown out of the game", which is the plain top consequence without the book ladder. The match tier is not voiced (brief item 33).
- "Read Body Contact and Battles before acting on any route that finishes on a player" (:13–15, CM :251). The script does not point at other episodes, so the gate itself is spoken instead.
- The Hockey Eastern Ontario sheet by name, the "International Drill Symbols" title, the Johnston and Walter citation, and the sheet's poor pedigree (trailer :303–307). These are provenance, not reading.
- That the IIHF key's lateral-crossover equivalent is "a different shape again" (:106–107).
- That the two-bar mark is called "stopping" on the other sheet (:84–85).
- The boards short form. **No boards act is taught.** The F one sentences say "goes to the puck" and never mention boards or corners. ⚠️ If a reviewer reads "F one goes to the puck" as a boards retrieval, item 26 would need the short form there too. FS :219 and :603 carry it.
- F one's angling job, "takes away one side" (FS :12, :16). I cut it because angling steers a body (item 6). Its full limb set (FS :202, :215, :221, :293) would not fit in a reading episode. **Whole or not at all, so out.** F two keeps "covers the way out that F one has left open", which is position, not contact.

### 7. Departures from the source and the template

- **Plain glosses not in any of the three sources:** "a diagram, a drawing of the rink from above"; "forwards are the centre and two wingers, the defencemen play behind them"; "lateral crossovers, crossover steps going sideways"; "dropping the puck means leaving it behind for a teammate". These are definitions of terms the source uses, as §2 requires. The remake's C-cut gloss was dropped because nothing traces it.
- **Applied examples written by me**, each a direct application of a source sentence, not a new claim: the forecheck-diagram example in idea one; the defenceman and support-forward relationship examples (the old source carried these, the current blob does not); "read the routes like steps"; "if your coach draws a single bar, ask"; "be as careful with the puck in the middle of the rink as in your own end" (from :146's "a mistake in either costs about the same"); and "Read it the wrong way round, and you could send a pass through the one place it must never go".
- **Numbers pull-up limb:** FS :583 says "if you have ended up behind them, play the puck". The "see their numbers, pull up" wording is the brief's (item 26), not the source's.
- **"Never hit a player… who can't see you coming"** is the brief's wording (item 22). FS :603 gives the reason, a hit "you can neither see nor brace for".
- **"A very expensive mistake"** softens FS :49's "the most expensive forechecking error there is", which is coaching opinion stated as a superlative.
- **Template shape:** the template's "rink one end at a time" has no counterpart in a notation page. I replaced it with three parts, the marks, the shading and other people's keys, each with its own recap. The spine misses the first-quarter target (§1).
- **The trailer is voiced twice**: the shove limb and, after review MJ2, the ejection limb.
