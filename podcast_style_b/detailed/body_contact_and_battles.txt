===== SCRIPT START =====

This episode is about body contact and battles. That means winning the puck on the boards, in the corners, at the net and in a race for a loose puck. And it means doing it without getting hurt, thrown out of the game or sent to the penalty box. We will take it slowly. For each piece I will tell you what to do, and also why it works. If you know why something works, you can still do the right thing when the play looks different from the picture in your head.

Before any instructions, a few words you will hear all the way through.

Body contact. This is two players leaning on each other while they both chase the puck. It is part of the game almost everywhere, at every age.

A body check. This is using your body on purpose to knock a player off the puck. That is a different thing. Many leagues and many age groups ban it.

The boards, or the wall. That is the fence around the ice. Most battles happen against it.

The other player. On the boards, that means the opponent coming at you, whether they are after the puck or just coming at you.

Their numbers. The numbers are on the back of a player's jersey. If you can see them, you are looking at the player's back.

The crease. This is the goalie's area, the blue paint in front of the net. In many leagues the red line around its edge counts as part of it.

The slot. This is the ice right in front of the net, between the two faceoff circles. It is where most goals are scored from.

The net front. This is the ice just outside the crease, where shots get tipped and rebounds land. A screen is a player standing there to block the goalie's view.

And the prices. A minor penalty is two minutes in the penalty box. A major penalty is five. Some penalties throw you out of the game. A match penalty throws you out, and keeps you out of later games until your case is dealt with.

That is the vocabulary. Now the job.

Most games are decided by small contests for a loose puck, on the wall, in the corner and at the net. Coaches will tell you that, though nobody has counted it. Those contests are won by position, balance and support more often than by strength.

I will start with four big ideas, because they matter most. If you only listen to the first part of this episode, take those four with you. After that we will go back through each kind of battle and add the detail.

First idea. Take the puck with your route and your stick, not your body. But first, one question to answer before you play. Does your game allow body checking? Nothing on the ice tells you. Your age group, your league and even the team you are playing can change the answer. So ask your league, and get the answer in writing. Until you have it, don't hit anyone.

Here is the good news. You don't need to hit anyone to take the puck. Most pucks are taken without a hit, and the way good defenders do it works in every league.

Start with your route. Angling means steering the player with the puck by the line you skate, not by hitting them. Skate at the ice they want, not at the player. If they want the middle, your line takes the middle away and leaves them the boards. Coaches call the middle good ice and the outside bad ice. Your job is to leave them only bad ice.

Why does that work? Near the boards the carrier has less ice to use, and a small enough gap leaves them no way to cut back inside. And an angle is legal in every league.

Pick your line early and hold it. Then add nothing when you arrive. If you push out an arm, a shoulder or a hip, or swerve into them at the last moment, the angle has turned into a check. That is true however gentle the contact was.

When you close in, your stick goes on the puck, not a hit. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call. And if you can see their numbers, pull up.

Then your stick. Stick on the puck is fine. Stick on their hands or their body is a penalty. The stick check worth learning first is the stick lift. Put your blade under their stick, low on the shaft, well below their bottom hand. Lift up, not sideways. Step through, take the puck, and let go. Never a two-handed shove with the shaft. Keeping your blade on the ice doesn't make it legal.

Why low on the shaft? Down there you are well clear of their hands, and every league protects the hands. It is also where the leverage is, so their stick comes up easily.

Never chop at their hands. A chop is never the cheap option. In every league it can get you thrown out of the game, even if nobody is hurt.

So that is the first idea. Find out whether your game allows checking, and until you know, don't hit anyone. Then take the puck with your route and your stick. Pick your line early, add nothing at the end, and lift the stick low and let go.

Second idea. Hold the ice you are standing on, and arrive at every battle with position. You are allowed the ice you are standing on. So you can defend a whole shift without hitting anyone. Get in front of the player first. Then hold that spot, and make their route to the puck longer. Stick down, free hand off them.

Why does that work? Standing in the place they need to be is a complete defensive act. You don't have to touch them. Making a player skate the long way round is often worth more than a hit, because it buys your teammates time to get back.

Getting there first is the part that matters. Get your spot by arriving, never by moving into someone. Moving sideways into a player to take their spot can be called interference. That is the penalty for getting in the way of a player who doesn't have the puck. Into a player with the puck, it's a body check, which many games ban. And in some leagues you keep only a spot you already have, and you never lean or use your body to keep it.

Now the limits, and they go with every battle in this episode. Hold your spot with no shove and no lean. Never your stick across anyone. Never into anyone's back, so if you can see their numbers, pull up. Never lift anyone off their feet. And even going slowly, a crash into the boards or a post can hurt.

The same idea decides every battle, on the wall, in a corner or for a loose puck. Arrive with your body between the other player and the puck, and the wall on the far side. Then get low and wide.

Here is why position beats speed. The player who gets there first decides where the battle happens. But arriving first with your body in the wrong place is worse than arriving second with it in the right place.

And here is why low and wide works. Feet wider than your shoulders, knees well bent, backside low, chest up. A low, wide player is very hard to knock off the puck, and can still shuffle, turn and step out. A stiff, upright player can only fall over. Coaches say your base often beats the other player's strength. That is coaching know-how, not measured, and a big enough strength gap still wins. But your base is the part you control.

So that is the second idea. Get in front first and hold your ice, with no shove and no lean. Arrive at every battle with your body between the other player and the puck, then get low and wide.

Third idea. Never hit a player who can't see you coming. Some hits are never yours, in any league, checking or not. Here they are.

Never hit a player whose back is to you. If you can see their numbers, pull up and play the puck. Never hit a player facing the boards. Never hit anyone in the head, face or neck. Never hit a player once the puck has gone. And never be the third player into a battle between your teammate and an opponent.

Why these? In every one of them, the other player can't see you, can't brace, or is already busy. A body that isn't ready for contact can't absorb it. That is how heads and necks get hurt, even when the hit looks small.

Here is what they cost. If you hit a player from behind, plan on being thrown out of the game. If you send a player head first into the boards or the net from behind, plan on being thrown out, in every league. If they couldn't protect themselves, some leagues make it a match penalty, and the referee has no choice.

A hit to the head is never capped at two minutes. It can get you thrown out even if nobody is hurt. So can a late hit, after the puck has gone.

These hits are banned in every division, non-checking included. So are charging and boarding. Building up speed into contact, even with two hard strides, can be charging. Boarding means putting a player into the boards in a way that is dangerous for them. Neither stops at two minutes, and either can get you thrown out.

Play as though avoiding a hit from behind is always your job, whatever the other player does. A neck injury isn't undone by a good call from the referee.

So that is the third idea. If you can see their numbers, pull up and play the puck. Nothing to the head, nothing after the puck has gone, and never third into a battle.

Fourth idea. Meet the boards on your terms. The boards can hurt you even when nobody hits you. So this matters in every league, and at every speed.

Keep your head up, always, and most of all when you might be hit. You can't brace for a hit you can't see. And in some leagues, skating with your head down means the rules protect you less, not more.

Two things you never do. Never turn your back to the other player. And never duck.

Both protect your head and neck. If you turn your back, the contact becomes a hit from behind that you can't see coming or brace for. If you duck, your head meets the boards first. With your chin tucked, a head-first crash can badly hurt your neck, even at walking speed. So a slow crash is not a safe one.

Here is how to meet the wall instead. Best of all, come into the boards on an angle, not straight on. Then an arm, a leg or your side takes most of it. Better still, get the puck off the wall before you get there.

If contact is coming and you can still choose, turn your skates parallel to the boards. Feet apart, knees bent, so you are low. Forearm and hip to the boards. Never the point of your shoulder, and not the whole shoulder if you can help it. Your shoulder faces the other player. It is the boards it should not meet. Lean into the impact, and spread it over as much of your side as you can.

If you are already facing the wall, try to turn parallel if there is any chance at all. If you can't, that is the last resort, not a choice. Get your stick and gloves up and spread on the glass, arms out, bending and giving like a shock absorber. Keep your head off it completely.

From here on I will use one short version of that, the same words every time, so you can learn it like a line. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the other player, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly.

And never turn your back on purpose to draw a penalty. It may not even get you the call, and it is your neck on the line. Don't crouch to dodge a check either. Crouching drops your head just like ducking does, and in some leagues it is a penalty too.

Let me pull those four ideas together, because they are the core of it.

One. Find out whether your game allows checking, and until you know, don't hit anyone. Take the puck with your route and a low stick lift, and add nothing at the end of the angle.

Two. Hold the ice you are standing on, with no shove and no lean. Arrive at every battle with position, then get low and wide.

Three. Never hit a player who can't see you coming. If you see their numbers, pull up and play the puck.

Four. Meet the wall on your terms. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the other player, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly.

Everything else in this episode adds detail to one of those four. So now let's go back through it, starting with your league and the ways to take a puck without a hit.

Fifth idea. Your league, in a little more detail. Body checking is banned in a lot of hockey. That includes the younger age groups, and all girls' and women's hockey under USA Hockey and Hockey Canada. It includes adult leagues that have made themselves non-checking. And being above a certain age doesn't settle it either, because a local association can make any age group non-checking.

Check the game, not just your age group. When a checking team plays a non-checking team, nobody may check in that game.

If you play in Britain, no rulebook gives an age for checking. Your own competition's rules do. So ask your club or league secretary, and get it in writing.

Women's hockey has its own rules. Under the international women's rules, which British women's hockey is built on, a step or glide into a player is a penalty. So is using the boards to push or pin them. The national women's league in Britain is non-checking at both its levels. And being a woman doesn't put you in a non-checking game on its own. If you also play in a mixed or men's league, ask what that league allows. In British women's hockey, any major penalty also gets you thrown out of the game. Treat British junior hockey the same way.

Where checking is banned, a hit is not capped at two minutes. It starts there. But a hard or reckless one can get you thrown out even if nobody is hurt, and in some leagues a reckless one is a match penalty.

Non-checking doesn't mean non-contact either. A non-checking game can be very physical, so expect to be leaned on, and learn to hold your ground. In at least one adult league even leaning on purpose is a penalty, and you may be expected to get out of the way of contact you could avoid. Ask whether that is your league.

One more thing before the puck drops. The warm-up counts. Keep your hands off opponents, and stay on your own side of the centre red line. Why does it count? It is the one stretch of the night when you share the ice with opponents, and there is usually no referee standing on it. That is why a shove there can turn into a suspension, not two minutes. A fight in the warm-up can get you thrown out before the game has started.

Sixth idea. Angling, in more detail. Angling is steering with your route. Here are the four things you are managing while you do it.

First, skate at the ice they want, not at them, so they are left only the boards. If you skate at the body instead, a good carrier moves the puck to their far side and goes round you.

Second, come in with your momentum heading back toward your own net, not sideways across the ice. Here is why. If they get rid of the puck, you are already going the right way, and you stay between them and your net. If you angle flat across the ice and get beaten, you are chasing.

Third, arrive under control. Slow your feet as you get close, knees bent, so you can still change direction. Don't cross your feet over in the last few strides. A crossover commits your weight one way, and a good carrier cuts back against it. And start early, because you can't close a gap from a standing start.

Fourth, keep your stick on the ice, covering the lane back to the middle, while your body covers the outside. When you arrive, if they still have the puck, your stick goes on the puck.

Where does the angle end? In a non-checking game, it ends with a seal. Your body takes away the last of their space, your stick arrives on the puck, and you take it or force them to shoot it away. A seal takes the space. It never pushes the player. No shove, no lean, no stick across anyone, never into a back, and never lift anyone off their feet. Even going slowly, a crash into the boards can hurt.

In a checking game the same angle can end with a check, but only a legal one. Your stick still goes on the puck first. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call. And if you can see their numbers, pull up. Either way, the angle comes first and the contact is the last step. Coaches say a hit thrown without an angle is thrown from bad position, and that is where most penalties come from.

Angling is a skating skill before it is a defensive skill. So the better you skate, the better you angle.

Seventh idea. Four stick checks, and why they keep you out of the box. The rule most players are taught is simple. Stick on the puck is legal. Stick on their hands or body is a penalty. Some people add, or stick on their stick. Don't count on that one. In many leagues a stick that pulls on theirs is hooking.

The poke check. This is a short, quick jab of your blade at the puck, when the carrier has it out in front of them. It works best when they are taking a pass, looking up or starting a deke. It is also the natural finish to an angle, when the carrier has run out of room.

Keep your top hand on the end of your stick and reach from your shoulder. Don't lunge with your whole body. Why not? The moment you commit your feet, a good carrier steps round your stick, and you are out of the play. If you can't reach the puck without lunging, you are too far away. Poke, recover, keep skating. And it is a jab at the puck, never at the hands, never a chop.

The stick lift, once more. Low on the shaft, well below the bottom hand, up not sideways, step through, take the puck, let go. Never at the hands, never a chop, never a two-handed shove. Now the timing. Lift late, as they are about to shoot, pass or take a pass, not while they are just carrying it. Why late? What keeps a lift legal is that it plays the puck. A lift on a player with no puck and no pass coming plays no puck, and it can be called hooking or holding.

The lift works from behind and beside a player, where you can't legally do anything else. Lifting the stick of a player who has the puck is playing the puck, not holding the player. But never hit a player who can't see you coming, and never from behind. The lift is your stick on their stick, nothing else.

It is also the right tool at the net front, in a board battle and on a backcheck. Those are the three places where players reach, hook or cross-check instead, and go to the box. And it leaves you balanced. You never lean past your own centre of gravity, so if it fails, you are still in the play.

One lift to be careful with. If the player is bent low over the puck, their face is down near the height of your stick. Take the lift low under the shaft, or leave it alone. A miss that rides up into their face is high-sticking, however low your own hands were. That can get you thrown out even if nobody is hurt.

The sweep check. This is a low, wide sweep of your stick along the ice, through the puck or the passing lane. Use it when you are beaten to the outside, or covering a pass across the slot, the ice in front of the net between the faceoff circles. Slide your bottom hand down, get low, and sweep through the ice, not the air. The blade stays on the ice, and your feet stay under you. A sweep at knee or waist height is a hook or a slash.

The stick press. This is the opposite of a lift. You lay your blade on top of theirs and pin it to the ice, so they can't shoot or pass while your teammate arrives. Use it in tight, at the net front or in a board battle, when you want to hold things up until help gets there. Press, don't chop. Never on their hands, and never a two-handed shove. And let go once the moment has passed.

With all four, never a two-handed shove with the shaft. Keeping your blade on the ice doesn't make it legal.

Why learn these? They take the puck with far less risk of a penalty than a hit does. All four work where checking is banned. If you want to become a better defender this season, learn the stick lift.

Eighth idea. Holding your ice, in more detail. Boxing out means getting your inside shoulder ahead of theirs, and your hips between them and the puck. Then you hold it. It is the same idea as boxing out for a rebound in basketball. Sealing is the same thing along the boards. You get into the lane they want first and hold it, and the boards close off the other side. You are taking the space, not pressing them into the wall.

Same limits as before. No shove, no lean, no stick across anyone, never into a back, and pull up on the numbers. Never lift anyone off their feet. Even going slowly, a crash into the boards can hurt.

Three things turn good position into a penalty. Holding your stick out to make yourself wider. Using your free hand to hold, pull or grab. And using your spot as cover for a hit that would be illegal anyway. A spot you were allowed to hold doesn't make a late hit, a charge or a hit from behind legal.

In some leagues your free hand may fend off a player or their stick. It may never hold, and never push off to make space. In other leagues even the fend is allowed only in narrow cases, so don't build your game on it.

If you lose your spot, the only legal way back is with your feet. Reaching with your stick is hooking. Reaching with your free hand is holding.

Let me pull this part together. Find out what your game allows, in writing, and expect contact even where checking is banned. Angle at the ice, with your momentum back toward your net, and arrive under control. Stick on the puck, with a low lift and a light press, never a chop. Hold your ice with no shove and no lean, and if you lose it, skate back.

Ninth idea. The board battle, a fight for the puck against the wall. Coaches say most possessions in most games start or end with one. Nobody has counted that.

You arrive the way the second idea says. Body between the other player and the puck, wall on the far side, low and wide. If you arrive second, your stick goes on the puck, not a hit. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call. And if you see their numbers, pull up.

Now use the wall. Put the puck between your skates and the boards, with your body on the outside of it. Here is why. Nobody can come at you through the boards. So instead of being attacked from every side, you only have to protect half the circle around you. The boards are a teammate that never gets tired.

Your feet are free when your stick is tied up. In a real battle your stick is often tangled, and the puck is under a pile of skates. So kick the puck with the inside of your skate. Back to a teammate, along the wall, or up onto your own stick. Or stand over it with your skates wide, so nobody else can have it. Kicking it out is often the best way to win a puck that is truly stuck. Practise this, because almost nobody does, and it wins pucks every game.

Kick the puck, never a player. Never use your skate to push a player, even without a kick. Kicking at a player, or even trying to, is a match penalty in most leagues, and it is never a two-minute mistake anywhere. And if you can't see the puck, don't swing your skate at where you think it is.

Sometimes you can't win it, and neither can they. If you are outnumbered, or tangled with someone just as stuck, or losing it here would give them a chance at your net, pin the puck against the boards with your skate or your stick blade, and call for help. Hold it only until a teammate arrives to dig it out, a second or two.

Why pin it? A puck going nowhere is far better than a turnover in front of your net. The pin buys time for your second player. It doesn't buy a faceoff.

Pin the puck, never the player. Never hold it for a whistle, and never fall on it or gather it in to your body. Both are penalties. In your own crease that is a penalty shot, a free shot alone on your goalie. And with your goalie pulled, treat it as a goal against.

Don't pin it because a change is due, or because you are protecting a lead late on. Those are reasons to get the puck out, not to sit on it.

Once you have the puck, move it before the next opponent arrives. A won battle is only half a play.

The common approach is to move it straight away to the support player you saw or heard. In their end that is often low to high, back to the point. In your own end it is up the wall, or off the glass and out if nobody is open. The other option, when your support isn't there yet, is to protect it on the wall and work it low until it is. Which your team wants in each end is a coaching choice, so ask which yours plays. Either way, keep your eyes up while you work.

And while you work on the wall, the same position. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the other player, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly. If you are caught facing the wall, stick and gloves up and spread on the glass, head off it. That is only for when things have gone wrong.

Tenth idea. The corner. A corner battle is a board battle with two differences. The corner is the one place on the ice where help is closest. And the player you are fighting has two ways out.

Two players usually beat one in a corner. That is coaching experience, not something counted. The reason is simple. The first player in only has to stop the puck moving, not win it.

If you are first in, get your body between the opponent and the puck, and seal them off it. Your job is to stop the puck moving, not always to win it. Pin the puck, never the player. Your stick goes on the puck, not a hit, and whether you may hit at all is your league's call. If you can see their numbers, pull up. No shove, no lean, no stick across anyone, and never lift anyone off their feet. Even going slowly, a crash into the boards can hurt.

If you are second in, stay out of the pile. Read where the puck will squirt out, and pick it up. Your job is the loose puck, not a hit. The commonest mistake down there is two teammates fighting the same player for the same puck, while a third opponent skates away with it.

The corner has boards on two sides, so the carrier can spin either way, up the wall or out behind the net. Angle them so the way out they have is the one you want. Toward the wall and up, away from the net. Never leave them a way back to the slot, the dangerous ice in front of your net.

A seal has a limit. If you wrap your arms round a player against the boards, it's holding, and in some leagues a hold can get you thrown out. Never push into a player from behind at the boards either. In at least one league even light contact like that costs you two minutes plus ten more off the ice, and a hard one throws you out.

Whose corner is it in your own end? That is your coach's choice, not a rule, and there are two common systems.

In a zone defence, called the low zone collapse, each player covers an area. The low corner belongs to the defenceman on the puck side, with the centre helping. The wingers stay high, covering the points just inside the blue line, and don't come to the corner. That keeps the points covered.

In man-on-man, each player covers a person, and a winger follows their player into the corner. So you get help low. The price is that a point may be left open.

Why does it matter which one? Coaches say that when it goes wrong down there, it is usually two players working on different ideas of who had whom. It is not a lack of effort. So ask which your team plays.

Eleventh idea. The loose puck. That is a puck nobody has and both teams can reach. A rim round the boards, a dump-in, a rebound.

Winning the race is not the same as winning the puck. The player who arrives half a step behind, but on the inside, usually ends up with it. On the inside means between the other player and the puck. So skate the inside lane, and make them go the long way round.

Arrive on a curve, so you get there already turned. Turn your hips so your backside is between them and the puck. Get low and wide, and keep your feet moving, because a player standing still on the wall gets pinned.

Hold the lane with your body, never with your hands, your stick or your skates. Never use the lane to deliver a hit. Your stick goes on the puck, and whether you may hit at all is your league's call.

In some leagues you hold only a lane you already have, and never lean or use your body to keep it. And never hold a lane just to clear the way for a teammate with the puck. That is interference. Same limits here too. No shove, no lean, no stick across anyone, never into a back, pull up on the numbers, never lift anyone off their feet. Even going slowly, a crash into the boards can hurt.

Get there early, then decide late. That is a coaching rule of thumb, not a law. A player who arrives flat out, already committed to one move, has given away their options. A player who arrives early, slows into a wide balanced stance, and then reads the pressure can do whatever the play needs.

The commonest way to waste a won race is to swing at the puck straight away and put it into a shin pad. So take a beat while your support arrives. The limit is your own slot, or a player bearing down on you. There, there is no beat. Get it out.

It is also the safest way to arrive. A player who is upright, balanced and looking around is far harder to hurt than one stretched out at full reach with their head down.

Decide before you get there what you will do with it. Take it off the wall, chip it along, or protect it. The best play is often a single touch. Chip it past them and skate onto it, bank it off the boards to a teammate, or rim it. A single touch turns a coin flip into a possession, and it takes you out of the collision. Race to the puck, not the player. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call, and if you can see their numbers, pull up.

A rim sends the puck on round the boards. It beats a player who is sealing you against the wall, because it goes past them. A reverse sends it back the way it came, into the space a player has just left. It beats one who has come in too hard and gone past you. So pick by where they are going, and pick before you arrive.

Puck races to the wall are where hits from behind and boarding injuries happen. So never arrive bent over with your back to the play. Get side-on. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the other player, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly.

Hitting a player racing for an icing into the boards counts as boarding. That protects you when you are the one chasing it. But don't rely on it.

Let me pull the battles together. On the wall, arrive with position, get low and wide, and put the puck between your skates and the boards. Use your feet on the puck, never on a player. Pin the puck to wait for help, never for a whistle. In the corner, the first player stops the puck and the second reads where it comes out. Steer the carrier up the wall, never to the slot. On a loose puck, take the inside lane, get there early and decide late, and pick rim or reverse before you arrive.

Twelfth idea. The front of their net. Goals come from there, so both teams send bodies to it. Attackers go to screen the goalie, tip shots and bury rebounds. Defenders go to hold their ground and take away sticks. Why there? In one sample of top professional games, about a quarter of all goals came from within two stick lengths of the net.

Get there first. A coach's way of putting it is that you have to commit to getting there before you can play there. Then stand in front of the goalie, in the line the shot travels, not beside them. Why in front? You are there to take the goalie's eyes, and the goalie is looking down the line the shot will come along. Two hands on your stick, blade on the ice. A tip, a screen and a rebound all need your blade ready. Eyes on the puck, and feel the defender with your body.

Stand outside the crease, and off its red outline too. In many leagues that red line counts as part of the crease, and a skate on it can cost your team the goal. So stand a skate's width further out. That costs you nothing anywhere.

Keep your hands, glove and stick blade down. The screen is your body in the goalie's line of sight, and nothing else. A hand or a blade up in front of the goalie's face can be a penalty with no contact at all.

The screen is a place you stand. You never need to touch anybody to set it. And if the goalie came out to play the puck, never get in the way of them heading back to the crease.

Standing in the shot's path is the job here, so know when the puck is about to be shot. Have your helmet and cage on properly. Chin strap done up, and the chin cup of a full cage touching your chin, not hanging below it.

The net front is the hardest place on the ice to take contact. Your eyes are on the puck, so a defender can move you from behind or the side without you seeing it. And the path they move you along has a steel goal post at one end and the goalie at the other.

So do two things. Know where the near post is before you plant. And take the shove braced. Wide base, knees bent, weight low, giving with your knees and hips rather than locked stiff.

Head up, chin off your chest, never duck. The goal post can hurt you the same way the boards can. That holds in every league, because a defender can push you along that path whether the push is legal or not.

Keep your spot against the defender, but don't push back or lean, and never your stick across anyone.

The goalie is different. If a goalie leans into you to get their position, don't brace against them and don't hold your spot. Give ground straight away. Then set the screen up again, outside the paint and off its red line. Why? Holding your spot against the goalie costs your team the goal or puts you in the box, never nothing.

Now contact with the goalie itself. Any contact with a goalie can be a penalty, and it can get you thrown out of the game. A goalie who is down or turned can't protect themselves, so contact can hurt them. It can also cost your team the goal. So avoid the goalie, in the crease or out of it. A goalie who has come out of the crease is still not fair game. And charging a goalie, building up speed into them, can get you thrown out in every league.

Going for a rebound, play the puck, and make a clear, obvious effort to avoid the goalie. Go round the goalie, never through. Pull up short. Head up. If a defender shoves you into the goalie, being shoved is not your defence. What protects you is clearly trying to avoid the goalie. So go to the net under control.

Thirteenth idea. The front of your own net. Here is the order to do it in, and the order matters. First, body position. Inside shoulder ahead of theirs, hips between them and the puck. If you are established inside, they have to come through you. Hold that ground, low and wide. No shove, no lean, no stick across anyone, never into a back, pull up on the numbers, never lift anyone off their feet. Even going slowly, a crash into the post can hurt. And head up, chin off your chest. The goal post can hurt you the same way the boards can.

Why position first? The rules protect a spot you already hold, not one you take by moving someone. In at least one league even holding a spot can be called interference, so get there early and quietly.

Second, take their stick, not their body. As the puck arrives, lift their stick up, low on the shaft, well below their bottom hand. Or press their blade lightly to the ice. Then let go. Never at the hands, never a chop. That takes away the tip and the rebound. Why the stick? It does the job a cross-check was meant to do, at far less risk.

The timing matters. A press kept on their stick before the puck is anywhere near can be called holding. One coach's warning is worth keeping. If you get physical before the puck is on its way, you're going to get a penalty.

Third, never walk the screener out. Never try to push them off their spot, in any game, checking or not. Moving them can be called interference in every league. It is usually two minutes, but a violent or reckless one can be a major that gets you thrown out. Where checking is banned, it's an illegal check too.

Fourth, eyes on the puck, body on the player. You can't beat them to a rebound you didn't see leave the stick. A defender who turns to look at the player they are boxing out has stopped watching the dangerous thing.

Fifth, don't get pushed back into your own goalie. You end up screening your goalie, and saves turn into goals. And you land backwards on a goalie who is usually down and can't see you, right beside the post. So fight the shove early, rather than ride it into the paint. That collision risk is coaching caution, not something anyone has counted.

Head up and chin off your chest there too, because the post is right beside you.

Never cross-check to make space. A cross-check means hitting or shoving with the shaft of your stick, held in both hands. Keeping your blade on the ice doesn't make it legal. No league caps a cross-check at two minutes, and it can get you thrown out even if nobody is hurt. If it rides up above the shoulders, some leagues throw you out automatically. And never put your shaft into their numbers. In some leagues that counts as a hit from behind, with no two-minute option.

One more thing, about where a body goes. A scrum, a lost edge, or a push that went further than you meant can move a player without anyone trying to. Never move a body across the front of your own net. That path has a steel goal post at one edge and your goalie at the other. The player moved along it is going sideways or backwards, and can't see either. That is coaching caution drawn from the neck injury danger, not a written rule.

Never drive a player from behind into the goal frame. That is a hit from behind, so plan on being thrown out. If they couldn't protect themselves, some leagues make it a match penalty, and the referee has no choice. And never take a player's skates off the ice there. A player whose edges are gone can't brace, and can't choose what meets the post first.

Whose job the front of your own net is, is also your coach's choice. In the low zone collapse it is usually the defenceman on the far side from the puck. In man-on-man it belongs to whoever's player went there, which can just as easily be a forward. Ask which yours plays.

Let me pull the net front together. At their net, stand in the shot's path, outside the crease and off its red line, hands and blade down. Know where the post is, take the shove braced, head up, chin off your chest. Give ground to the goalie, and never go into the goalie, in the crease or out. At your own net, get inside first, lift or press their stick as the puck arrives, and let go. Never walk the screener out, never cross-check, never move a body across your goalmouth, and head up at the post.

Fourteenth idea. Taking a hit, anywhere on the ice. Get a wide base, knees bent, weight low. A stiff, upright player gets knocked over. A player in an athletic stance rides the contact. Standing upright with your feet together is the mistake to avoid.

Skate through it. Keep your legs moving through the contact, rather than slowing down or stopping. Why? It keeps you balanced and in the play. Meeting contact with your body ready and your feet moving costs far less than standing still and being hit. But meet it, don't go and get it. Never turn and skate at a player who is coming at you. That adds your speed to theirs, and then neither of you controls the collision. You may hold your ground instead.

Keep your hands and stick in front of your body, not out to the side. That keeps you ready to play the puck the moment the contact ends. And let your knees and hips give a little, rather than taking it all through a locked, stiff frame.

If you can't skate through it, keep your arms up, your knees bent and your head up. For a hit with no warning at all, there isn't much technique. Your best protection is knowing it is coming. So check over your shoulder to see where opponents are.

The best protection on the wall is not getting pinned at all. Know what's around you before the puck arrives, and have a plan before you get there. A chip, a rim, a reverse, or a spin off the wall with your head up, never turning your back to the other player. Players who arrive with no plan get pinned. Players who arrive with a plan get out.

And talk to each other. When a teammate goes back for a puck with their head down and pressure coming, call it, loud and early. Time means they have space. Man on, or their name and behind you, means they don't. Only say time when you are sure. A wrong time sends a teammate into a hit they weren't ready for. Those calls are common ones, not rules, so agree yours as a team. A player can't brace for a hit they can't see, and your call gives them the chance to.

Fifteenth idea. Giving a legal body check, but only where your game allows one. First, the gate. If checking is banned in tonight's game, everything in this idea is a penalty, and not a cheap one. Ask your league whether your game allows it, and get it in writing.

A body check is the last step, not the first. One national coaching manual teaches it at the end of a ladder. Skating, angling, stick checks, being comfortable with contact, body blocking, rub-outs, and only then a solid body check. Why that order? The earlier steps are what make the last one safe. If you can't yet angle a carrier, lift a stick cleanly or stay balanced through contact, that is the step you skipped.

Here is what a legal check is. Its purpose is to win the puck, not to punish or scare anyone. It goes on a player who has the puck. It comes from the front, diagonally from the front, or straight from the side. You hit with your hips and shoulders only. Hands, forearms, stick and elbows are all illegal. It lands above the knees and at or below the shoulders, and in at least one league it must land above the hips.

Start the check with your stick on the puck, blade low. That keeps your hands and elbows down. If your stick is up, you are about to commit a cross-check, a high stick or a hit to the head. An elbow pushed out is elbowing, hurt or not.

Play the body, not the head. Aim for the chest, square. Don't stretch up or out to reach.

Getting low means your own base, not hitting them low. If you drop your body to hit them low, on the legs, that is clipping, in every division. If your knee is what lands on them, that is kneeing. Neither stops at two minutes. And never go in low at a turned back. That counts as a hit from behind.

Hit through, not at. Keep your feet moving through the contact, so you finish standing over the puck.

Time it, don't chase it. Building up speed into contact, even with two hard strides, can be charging. So can a running or jumping hit. For an open-ice hit, where your game allows one, the player should be one stride away. Drive your shoulder into their chest, and don't lunge. If you need more strides, you don't have the gap, so angle them instead. Never leave your feet. A jump into a check is charging however short your run-up. And no league caps charging at two minutes. A violent or reckless charge can get you thrown out.

Everything from the third idea still holds. Never a hit from behind, to the head, after the puck has gone, or on a player already battling a teammate. If you can see their numbers, pull up.

Sixteenth idea. Blocking shots. It's useful and it's dangerous, and both matter. How much your team blocks is a coaching choice. Some teams ask defenders to block a lot and collapse in front of the goalie. Others ask them to take away the lane on their feet, and let the goalie see everything. Ask your coach which yours plays, and ask your goalie what they want.

If you block, here is the whole method, and it goes together.

Get close to the shooter. Close up, you take away more of the net, and the puck has less time to rise or change direction. Coaches say closer is also safer. That is coaching know-how, not measured.

Stay on your feet if you can. A standing block keeps you in the play, and a puck blocked on your feet often turns into a breakaway the other way. A dropped block takes you out of it, whether it works or not. And if a dropped block misses, the shooter walks round you.

Square up to the puck, with your padded parts facing it. Shin guards front-on, thigh, hip. Head up and out of the shot's path. Feet on the ice. Hands behind your body, with the backs of your gloves out. Tucking your hands behind your shin pads is for once you are down on the ice. Standing, it makes you bend forward, which brings your head down into the shot.

Never turn your back, never turn sideways, never lift a leg. All three put the parts your pads don't cover in the puck's path, and turning away means you can't see it. Don't lead with your stick either. Keep it flat on the ice beside you, or out of the lane. Coaches say a puck off a blade held out in front goes up, often into your own face. Close the distance with your feet. Don't lunge, because a lunge that misses leaves you out of the play.

Now, when not to block. This is the part that gets skipped, and it is where blocking costs goals.

On a two-on-one, two attackers against you alone, your job is the pass, and the goalie has the shooter. Don't block from right in front of your own goalie, where they can't see the puck. A shot deflected off a body they couldn't see is a goal. Either commit fully and take the shot, or get out of the lane so they can see it. Never stand half in the way. Don't dive at a point shot while their player stands unmarked at the side of your net. And stay up until the puck is actually shot, because going down early is exactly what a fake shot wants.

Because blocking puts you in the puck's path on purpose, wear shin guards that cover your shin and ankle, and gloves that aren't worn through in the palm.

Let me pull this part together. Taking a hit, stay wide and low, skate through it, and never turn into it. Have a plan before you reach the wall, and call time only when you are sure. Giving a check, only where your game allows it, only on the puck carrier, chest square, stick down, feet on the ice. Blocking, get close, stay standing and square, head out of the lane, and never block in front of your own goalie's eyes.

Seventeenth idea. Staying out of the box. Coaches say most amateur penalties aren't dirty. They are late, frustrated or lazy. Nobody has counted that, so use it as a way to look at your own game.

Late means you were beaten, so you reached. Hooking, holding, tripping and interference are what a beaten player reaches for. Frustrated means the shove back after the whistle. Lazy is the stick left on a player's hip because you couldn't be bothered to skate.

So skate more, and reach less. Keep your blade on the puck, never on the player. A stick that has to reach is a stick that hooks, holds or trips. If your first thought is their body, your stick is somewhere it shouldn't be.

Hooking, tripping and interference are usually two minutes, but not always. A violent or reckless one can be a major that gets you thrown out.

Play the whistle, then stop. Don't retaliate. That turns one penalty into two.

Penalties add up, too. In some leagues, three penalties in one game throw you out, even if each one was only two minutes.

Three habits for a scrum. First, never put your hand on an opponent's cage or facemask. It's an easy reflex, and it can get you thrown out of the game.

Second, keep your head out of theirs. Leaning cage against cage in a scrum counts as head-butting, even if nobody swung. In some leagues head-butting throws you out with no lighter option. So back your head away.

Third, keep your leg in front of you, never behind theirs. Putting your leg behind a player and taking their feet away is slew-footing. They go down backwards with nothing to break their fall. In most leagues even the lightest call for it gets you thrown out. And saying you played the puck first is no defence anywhere.

Don't take a penalty out of frustration at the referee. It's the cheapest goal you will ever give away. Every league calls the game a bit differently, so watch how the first five minutes are being called, and adjust.

Eighteenth idea. When it boils over. This episode is about legal contact, and fighting isn't it. But once a battle turns into a shoving match, the rules you play under are not the ones you see on television. The top professional rules are the lightest. The amateur rules are much harsher.

Gloves on, helmet on, hands down, skate away. That isn't weakness. It's the only way to stay on the ice. In most amateur leagues a fight gets you thrown out of the game, and in at least one, a punch that misses still counts as a fight.

Don't skate in to help a teammate, and don't skate in to break it up. The third player in can be thrown out. In some leagues the player who comes in to make peace is thrown out too, whatever their reason.

If it kicks off while you are on the bench or in the penalty box, stay there. Every league penalises leaving. In some, every player who leaves is thrown out, even one whose penalty has just run out and who only skates to their own bench. This is the one place where skating away is itself the offence. The rule is about leaving at all, not what you do once you are out. So wait until the officials wave you out.

Keep your helmet on, and never touch theirs. In some leagues taking your own helmet off in a scrap, or pulling theirs off, gets you thrown out. And do your chin strap up properly, so it stays on.

A game misconduct, being thrown out of the game, is never only tonight either. Every league can make it cost you the next game or more.

A team that plays hard, wins battles and takes no penalties is far more physical than one that hits everything and spends nine minutes a game a player short.

Nineteenth idea, and the most important if it ever happens. When a player is hurt. Treat every knock to the head as a possible neck injury. If a player may have hurt their head or neck, don't help them up, and don't move them. Helping them up is the most common and most damaging mistake. Somebody phones for help first. Make sure they can breathe and their airway is clear. Reassure them, keep them warm, and tell them not to move. Nobody moves them, except to keep their airway clear, unless they are trained to do more. And their helmet stays on. No exception.

Phone for an ambulance if anything looks serious, like being knocked out, a fit, neck pain, or numbness or tingling. But don't wait for any of those before treating it as a neck injury. That starts with the knock.

The commoner case is a player who takes a knock to the head and gets up. If a concussion is suspected, they come off the ice, and they don't come back that day. A medical professional decides when they play again. They don't have to have been knocked out. And don't let the player decide. Judgement is one of the things a concussion affects, and a concussed player will tell you they are fine.

Feeling better is not the all-clear either. There is no return to anything with a risk of a knock, a fall or a collision, sport included, until they have been checked by a medical professional. That holds even if the symptoms clear up. Someone stays with them. They don't drive, and they don't go home alone. And if their symptoms get worse, get medical help straight away.

If you play in Britain, the warning signs that mean an ambulance are on a slightly different list. Learn that one too, and act on whichever list tells you to act first. The Conditioning and Recovery episode goes through both.

Let me pull it all together.

Before you play, find out whether your game allows checking, and get it in writing. Until you know, don't hit anyone. Take the puck with your route and your stick. Pick your line early, add nothing at the end, and lift the stick low and let go.

Hold the ice you are standing on, with no shove and no lean. Arrive at every battle with your body between the other player and the puck, then get low and wide. Never hit a player who can't see you coming. If you see their numbers, pull up and play the puck.

At the wall, head up always. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the other player, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly.

In battles, use your feet on the puck, never on a player, and pin the puck, not the player. At their net, screen from outside the crease line with your hands down, and give ground to the goalie. At yours, lift their stick, don't cross-check, and never move a body across your goalmouth. Head up at the post, at both ends. When it boils over, gloves on, helmet on, skate away, and stay on the bench. And if someone may have hurt their head or neck, nobody moves them, their helmet stays on, and somebody phones.

Much of this depends on your team and your league. So here are three questions for your coach, and one for your league. Whose corner is it in our own end? Whose job is the front of our net? How much do you want us blocking shots? And for the league. Do we play checking or non-checking, and can we have that in writing?

Go and ask. Their answers tell you how your team wants you to play, and now you know why each answer makes sense.

2. **"One player" trap.** No "one player you never…". At their net: "Keep your spot against the defender, but don't push back or lean, and never your stick across anyone." Then "The goalie is different…".
3. **"The other player"** is defined once: "whether they are after the puck or just coming at you." The short form uses it every time.
4. **"Time" call.** Fourteenth idea: "Only say time when you are sure. A wrong time sends a teammate into a hit they weren't ready for."
5. **Boards short form** five times, each followed by "Keep it up even going slowly." The facing-the-wall fallback is taught in full in the Fourth idea and repeated in the Ninth.
6. **Body-moving acts.** The limits line ("no shove, no lean, no stick across anyone, never into a back, pull up on the numbers, never lift anyone off their feet, even going slowly a crash can hurt") is in the same paragraph or the next as: holding your ice (Second idea), the seal (Sixth), boxing out and sealing (Eighth), the corner's first player (Tenth), the inside lane (Eleventh), and own-net body position (Thirteenth).
7. **Stick acts.** Lift: low on the shaft, up not sideways, let go, never chop at the hands (First and Seventh ideas, and own net). Poke: "never at the hands, never a chop". Sweep: blade on the ice. Press: "Press, don't chop. Never on their hands. And let go." "With all four, never a two-handed shove with the shaft. That is a cross-check, whatever your blade is doing." Own net: "Never cross-check… Keeping your blade on the ice doesn't make it legal."
8. and 24. **Goalie contact**, in this order: "Any contact with a goalie can be a penalty, and it can get you thrown out of the game. A goalie who is down or turned can't protect themselves, so contact can hurt them. It can also cost your team the goal."
9. **Whole or not at all.** The block method keeps every limb; the two-on-one slide is left out (§4).
10. and 21. **Injury.** "Treat every knock to the head as a possible neck injury… don't help them up, and don't move them… Somebody phones for help first… their helmet stays on." "Phone for an ambulance if anything looks serious, like being knocked out, a fit, neck pain, or numbness or tingling. But don't wait for any of those…" Concussion: off the ice, not back that day, a medical professional decides; someone stays with them; they don't drive or go home alone.
11. **A limb borrowed must fit the act.** The lift limbs are used only for stick lifts on skaters. The faceoff is not taught.
12. **Charging** is never "several" or "a long run": "Building up speed into contact, even with two hard strides, can be charging" (Third and Fifteenth ideas).
13. and 17. **Pressure gate** ("your stick goes on the puck, not a hit; whether you may hit at all is your league's call; if you can see their numbers, pull up") is with: closing on a carrier (First idea), the end of the angle (Sixth), arriving second in a board battle (Ninth), the corner's first player (Tenth), and the inside lane (Eleventh, with the numbers limb in the limits line). No carve-out implying a legal hit elsewhere: "pin the puck, never the player" is unconditional, and the women's-hockey boards rule is not voiced as an exception.
14. Nothing says the ice behind the net, or any ice, is "yours".
15. **Ejections are never understated.** See §6.
16. **No conditional imperatives.** Every sentence that opens on an act verb followed by ", and" was checked. "Turn your back, and…" and "Duck, and…" were rewritten as "If you turn your back…" and "If you duck…".
17. **Clipping** is "low, on the legs", never "at or below the knees".
18. **Net-front head up** at their net ("Head up, chin off your chest, never duck. The goal post can hurt you the same way the boards can."), at your own net (in the body-position step, and again after the backing-into-your-goalie step), and in both recaps.
19. **Skate push-off.** "Never use your skate to push a player, even without a kick."
20. "The other player" is never defined with "while you have the puck".
22. **Backcheck.** The lift "works from behind and beside a player… But never hit a player who can't see you coming, and never from behind."
23. **Charging a goalie** "can get you thrown out in every league" (KT11 :2099).
25. **Rebound.** "Go round the goalie, never through. Pull up short. Head up."
- **Review M2: the chip-and-race.** The single-touch paragraph (chip past them and skate onto it) now carries the pressure gate in the same paragraph: "Race to the puck, not the player. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call, and if you can see their numbers, pull up."
- **Review M1: women's hockey.** The women's paragraph no longer permits a hit, and no longer implies women are never fully hit. See §11.

### 7. Departures from the source or the template

- **Structure.** The template goes through the rink one end at a time. This is a technique page, so after the spine the parts go by situation instead: taking the puck without a hit, the battles, the net front at both ends, giving and taking contact, then the box and injuries. Each part ends with a recap, as the template requires.
- **"The other player"** replaces the source's "checker" in the boards limbs (brief items 3 and 20). Rim and reverse still describe "a player who is sealing you".
- **Limbs generalised by the brief.** "Never lift anyone off their feet" (source :1384, net front only), "even going slowly, a crash can hurt" (source :863, boards), "pull up on the numbers" and "never your stick across anyone" are attached to every body-moving act (brief item 6). The skate push-off limb (item 19), the rebound limb (item 25), the goalie-contact order (item 24) and the two-stride charging wording (item 12) are the brief's wording.
- **Stated more strictly than the source.** "When a checking team plays a non-checking team, nobody may check" is unscoped; the source scopes it to USA Hockey 604(b). "Holding your spot against the goalie costs the goal or the box" is unscoped; the source gives it for the NHL and IIHF (69.3). "Never push into a player from behind at the boards" drops Hockey Canada's narrow pin exception. "If you hit a player from behind, plan on being thrown out" is harsher than USA Hockey's floor.
- **Softened for the owner's "aware, not freaked out".** "A tucked chin is how a vertebra fractures" (KF :33) is voiced as "a head-first crash can badly hurt your neck, even at walking speed". The spinal mechanism, the research and the "almost all" figure are not voiced.
- **"The national women's league in Britain"** names the WNIHL without saying its name. Source :148 says it is non-checking at U16 and senior; the script says "at both its levels", not the reviewer's "at every level", because those are the only two the source gives.
- **Idea headings are joined to their first paragraph** for the seam rule (§2). The spoken words are unchanged.
- **Three-source traceability.** The slot definition is from `language_and_glossary.md` :451 and the ambulance examples from `conditioning_and_recovery.md`, as the brief allows.
- **"That keeps the points covered"** (collapse) is my plain reading of :1237's "wingers stay high covering the points". It is not a separate claim in the source.
- **Questions.** Three for the coach and one for the league, as in the remake. The template's close also invites the listener to ask.

### 8. What I found unclear or contradictory in the source

- **KF :29 says "never touch the goalie", while :1339 records that the USA Hockey Casebook lets a goalie holding the puck outside the privileged area be checked in a checking game.** The Key focus is the safe instruction, and the script follows it. It is not a contradiction, but a reader who meets both may wonder.
- **The source never defines "the slot"**, though it uses it (:1220, :1509). The script borrows the glossary's definition.
- **:1264's give-ground duty is written for the NHL, IIHF and PWHL**, but KF :29, KT11 and CM :1991 voice "give ground" generally. The script voices it generally, which is the safe direction.
