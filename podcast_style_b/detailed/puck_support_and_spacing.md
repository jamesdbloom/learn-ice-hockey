===== SCRIPT START =====

This episode is about puck support and spacing. That means where you go when you don't have the puck, so the teammate who does have it has somewhere to put it. For each piece, I will tell you what to do, and also why it works. If you know why something works, you can still do the right thing when the play looks different from the picture in your head.

First, a few words you will hear all the way through, so they don't get in the way later.

The puck carrier. This is the player with the puck. I will often just say the carrier.

An option. This is a teammate the carrier could pass to right now. The straight line the pass would travel along is called the passing lane.

Strong side and weak side. The strong side is the side of the ice the puck is on. The weak side is the other side, away from the puck.

The slot. This is the ice right in front of the net, between the two faceoff circles.

The point. This is the area just inside the blue line where defencemen usually stand. It is an area, not the line itself.

The half-wall. This is the stretch of boards roughly level with the faceoff dot, between the corner and the point.

The checker. This is the opponent marking you. It also means any opponent coming at you near the boards, whether they are after the puck or just coming at you.

That is the vocabulary. Now the job.

Here is the big idea behind this whole episode. Your right spot on the ice is not a fixed place. It is a distance and an angle from two things that keep moving. One is the puck. The other is your teammates. So every time the puck moves, your right spot moves too.

I will start with four big ideas, because they matter most. If you only listen to the first part of this episode, take those four with you. After that we will add the skills that make you a good option anywhere, then go through the rink one end at a time.

First idea. Give the carrier two options at different angles. A triangle, not a line.

Start with why two.

With no option at all, they have to beat their checker one-on-one or lose the puck. Even very good players lose that duel a lot of the time.

With one option, a defender can cover that one passing lane. A good defender will.

With two options at different angles, the nearest defender can't cover both. Someone else has to come across to help. And the moment a second defender commits, a third option opens up somewhere else.

That is why average players who keep giving each other two options can beat pressure that would strip better players who give none. Over a whole game, the team that offers two options has the puck more. That is reasoning, not a measured result. But it is why spacing is a big part of keeping the puck, not a footnote to skill.

Now why different angles. Two teammates in the same lane are really one option, because one defender's stick covers both.

Picture three of you strung out in a line up the boards. One defender standing in that line is between the carrier and both of the others. One stick blade kills two options at once. A line is the cheapest shape in hockey to defend.

Now pull one of the three off the line, into the middle. You have a triangle. Three things become true.

First, no single defender can be in both lanes. Covering one means turning away from the other.

Second, each of you has two teammates at different angles. So whoever ends up with the puck already has support.

Third, and this matters most, the triangle survives the pass. When the puck moves from one corner to another, it is still a triangle, and nobody has to sprint to rebuild it. A line has to be built again after every pass.

So the triangle is not a formation you set up once. It is a shape you keep while everything moves.

Second idea. Be the angle that is missing. There are three kinds of support, behind the puck, level with it, and ahead of it. They do different jobs.

Behind the puck is the outlet. You are between the puck and your own net, or at least further back than the carrier. A pass back to you goes into space the other team has already left. So it is the least contested pass on the ice.

Here is why the outlet matters so much. It is the option that is still there when the other two are covered. Without it, the only answer to good pressure is dumping the puck away, and that is a turnover you chose. Whoever is lowest gives the outlet. In your own end, that is usually a defenceman or the centre. The usual call for it is back.

Level with the puck is the side switch. You are about the same distance from your own net as the carrier, but across the ice from them. Most teams defend by packing onto the strong side. So a pass across to you moves the puck to where they are thinnest, and every defender has to reset.

Ahead of the puck is the attacking option. You are further up the ice than the carrier. It is the only one of the three that gains ground. It is also the easiest pass to intercept, and if it fails, you are behind the play.

You need all three at once. That is what good spacing actually means. All behind, and you are safe but going nowhere. All ahead, and there is no outlet, so pressure takes the puck off you. All level, and you go side to side and never up the ice.

So when you skate to support, don't ask whether you are open. Ask which of the three is missing. Look at where your teammates are, and go and be that one.

There is one place the side switch changes. In your own end, never pass across the middle. That pass goes through the slot. An interception there is not a turnover you can recover from. It is a shot from point-blank range, by a player already facing your net. So in your own end, the side switch goes behind your own net, never through the slot. Or go up the wall.

Third idea. Skate to the space, not to the puck.

Coaches name this as the habit that most often separates beginners from better players. That comes from coaching material, not a measured ranking.

On a young or new team, the puck seems to pull everyone in. Ten players end up in a small circle. The puck squirts out to whoever has a teammate outside the pile. Then it all happens again twenty metres away.

Why do players do it? Your eyes follow the puck, and your legs follow your eyes. Skating hard at the puck feels like effort, and skating away from it feels like slacking. And nobody wants to be the player who wasn't there.

But most of the time it is wrong, and there are four reasons.

First, you don't arrive alone. Your checker comes with you, so the space gets more crowded, not less.

Second, you remove an option. A teammate five metres away is an option. One a metre away, inside the same checker's reach, is not.

Third, pucks come out of piles into empty ice. If everyone is in the pile, that empty ice belongs to the other team.

Fourth, nobody is left back. Then a normal turnover becomes an odd-man rush the other way, with more of them attacking than you have defending.

So every time the puck moves, ask yourself one question. Who is closest? If it isn't you, and your system hasn't sent you in, your job is the space, not the puck. If it is you, go for the puck, not the player. If you can see their numbers, pull up.

Skate to where the puck is going to come out, not to where it is. From a corner battle, it comes out either up the wall or behind the net. Go and stand in one of those two places, with your stick on the ice.

How many of you go to the puck is decided by your team's system, not made up on the spot. The habit to break is everybody going. So learn your role before you move.

Fourth idea. Before you go below the puck, know who is above it.

Two new words first. Above the puck means between the puck and your own net. Below the puck means the puck is between you and your own net.

This has nothing to do with which end of the ice you are in. A forward deep in the other team's corner is below the puck if the puck is up at their point. And it flips the moment the puck changes hands. Players who were usefully below the puck while attacking are suddenly stuck below it, defending.

So somebody stays above the puck, in every zone. That player is not part of the attack. They are the insurance in case the attack fails. If the puck turns over and nobody is above it, the other team is off on a rush with nobody in the way.

Who it is depends on your system, so find out. Know who, not which position, because under some systems the player up there is a winger. And if nobody is above the puck, it's you.

Let me pull those four ideas together, because they are the heart of the job.

One. Give the carrier two options at different angles. A triangle, not a line, because a triangle survives the pass.

Two. Be the angle that is missing, behind, level or ahead. And in your own end, the side switch goes behind your net, never across the slot.

Three. Skate to the space, not to the puck. If you are not closest and your system hasn't sent you in, your job is the space.

Four. Before you go below the puck, know who is above it.

Everything else adds detail to those four. Next, the skills that make you a good option anywhere.

Fifth idea. Support from a distance the pass can beat.

The test is time, not a tape measure. Ask yourself this. If my teammate let the pass go right now, would it reach me before that defender does? If yes, you are in range. If no, you are not really an option.

That is why the same distance can be right in one place and wrong in another. Twelve feet is safe in open ice. Under a forecheck, it is useless, because the defender gets there before the pass does.

Too close is a problem, for three reasons. If you are within a stick's reach of your teammate's checker, that one checker is guarding two of you, and somebody else is free. You also stand in the space your teammate needed to turn into. And if they pass to you from two metres away, their checker is on you before the puck settles. The pass hasn't helped. So back off until one defender can't reach both of you.

Too far is a problem too. A long pass spends longer on the ice, so every defender has time to read it and get a stick in the lane. It arrives faster, often bouncing, with a defender already closing on you. And after the pass you are too far away to help the teammate who passed it. So close in until a pass let go now would reach you before the defender does.

As a starting point, support from about two to three stick lengths. That is about ten to fifteen feet, or three to five metres. Why there? It is close enough for a hard, flat pass that takes about a second, and far enough that one defender can't mark both of you.

Then adjust. When the carrier is under heavy pressure on the boards, tighten to eight to ten feet. The pass has to be immediate, and short and safe beats good and late. Go shorter in your own end too, where a turnover is a chance against. Go shorter below their goal line, behind their net and in the low corners, because it is tight and fast down there.

Go longer in open ice, where you can afford the extra half-second, and it stretches the defence. Go longer above the faceoff dots in their zone. Players at the point are far from the puck on purpose, and that is why they are open.

Those numbers are a coaching rule of thumb, not a rule of hockey, and coaches differ. One coach describes possession play as short passes of eight to ten feet or less, which is the tight end of the same range. So let your coach adjust it.

The size of the rink matters a little. On a bigger rink the distances stretch, but the angles stay the same. Only the very widest rinks call for slightly longer support. In Britain the rules allow rinks smaller than the international size. So there your distances get shorter if anything, not longer. Measure your rink rather than guessing.

If you play defence, don't mix this up with your gap. Support distance is teammate to teammate. A gap is how much room a defender leaves the attacker they are defending. Both get measured in stick lengths, but between different people. Never carry a number from one to the other.

Sixth idea. Make yourself available, not just unmarked.

Players who complain that nobody passes to them are usually mixing these up. Unmarked means no defender is standing next to you. Available means the pass can actually be completed. That needs three things at once.

First, a clear lane. No stick, skate or body between the puck and you. A defender ten metres away who happens to be standing on that line takes you out of the play just as much as one marking you.

Second, a target. Stick on the ice, blade flat, angled so the puck can be taken. A raised stick is an invisible one. The passer has nothing to aim at.

Third, a carrier who knows you are there. Their head is often down. So use your voice. Here. Middle. Back.

Making yourself available is your job as the receiver, not the passer's job. Most players wait to be found. Good support players go and make themselves easy to find. Here is how.

If the lane is blocked, move, even a metre. One stride sideways can put you on the other side of the stick in the way. You don't need more space around you. You need a clear line from the puck to your stick.

Move to the passing angle, not to the biggest patch of empty ice. The biggest patch is often one nobody can get a pass into. A smaller gap with a clean line to the puck is worth more.

When an opponent pins the carrier against the boards, the lane up the wall is gone. Get below them or behind them, where that seal doesn't reach.

And keep adjusting every time the puck moves. Your lane was clean two seconds ago. Most failed support is a player who found a good spot once, and then stopped.

Seventh idea. Pass and move.

The moment the puck leaves your stick, your job is not finished. The teammate you gave it to now needs two options, and you are the nearest one. So go again straight away.

Stop after your pass, and they have one option fewer. That is why pass and move is how the triangle survives the pass. The pass moves one corner of the triangle. Your next stride rebuilds it.

Let me pull those skills together. Judge your distance by time. Would the pass beat the defender to you? Start at two to three stick lengths, tighter under pressure. Make yourself available, stick down, voice on, and move to the angle. Keep adjusting as the puck moves. And after every pass, go again.

Now the rink, one end at a time, starting in your own end.

Eighth idea. Support in your own end. The breakout triangle.

A breakout is your team getting the puck out of its own end. Say your defenceman picks up the puck behind your net. Before they turn, they need three options.

The strong-side winger stands on the wall, roughly level with the faceoff dot. That is the short option up the boards.

The centre swings low through the middle and curls toward the same side. That is the option that turns a breakout into an attack.

The defence partner is on the far side, reached behind the net. Never through the slot.

The weak-side winger sits higher and wider than everyone else. Why? That width stops the other team loading up on one side.

In your own end, support short and keep your angles safe. Everywhere else on the ice, the attacking option comes first. Here, the safe outlet does. Why the switch? Because a turnover here is a chance against. Getting the puck out tidily is a win. Something clever that fails is a goal against.

Further up the ice, arriving a little late with speed can be a good habit. In your own end, never delay. The carrier needs the outlet now. A late arrival here is an absent one.

Give the carrier a short option, because otherwise their only play is to fire it the length of the ice. That is icing, sending the puck from your own half across their goal line untouched. Play stops, and the faceoff comes back to your own end. Under the pro and international rules, and some others, your tired line also has to stay on for that faceoff, with no change. A hurt player can still come off. A tired one can't. That is the price of a breakout with no support.

Ninth idea. Your goalie is part of your support.

Your goalie has the best view on the ice, and no puck to watch. So their calls tell the carrier which option is really there. Man on means an opponent is closing on you. Time means nobody is near, so lift your head and make the good play. Reverse means send the puck back the way it came.

The same goes for you, if you make those calls. Only say time when you are sure. A wrong time sends a teammate into a hit they weren't ready for.

Whether your goalie can also come out and play the puck behind the net depends on your league. In some leagues there is a trapezoid, a shape painted behind the net. The goalie may only play the puck back there inside it, and playing it anywhere else behind the net is a two-minute penalty. Other leagues have no trapezoid, and there your goalie can be a real fourth option behind the net.

Even then, a goalie who has left the crease, the blue ice in front of the net, can't freeze the puck back there. Freezing means holding it still for a whistle, and that is a penalty. So expect the puck back moving, not left sitting for you.

Ask your league which applies. If you aren't sure, or you play in Britain, keep your goalie inside the trapezoid. It costs nothing, and the alternative is two minutes.

Let me pull your own end together. Three short options for the player behind the net. The wall, the centre swinging low, and the partner behind the net, never through the slot. The weak-side winger high and wide. Support short and safe, and never late. And use your goalie's eyes.

Now the middle of the ice.

Tenth idea. The neutral zone, and its three lanes.

The neutral zone is the middle of the rink, between the two blue lines. Think of it as three lanes. The boards on the strong side, the middle, and the boards on the weak side.

The usual default is one player in each lane. Why? Two players in the same lane can be covered by one defender. Coaches put it as same lane, same checker.

That default is a coaching choice, not a law. The realistic alternative is a one-three-one regroup. A regroup is turning the puck back to reset and come again. The one-three-one puts players in the middle lane on purpose. Why would a team do that? To overload the gap between their defenders, and pull the other team's defence inward.

So find out whether your team spreads out or stacks the middle. The two look the same for the first stride. Then they need opposite decisions.

Either way, two habits help. Use different lanes at different speeds, so the defence can't set one gap that handles everyone. And skate curved routes into open ice, not straight lines. A curve lets you take the pass already facing forward.

Eleventh idea. Stay wide, even when it feels useless.

A winger hugging the boards in the neutral zone often feels useless. They are far from the puck and they rarely get it. That feeling is the point.

Here is why. Two defenders can't cover both the middle and both sets of boards. A winger standing wide keeps a defender out there with them. That defender is now not in the middle. The middle is the most valuable ice there is, and it is open only because someone is standing out wide.

The moment that winger drifts inside to get closer to the play, their checker drifts in too, and the middle closes. The winger hasn't gained an option. They have taken one away from a teammate, and they are now in their centre's lane.

So stay wide, unless your team's rotation sends you inside, or you are driving the net on a shot. If you are driving the net, arrive under control, and pull up short of the goalie. Head up, chin off your chest. The goal post can hurt you the same way the boards can. Go round the goalie, never into them. Any contact with a goalie can be a penalty and can get you thrown out of the game. Contact can hurt a goalie, and one who is down or turned can't protect themselves.

Twelfth idea. Arrive late with speed, not early and still.

Arriving at the right place at the right moment is worth far more than standing in it.

Get to the good spot early and stop, and the defence has as long as it likes to come over and stand next to you. By being early, you turned yourself from an open teammate into a marked one.

A moving player also takes the pass better. You already have speed, so you can attack straight away. A player standing still in traffic is a turnover waiting to happen.

The cue in the neutral zone is puck first, feet second. Let the carrier draw a defender before you go. A half-second wait before you speed up does three things. It keeps you onside at the blue line. It means the defender's head has already turned toward the puck. And it means you cross the space at full speed while they are standing still.

That is what people mean when they say a player arrives late. It is praise, not criticism. Arriving late with speed beats a defender who has set a gap. By the time they react, you are already past the point where they could have set one.

The trailer on a rush works this way. The trailer is a teammate arriving late behind the play. So does the defenceman jumping into the slot, and the weak-side winger cutting to the far post. If that is you at the far post, head up there too, chin off your chest, and go round the goalie, never into them.

At the blue line, be exact about offside. You are offside if both your skates are completely over their blue line before the puck has completely crossed it. So time your route so the puck crosses first, or at the same moment as your second skate. Keep one skate on the line or behind it until then.

And keep that back blade on the ice. In some leagues a skate held up in the air over the line still counts as onside. In others, only a skate touching the ice counts. Keep the blade on the ice, and you are onside wherever you play.

Let me pull the middle of the ice together. One player per lane is the usual default, so ask whether your team spreads or stacks. Different lanes, different speeds, curved routes. Wingers stay wide, because the middle only exists while someone holds the outside. Arrive late with speed, puck first, feet second. And keep your back blade on the ice at the line.

Now their end.

Thirteenth idea. Spread the zone deep as well as high.

Three is the minimum for a triangle, not the maximum. With five skaters, three of you make the triangle around the puck. A fourth gives width on the far side. A fifth sits back above the play, as the safety.

Depth is spread up and down the ice. Width is spread across it. Depth mostly happens by accident, as players string out along the play. Width has to be deliberate. In their zone, depth has to be deliberate too.

So in their zone, have one player below their goal line and one at their blue line. Why? That stretch forces the defence to choose which end of the zone to defend. If all five of you are between the faceoff dots and the goal line, it isn't an attack. It is a crowd in front of their net.

Fourteenth idea. In their zone, support is rotation.

In their zone, the puck moves round the outside faster than anyone can skate. So no single spot stays right for long. What has to stay right is the layers.

At all times, have someone below the puck, someone level with it, and someone above it. Those are the same three angles from the second idea, in their zone. Below is behind or beside the net, the reset when the play on the wall dies. Level is the half-wall or the slot. Above is the point, often with a third forward high, as the safety and the shot.

When you leave one of those layers, someone fills it. That is the rotation, and it is the part newer teams skip. The layer has to stay filled, not your spot.

Who stays above the puck in their zone depends on your system. Here are the versions you are likely to meet.

In a common setup, both defencemen hold the points unless one of them deliberately joins the attack, and the third forward supports high.

A forecheck is your team trying to win the puck back in the other team's end. Under a one-two-two forecheck, the deepest forward is alone below the puck, and two forwards sit above as a layered wall.

In a single-high setup, one defenceman may walk the line, and the weak-side winger becomes the safety. Other teams insist both defencemen hold.

So find out which your team plays.

A bench will give this up on purpose in three situations. A called full-ice press, where your team pressures all over the ice. A delayed penalty, when a penalty against the other team is waiting to be called. Or six skaters against five, with your own goalie pulled. Why give it up then? Because in each of those, a counter-attack has stopped costing you anything. Unless one of them has been called, assume somebody stays above the puck.

How your team rotates is a coaching choice, and it changes who is allowed to go below the puck. Guess wrong, and you end up as the extra player deep, with nobody home. So ask your coach who may go below the puck, and when.

Let me pull their end together. One player below the goal line and one at the blue line. Someone below the puck, someone level and someone above it, all the time. When you leave a layer, someone fills it. Know who stays above the puck in your system, and ask who may go below it.

Now the moments that decide most of it. Battles on the boards, the second after the puck changes hands, and defending.

Fifteenth idea. To help a teammate in a battle on the boards, usually move away from it.

Your teammate is pinned on the boards, fighting for the puck. Every instinct says go and help. Usually, don't. At least, not into the battle.

Here is why. Your teammate needs to get the puck out of the pile. Skate in, and you add a body where there is no room. You bring your checker too, so the way out gets more crowded.

Instead, move to open ice about five metres away, with your stick on the ice. Now the puck has somewhere to go. And when it squirts free, you have it with time.

There is a real exception. Go in to win the puck when it is a genuine fifty-fifty loose puck that nobody has. And many teams name a support player whose job is to arrive at the wall. Find out if that is your job. Stay out and give an exit when your teammate already has the puck and is trying to hang on.

When you do go in, how you arrive matters more than winning the puck. Loose pucks at the wall are where hits from behind and boarding injuries happen.

So come to the wall on an angle, with your feet apart and your knees bent. Get your skates parallel to the boards before the contact. Forearm and hip to the boards. Never the point of your shoulder, and not the whole shoulder if you can help it. Head up, chin off your chest. Never turn your back to the checker. And never duck.

Those last two protect your head and neck. If you turn your back, you can't see the hit coming or brace for it. If you duck, your head meets the boards first. Neither is only for fast arrivals. Keep it up even going slowly.

If you really can't turn in time, and you are already facing the wall square on, that is the fallback, not the target. Get your stick and gloves up and spread wide on the glass, and keep your head off it. That is only for when things have already gone wrong.

From here on I will use one short version of that position, the same words every time, so you can learn it like a line. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the checker, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly.

And when you get there, it is stick on the puck, not a hit. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call. If you can see a player's numbers, pull up and play the puck.

Never push or hit the opponent on the wall from behind. If their back is to you, go for the puck at their feet, with your stick on the puck, not on them. A push from behind into the boards is checking from behind, even where body checking isn't allowed. Even a light push from behind can cause a serious head or neck injury. In most leagues, plan on being thrown out of the game for it. If it sends them into the boards unable to protect themselves, plan on a match penalty, which goes past being thrown out.

Sixteenth idea. The second or two after the puck changes hands.

This is when support is hardest. All five of you are still standing where the old possession wanted you.

When your team wins the puck, the nearest player away from the pressure becomes the outlet straight away. Behind or level with the winner, blade on the ice, calling for it. Everyone else gets wide and into their lanes, rather than turning to watch the puck. Why the hurry? A player who wins a battle with nowhere to put the puck often loses it again a second later.

When your team loses the puck, the nearest player pressures the new carrier at once, before they can look up and find a pass. The next player layers behind, rather than joining in. Everybody else gets back above the puck.

In their end, that first pressure is the forecheck. How many of you go is set by your system. In a one-two-two forecheck, one forward pressures and the others hold their places. In a two-one-two, two forwards go deep on purpose, and the third reads the play behind them.

That pressure is stick on the puck, not a hit. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call. If you can see their numbers, pull up and play the puck. Never hit a player who can't see you coming, and never from behind. Getting back above the puck is a race for the ice, not for a player.

Sending that nearest player straight in is the common version. Some teams want everyone back above the puck first, and only pressure once they have numbers back. That is a coaching choice, so ask your bench which yours plays.

Seventeenth idea. Support when you are defending. Layer, don't converge.

Attacking, the second player's job is to give the carrier an option. Defending, the second player's job is to back up the first defender, not to copy them. Most defensive breakdowns come from getting this wrong.

Say your teammate is pressuring the puck carrier, and you are the second defender. Their pressure is stick on the puck, not a hit. You have two choices.

You can converge, which means skating at the same carrier. Now two of you are on one puck, and one pass beats you both.

Or you can layer. That means sitting behind and to the side of your teammate, covering the space and the passing lane they can't. You are the answer if they get beaten. And you are the reason the carrier can't simply pass out of the pressure.

Converging is not a small mistake. With two of you on one carrier, the other three of you are covering four of them, and it is the free player who scores.

Three things make the layer a real position.

First, read which way your teammate is steering the carrier, and stand where the carrier goes if your teammate is beaten.

Second, get the distance right. Too close, and one move beats you both. Too far, and the carrier skates through the gap between you. Aim to be able to get there in about a second. That is about the same two to three stick lengths, offset diagonally, not straight behind.

Third, talk. I've got your back. Man on. You have time. The layer only works if your teammate knows it is there. And only say time when you are sure. A wrong time sends a teammate into a hit they weren't ready for.

Layering is almost always right. It sits inside every named defensive system.

Whether you may hold the layer at all depends on how your team covers in its own end. Under man-on-man coverage, where each player follows one opponent, your job can take you away from the layer. So ask how your coverage changes it.

The exception is a deliberate double-team, two players sent at one carrier. That is a coaching decision, not a default. Many teams double a puck on the boards below their own goal line. Why there? Because a turnover there is contained by the net and the boards. Ask your coach where and when you may double. Doubling on your own is how the other team scores at the back door, the far post.

And going in for a double-team doesn't change how you arrive. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the checker, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly. Stick on the puck, not a hit. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call, and if you can see their numbers, pull up. Never push or hit the opponent from behind. Even a light push from behind can cause a serious head or neck injury. Go for the puck at their feet.

Let me pull that part together. To help a battle, usually move away and give an exit, with your stick down. If you do go in, arrive safe before you arrive first. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the checker, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly. Stick on the puck, not a hit, and never push or hit anyone from behind. When the puck changes hands, the nearest player becomes the outlet if you won it, and the pressure if you lost it, stick on the puck. And defending, layer behind your teammate instead of joining them on the same carrier.

Eighteenth idea, and the last. Learning to see it.

Spacing is hard to learn because it is invisible. Everyone sees the great pass. Nobody claps the teammate whose positioning made it possible three seconds earlier. So most players never get told about their spacing, and they never fix it. You have to give yourself that feedback.

When you watch a game, stop watching the puck. For one full shift, watch one player who doesn't have it. It feels odd, and it is the fastest way to learn the game.

Next, count the options. Every time a player gets the puck, count the teammates who could take a pass right now. Good teams average two or more. Ask whether you could draw a triangle around the puck.

Watch the far side. The weak-side winger is the best single sign of a team's spacing. Are they wide and high, or have they drifted in to watch?

When the puck changes hands, count how many are below it. That tells you whether the next half-minute is a rush against. And watch the trailer on a rush, not the two who carried it in. The dangerous chance often comes from that third player arriving late.

After a shift of your own, ask where you were each time the puck moved, and whether the carrier could have used you. If you can, ask to be filmed, because you can't see your own spacing from inside the play. And small games in practice, across half the ice or less, punish crowding straight away. So the habit breaks itself.

On the ice, ask yourself one question, over and over. Where are my two options? When you have the puck, ideally before it arrives, name the two. If you can only name one, buy time for a second to appear, or move the puck to someone who has two. When you don't have the puck, ask whether you are one of somebody's two options right now. If not, you are watching, not supporting. Move.

Let me pull it all together.

Give the carrier two options at different angles, a triangle, not a line, because a triangle survives the pass. Be the angle that is missing, behind, level or ahead. In your own end, the side switch goes behind your net, never across the slot.

Skate to the space, not to the puck. And before you go below the puck, know who is above it.

Judge your distance by time. Make yourself available, stick down, voice on. Pass and move.

In your own end, support short and safe, and never late. Through the middle, stay wide, and arrive late with speed, back blade on the ice at the line. In their end, keep the layers filled.

To help a battle, usually move away. If you do go in, arrive safe. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the checker, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly. Stick on the puck, not a hit, and never push or hit anyone from behind. And defending, layer, don't converge.

Much of this depends on what your team plays. So here are three questions to ask your coach. Who is our safety above the puck, and who may go below it? In the neutral zone, do we spread out or stack the middle? And when do we double-team?

Go and ask. Their answers tell you where your team wants you when you don't have the puck, and now you know why each answer makes sense.

### 8. Where I departed from the source or the template
