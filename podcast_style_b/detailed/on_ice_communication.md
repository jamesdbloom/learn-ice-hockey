===== SCRIPT START =====

This episode is about talking on the ice. What to shout, who shouts it, and what you do when you hear it. For each piece, I will tell you what to do and also why it works. If you know why a call works, you can still use it when the play looks a little different from the picture in your head.

Before any instructions, a few words you will hear all the way through. I will explain them now, so they don't get in the way later.

A call. This is a short word or two you shout to a teammate during play, to tell them something they can't see.

The puck carrier. This is the player who has the puck.

The boards and the glass. The boards are the wall around the rink. The glass is the clear panel on top of them.

Your own end and their end. Your own end is the part of the rink inside your blue line, where your goalie is. Their end is the same at the other end. Between the blue lines is the middle of the ice.

The slot. This is the ice right in front of the net, between the faceoff circles. The crease is the painted area right in front of the goal, the goalie's space.

Strong side and weak side. The strong side is the side of the ice the puck is on. The weak side is the other side.

Those are the words. Now the job.

I will start with four big ideas, because they matter most. If you only listen to the first part of this episode, take those four with you. After that we will go back through the rink, one end at a time, and add the calls themselves.

First idea. Whoever can see it makes the call.

Start with one plain fact. The puck carrier knows less than anyone else on the ice. Their eyes are down on the puck, or looking ahead. And an opponent closing from behind is in the one place they can't look. Everybody else can see it.

So the information that would help them is already sitting in someone else's eyes. It costs nothing to pass on. And until somebody says it out loud, it is no use to anyone.

Here is a picture. A defenceman goes back into their own corner for a puck. They have three plays to choose from, and must choose before they get there. Without information, they guess. A guess in your own end is how goals get scored. One word from the goalie, and they aren't guessing any more.

Notice who could see it. The goalie faces the whole play. The defence partner across the net can see the side the defenceman going back can't. Neither of them has to decide what to do with the puck. A voice joins the player who sees to the player who decides.

So the duty to speak belongs to whoever can see. Not the captain, not the oldest player, not the loudest. A rookie shouting at a veteran is doing exactly the right thing. A team where that feels wrong will give up goals for it.

Here is why this matters so much. A team that talks makes fewer blind turnovers. Its players arrive at loose pucks with a decision already made. And it covers its own end without anyone stopping to work out whose job it is. From the stands, that looks like skill. Most of it is information. And it is the cheapest improvement a team can make. It needs no fitness, no skill work and no extra ice time.

Second idea. The two most useful calls in hockey are man on and time. And both usually go unsaid.

Man on means an opponent is closing on you, and you can't see them. When you hear it, stop trying to make a play. Protect the puck with your body, so you are between the pressure and the puck. Then move it. Rim it hard around the boards, chip it off the glass, or make a short pass to the nearest teammate. And don't put your head down.

Man on also carries the most important safety point in this episode. It is usually shouted at a player near the boards who can't see the hit coming. So the right answer is a body position, and it holds whether anyone calls it or not.

First, one word. The checker is any opponent coming at you near the boards, whether they are after the puck or just coming at you.

Never turn your back to the checker. And never duck.

Both protect your head and neck. If you turn your back, you can't see the hit coming or brace for it. If you duck, your head meets the boards first. That is true even at slow speed, so a slow checker doesn't make it safe.

So here is the position you aim for. Skates parallel to the boards, feet apart, knees bent. Forearm and hip to the boards. Never the point of your shoulder, and not the whole shoulder if you can help it. Head up, chin off your chest.

If you are already facing the wall and can't turn in time, that is the last resort, not a choice. Get your stick and gloves up and spread wide on the glass, with your head off it. That is only for when things have already gone wrong.

From here on I will use one short version of that position, the same words every time. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the checker, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly.

Now the other word. Time means nobody is near you. When you hear it, slow down. Lift your head, take an extra stride, and make the good play instead of the safe one.

Here is why time is worth just as much as man on. A player who fears pressure that isn't there throws the puck away just as surely as one who never saw the pressure coming. Time takes that fear away.

But only say time when you are sure. A wrong time sends a teammate into a hit they weren't ready for. After one of those, they will stop trusting your calls. So if you are not sure, say nothing.

Man on is the other way round. Shout it early, even if you are not certain.

Third idea. How to make a call that actually works. Early, short and loud, with a name on it. Answer every call. And agree your words as a team.

Early beats accurate. A slightly wrong call before the puck arrives is more use than a perfect one as it lands. Here is why. The receiver's decision is already forming while the puck travels. You are trying to inform that decision, not correct it afterwards. Once the puck is on their stick, they are already committed. Man on shouted as the hit lands isn't a warning. It is commentary.

There is one exception. Time. Only say time when you are sure. A wrong time sends a teammate into a hit they weren't ready for.

Short beats specific. One or two syllables. Man on gets through a noisy rink. There's a player coming from behind your left shoulder does not. If your call needs a pause in the middle, it is too long.

Loud beats clever. Rinks are loud, helmets and cages muffle everything, and you are both moving. A perfect call at normal talking volume did not happen. Coaches say that is the most common reason a call fails.

Use names. Sam, man on gets acted on. Just man on gets four players looking round. For opponents, use jersey numbers. I've got seventeen tells everyone exactly who you mean.

Answer every call. Yep, got it, a raised glove, anything. Here is why. A call that gets no answer gets made more quietly next time, and then not at all.

And agree your words as a team. There is no official hockey vocabulary. Calls change from team to team, region to region, level to level, and coach to coach. That matters, because a call only works if the receiver understands it in under half a second. A call they have to translate is worse than silence. Silence at least doesn't cost them a beat.

So agree your list at the start of the season, and drill it in practice. Which words you pick matters far less than everybody understanding them instantly. And if you move teams, or move countries, don't assume your calls come with you. Ask.

Fourth idea. In your own end, call the coverage and answer it, and never call the puck across the front of your own net.

Your own end is where talking matters most. Here is why. A lost puck or a lost player there turns straight into a goal.

Start with back door. The back door is the far post, the side of the net away from the puck. Back door means an opponent is alone there. When you hear it, the nearest player covers that opponent, straight away. Cover them by getting between them and the net. That means position, not a hit.

Here is why the call exists. The player who can see the back door is almost never the player who can cover it.

You will know the goal it stops. Both defenders get drawn to the puck. Nobody is in front of the net. Their player taps it in. Both defenders were working hard, at the same job. So whoever sees the far post shouts back door, and the nearer defender leaves the puck and covers that player.

Now switch. Switch is for when two attackers cross in front of two defenders. The player who wants to trade says switch, out loud, and the other one answers. Then each of you covers the nearer opponent.

Whether you switch at all is a team choice, and there are two common ways. In man-on-man, each of you follows one opponent. Many teams playing it trade only on a clean switch, called and answered. Otherwise you follow your player through the crossing. In a zone defence, each of you covers an area. There, the crossing trades your players for you, with no call. Never do half of each. Ask which your team plays.

Whichever it is, a switch nobody answers is worse than no switch at all. Two defenders end up on one attacker, and the other one walks in alone.

Now the puck. Over means move the puck to the other side. D-to-D means a pass across to your defence partner.

Never send either one across the front of your own net. Here is why. An interception in the slot is a shot from right in front of your goalie.

That part is fixed. The route is a team choice. Most teams send it behind the net, below the goal line. Some pass it high instead, above the tops of the faceoff circles. Ask which yours uses. But the front of your own net is never the route.

One thing before the calls themselves. Many of them are in common use, but no coaching source writes them down as words you shout. So treat every call from here on as an example, and check it against your own team's words.

Fifth idea. The breakout, which means getting the puck out of your own end, and who calls it.

Picture that defenceman going back for a dumped puck again. They face their own end boards. The forecheck is coming from behind them. That is the other team chasing the puck to win it back, and each player doing it is a forechecker. So the player going back can't see the very thing they most need to know.

That is why the defencemen call the breakout, along with the goalie. The breakout calls, up, over, reverse and wheel, are almost always called to the puck carrier by someone else. The carrier doesn't choose them. A common drill makes this the weak-side defenceman's job. They read where the second forechecker is, and make the call for the teammate under pressure.

The loudest voice in your end should be your goalie. Here is why. The goalie faces the whole play, all the time. They are more or less standing still. And nothing in the zone is behind them. Every other player has a blind side. That is exactly the view the defenceman going back lacks. Teams that ignore their goalie's voice give up more goals, for that simple reason.

So when you go back for a dumped puck, listen for your goalie before you touch it. Shoulder check as you go as well. And goalies, call it every time you can see it.

And where two of you can see it, both of you call it. Saying it twice costs nothing. What you are guarding against is silence, not saying it twice.

Going back for that puck is a chase, and a forechecker is often arriving as you get there. You are going for the puck, not the player. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call. If you can see their numbers, pull up. And set your body for the boards. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the checker, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly.

Here are the calls you will hear first. Time, or man on, usually from the goalie. Only say time when you are sure. A wrong time sends a teammate into a hit they weren't ready for.

Some goalies add a count. One on, or two on. The count changes your play. You can beat one forechecker with a reverse or a quick turn. Two means you are being sealed in, and the puck has to leave. Rim it, or glass it out.

Then the direction. Up means the puck goes up the same side, usually to the winger on the boards. Often it is really the defenceman telling the goalie where they want it. Over means it switches sides behind the net. It is the call that moves the play away from the forecheck's pressure, and it usually comes from the weak-side defenceman.

Reverse means send it back the way it came. Rim means fire it on around the boards, with the flow, to a teammate further round. They are opposite plays, and you choose between them off the same look. A rim beats a forechecker who is sealing you in, because it goes past them. A reverse beats a forechecker who has come in too hard and skated past you, because it goes into the space they just left.

Keep a rim hard and high on the glass, so it doesn't die in the corner. A soft rim is a turnover with extra steps.

Wheel means skate it yourself, usually around behind your own net and up the far side. When you hear it, take off. The player who called it can see the route is clear, so commit rather than hesitate. Turn to the open ice, not into the boards. Some teams say turn for the same thing. Wheel is the clearer word, so keep turn only if your whole team already agrees what it means.

Chip, from a forward, means put a short pass past the defender into space for them to skate onto. Don't try to hit their stick. Put it where they are going.

Leave it means the goalie stops the puck behind the net and leaves it there for a teammate, usually a centre swinging through. Some teams say set instead. Leave it is easier to tell apart from up and over, at speed, in a noisy rink. And the centre says it early, while still in the middle of the ice, not while arriving behind the net.

I've got it means the goalie is playing the puck. Stay out of their way, and get to your breakout spot instead of going to the puck.

Sixth idea. Your goalie handling the puck, and the calls that can get them, or you, in trouble.

Where your goalie can play the puck behind the net depends on your league. Some leagues paint a trapezoid behind the net, narrow at the goal line and wider at the end boards. There, a goalie who plays the puck behind the goal line outside it gets a penalty. So leave it and I've got it only work where the puck actually is, and a puck in the corner outside the trapezoid is a defenceman's. Other leagues have no trapezoid, and your goalie can be a real third puck-handler behind the net. Find out which yours is.

In Britain, play as though the trapezoid binds your goalie. But when you dump the puck in, expect their goalie to come out for it anyway, because it is not clearly enforced in every competition.

Never call your goalie up past the centre red line for a loose puck. In every league, a goalie who deliberately joins the play past that line gets a penalty. In some, it counts as soon as their skates cross, even with the puck still on your side.

I've got it doesn't mean I can sit on it, either. In some leagues, a goalie who had time to play the puck and froze it anyway gets a penalty. A true, early time call helps them play it instead. Only say time when you are sure. A wrong time sends a teammate into a hit they weren't ready for.

But never shout move it at a goalie who has just covered the puck after a save. Leave that one to them.

And if you talk your goalie into a risky play, remember who pays for it. A goalie doesn't serve their own minor penalty. The coach names a skater who was on the ice to serve it. If you made the call, you were on the ice. So it can be you.

Seventh idea. When there is nowhere to put the puck. Off the glass, or just glass, means clear it high off the boards and out of your end. Aim high and wide, and keep it inside the rink. Here is why. A glass-out rarely gets called for icing, because it usually dies in the middle of the ice. What it can cost you is two minutes. In some leagues, a puck that goes straight over the glass from your own end is a penalty, even by accident. On purpose, it is a penalty wherever you play.

Never aim for the players' bench. It looks like a safe target, and it isn't. In some leagues, a puck that clears the glass and drops into the bench is still two minutes.

Eat it means there is no good option. So protect the puck on the boards and take the contact, rather than force a pass that gets picked off. Your body between the checker and the puck, and the puck on your stick until help arrives. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the checker, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly.

Never pin the puck against the boards to get a whistle. That can cost you two minutes wherever you play, even while you are being checked.

Be careful with eat it. In some dressing rooms it means something else, that only a few seconds are left, so keep the puck where it is. Same two words, two different instructions. Agree which your bench means. Or say eat the puck for the boards, and pick another word for the clock.

Ice it means get it all the way down the ice, even if that means a whistle. Know what you are buying. At even strength it is expensive. Play stops, and the faceoff comes back to your end. In some leagues your tired players can't change after your own icing, though a hurt player can still be replaced. That is exactly when your bench most needs to talk, because a tired line is about to take a faceoff in front of its own net.

On the penalty kill, ice it is a normal call. A team that is a player short can usually ice the puck with no whistle. But not at every level. Under USA Hockey the younger age groups don't get that, so there the shout buys a faceoff in your own end, not a rest. And it only lasts while you have fewer players on the ice than they do. So check your league before ice it becomes a habit.

Eighth idea. The front of your own net. Screen, net front, clear and sag.

A screen is an attacker standing in your goalie's line of sight. Screen is almost always called by the goalie, because only the goalie knows they can't see. But if you can see your goalie has no view, say so too. Here is why it is the goalie's most important call. Nobody else on the ice can know.

Net front means something close to it. Nobody is covering the player standing in front of your net. Under the usual zone coverage, that player belongs to the weak-side defenceman. The call exists for when that hasn't happened.

An attacker standing outside the crease, in your goalie's line of sight, without touching anyone, is allowed to be there. No whistle is coming, and nobody is coming to help. So deal with it fast. On a screen, you have two choices. Move yourself out of your goalie's sightline. Or take position beside the player and hold it. On net front, you take position and hold it.

Here is the whole of holding it, and it goes together. Stand your ground beside them. No lean, no push, never your stick across them, and never into their back. If you can see their numbers, don't push. Never lift anyone off their feet. Keep your head up and your chin off your chest. The goal post can hurt you the same way the boards can, and even going slowly, a crash there can hurt.

As the puck arrives, lift their stick. Low on the shaft, well below their bottom hand, never at the hands. Lift it up, not sideways. Then let go.

Here is why it is done that way. Low on the shaft is where your leverage is, and it keeps you away from their hands. If you keep hold of the stick, it can be called holding. A lift up at the hands can be a penalty too, in some leagues. And never chop at their stick or their hands. A chop is slashing. At the hands, it can get you thrown out of the game in every league, even if nobody is hurt.

Keep the spot you already have, and never lean or push to keep it.

What you never do is try to move them out of the lane, in any league. It is interference wherever you play, checking league or not. Two minutes is the least it costs, and a hard shove can get you thrown out of the game in every league.

And never cross-check them out. A cross-check means using the shaft of your stick, held in both hands, to hit or shove a player. Never shove with both hands on your stick. Keeping your blade on the ice doesn't make it legal. A cross-check bad enough for a major gets you thrown out of the game in every league. And a reckless shove or cross-check can mean a ban for games after this one, too.

So the instruction that holds wherever you play is short. Hold your ground. Don't lean. Don't push. Lift the stick low as the puck arrives, then let go.

Clear means the puck is loose right in front of your net, and the nearest player has to get it out now. Don't stickhandle. Nearest player, get it out.

Sag, or collapse, means tighten in toward the net. Give up the outside and protect the slot. That is the language of one system, called the low zone collapse, where each player covers an area and the shape closes in around the net. In man-on-man it means nothing, because you go where your player goes. In a hybrid, which mixes the two, it only applies above the faceoff dots. Find out which your team plays.

The centre has a call to make here too. The centre's job moves with the puck, so their assignment is the one most often unclear. When the centre goes into the corner to help, someone has to cover the high slot. That is the top of the slot, between the faceoff dots and the tops of the circles. Under the usual coverage, that is the weak-side winger. Anyone with a clear view can call it. But the centre leaving is the one who knows it is happening, so the centre says so.

Let me pull your own end together before we move up the ice. The defencemen and the goalie call the breakout to the player going back, and that player listens before touching the puck. Going back, play the puck, not the player, and if you see their numbers, pull up. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call.

Up, over, reverse, rim and wheel. Rim against a forechecker sealing you in, and reverse against one who has skated past you. Know where your goalie may play the puck, and never call them past the red line. Off the glass, never over it, and never at the bench. Know what ice it costs.

On a screen or net front, hold your ground, no lean, no push, and lift the stick low as the puck arrives, then let go. Head up, chin off your chest. The post hurts like the boards. Never move them out.

Ninth idea. The middle of the ice, and telling the puck carrier you are there.

The carrier's head is often down. Without a voice, you are not an option. These calls tell them you exist, and where you are.

The simplest call in hockey is here. It means I'm open. When you hear it, look toward the voice. Even if you don't pass, you now know there is an option on that side. And if you shout here, be there.

Middle means I'm open through the centre. In their end and in the middle of the ice, that is the most valuable place to move the puck. In your own end, it is the pass that crosses the slot, so there it has to be certain. Judge it by where you are on the ice.

Back means I'm behind you, ready for a drop pass. When you hear it, you can leave the puck rather than carry it into trouble.

With you means I'm on your hip, keep the puck alive. When you hear it, you have two attackers, not one. Slow down half a step and use them.

I'm high means I'm the option up near the blue line. When the low options are covered, that is usually your safe reset.

Far side means your help is across the ice, not near you. That pass exists, but weigh it. In your own end, a pass across the ice goes across the front of your own net, and an interception there is a shot from right in front. Don't take it automatically.

Outlet means I'm your way out of the zone, usually a winger on the boards or a centre swinging low. Move the puck and start the breakout.

Stretch means a teammate is long, behind their forwards. Put it up to them. But check the blue line first. A teammate who is already across their blue line with both skates when the puck crosses is offside. One skate on the line keeps them onside. If you are the one waiting at the line, keep that skate touching the ice. In some leagues a skate lifted over the line doesn't count.

Two players calling for the same puck, both arriving and neither taking it, is a common mess. The fix is a standing rule on who has priority. Usually the player facing up the ice, or whoever called first.

Tenth idea. The rush, when they come at you up the ice.

This is where talking breaks down most. Everyone is skating flat out, and nobody wants to be the one who called it wrong.

Odd man means they have more attackers than you have defenders. When you hear it, defenders stop thinking about the puck and start thinking about numbers. Published call lists use just the number. Two, or one. Short wins at speed.

A two-on-one is two attackers against one defender. The usual way to defend it splits the job. The defenceman takes the pass, and the goalie takes the shooter. The defenceman gets in the passing lane, stick flat on the ice, with no slide until the pass is released. Here is why the split works. Each player owns one half of the threat. But it only works if both of them say out loud which half is theirs.

That is one of two real systems. Some teams coach a timed slide across the lane instead, to force the shot. So find out which your team plays before you need it.

Some defencemen say I've got the pass, and the goalie answers take the shooter. The goalie's own version is one word. Shot.

Here is the trap with that word. When a goalie shouts shot, they are not warning you a shot is coming. They are telling you they have the shooter, and you take the pass. Here is why it is used that way. A warning tells a defenceman nothing they can act on. I have the shooter tells them where to put their stick. So stay in the passing lane and don't drift toward the puck. If your team has been using shot as a warning, pick another word for the warning. You have half a second to decode it.

Backcheck means we have lost the puck, get back. A backcheck is racing back to defend. When you hear it, turn and sprint first, and look second. Here is why. Watching the puck while drifting backwards is how the middle lane opens up.

On the way back, you are there for the puck and the open player, not a hit. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call. Never hit a player who can't see you coming, and never from behind. If you can see their numbers, pull up.

Pick them up, or take the middle, means there is an attacker nobody has, and you are the closest. Get on the defensive side of them, between them and your net, not behind their shoulder.

Who takes which lane on the backcheck is a coaching choice. Most often the centre takes the middle and the wingers take the outside lanes. Some teams send the first player back to the middle, whatever their position. Others pick up man-on-man, starting from the puck and working outward. Find out which your team plays. Whichever it is, somebody owns the middle, and nobody assumes it is somebody else.

And backcheckers call their own pickups. I've got the middle, or I've got seventeen. Here is why. The players skating back are the only ones who know who they have. Once they say it, your defencemen can commit to the puck instead of hedging.

Eleventh idea. Line changes.

Some games, especially for younger players, change lines in a set way, and then your coach and the officials tell you how. In games where your line chooses when to change, changing while play goes on is run almost entirely by voice. For a moment your team is out of shape, so the timing matters.

Change means the line is going off now. Go as a group. Never change during a scramble in your own end, or when you are the player caught deepest.

Stay means don't change now, it is too dangerous. Finish the shift. Here is why. A tired player in position beats a fresh player arriving late.

On the bench, I've got first player off claims the seat of the first teammate who comes off, so nobody else jumps for it. Next shift means you are up after this one. Get to the door, get your head into the game, and know who you are replacing.

Don't use last change or last man for any of this. Both already mean something else, and they will get you the wrong reaction. Last change is the home team's right to put its players on the ice after the visitors. Last man means the deepest defender, the one player who must not leave. That is the opposite of the change is done, go. Pick a word neither of them owns.

And the bench penalty isn't about seats. It is about distance. The player coming off has to be right at the bench and out of the play before the new one joins in.

The bench has one more job. It warns about what the players on the ice can't see. A player coming off their bench, a matchup, a shift that has gone on too long.

Let me pull the middle of the ice together. Say here, and be there. Weigh middle and far side by where you are, and be certain in your own end. Check the blue line before a stretch pass. On the rush, odd man means think about numbers, and on a two-on-one, the defenceman takes the pass and the goalie takes the shooter. A goalie's shot means exactly that.

On a backcheck, sprint first and look second. You are there for the puck and the open player, not a hit. Never hit anyone who can't see you coming. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call, and if you see their numbers, pull up. Somebody always owns the middle. And change as a group, never in a scramble.

Twelfth idea. In their end. All alone, point, and the forecheck.

All alone first, because you hear it as you arrive. All alone means you are carrying it into their zone with no support. One published call list says it also means you are outnumbered. Some teams use it for the support half only. Agree which, because they lead to different decisions. No support says wait. Outnumbered says don't go in at all. Either way, when you hear it, don't attack their defence on your own. Slow down, protect the puck, or chip it in and wait for help.

Point means the defenceman at the point, just inside the blue line, has space for a shot. Get the puck up to them. Then get to the front of the net for the tip or the rebound.

Get there under control, and set up beside the goalie, never into them. Keep your spot. Don't push back or lean on anyone, and never your stick across someone. Any contact with a goalie can be a penalty, and it can get you thrown out of the game. A goalie who is down or turned can't protect themselves, so contact can hurt them. It can also cost your team the goal. And keep your head up, chin off your chest. The goal post can hurt you the same way the boards can, even going slowly.

Now the forecheck. Forechecking means pressuring the other team in their own end to win the puck back. F one, F two and F three are not positions. They are jobs, set by who gets to the puck first, and they change hands during a shift. So nobody knows which one they have until somebody says it.

I've got F one claims the first-in job. I'm high claims the job of staying above the puck. Here is why it matters. It takes half a second, and it stops the most expensive forechecking mistake there is. Two players doing the same job, while nobody does the other one.

Going in first is a chase. You are going for the puck, not the player. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call. If you can see their numbers, pull up. And if the puck is on the boards, set your body for the boards. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the checker, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly.

Thirteenth idea. Warnings you can hear anywhere on the ice.

Feet, or under, means the puck is in your skates and you have lost sight of it. When you hear it, look down, knock it to your stick with your skate, and reset. It is one of the most useful calls there is. A puck in your feet is invisible to you and obvious to everyone else.

Send the skate at the puck. Never at the player beside it, and never at the blade of your own stick. Here is why that needs saying. A puck in your feet usually has an opponent's feet next to it, so this is where a kick at a player happens.

If you kick at an opponent, or even try to, you can be thrown out of the game in every league. In most, it is a match penalty, whether or not anyone is hurt. And never use your skate to push a player, even without a kick. In some leagues that gets you thrown out too. Kicking your own stick blade to drive the puck is no shortcut either. Depending on the league, it stops play, it is a penalty, or the goal doesn't count.

Notice that feet sends your eyes down, and man on tells you to keep them up. They are opposite instructions, so be sure which one you heard.

Heads up means something is coming that you haven't seen. It is less precise than man on, but faster to shout. When you hear it, get your head up and brace. At the boards, take the boards position, never your back to the checker, never duck. Expect contact.

Heads, without the up, is a pure safety call. A puck or a stick is flying through the air toward someone. Turn away and cover up. Never use heads to mean anything tactical. And it is never an instruction to duck into contact near the boards. Ducking a flying puck in open ice makes sense. Ducking a checker at the boards is how spinal injuries happen.

Behind you means an opponent or the puck is behind you. That is its weakness, because you can't tell which. Some teams keep behind you for the puck, and use man on for opponents. If yours hasn't split it, check over your shoulder straight away and be ready for either.

Fourteenth idea. Faceoffs. Before a faceoff, nobody is moving and everyone can hear. So this is the easiest talking in hockey, and the most often skipped.

Before you line up, the centre tells the other four skaters what the play is. Not a code word shouted at the last second. An actual sentence, said while the linesperson is still skating in. Here is why. Coaches who run set plays off the draw agree that they only work when every player knows their job, and this is the moment that gets settled.

Keep the menu tiny. Two or three plays that everyone knows cold beat ten that nobody remembers. For example, win it back to the point for a shot, with traffic already in front. Or win it to the winger on the strong side for a quick chance. Or, in your own end, win it to the defenceman on the boards side, who rims it out straight away.

And name a fallback. Most draws aren't won cleanly. So agree what happens in a scramble, usually that everyone takes their player and you sort it out. Then a lost draw doesn't become a lost coverage.

Two things the wingers must be told. First, if a winger creeps inside the faceoff circle before the puck drops, in most leagues it is the centre who gets thrown out of the draw, not the winger. So stay out of the circle, and behind your hash marks, the short lines on the side of the circle, until the puck drops.

Second, agree in advance which winger steps in when the centre is thrown out. A second violation by your team on the same draw can cost a two-minute penalty, so the replacement has to be clean. Nothing clever. If you step in and tie up the other centre's stick, it is stick on stick only, over or under the blade, for an instant. Never a hand on their stick, and never your stick on their hands. Get low from your knees and hips, not by pushing your head out over the dot.

Hand signals work well at faceoffs. You can be seen but not heard across a circle, and the other team can hear you. A tap on the boards, a glove pointed at a spot, a stick blade angled at the target. But only signals agreed in the dressing room. Never invent one in the middle of a game.

Let me pull their end and the warnings together. On all alone, don't attack their defence on your own. On point, move it up, then go to the net under control, beside the goalie, never into them, head up. Contact with a goalie can get you thrown out.

Forecheckers name their order, and the first one in plays the puck, not the player. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call, and if you see their numbers, pull up. On feet, the skate goes at the puck, never at a player and never at your own blade, and never use it to push a player. Heads means turn away from a flying puck, never duck at the boards. And at a faceoff, the centre says the play, and the wingers stay out of the circle.

Fifteenth idea. Talking without your voice.

Rinks are loud, and sometimes a voice won't carry. These signals back up your words. They don't replace them. And some things are better unspoken, because the other team can hear you.

Put your stick blade flat on the ice, out in front of you, as a target. That says pass it here, and gives the passer something to aim at. A stick held up in the air says the opposite, that you are not ready.

Tapping your stick on the ice is the old I'm open signal. But every tap sounds the same. An opponent can tap and get a blind pass sent straight to them. So use a tap to back up a call, never on its own, and never pass blind on a tap.

Make eye contact before the pass. A tenth of a second tells you two things at once. They have seen you, and they are ready.

Look a defender off. If you stare at the teammate you want, the defender covering them looks there too. So look at one option, then pass to another. Here is why it works. Defenders read heads and eyes as a shortcut. That is why a defenceman facing a rush is taught to read the attacker's chest and hips instead.

Point your glove or stick at the space where you want a teammate to go, then put the puck there. It is common on breakouts and on the power play, where the passer has a moment of calm and the receiver is looking up.

And read hips. Where your feet and hips point tells a teammate what you are about to do. Hips open to the middle say you are cutting inside. Square to the boards says you are staying wide. Linemates who have played together a long time look like they can read minds. What they are really doing is reading hips.

Sixteenth idea. Building the habit.

Almost nobody talks on the ice at first. The problem isn't tactics. It is that being loud in front of people feels uncomfortable. Newer players worry about being wrong, about being loud, about being the only one making noise. That is normal, and it fades with practice. Nobody has measured how common it is or how long it takes, so judge it against your own team.

So talk in every drill, even the ones where it feels silly. A simple passing drill with nobody in the way is where the habit gets built. Here is why. You can talk without having to think about anything else. Coaches who accept silence in drills get silence in games. Treat a missing call the way you would treat a missed backcheck.

Start with man on and time. Get those two automatic before you add anything. And from the very first drill, only say time when you are sure. A wrong time sends a teammate into a hit they weren't ready for.

Make one player responsible for noise in each drill, and rotate the job. Here is why. The quiet players get pushed through the awkward stage, instead of hiding behind the loud ones. Talk on the bench too. Players who talk on the bench talk on the ice. And afterwards, say out loud which calls worked. That over saved us a turnover does more for a team's talking than any general encouragement.

To agree your words, sit down in the dressing room at the start of the season, not on the ice in game three. Twenty minutes and a whiteboard is enough. One word for each meaning. The classic mix-up is one call carrying two meanings, like behind you. Ten to fifteen calls covers almost everything, and a longer list won't survive a real game.

Bring the goalie into it, because theirs are the calls the defencemen have to act on instantly, and they are the ones most often left out. Look at the list again about a month in. You will find two calls nobody uses and one situation nobody has a word for. And if you want a new call, bring it to practice before you shout it in a game.

One more thing about your voice. Keep it for your teammates, not the referee. Arguing doesn't change the call, and it takes your attention off the play. Disputing a call can be a penalty in every league, and keeping on at the official after that can get you thrown out of the game.

One honest note. Nobody has counted what gets said on amateur ice, or ranked the mistakes. So when I have called something the most useful call, or the most common mistake, that is what coaches see, not a measurement.

Let me pull it all together.

The puck carrier can't see what you can see. Whoever can see it makes the call, and where two of you can see it, both of you call it.

Man on and time are the two calls that matter most. Shout man on early. Only say time when you are sure, because a wrong time sends a teammate into a hit they weren't ready for. And at the boards, called or not. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the checker, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly.

Call early, short and loud, with a name on it, and answer every call. In your own end, call back door and call the switch. Never send the puck across the front of your own net.

On a screen, hold your ground, no lean, no push, and lift the stick low as the puck arrives, then let go. Head up, chin off your chest. The post hurts like the boards. Never move them out. Going back for the puck, on the backcheck and on the forecheck, play the puck, not the player. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call. If you see their numbers, pull up. On feet, kick the puck, never the player and never your own blade, and never push a player with your skate. And agree your words as a team, one word for one meaning, all season.

Much of this depends on what your team plays. So here are three questions for your coach. What are our calls? Do we switch on a crossing, or follow our player? And on a two-on-one, do you want me in the lane, or sliding? And one for your league. Which rules do we play under?

Go and ask. Then use those words on every shift. Now you know why each one works.

### 8. What I found unclear in the source

- **S :274 and :307 give the screener's lack of the puck as the reason moving them is interference** ("they have no puck, so walking them out is interference"). Heard alone, that is the brief's item-1 "so" trap: it implies moving a puck carrier is different. The script states the ban without the condition. The source sentence is accurate as rule text, but a page that is read aloud carries the same shape; worth a look by whoever owns S.
- **KF :9 and :25 say "never your back to the checker" with no definition of "the checker"**; :108 calls it "the check coming". The script adds the brief's definition. A reader of S could take "the checker" to mean only a player legally checking, which narrows the limb in a non-checking game.
- **S :108 teaches only part of the boards posture** (never the point of the shoulder, head up, chin off the chest, the facing-wall fallback). Skates parallel and forearm and hip to the boards are not in S. Per §2 of the standard this should be a plan row so S gains them, or the script should drop them; the script keeps them, as the remake did.
- **S :68 and :128 present "time" as the goalie's first call with no "only when sure" limb in those units**; the limb lives in KF :15, :556 and CM :639. A listener-facing rendering of :128 voiced alone would teach the call without its limb.
- **S gives no reason a team picks man-on-man or zone for the switch, the high D-to-D route, the timed slide, or the backcheck lanes.** The script says how each works and stops (plan decision 6).

### 9. Where this script is weakest

- **The spine lands at 24.0%**, just inside the first quarter. Any addition before the fifth idea pushes it over.
- **Sixteen ideas in 45.2 minutes**, and the call lists (fifth and ninth ideas) are runs of definitions. A listener may find them dense even with the recaps.
- **"Here is why" is used 22 times.** It is the template's device, but it may sound mechanical aloud.
- **The eighth idea's holding block is the most rules-heavy stretch**, about 2,300 characters of limbs and consequences in a row. It is needed, and shorter than the remake's, but it is the least plain part of the episode.
- **"If you can see their numbers, pull up" on the dumped-puck retrieval** is the brief's gate applied to a player who usually has the forechecker behind them. It is true, but it may sound odd.
- **The first seam splits the switch idea** (§1).
- Backward and forward pointers were audited by hand for shape. The remaining signposts are "From here on I will use one short version", "Everything else in this episode adds detail to one of those four", "treat every call from here on as an example", and the recap openers. "There is one exception. Time." in the third idea refers to the call taught in the second, and restates its limb in full.
