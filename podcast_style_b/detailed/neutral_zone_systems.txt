===== SCRIPT START =====

This episode is about the neutral zone, the middle of the ice, and what you actually do there. It takes its time. For each piece I will tell you what to do, and also why it works. If you know why, you can still choose well when the play looks different from the picture in your head.

Before any instructions, a few words you will hear all the way through. I will explain them now, so they don't get in the way later.

The neutral zone. This is the middle third of the rink, the space between the two blue lines. A strong skater crosses it in about three seconds. That is all the time you get to decide anything there.

The red line. This is the line across the very middle of the rink.

The lanes. Picture the ice split lengthways into three strips. The left boards, the middle, and the right boards. Each strip is a lane.

Strong side and weak side. The strong side is the side of the ice the puck is on. The weak side is the other side.

The carrier. That is whoever has the puck.

F one, F two and F three. These are the three forwards, numbered by the order they arrive at the puck. F one gets there first. It is about who arrives, not about who plays centre or wing. D one and D two are the two defencemen. D one is on the strong side, and D two is on the weak side.

The gap. This is the space between a defender and the attacker they are responsible for. A tight gap is about one to one and a half stick lengths. That is close enough to touch their stick with yours if you reach. That distance is coaching convention, and coaches don't all agree on it.

That is the vocabulary. Now the job.

Losing the puck in the middle of the ice tends to hurt most. A puck lost deep in their end still leaves them the whole rink to skate. A puck lost in your own corner finds your whole team at home. A puck lost in the middle catches two or three of your players skating the wrong way, with your defence standing flat-footed. That is how you end up facing a two-on-one, two attackers against one defender. Coaches agree on this, but it is their judgement, not a measured figure.

And the team that controls the middle decides the kind of game. Rushes up and down the ice, or pucks shot in and chased. Neither is better, but the choice should be yours.

I will start with four big ideas, because they matter most. If you only listen to the first part of this episode, take those four with you. After that we will go through the rest one piece at a time.

First idea. Know which plan your team is playing this shift, and play the same one as the other four.

When the other team has the puck in the middle, your team has a plan for where its five skaters stand. That plan is called a structure, or a shape. Some structures go and pressure the puck. Some sit back and wait for a mistake.

Here is why playing the same one matters more than which one it is. Say three of you are pressuring and two are holding back. Now there is a gap straight through the middle of your own team. That gap is exactly what the other team is looking for.

Coaches say most breakdowns in the middle are not about effort. They are two players on one shift with different ideas about the plan. That is coaching agreement, not a counted figure.

Which structure you play is your coach's choice, not a law of hockey. Most teams use more than one, depending on the score and the shift. So ask two questions. What is our usual one? And what is the call to switch? Then confirm the plan on the bench, not in the middle of the shift.

Second idea. When your team loses the puck, get back the right way.

First, get on the defensive side of the player you are covering. That means between them and your own net. A backchecker level with the attacker is worth more than a faster one on the wrong side.

Then skate home through the middle of the ice, with your head up. Not along the boards. The dangerous pass goes through the middle, and the middle is the shorter way home.

And don't hang at the red line, waiting for a breakaway pass.

Here is why. Your defenceman can only keep a tight gap if getting beaten is not a disaster. It is not a disaster when a forward is coming back behind the attacker. That forward coming back is called backpressure. The attacker who beats your defenceman just skates into a second defender.

So the gap is a job for all five of you, not just the defence. With three forwards coming back, your defencemen can step up and squeeze. The attacker has no time and usually chips it in. With one forward gliding and two coasting, your defencemen have to back off. Then the attacker comes in with speed and the puck.

The same defencemen can look brilliant or hopeless, depending on what the forwards did forty feet earlier. So if your team keeps getting skated through, the fix is usually not the defence.

There is one exception. Some coaches want one forward to stay up for a long pass, called the stretch option. If your coach has given you that job, you are following a plan. But your team is defending four against five while you do it.

Third idea. At your own blue line, you either stand up or back off. Pick the right one, and say it out loud.

This one is mostly for defencemen. Standing up means meeting the carrier at your blue line and refusing to give any more ice. The carrier then has to dump it in, pass it, or lose it. Backing off means skating backwards at the carrier's speed. You keep them in front of you and to the outside. You give up the line, but never the middle.

Why stand up? A carried entry into your zone is worth more to the other team than a dumped one. If you turn their carries into dump-ins, your night gets easier. It also keeps the play far from your net, and gives your forwards time to get back.

Why back off? You can't be beaten wide by pure speed. The attacker ends up on the outside, where a shot is a poor one. And it is very hard to make a disastrous mistake doing it.

So which one, when? Standing up is a bet that you have help behind you. If you are beaten standing up, you are beaten completely. Your feet have stopped and theirs have not. So stand up when your forwards are coming back. Or when the attacker is out wide, or is fighting the puck. Back off when nobody is coming back. Or when the attacker is faster than you, on a two-on-one, or when you are the last player back.

Check over your shoulder before you commit. Then call it. I'm up. Or, I've got nothing, back. How hard to hold the line is a coaching choice. Some want it held hard, and some want everything kept to the outside. Ask which yours wants.

If you stand up and the carrier chips the puck past you, turn and skate to the puck. Never step into the carrier, and never hit them. Blocking a player's path with your body can be a penalty, called interference.

Now the most important safety point in this episode. Standing up means refusing ground with your feet. It never means building up speed into a hit. Stick on the puck, not a hit. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call. And if you see their numbers, pull up.

A carrier with their head down, or fighting the puck, tells you one thing. They can't change direction quickly. That is all. It is not an invitation to arrive harder.

Building up speed into contact, even with two hard strides, can be charging. Speeding up through the hit is enough. Leaving your feet to hit someone is charging too. Charging can be a penalty in every league, whether checking is allowed or not.

In every league it can cost more than two minutes, and in some it always does. If the player gets hurt, you can be thrown out of the game, and in some leagues that is automatic. A carrier with their head down, who hasn't seen you, may be a player who can't protect themselves. In some leagues, charging that player is a match penalty, so plan on one. A match penalty goes past being thrown out of the game.

So close the space with your feet. Keep the carrier in front of you, and play the puck.

Fourth idea. When you have the puck, read the first opponent who comes at you.

Say you are coming out of your own end with the puck. You have about a second and a half to decide. Your biggest clue is their F one, the first forechecker coming to pressure you. Watch their route, not their speed.

F one skating straight at you, hard, means they are pressuring. Beat that with one quick pass past them. If you beat the first player, you are attacking with more players than they have back.

F one curving across the ice, showing you the boards and not really closing, means they are steering you. They want you to go where their teammates are waiting. So don't go where you are being steered. Move the puck instead of carrying it into them. Switch it across to your defence partner, or get ready to chip it past the player who steps up to meet you.

F one standing near the red line doing nothing means no pressure is coming. Take your time, look up, and get ready to come again from the back.

Let me pull those four ideas together, because they are the core of the job. One. Know your team's plan this shift, and play the same one as the other four. Ask for the usual one and the call to switch. Two. When the puck is lost, get between your player and your net. Come home through the middle with your head up, and don't hang at the red line. Your backpressure is what lets your defencemen play tight. Three. At your blue line, stand up only when you have help behind you. Otherwise back off, and call it. Stand up with your feet, never with a hit. Four. With the puck, read F one's route before you decide.

Everything else in this episode adds detail to one of those four. So now let's go through the shapes your team might play when the other team has the puck.

Fifth idea. How the shapes are named.

Teams name their structures with numbers. You count from the puck back toward your own net. So a one-two-two is one player pressuring, two across the middle, and two at the back.

The same numbers also name forechecks and power-play shapes, which are different. So when your coach calls a number, check which zone they mean.

Sixth idea. The one-two-two, the classic.

Here is the shape. F one pressures near their goal line. F two and F three sit across the middle of the neutral zone. The two defencemen hold the back, around your own blue line.

Now how it works. F one is not really trying to steal the puck. F one's job is to take away the middle and force the carrier to one side. Skate a curved route that shows the carrier the boards. Steer with your route and your stick, never your body. If you steal it, good. But steering the carrier is the real job.

F two covers the boards on the puck side. When the puck goes to their winger there, F two steps up, timed to arrive with the puck. Play the puck, not the player. Touching that winger before the puck gets there is interference. When it arrives, you still play the puck.

Even where hitting is allowed, the step up is not meant to be a hit. Stick on the puck, not a hit. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call. And if you see their numbers, pull up. Arrive with your feet and your stick, not with speed built up into the player. Speed built up into a player is the picture charging is about. And driving them hard into the boards is boarding.

On the boards, keep to these every time. No shove, no lean, and never your stick across someone. Never into anyone's back, and never lift anyone off their feet. Even going slowly, a crash into the boards can hurt.

F three holds the middle and the weak side, reading for the pass across the ice.

D one steps up on the boards if the puck gets past F two. That is the decision with the least cover behind it. If D one is beaten, only D two is left. So D one plays it the same way. Stick on the puck, not a hit. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call. And if you see their numbers, pull up. No shove, no lean, and never your stick across someone. Never into anyone's back, and never lift anyone off their feet. Even going slowly, a crash into the boards can hurt.

D two stays home in the middle, as the safety for when everything goes wrong.

Why would a team play it? It is balanced. It never leaves you short of players behind the play. It forgives one player's mistake, and it asks nobody for exceptional skating. It is the safest shape to teach a new team.

What does it give up? The first pass. F one alone can't create real pressure, so a calm defence usually gets out cleanly. And if F one chases instead of steering, the whole shape is on the wrong side. The alternatives are the trap, which sits deeper, and pressure, which hunts the puck. Ask which yours plays.

Seventh idea. The trap.

The trap is a one-two-two with everyone dropped back. F one sits high, near their blue line. F two and F three sit at the red line, by the boards. Your defence holds your own blue line.

How it works. The trap lets the carrier skate. It steers them to the boards, and meets them at the red line with F two stepping up. There is no way through the middle, because F one took it away earlier.

F two plays it the same way as before. Stick on the puck, not a hit. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call. And if you see their numbers, pull up. No shove, no lean, and never your stick across someone. Never into anyone's back, and never lift anyone off their feet. Even going slowly, a crash into the boards can hurt.

Why would a team play it? The turnover happens with the other team's forwards ahead of the puck. So you are not stopping their attack. You are inviting it to a place where winning the puck gives you a counterattack straight away.

How do you tell a trap from the ordinary one-two-two? Look at F two's depth, and F one's speed. Players up near their blue line, with F one really closing, is the ordinary version. Players back at the red line, with F one just gliding, is a trap.

The trap once ruled top professional hockey, partly because a pass from your own end over the red line was banned. That has changed. So at the top level it is now a tool for part of a game, like protecting a lead. The alternative is the ordinary one-two-two, which still fights for the exit. Ask which yours plays.

Eighth idea. The one-three-one.

Here is the shape. F one pressures. Three players stand spread in a line across the ice. One defenceman sits behind them as the last player back.

Usually the three across are F two and F three on the boards, with a defenceman in the middle. Some coaches put a forward in the middle instead. But never three forwards across, because F one is already one of your three forwards.

How it works. If you are one of the three, hold your lane and slide with the others toward the puck. Your job is not to attack the puck. Your job is to leave no way through. The defenceman at the back picks up anything that gets through, and every puck that is dumped in.

Why would a team play it? It takes away the middle better than any other shape. It protects a slower pair of defencemen. And with the back defenceman holding, it almost never gives up an odd-man rush, a rush where the attackers outnumber the defenders. That is why so many teams switch to it with a one-goal lead late in a game.

A one-three-one doesn't try to take the puck from you. It offers you the puck on terms you won't like.

What does it cost? It gives their defencemen all the time they want in their own end. It is passive, so when you are behind it won't win you the puck back. And it needs strict lane discipline. If one of the three leaves their lane to chase, a gap opens straight away. The one defenceman behind can't cover two lanes. Ask which version yours plays, and who stands in the middle.

Ninth idea. Pressure.

Pressure is what the middle looks like when your team forechecks hard. Two of your forwards are in their end, hunting the puck. That is called a two-one-two forecheck. Hunting still means stick on the puck, not a hit. This part is about what the other three do behind it.

F three holds the middle, level with the puck or a little ahead. Don't drift to the boards. The pass that hurts you comes up the middle.

The strong-side defenceman steps up hard when the puck goes to the boards. Hard means closing the gap fast, not going into a hit. Stick on the puck, not a hit. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call. And if you see their numbers, pull up. No shove, no lean, and never your stick across someone. Never into anyone's back, and never lift anyone off their feet. Even going slowly, a crash into the boards can hurt.

The weak-side defenceman stays home. With two forwards deep, that player is the only real safety.

Why would a team play it? It is the one approach that really takes the puck back, instead of waiting for a mistake. It wears down a defence that can't handle pressure. And it is the right choice when you are chasing a goal.

What does it cost? One pass past the two forecheckers leaves F three alone against three attackers. Nobody can pressure like this for a whole game, either. And a team with one skilled puck-moving defenceman punishes it badly. The alternative is to contain, in a one-two-two or a one-three-one. Ask which yours plays.

Tenth idea. The one-four, and the left-wing lock.

The one-four puts one forechecker up, and four players in a flat line across your own blue line. It is the most cautious shape there is.

Why would a team play it? Almost nothing gets through it. Carrying the puck in against it is close to impossible, and odd-man rushes almost never happen, because everyone is home. Teams use it in the last minute or two of a lead, or when the other team has pulled its goalie.

What does it cost? It gives away the whole neutral zone, and it creates no attack at all. You will spend the shift in your own end. So don't play it as your normal shape. Some youth teams do, and they shouldn't.

The left-wing lock is a mix of a forecheck and a neutral-zone shape. Two forwards forecheck hard in their end, usually the centre and the right wing. The moment your team loses the puck, the left wing drops back into line with the two defencemen. Now there are three across the back, each covering about a third of the ice.

Why would a team play it? You get real forechecking pressure without the usual risk of an odd-man rush. The third player back is already there. And it is simple to teach. Left wing, you are the third defenceman.

What does it cost? It takes one player out of the attack, and asks them to do a job most wingers aren't trained for. Many teams now lock whichever winger is highest instead. That needs a loud call, straight away. Ask which yours plays.

Eleventh idea. Which shape, and when.

That is your coach's call, and the answer is usually more than one. Here is the rough guide. Chasing a goal tends toward pressure. Protecting a one-goal lead tends toward the one-three-one or the trap. The last minute and a half against a pulled goalie tends toward the one-four. A slow pair of defencemen on the ice tends toward the one-three-one.

One more thing for defencemen, in the shapes that press. When your team loses the puck in their end, a common cue is for both defencemen to step up inside the faceoff dots. That closes the gap and buys your forwards time to get back.

Four limits on that cue. A step up is not a pinch. Your feet keep moving backwards and the attacker stays in front of you. It is not a licence to finish with a hit. Stick on the puck, not a hit. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call. And if you see their numbers, pull up. It needs backpressure to be safe. And it is not every team's cue, so ask whether yours wants it.

Let me pull the shapes together. Shapes are named from the puck back. The one-two-two steers the carrier to one side and is the safest to teach. The trap drops back and kills the play at the red line. The one-three-one holds a line of three and never chases. Pressure hunts the puck, with F three holding the middle. The one-four is for the last minute only.

And every step up is your feet and your stick, never a hit.

Now your team has the puck. Twelfth idea. If there is no good way forward, go back and come again.

That is called a regroup. You turn the puck back toward your own end, reset, and attack again. You are not retreating. You still have the puck. You are turning down a bad way in, to get a better one. A backwards pass is not a failure.

Why does a regroup beat forcing it? Forcing the puck into a set defence usually ends in a turnover at the line, a dump-in you don't get back, or an offside. All three end your possession. A regroup keeps the puck, and while you hold it three things happen. Their forecheck has to reset. Their forwards, who were skating toward you, now have to stop and turn round. Turning is the slowest thing anybody does on skates. And your forwards get to build up speed toward the line. That speed is the real prize.

Thirteenth idea. How the regroup goes.

A forward who can't go forward passes back to a defenceman. The defenceman takes it with their hips open up the ice, not facing their own net. That way they can see the whole rink.

If the near side is covered, the defenceman passes it across to their partner. That pass across is called a D-to-D. It makes the other team's whole shape slide over. As it goes, all three forwards change lanes and build speed. The defenceman then passes to whichever forward has the most speed and the most ice.

One firm rule on that pass across. Make it flat and hard. Never make it blind across the middle with a forechecker between you. If it is picked off, it is a two-on-one straight away, with both your defencemen on the wrong side. If the lane is not clean, keep the puck, or carry it back yourself.

Fourteenth idea. The hinge.

If you are the defenceman who just passed it across, don't follow the puck up the boards. Drop back into the middle, behind your partner. That is called the hinge.

Here is why it works. Your partner moves up, finds everything covered, and gives it back to you. Now you are the best-placed player on the ice. You are behind the play, in the middle, facing up the ice, with nobody near you. The forecheck has committed to the other side.

The better version is the skating hinge. Start dropping back as you make the first pass. Then you take the return pass already moving. That gives you room to speed up, an extra second to read, and a chance to draw a forechecker before you pass.

Whether the hinge is there depends on how they forecheck. If their F one takes away the pass across to your partner, the hinge is gone. Look for a quick pass up, or use the boards. If they take away the pass across to the far forward, they have left the hinge open.

Fifteenth idea. The low regroup.

If a reset in the middle isn't enough, you can take it right back below your own blue line. Then you start again, like a breakout.

Why? It costs you ice, but it buys time. And it turns the problem into a breakout, which your team already has a plan for.

The trade is real, though. Their forecheck comes with you, and a turnover down there is a scoring chance against you. So the low regroup is a possession play, not a panic play. If you are panicking, put it off the glass and out of your zone instead.

Patience means moving the puck, not standing on it. If you hold it up on purpose, the referee can give your team a penalty for delaying the game.

Sixteenth idea. Forwards, take the puck moving.

The regroup is a defenceman's pass but a forward's tactic. And forwards get it wrong in two ways.

One is standing still and calling for it. Then you are an easy target with no speed, and you will be closed down at once. A forward who takes a regroup pass standing still has wasted the regroup.

The other is leaving too early. That means turning up the ice before your defenceman has the puck under control. Then the pass has to be perfect. If it isn't, you are offside, or the puck is behind you.

The right way goes like this. As your defenceman gets the puck, curl back in the same direction the puck is going. Stay below it, nearer your own end than the puck. Then, as the pass goes across, turn and drive up the ice into your lane. You want the puck when you are already moving forward, close to top speed, facing the play.

And don't all curl to the same place. One forward stays close for a short pass. One swings wide for the outside lane. One stays high for the long pass.

Seventeenth idea, and it is short. When not to regroup.

Not when you have more attackers than they have defenders. Take the three-on-two. Throwing away an odd-man rush is not clever.

Not when your line change is due. A regroup on tired legs means coming into the zone with nothing left. Then a hard dump past the red line and a change is a fair play, not a failure. Some adult leagues count a big wind-up as a slap shot, which is a penalty there. So keep the backswing short.

And not when the pass back isn't clean. A regroup into pressure is just a turnover in a worse place.

Let me pull the regroup together. When there is no way forward, go back and come again. It keeps the puck, makes their forwards turn, and gives yours speed. Make the pass across flat and hard, never blind through the middle. Defencemen, drop back for the hinge. Forwards, curl with the puck and take it moving. And skip it when you have numbers, when you need to change, or when the pass back isn't clean.

Now let's attack through the middle. Eighteenth idea. One forward in each lane.

Remember the three lanes. Left boards, middle, right boards. Put one forward in each.

Here is why. Three forwards spread out are three different passing options. A flat line of defenders can't cover three lanes without spreading thin. If two forwards share one lane, one defender covers both. Now your team has one fewer option than it could have.

It goes wrong because everyone follows the puck. The puck goes to the left boards, and your centre goes there too. Your right winger drifts over to help. Now the whole right side is empty, the defence has slid over, and there is nowhere to go.

So when the puck is in a lane that is not yours, hold your lane. Stay level with the puck or ahead of it. Out here, helping the carrier means being open in a different place, not being close.

A lane belongs to whoever is in it, not to one player. You will swap all the time. So call it. I've got middle.

And if you are carrying the puck and both your other forwards are in your lane, there is no pass. Slow down, hold the puck, and let them spread out.

Nineteenth idea. The middle lane.

A puck touched in the middle makes every defender reset.

Here is why. A defence covers one side at a time, sliding toward the puck. One quick pass into the middle, and back out to the far boards, makes them slide twice. A defence that is sliding has gaps. Boards, middle, far boards. It takes about a second, and it is the most reliable way to get in with the puck against an organised defence.

The catch is that losing the puck in the middle costs the most, in coaches' judgement. So play the middle pass with one touch. Pass it straight on, without stopping it. If you stop, turn and look around, you will get hit, and the puck will be going the other way. And expect to get hit there. Coaches say the middle is where open-ice hits happen, though nobody has counted it. A one-touch pass doesn't keep your head up for you. You have to do that yourself. So take it with your head up, because you can't brace for a hit you never saw. Do your looking before the puck arrives. The same goes when you skate home through the middle, and when you skate onto a long pass.

Twentieth idea. Taking a pass on the boards, with their defenceman stepping up.

The puck comes to you on the boards, and their defenceman is stepping up to meet you. You have about a second, and three good answers.

Bump it into the middle with one touch, if your centre is there and moving. The defender who stepped up is now behind the play.

Chip it off the boards past the defender's outside, and skate around them. Only do that if a second forward is coming to support you.

Or turn it back to a defenceman and regroup.

What you must not do is stop on the boards and try to beat the defender one-on-one with nobody behind you. A turnover there is a rush the other way, with you on the wrong side of it. Some teams chip every puck on the boards, and some play it to the middle. That is a coaching choice, so ask your bench which comes first.

Now take that pass the safe way, because a defender stepping up on you is exactly when it matters.

Get your skates parallel to the boards. Forearm and hip to the boards. Never the point of your shoulder, and not the whole shoulder if you can help it. Head up, chin off your chest.

Never turn your back to the other player. And never duck. By the other player I mean whoever comes at you on the boards, whether they are after the puck or just coming at you.

Both protect your head and neck. If you turn your back, you can't see the hit coming or brace for it. If you duck, your head meets the boards first. That holds even going slowly.

And it holds where checking isn't allowed, too. The boards don't need an opponent to hurt you. A legal angle, where a defender skates you toward the boards and takes away your room, can still put you into them.

If you are already facing the boards and can't turn, that is the last resort, not a choice. Get your stick and gloves up and spread wide on the glass, with your head off it.

From here on I will use one short version, the same words every time. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the other player, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly.

Let me pull that together before we go on. One forward in each lane, and hold yours. Use the middle with one touch, and your head up. On the boards, with a defender stepping up, bump it, chip it with support, or go back.

Twenty-first idea. Reading the middle while you carry the puck.

You have already read F one's route. Now two more reads.

Count the bodies. Lift your head at your own blue line, and count the opponents between you and their blue line. Two means there is space, so attack it. Three across means a one-three-one, so look behind the line before you look through it. Four in a line means there is nothing there. Start again, or chip it in and change.

Then watch their defencemen's feet. Make this read early, around the red line, not at their blue line. If their feet are moving backwards, the defenceman is backing off. Carry it in, because you have the line. If their feet have stopped, or they are squared up at the line, they are standing up. Chip it past them.

Twenty-second idea. Chip and chase.

When their defenceman is standing up with a tight gap, don't try to carry through them. Chip the puck past them into space, and skate onto it. Off the boards, off the glass, or softly into a spot behind them.

Why does it work? A defenceman whose feet have stopped can't win a race to a puck behind them.

The chip is a placement, not a clearance. Put it in a spot you can get to first. Soft off the boards, so it dies in the corner you are already skating to. Or flat past their hip on their stick side, so you can go round them on your forehand.

And the second and third forwards have to chase it. A chip nobody chases is just giving the puck away. Chasing still means this. Stick on the puck, not a hit. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call. And if you see their numbers, pull up.

Get it over the red line first. Icing is shooting the puck from your own half across their goal line, with nobody touching it. Play stops, and the faceoff comes back to your end. In some leagues, your tired line can't change before that faceoff either. So get it across the red line, or chip it soft enough that it stays in. Some adult leagues use the blue lines for icing instead, so ask your league where its line is.

Twenty-third idea. The stretch pass.

A stretch pass is a long pass to a forward who has stayed high, near or past their blue line. It skips the middle entirely.

Why does it work? It beats a line of defenders that is still standing out in the middle. A trap at the red line, or the three across a one-three-one. There is real ice behind that line. Against a one-three-one, a forward behind the line is in a race with one defender.

How to do it well. Get behind the line, not level with it. Level, a defender can cover you. Behind, you are racing them for the puck. Pass to space, not to feet, flat and hard. A long pass to a player standing still is a turnover.

Stay onside. Offside means both your skates are completely over their blue line before the puck has completely crossed it. So keep one skate touching the line, or on your side of it, until the puck crosses. The safe way is to drag your blade along the line. That works in every league. A skate lifted in the air doesn't count everywhere, so don't rely on it. Time your run, rather than waiting out there.

Leaving a forward high has a cost, because you defend four against five while you do it. Some coaches want a stretch option every time. Others forbid it in some score situations. So ask which yours wants.

Twenty-fourth idea. The late player.

When you attack, one forward can stay ten to fifteen feet behind the first two on purpose. Then they arrive into the space the first two have emptied. That space is usually the high slot, the ice in front of the net between the faceoff dots and the tops of the circles. This player is called the late man, or the trailer.

Why does it work? Defenders watch the puck and the attackers nearest it. So the player arriving second is the one nobody picks up. Coaches rate it as one of the best scoring chances in hockey, though nobody has counted that.

The discipline is to be really late. That means a full gap back, not five feet. Then the defence has to choose between you and the puck. And if the puck turns over, you are first back. That is the deal.

The other team will use the late player on you too. In the usual setup, the forwards coming back take that player, not the defencemen, who are busy with the first attackers. But some coaches give the trailer to the far-side defenceman. Ask which your team plays.

Twenty-fifth idea. Beating a trap.

A trap hands you the puck and defends the ice instead. So having the puck tells you nothing on its own. What follows works against any shape that won't come and get the puck. A trap, a one-three-one, or a one-four.

First, move the puck faster than they can slide. A line of defenders covers one side at a time. When the puck goes across, they all have to skate across with it, and a hard pass gets there first. A trap beats teams that carry the puck. It doesn't beat teams that pass it.

Second, use the boards on purpose. A hard rim, with a short backswing, goes along the boards around the curve of the rink and past a defender who is guarding the ice. So does a chip off the boards to a forward arriving behind the pressure.

That forward should expect to be met. Stepping up hard on a pass to the boards is a defenceman's job in most of these shapes. So the same position every time. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the other player, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly.

Third, stretch a forward behind the line, while it is still out in the middle. But not against a one-four. A one-four holds its line on its own blue line. A forward already behind it is offside the moment the puck crosses. So against a one-four, chip the puck hard past the flat line, and race for it. Nobody in a flat line is deeper than anybody else, so it is a real race. Race for the puck, not the player. Stick on the puck, not a hit. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call. And if you see their numbers, pull up. And if their goalie comes out for it, pull up. Take away the goalie's pass, and never hit them. A goalie racing for the puck is watching the puck, not you, and can't protect themselves. A hit there can hurt them. Unnecessary contact with a goalie is a penalty in every league. And no league makes a goalie outside the crease fair game.

Fourth, the seam. That is the gap between two defenders. A skilled carrier cutting back through the middle, behind a forechecker who has stepped up, can break the whole trap. It is the biggest reward and the biggest risk. Amateur players often try it first. Try it last. If you can't clearly see the seam, it isn't there.

Fifth, if the way in is not on, dump it deep past the red line on purpose. Then hunt it with three forwards. Stick on the puck, not a hit. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call. And if you see their numbers, pull up. A team with five players back in the middle is built to stop rushes, not forechecks.

Tracked games in top professional hockey found that carrying the puck in produces about twice the shots of dumping it in. That same work found players give the puck up at the line too easily, not too rarely. So against a trap, a dump-in you chase beats a turnover at the blue line. But that is a read on the trap in front of you, not a reason to dump every time.

Sixth, be patient. A trap lets you have the puck and waits for you to make a mistake with it. So if nothing is open, regroup, and make them come out and get it. But keep the puck moving, not standing still.

Let me pull the rest of the attack together. Watch their defencemen's feet. Backing off, carry it in. Standing up, chip it past and chase it, over the red line first.

Use the stretch pass only behind a line still out in the middle, and drag your blade to stay onside. Against a one-four, chip and race. If the goalie comes out for it, pull up. Never hit a goalie. Against a trap, move the puck, and try the seam last.

Twenty-sixth idea, and a short one. Faceoffs in the middle of the ice.

A faceoff in the neutral zone turns straight into a rush, because everybody is already facing the right way. So before the puck drops, know two things. Are we winning it forward or back? Your centre should tell you. And who is your player if we lose it?

Then don't creep. Everyone else stays about fifteen feet away from the two centres. If a teammate creeps in early, your centre gets thrown out of the faceoff. If your team does it a second time on the same faceoff, it can be a penalty. In most leagues that is automatic, so plan on it.

Where you line up is a coaching choice, and it varies a lot. Ask your coach for yours, and hold your line until the puck drops.

Let me pull it all together.

The middle of the ice gives you about three seconds, so decide before the puck reaches you. Know which shape your team is playing this shift, and play the same one as the other four.

When the puck is lost, get between your player and your net, and come home through the middle with your head up. Don't hang at the red line unless your coach has made you the stretch option. Your backpressure is what lets your defencemen play tight.

At your own blue line, stand up when you have help behind you, back off when you don't, and call it. Stand up with your feet, never with a hit. Stick on the puck, not a hit. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call. And if you see their numbers, pull up. A head-down carrier tells you they can't change direction, and nothing more.

With the puck, read F one's route. Keep one forward in each lane. When the way in isn't there, regroup, and take the puck moving. Against a trap, move the puck, and try the seam last.

And on the boards, the same position every time. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the other player, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly.

Much of this depends on what your team plays. So here are three questions to ask your coach. Which shape do we play, and what is the call to switch it? How hard do you want the blue line held? And do we leave a forward up for the stretch pass, and who is it?

Go and ask. Their answers tell you how your team wants you to play the middle of the ice, and now you know why each answer makes sense.

### 8. What I found unclear in the source

- **:752 against forechecking :877.** :752 says a goalie outside the crease "is not fair game in any rulebook here", which is true. Forechecking :877 records that USA Hockey's Casebook lets an attacker battle a goalie who holds the puck outside the privileged area.
