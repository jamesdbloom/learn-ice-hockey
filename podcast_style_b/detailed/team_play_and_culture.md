===== SCRIPT START =====

This episode is about playing as part of a team, and what that means you actually do, on the bench, on the ice, and around the game. It takes its time. For each piece I will tell you what to do and also why it works. If you know why something works, you can still do the right thing when the moment looks a little different from the picture in your head.

Before any instructions, a few words you will hear all the way through. I will explain them now, so they don't get in the way later.

A shift. That is your turn on the ice. It usually lasts somewhere between thirty and eighty seconds.

Your line. The group of players who go on the ice together. For forwards that is a centre and two wingers. Defencemen usually go in pairs.

A change. Swapping players on the ice for players from the bench. A change on the fly means swapping while the game is still going, without waiting for a whistle.

The bench and the penalty box. The bench is where your team sits during the game, behind the boards, with two doors onto the ice. The penalty box is the separate box where a player sits out a penalty.

The slot. The ice in front of the net, between the faceoff circles. Its top part is called the high slot.

The breakout. Your team's planned way of getting the puck out of its own end.

The backcheck. Skating hard back toward your own net when the other team has the puck. On a backcheck you go for the puck and the space, not the player.

Body checking. Using your body to knock a player off the puck. Some games allow it and many don't. A game that doesn't is called non-check. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call, never yours.

That is the vocabulary. Now the job.

Hockey swaps players while the game is still going. And you play with people you did not pick. So almost everything you do depends on a teammate you can't see doing what you expected. That is why a team's culture shows up in the score, not just in the dressing room. And it is why being reliable matters more than being skilful.

I will start with four big ideas, because they matter most. If you only listen to the first part of this episode, take those four with you. After that we will go back through, one part at a time, and add the detail.

First idea. When a teammate leaves their spot to go after the puck, fill it. And when somebody has filled yours, get back to it as soon as the play lets you.

Here is why the rest of the episode grows out of this one. On a hockey team, trust is not really a feeling. It is a prediction. It means being able to predict what a teammate will do.

Picture a defenceman starting the breakout. They often pass the puck to a spot before the winger gets there. That pass is a bet on where the winger will be. It only works if the defenceman knows what that particular winger does.

When players stop trusting each other, you can see it on the ice. They stop leaving their own job to cover someone else's, because they don't believe the cover will come back.

Here is one example. In your own end, the centre goes into the corner to help. The winger on the far side from the puck is meant to drop into the high slot and fill the centre's spot. But last time they did it, the centre never came back, and the winger got blamed for the goal. So this time they stay where they are. And your team gives up shots from the slot.

Here is another. Defencemen stop stepping down from the other team's blue line to keep the puck in the zone. That step down is called a pinch. They stop because they don't believe a forward will come back to cover behind them. And a team that never pinches can never keep the pressure on in the other team's end.

A pinch is a play on the puck, not on a player. Stick on the puck, not a hit. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call. And if you can see a player's numbers, pull up.

None of that needs anybody to dislike anybody. It just needs doubt. A team that trusts each other plays a more aggressive game, with more support, than a team of equal skill that doesn't.

So the thing to do is keep the exchange going. Fill the gap when a teammate goes. Then get back to your own spot quickly, so the next cover is worth making. If you don't come back, your team will stop covering at all.

Which spot you fill, and when, is set by your team's system, its plan for who goes where. The rotation I just described, where the far winger drops into the high slot, is one common version. Your team may do it differently. So find out what yours plays.

Second idea. The bench is a working position, not a rest.

Before you step over the boards, know the game. The score, the clock, and the situation. Are you killing a penalty? Is the next faceoff in your own end? Watch who is on the ice for the other team, and who just came off their bench. That tells you who you are about to face.

Now the change itself. Some games, especially for younger players, change lines in a set way, and then your coach and the officials tell you how. In games where your line chooses when to change, here is how it works.

When your line is next, get to the door before you need to. Stand up, gloves on, stick in hand. Know exactly who you are replacing. Not a forward. A person. In the last few seconds, watch that player, not the puck.

As you go, say their name out loud. I've got Sam. Here is why. Too many players on the ice is usually two players who both think the same spot is theirs. Saying the name stops that, and it costs nothing. Too many players is a two-minute penalty against your team, called a bench minor.

Then the timing. The player coming off has to meet two conditions before you step on. They have to be at the bench. And they have to be out of the play. Most players remember the first and forget the second, and the second is the one that gets called. So step on as they arrive, not before.

And if you are the one coming off, skate straight to the bench. No coasting, and no last look at the puck.

Change while you still have legs. A player who stays out too long gets to the bench exhausted, and is still tired when they go back over the boards.

And pick your moment, because when you change matters more than how. Don't go while the other team has the puck in your end. Don't go when you are the last player back. Wait until the puck is out of your end, or your team has it.

Third idea. Never retaliate. Never go over the boards during a fight or a scuffle. And never leave the penalty box until an official lets you out, even if your time is up.

Retaliation is what makes things worse, every time. And retaliating, or going over the boards for a teammate, are the same instinct at two different sizes. A teammate is in trouble, the door is right there, and going through it feels like loyalty. It isn't.

Here is why. Say it goes up on the ice and you are on the bench. The teammate you want to help is already being pulled apart by the officials. They are going to the penalty box, or off the ice, whatever you do. All you add is yourself, whoever follows you through the door, and a team that finishes the game short. From the bench, the only useful thing you can do is not be on the ice.

So sit down, and stay sat down. If you are the player nearest the door, that is a job, not a seat. Say the name of whoever is halfway over the boards. It usually stops them.

If you are in the penalty box, you have the hardest version. The trouble may be right in front of you, and your time running out is not permission to leave. Stay there until an official lets you out.

And don't go out afterwards to settle it either. That is the same retaliation one step later, and everyone can see who started the second incident.

Here is what it costs. If you leave the bench or the penalty box during a fight, plan on being thrown out of the game. That is true in every league for the first player off. In most leagues it goes for any scuffle, not only a fight. And it doesn't stop at the first player off. Several leagues add more on top. That can be a longer penalty your team has to kill, or a ban for games after this one.

The teams this never happens to didn't decide it in the moment. Somebody said out loud, before the season, that nobody leaves the bench. Get your team to say it.

Fourth idea, and it is the one where getting it wrong can be permanent. When someone goes down and isn't getting up.

Stop playing, and wave for the officials and the bench. Then treat it as a possible head or neck injury. A knock to the head counts, because any head injury could also be a neck injury.

That means three things. Somebody phones for an ambulance, straight away. Nobody helps them up or moves them, except to keep their airway clear, unless you are trained to do more. And their helmet stays on.

One person kneels beside them and keeps them still. Somebody else makes the call.

Here is the part people get wrong, and it isn't the first aid. It is the phoning. Waving at your bench is a rink habit. It tells your own teammates that something has happened. It does not bring an ambulance. In a rec game there may be nobody at the rink who is trained, so nobody arrives just because somebody waved. Make sure somebody has actually phoned.

And don't reach down to help them up. That is the instinct. With a neck injury it is the one thing most likely to make it worse.

Even when a player gets up after a knock to the head and says they're fine, they are done for the day. Off the ice, no return that day, and a medical professional decides when they play again. That holds even if they feel fine, and even if they were never knocked out. And someone stays with them afterwards. They don't drive, and they don't go home on their own. If they get worse, get medical help straight away. If their neck hurts, phone for an ambulance, even if they got up.

Let me pull those four ideas together, because they are the core of being a good teammate.

One. When a teammate leaves their spot to go after the puck, fill it. When somebody has filled yours, get back to it. That exchange is what trust looks like on the ice.

Two. The bench is a working position. Know the game before you go. Name the player you are replacing, out loud. Step on only once they are at the bench and out of the play. And never change while they have the puck in your end.

Three. Never retaliate, never leave the bench during a fight, and stay in the penalty box until an official lets you out.

Four. When someone is down, treat a head knock as a possible neck injury. Somebody phones for an ambulance. Nobody moves them except to clear their airway, and their helmet stays on. After any knock to the head, they are done for the day, a medical professional decides when they play again, and they don't drive or go home alone.

Everything else in this episode adds detail to those four. So now let's go back through, starting on the bench.

Fifth idea. The change, in more detail.

Don't stay out because you don't trust the next line to hold on. Players who do that stay out an extra twenty seconds, and arrive at the bench exhausted. Then they are still tired when they go back over the boards. So change while you still have the legs to get to the bench.

Now a situation that catches tired lines. Your line is worn out and pinned in its own end. The tempting thing is to fire the puck the length of the ice and get off.

That is called icing. It means shooting the puck from your own half all the way past the other team's goal line, with nobody touching it. Play stops, and the faceoff comes back to your end.

Here is why that is a trap when you are tired. In many leagues, including British league hockey, a team that ices it can't change. So the same tired five stay out, for a faceoff in their own end. An injured player can still be replaced. Some leagues don't have this rule, so find out which way yours plays. Either way, the better habit is to get the puck out under control. Use a pass, or a chip off the boards to a teammate.

Next, the extra player late in a close game. Never send an extra player over the boards on purpose to break up a rush. That can be a penalty shot against you, in every league, which is a free shot alone on your goalie. An honest mistake with a change late in the game is still the ordinary two minutes. It is the deliberate one that gets punished that hard.

Then the goalie change. Sometimes your team pulls its goalie for an extra attacker. The skater going on waits until the goalie is almost at the bench. If the skater goes a little early, it is only a stoppage and a faceoff, provided the goalie keeps going. So, goalie, once you are heading off, keep going. If you turn back to play the puck after the skater is on, that can be too many players on the ice.

Sixth idea. Working the bench.

Sit in line order, next to your linemates. Here is why. Your line goes over the boards from seats side by side, so you arrive at the play together instead of one at a time. You can talk to the people you play with between shifts. And the coach can speak to one line without shouting down the whole bench. If you don't know where to sit, ask. Sitting in someone's seat is a small thing. Being in the wrong place when your line changes is not.

When your line is off, watch your own linemates' shifts from the bench. You are learning their habits for free, and their habits are what you have to adapt to.

Benches have two doors. Most teams use one as the on door and one as the off door, but which is which varies by rink and by team. Find out at your first game. Don't guess. And not every rink's doors are built to swing inward, so in a rink you didn't build, look before you lean on one.

Whoever is nearest the door works it. In most rec leagues nobody else will. So if that is you, watch the ice, not your phone. Hold it open for the player coming off, then close it, because an open door during play is a hazard. Don't stand in the gateway getting ready while someone is trying to come off. And keep your stick off the floor by the door, or somebody will step on it and snap it.

Don't sit on the boards with your skates hanging over the ice. Coaches warn that an exposed blade can end up near someone's face. And in some leagues it is a penalty, after one warning to your team. Sit on the bench with your skates inside.

And don't stand up and reach onto the ice. Playing the puck from the bench is a penalty.

Seventh idea. Changes at a whistle, and what last change means.

Changes at a stoppage work differently from changes on the fly. At a whistle, the visiting team puts its line out first. Then the home team answers.

That order is what people mean by last change. Why does it matter? Because it lets the home coach see your line and then pick theirs to match. The home team does lose it after an icing it committed itself.

Most rec leagues don't run this formally at all. If yours doesn't, your only way to match lines is the change on the fly. So ask how your games do it.

Let me pull the bench together. Know the game before you go, and sit in line order. Change while you have legs, never while they have the puck in your end, and when you are pinned in, get it out under control rather than icing it. Never send an extra player on to break up a rush. Learn which door is which, work it if you are nearest, and keep your skates inside the boards.

Eighth idea. Talk.

On the ice, call early. Man on means an opponent is closing on a teammate who can't see them. Call it in the words your team has agreed, even when you are not sure. Here is why. A wrong call costs less than no call. A call takes a small bit of courage, because you might be wrong. And a bench that mocks a wrong call is how a team goes quiet. Then the goals that follow are the ones nobody called.

To make calls, you need names. Learn them fast. Hey doesn't tell anyone who you mean.

On the bench, between shifts, you have the best chance in hockey to talk. You are standing still, the whistle isn't blowing, and the other team can't hear you. Use ten seconds to tell a linemate one specific thing you will do. On the breakout I'm coming low, not to the wall.

Never say anything that judges them. You need to be sharper is not information.

Ninth idea. Adapt to your linemates. They will not adapt to you.

Every player has a small set of things they reliably do. Your job is to learn your linemates' set. Here is what is worth knowing, and why each one changes what you do.

Where do they want the puck? Out in front, or in their feet? A player who takes everything on their forehand needs the puck led differently from one who catches it in their feet and protects it.

Do they hold it, or move it on fast? A holder needs you to get open twice. A mover needs you to be moving already.

Do they go to the net, or stop at the top of the circle? That changes your pass from the corner. Hard around the boards for one. Soft to the front of the net for the other.

How fast do they really skate? Not how fast they think they skate. A pass into space is only a good pass if they get there.

Which way do they turn under pressure? Almost everyone has a favourite way out.

And on the backcheck, do they take the middle or the player they are covering? That decides what you have to take.

Then pass to the player who exists, not the one you wish you had. Here is why the adaptation runs from you to them. You can't change a linemate's game in the middle of a season. You can change what you give them tomorrow. If you keep explaining that they should have been somewhere else, you have picked the half of the problem you can't fix.

Tenth idea. Be the same yourself, every shift.

A predictable teammate is worth more than a brilliant unpredictable one. Here is why. If passing is a prediction, then surprises make it harder.

Picture the breakout again. A winger who is in the same place every time lets the defenceman pass early, under pressure, without looking. A winger who is somewhere different every time makes the defenceman look up first. And looking up first is how the defenceman gets hit and turns the puck over.

So decide what you do, and then do it every time. If you support the boards on the breakout, always support the boards. If you change your mind, say so on the bench first.

Play your team's system too, even when you think another one would be better. You can disagree. You still play it. A player who plays a system they dislike properly gets listened to later. A player who goes their own way doesn't.

Eleventh idea. Chemistry, when a line or a defence pair just works.

It sounds mysterious, but it comes down to two things.

The first is habits that fit together. A centre who carries the puck and a winger who gets open beat two of either. A defenceman who likes to join the attack and one who is happy to stay back cover both jobs. Two who both want to join the rush leave the blue line empty.

The second is shared assumptions. Say the puck goes into the far corner of the other team's end. All three forwards already know who goes in first, who supports, and who stays high, holding the top of the zone behind the play. Coaches call those roles F one, F two and F three. They are set by the order you arrive, not by your position. A line with chemistry sorts them out without anyone saying a word. And whoever goes in first is going for the puck, not the player. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call. Never from behind, and if you can see a player's numbers, whether they have the puck or are just going for it, pull up. Head up, chin off your chest, never turn your back to the other player and never duck, even going slowly.

It is mostly not friendship. Plenty of good lines don't socialise, and plenty of best friends can't play together.

So where does chemistry come from? Mostly from repetition. Chemistry is learned habits, and you learn habits by watching. That is why coaches keep lines together through a bad patch, and why constant line juggling has a cost. It also comes from talking about the situations that keep coming up, like the breakout or who takes the front of the net, rather than about hockey in general.

Some pairings don't work. Three players who all want to carry the puck. One who wants to play fast up the ice beside one who wants to slow it down. They spend the whole shift half a second out of step. Or one who forces risky plays through the middle beside one trying to play it safe. Both end up angry.

If a pairing isn't working, that is information, not a verdict on anyone. Say it to the coach that way.

Let me pull playing with other people together. Call early, in your team's words, and use names. Tell a linemate one specific thing you'll do. Learn their habits and adapt to them, because they won't adapt to you. Be in the same place every time, and play the system your coach asks for. And keep the exchange going. Fill their spot, and get back to yours.

Twelfth idea. When a teammate is struggling.

Start with the goalie who just let in a soft one. Every goalie knows exactly how bad it was without being told.

So say something short and move on. Next one. A tap on the pads. That's it.

Then help with your play. Clear the puck, win the next faceoff, and play a simple shift. The best thing you can give a goalie is a stretch of boring, organised hockey where nothing comes from the slot.

And tell your goalie what you are doing in front of them. I've got the net front. Shot coming, I'm screening, going left. Here is why. That information gives them back a sense of control.

What makes it worse? Going over the goal with them, even kindly. Especially kindly. Suddenly packing five players around the crease, the goalie's painted area in front of the net. That tells your goalie you don't trust them, and it gives up exactly the screened and deflected shots that make another soft goal. And a bench that goes silent after the goal, because silence reads as blame.

Now a skater having a bad night. Keep giving them the puck. Cutting them out of the play is the most common reaction and the most damaging one. It confirms what they already fear, and it makes sure the night stays bad.

Give them an easy pass first, one they can't miss. Confidence comes back from simple things done right, not from a hard chance to make up for it. Praise the effort, like a good backcheck or a good stick, not the result. Praise for the result after a bad night sounds like pity. And in a rec league, say something that lowers the stakes. It's Tuesday, mate.

What makes it worse is telling them to just relax. Or coaching them in front of everyone from the bench. Or going over the turnover again. And worst of all, rolling your eyes where they can see.

Thirteenth idea. Keep your frustration off your face.

At some point you will be furious with a teammate in the middle of a shift. The feeling isn't the mistake. Showing it is. And the reason isn't manners. It is that showing it loses games.

Throwing your head back, slapping your stick on the ice, or pointing at where the pass should have gone does four bad things.

It tells the other team where your weak spot is. A bench that sees your defence pair arguing will go after that pair.

It brings down your own bench. Everyone saw it, and the next player to make a mistake knows what's coming.

It takes you out of the play. The two seconds you spend showing it are two seconds you are not backchecking, and that is often where the goal comes from.

And it makes your teammate worse. A player who is worried about your reaction plays slower, and gets rid of the puck sooner.

So react to your own mistakes visibly at most, and to a teammate's never. Skate straight back into the play.

If it still matters after the game, deal with it then, once you have both calmed down. Not in the car park at full volume, and better not in a full dressing room. Talk about one specific play, once. Talk about what you will do differently, because that is the half you control. Most of these arguments are two players assuming different things, and saying the assumption out loud fixes it. If you can't sort it out between you, take it quietly to the coach or the captain, as a problem to solve, not a complaint.

What you must never do is retaliate on the ice, or stop passing to a teammate you're annoyed with. Everyone can see both. And both turn a private annoyance into a team problem.

Fourteenth idea. The referee.

What to do is short. During play, say nothing. At a stoppage, let your captain ask one calm question.

Here is why. Arguing has essentially never reversed a call. A referee can't take back a penalty because a player protested. What arguing does reliably is get you a second penalty, and push the next close call the other way.

Now how most teams handle it. It is close to a system. The captain wears a C on their jersey, and the alternate captains wear an A. Having a letter means you are one of them. Only the captain may raise a question about the rules with the referee, and in some leagues only when the referee invites it. An alternate captain does it when the captain isn't on the ice. So if you think a call was wrong and you don't have a letter, tell your captain and let them handle it. Then go and win the next faceoff.

Why have one voice? So nobody else has to do it, and nobody else takes a penalty for trying. That is a big part of what the letter is for.

Not every league works that way. In some, the team names one player who talks to the referee, whoever that is. And plenty of rec teams play with no letters at all. So find out, before the game, who on your team does the talking.

When the captain asks, the way they ask matters. Ask a question, don't make a statement. Asking what the referee had on that one gets you an answer. Saying it was a terrible call gets you two minutes. Stay calm and quiet, because referees react to your tone before your words. And ask once. Keeping on is exactly what the bigger penalties are there to punish.

Here is the cost, kept short. In most leagues, just disputing a call is a two-minute penalty. A complaint about a penalty is not a question about the rules, and in most leagues it draws a penalty of its own. And coming off the bench to argue can be a penalty in its own right, so stay on the bench.

Don't count on a warning first, either. Some things skip straight to the top. Never put a hand on an official. Never spit at one, swear at one, or make an obscene gesture. Never throw anything toward one. And never leave the penalty box to argue. Any of those can end your game with no warning, and putting a hand on an official or spitting can mean a ban for games after this one, too.

And keep your stick off the boards. Tapping the boards from the bench is the bench's applause, for a goal or a good shift, so keep it a tap. Never bang the boards or the glass at a call, or to cheer a hit. That can be a penalty too.

The one most likely to catch you comes just after you've been called. When an official tells you to go to the penalty box, go straight there. And stay out of the referee's crease, the marked area where the referee stands to report the call. If you get either wrong, it can add a misconduct penalty on top, with no warning.

Let me pull keeping your head together. After a soft goal, say next one and play a simple shift. Keep giving a struggling teammate the puck, and an easy one first. Keep your frustration off your face, and sort it out after the game, one play, once. Say nothing to the referee, and let your captain ask one calm question. Never touch, spit at or gesture at an official. And when you're told to go to the box, go straight there.

Fifteenth idea. The handshake line, after the game.

Whether it happens at all is a custom, not a rule. A referee can call it off after a rough game, to keep the two teams apart, not to punish them.

How you go through it is what matters, and it is simple. Helmet on and done up properly. Gloves and stick left at the bench. Shake every hand, say good game, and keep moving. Say nothing else, and keep your hands to yourself.

Check your chin strap at the bench before you go. If more than one finger fits between your jaw and the strap, it isn't done up properly.

Why all the fuss about helmets? Because the game isn't over for the rules just because the buzzer went. In some leagues going down the line without a helmet is a penalty on its own. And if it goes up, a helmet coming off can get you thrown out of the game. That means taking your own off, pulling theirs off, or losing yours because it wasn't done up. So keep your hands off their head, which means their helmet, their cage and their chin strap. In some leagues those can also mean a ban for games after this one.

If it goes up in the line, back off, and let the officials in first. A fight there can get you thrown out, and can mean a ban for games after this one. And hold back however obvious it looks that somebody should pull them apart. Going in to break it up can get you thrown out in every league, even if you were only trying to make peace. In some leagues it is automatic for the first player in.

Then go where the referee sends you, and leave your stick and gloves alone. They go back to the bench, not onto the ice.

If you are watching from the bench, stay on it. Coming over the boards after the handshake costs the same as it would in the first period.

Say nothing on the way down the line. Abusive words after the game can get you thrown out, and hateful or discriminatory language can mean a ban for games after this one. A shove after the whistle can be a penalty too, and a reckless one can get you thrown out.

Then shake every hand, say good game, and keep moving. If you lost badly, or something happened in the game, that is exactly when doing it well matters.

Sixteenth idea. Your own goalie, in the warm-up and in drills.

In the warm-up, don't shoot at your own goalie's head. In a drill, don't fire pucks at them from ten feet. Aim at the pads, the glove and the blocker, and shoot from where the drill puts you.

Here is why it happens so often. The warm-up is where shooters are loosest and least aimed. And in a drill line, the distance quietly shrinks each time round.

And here is why it matters. No rule makes shooting at your own goalie an offence, and that is a reason to keep to it, not to relax it. Play only stops for a hurt goalie once an official judges that the goalie is hurt. A cage hides most of what they would judge it by. So a teammate who doesn't shoot there is the one protection that doesn't depend on somebody noticing.

And stay in your own end during the warm-up. Every league requires it. Shooting at the other team's goalie then is what usually starts a fight. It can cost you a penalty on the spot, or the league can decide afterwards whether you play next week.

Seventeenth idea. Their goalie, and their crease.

After a whistle, don't skate through their crease, don't stand in it, and don't flip the puck at their net. Here is why. Goalies treat that space as theirs, and standing in it after a whistle is the standard way to start something. Flipping a puck at the net after the whistle can be a penalty as well.

During play, screening the goalie, standing in their line of sight, is normally fine. Do it from outside the crease, with your skates out of the blue paint, and without contact. Hold your spot without leaning or shoving, and never put your stick across anyone. And keep your head up, chin off your chest. The goal post can hurt you the same way the boards can.

Getting into the crease, or stopping the goalie moving in it, is what costs your team the goal. And never go into the goalie. Any contact with a goalie can be a penalty, and it can get you thrown out of the game. A goalie who is down or turned can't protect themselves, so contact can hurt them.

Now a moment people get wrong. Their goalie has come out of the crease to play the puck, and is skating back to the net. Get out of their lane, even if you never touch them. Moving into their path can be a penalty, and it can cost you the goal. In some leagues it can get you thrown out. And standing still doesn't automatically win you the call either. Just get out of the way.

Let me pull the customs together. In the handshake line, helmet on and done up, gloves and stick at the bench, hands off their head, and nothing but good game. If it goes up, back off and let the officials in. Shoot at your own goalie's pads, glove and blocker, never the head. Stay in your own end in the warm-up. After a whistle, stay out of their crease. Never into the goalie. And get out of the way of their goalie going back to the net.

Eighteenth idea. Rec and beer league hockey, where most adults play.

A single bench can hold a former junior player, someone who learned to skate at thirty-five, and everyone in between.

Adjust your pass to the person receiving it. A hard, flat pass to someone who can't take it isn't a good pass. It is a turnover you caused.

Cover more ice than your job if you can. If you are the strongest skater on your line, you are the one who backchecks. Backcheck for the puck, not the player. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call. Never hit a player who can't see you coming, never from behind, and if you can see their numbers, pull up.

And don't coach your teammates from the bench. Nobody paid league fees to be taught by a teammate. If someone asks, help. Otherwise, tell them what you will do, not what they should.

There is one sentence that sums up most of rec hockey. Everyone has work in the morning.

A late game isn't worth an injury. Nobody is getting a contract. So no retaliation, because retaliation is what makes things worse, every time. Never slash. Never shove anyone with both hands on your stick, and keeping your blade on the ice doesn't make it legal. Sticks down, blades on the ice, and no follow-through above the waist.

Let the referee referee. They are often a teenager or a volunteer, on their fourth game of the night.

And be somebody people are pleased to see on the ice. Rec teams fill their rosters by word of mouth, so that is how you get invited back.

Nineteenth idea. Find out whether you may body check at all, and what non-check really means.

Start with the question. Whether body checking is allowed depends on which category your league plays in. Your age alone doesn't answer it. And in some leagues it can change from one game to the next, depending on who you are playing. So ask your league, and get the answer in writing.

In a non-check game, a body check is a penalty however gently it lands. If the referee judges it reckless, it can get you thrown out, and it can mean a ban for games after this one.

Now the part that causes most of the arguments. Non-check does not mean no contact. Some contact is part of the game everywhere. You may hold the ice you already have, meaning ice you got to first, not ice you step into their path to take. And you may angle a player off. Angling means steering a player with your skating route, not your body. Pick your line early, keep it, and add nothing when you arrive.

So here is what you never do in a non-check game. Never make contact without a try for the puck. Never step or glide into a player. Never throw a hip, a shoulder, a forearm or your body into them. Never use the boards to push or pin them, even while you are going for the puck. No shove, and never your stick across anyone. Never into anyone's back. If you can see their numbers, pull up. And remember that even going slowly, a crash into the boards can hurt. And finishing the check, hitting someone to punish them rather than to win the puck, has no place in a non-check game.

Leaning into each other while you both play the puck is where leagues differ. Some allow it. Some don't. In some Canadian adult rec leagues, called CARHA leagues, you never use your body on anyone. There, don't lean, and don't plant yourself in a player's path. Get out of the way of contact you can avoid. And when your team has the puck, don't skate into a defender's way to free up your carrier. And in British women's league hockey, the WNIHL, play it as no body checking at all. So unless you know your league allows leaning, don't lean.

Whatever your league, there is one custom on top of the rules. How physical a battle gets is set by the least willing player in it, not the most.

Twentieth idea. When the game is badly one-sided.

Say you are playing a much weaker team. There is no rule against scoring, though many local leagues have a mercy rule, so check yours. The custom, once the game is out of hand, is to stop trying to add to the margin.

Here is how. Roll all your lines evenly, and give the least-used players more shifts. Stop forechecking hard in their end, and play in the neutral zone instead, the middle of the ice between the blue lines. Work on things, like completing passes and cycling the puck, rather than hunting chances. Don't celebrate goals showily. And don't pull anyone's stick, and don't hit anyone you don't need to.

What you must not do is visibly ease off. Why not? Because that insults the other team more than a goal does. The point isn't to play badly. It is to stop chasing a bigger score.

Now the other way round. Say you are the much weaker team. Keep it simple. Keep your shifts short, and take away the middle. Trying to trade rushes with a much better team is how a close game turns into a rout.

And whichever side of the mismatch you are on, keep your stick down, and remember that whether you may hit at all is your league's call. A mismatch is exactly when injuries happen.

Let me pull rec hockey together. Adjust your pass to the person receiving it. If you are the strongest skater, you backcheck, for the puck, not the player. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call, and if you see their numbers, pull up. Never slash, and never shove with both hands on your stick. Find out whether your game is checking or non-check, and get it in writing. In a non-check game, hold your ice, angle with your skating, and never step into anyone. And in a one-sided game, stop adding to the margin without visibly easing off.

Twenty-first idea. Being new to a team.

Being reliable matters more than skill, and it isn't close. Your skill decides which line you play on. Whether people can count on you decides whether you are on the team next season.

So turn up on time, every time. In hockey, on time means dressed and ready before the stated time, not arriving at it. That is custom, not a rule. But the ice is paid for, and it starts on the minute. If you will be late or absent, tell someone. Answer the team's messages quickly. And pay your fees before you are asked.

In the dressing room, say hello when you walk in. One sentence. Listen more than you talk for the first few weeks, because they are for finding out how this team does things. Don't criticise anything, not the systems, not the lines, not the goalie. Don't compare it to your old team. Ever. And leave the goalies alone in the last stretch before the warm-up. Many of them want that time to get their head right.

In your first week, ask what the systems are. What forecheck, what coverage in your own end, and who calls the breakout. Then play them, even if you think a different one is better. Ask which bench door is on and which is off, and where you sit.

Then play simple for the first few games. Complete your passes, and finish your shifts. A team forms its first impression of you fast, and safe is a better first impression than flashy.

Say yes to the drink after the game, at least sometimes. Half of a team's shared assumptions get built there.

And don't apologise for every mistake. Make the mistake, reset, next shift.

Twenty-second idea. Leading, with or without a letter.

The captain's real job is mostly off the scoresheet. They set the standard for what a normal shift looks like. Not the best shift, the normal one, because teams match their captain's floor, not their ceiling. They are the link to the coach. They handle the officials, so nobody else has to. And they notice who isn't talking.

But most leadership on a team has no letter at all. Think about who really shapes a room. The player who is always first dressed and last off the ice. The one who talks on the ice. The one who welcomes new people. The one who doesn't sulk after a bad shift, because a mood spreads both ways. And the one who does the unglamorous jobs without being asked, and never mentions it. Taking the faceoff in their own end. Killing the last minute of a penalty.

None of that needs skill, or being senior, or permission. If you want to lead, be reliable where everyone can see it.

Twenty-third idea. What coaches notice, and how to ask them things.

Coaches, in their experience, watch effort and positioning far more than skill. That is coaching opinion, not a measured fact. Here is the reasoning. Skill hardly changes within a season, and a coach can see it in the first ten minutes. Effort and position are what a coach can change, so that is what they watch for.

So they notice whether you backcheck all the way, or coast in at the top of the circles. Backcheck for the puck, not the player. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call, and if you can see their numbers, pull up. They notice where you are when the puck is on the other side of the ice, because that shows whether you understand the system. They notice how long your shifts are. A player who changes at forty seconds is easier to use than a better one who stays out for ninety. They notice whether your head is up on the bench. And whether a bad shift changes your next one.

If you want feedback, ask at a good time. After practice, not during a game, and not just after a loss.

Ask a narrow question. How am I doing gets you a yeah, good. Ask about a specific shift instead. Third period, the shift where they scored, where should I have been? Ask what would get you more ice time. Then do it, and don't mention that you're doing it.

When you ask about systems, ask what and when, not why not something else. Asking whether the coach wants you to chip it or hold it when their defenceman steps up is a question. Asking whether a different system would work better is a challenge, whether you meant it as one or not.

And accept the answer. You can disagree, and you still play the system.

Let me pull it all together.

When a teammate leaves their spot to go after the puck, fill it, and get back to yours when somebody has filled it. That is what trust looks like on the ice.

Treat the bench as a working position. Know the game, name the player you are replacing out loud, and step on only once they are at the bench and out of the play. Change while you have legs, never while they have the puck in your end, and don't ice it to get off.

Talk early, in your team's words. Learn your linemates' habits, adapt to them, and be in the same place every time.

Keep giving a struggling teammate the puck, and keep your frustration off your face. Never retaliate, never leave the bench during a fight, and stay in the box until an official lets you out. Say nothing to the referee, and never touch one.

In the handshake line, helmet on and done up, hands off their head, and back off if it goes up. Shoot at your own goalie's pads, never the head, and never go into theirs. In a non-check game, play the puck, never step into anyone, and find out whether your league allows leaning before you lean.

And when someone is down, treat a head knock as a possible neck injury. Somebody phones for an ambulance. Nobody moves them except to clear their airway, and their helmet stays on. After any knock to the head, they are done for the day, a medical professional decides when they play again, and they don't drive or go home alone. If they get worse, get medical help straight away. If their neck hurts, phone for an ambulance, even if they got up.

Much of this depends on what your team does. So here are three questions to ask your coach. Which system do we play, and which spot do I fill when a teammate goes? Who on our team talks to the referee? And can we agree, out loud, before the season, that nobody leaves the bench?

And one for your league, because only they can answer it. Is our game checking or non-check, and is leaning allowed?

Go and ask. Their answers tell you how your team wants you to play together, and now you know why each answer makes sense.

### 7. Departures from the source and the template

**From the source:**
- **Additional source BC** for every limb in §6 marked BC. The source points to BC for the full on-ice response (:343) and for contact pricing.
- **"No slashing back, no crosscheck in the back" (:564)** becomes "Never slash. Never shove anyone with both hands on your stick, and keeping your blade on the ice doesn't make it legal." The source wording narrows the ban to a cross-check *in the back*, which is the brief item 2 and 31 trap. The new wording is stricter.
- **The USA Hockey leaning permission (:547, :553)** becomes "unless you know your league allows leaning, don't lean". Stricter.
- **The pinch, "who goes in first" and the backcheck** carry gates the source does not attach there (brief 13, 17, 22, 26).
- **Goalie contact** carries "can get you thrown out" and the "can't protect themselves" reason (brief 23, 24). The source says only that contact costs the goal (:313).
- **The injured-player unit** says phone "straight away" for any possible head or neck injury, which follows the source (:28, :40). It does **not** use brief item 21's "phone if anything looks serious, like…" list: that would gate the phone call on symptoms, which is weaker than the source. "A fit" is not voiced, because neither source carries it.
- **"Referee's crease, the marked area where the referee stands to report the call"** is inferred from :405. The source gives no definition.
- **The slot and icing definitions** are not written in the source (§6).
- **"Only the captain may raise a question about the rules… in some leagues only when invited"** compresses NHL 6.1 against the IIHF's no-invitation rule (:381, :391).

**From the template:**
- The parts are not rink ends. They are the bench, playing with other people, keeping your head, the customs, rec hockey, and new to a team.
- There are 23 ideas, not 18. Each one is short, so this is about pacing, not more content.
- There are seven recaps, not five.
- The close asks three questions for the coach, plus one for the league, as the Winger pilot does.
- **Four beats** are used for three things: the coverage rotation (what, how, why, and "yours may differ, find out"), last change (what, how, why, and the rec-league alternative) and the captain-only rule (what, how, why, and the USA Hockey and no-letters alternative).
