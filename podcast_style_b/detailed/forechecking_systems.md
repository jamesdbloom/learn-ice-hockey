===== SCRIPT START =====

This episode is about forechecking. We will build the job up one piece at a time, and for each piece I will tell you what to do and also why it works. First, a few words you will hear all the way through.

Forechecking. This is the pressure your team puts on the other team in their own end, while they have the puck and you don't. It is the opposite of backchecking, where you race back to defend your own net.

The breakout. This is the other team's planned way of getting the puck out of their end. Your forecheck is there to break it.

High and low. High means toward the blue line you are facing, away from the net. Low means toward the goal line, the line the net sits on. So in their end, high is back toward the line you came in over.

Strong side and weak side. The strong side is the side of the ice the puck is on. The weak side is the other side. Both switch the moment the puck does.

The point. This is the ice just inside the blue line, where the defencemen stand. It is an area, not the line itself.

The neutral zone. This is the middle third of the rink, between the two blue lines.

A dump-in. This is when your team shoots the puck into their end on purpose, for a teammate to chase.

That is the vocabulary. More words will come later, one at a time.

A forecheck can win in only two ways, and everything in every system serves one of them.

The first win is taking the puck back deep in their end. Their forwards have already started up the ice, looking for a pass out, and can't get back in time. So a turnover down low is a scoring chance almost at once.

The second win is quieter. You never touch the puck in their end. You take away every good option until they chip it out blindly, and one of your players is standing where blind chips go. You pick it up in the neutral zone, moving forward with speed.

Every system you will hear about is a choice between those two wins. The passive systems aren't lazy. They have picked the second win, because it is more forgiving.

Pressure and risk are the same dial. There are five of you on the ice. Every player you send deep to hunt the puck is one fewer between the puck and your own goalie.

One chaser is easy to beat, but almost never costs you anything. Two chasers win a lot of pucks, and sometimes leave you three against two coming back the other way. Three forwards plus a defenceman win pucks all the time. When they fail, somebody is alone on your goalie.

That is why there is no single correct forecheck. A coach sets the dial by the score, the clock, how good their defencemen are with the puck, and how much your legs have left. Expect your team to have at least two forechecks. One is its normal one. The other is the one it switches to with a lead. So ask your coach which forecheck your team runs, and which one you switch to.

I will start with four big ideas, because they hold in every system. If you only listen to the first part of this episode, take those four with you.

First idea. F one, F two and F three are jobs, not people.

Coaches call the three forwards on a forecheck F one, F two and F three. The jobs are set by the order you arrive at the puck, and by nothing else. Whoever gets there first is F one. Whoever arrives second is F two. The one who is left is F three.

The puck won't wait for the right person to get there. If your left winger is closest when the puck goes into the corner, your left winger is F one, straight away, and your centre is F two. Some coaches give the centre F one as a shortcut for beginners. That is the usual case, not the rule.

Each job in one line. F one takes away one side of the ice. F two goes to the escape route F one has left open, not to the puck. F three stays high, and is the first player back if the puck gets out.

Say which one you are, out loud. I've got F one. I'm high. Saying it stops the most expensive mistake on a forecheck, two of you doing the same job while nobody does the other one.

Now the part that saves your legs. The jobs change in the middle of a shift. Say you are F one, and their defenceman passes the puck across to their partner. That is called a D-to-D pass. You have just been beaten, and you are behind the play.

So the forward closest to the new puck carrier becomes F one, and goes. You don't turn round and chase. You usually drop in as the new F three, high in the zone. Everyone else moves one job along.

Why not chase? If you chase every puck you touch, you are doing three players' jobs. You will be finished by your second shift. If you take the high job when you are beaten, you get a rest built into the system.

Second idea. Skate at the space you want to take away, not at the puck.

This is called angling. It means forcing the puck carrier to go where you want them to go.

Pick a side before you get there, and take it away. Then the only route the carrier has left is the one a teammate is already standing on. You make the carrier's next play predictable, and a teammate collects it.

Which side? The usual choice is to take away the middle and force the carrier toward the boards. Here is why. The boards are a wall that helps you, and in the middle the carrier can go anywhere. But that is a coaching choice. Some coaches flip it, and force the play into the middle, where three of your players are waiting.

What can't work is F one and F two disagreeing, because F two closes off the route F one leaves open. So find out which way your team angles before your first shift, not during it.

Come in on a curve, so your body is between the carrier and the middle of the ice. Your momentum should carry you along the wall, the same way the carrier is going, not across it. Why a curve? It keeps you able to react. A straight line commits you to one spot, and any change of direction beats you.

Your stick does the cheap half of the job. Stick out in front, blade on the ice, in the passing lane you are taking away. That pass now doesn't happen, and you gave up no position to stop it.

Aim the blade at the puck as you close, never a chop and never at the hands. Even if you never touch anyone, the carrier has to protect the puck and look at you. So they aren't looking for their pass.

The aim is to take away options, not to land a hit. A hit takes two players out of the play, the carrier and you. If you miss it, or fly past, you have taken yourself out and left your team a player short. Coaches agree that angling a player into one bad pass, with your F two already on it, wins the puck more often. That is coaching agreement, not a count.

So steer, don't finish. Pick your line early and hold it. Steer with your route and your stick, never your body. If you veer off your line at the last moment to make contact, that is a body check, however light. The same goes for an arm, a shoulder or a hip stuck out at the end of the angle. Whether you may body check at all is your league's call. And some leagues that allow a little contact for the puck still make any step or glide into a player a penalty. A hard one can get you thrown out. So hold your line, and let your stick do the work.

Keep to these everywhere. No shove, no lean, and never your stick across someone. Never into anyone's back, and if you can see their numbers, pull up. Never lift anyone off their feet. Even going slowly, a crash into the boards can hurt.

Where body checking isn't allowed, angling is your whole toolkit, and it is the real skill.

Go into a corner on an angle, not straight at the boards, whether your league allows checking or not. On an angle, a leg, a side or an arm takes most of the impact, not your helmet.

And when you get to the wall, take this position. Skates parallel to the boards. Forearm and hip to the boards. Never the point of your shoulder, and not the whole shoulder if you can help it. Head up, chin off your chest.

Never turn your back to the other player. And never duck. By the other player I mean whoever comes at you on the boards, whether they are after the puck or just coming at you.

Both of those protect your head and neck. If you turn your back, you can't see the hit coming or brace for it. If you duck, your head meets the boards first. That holds even going slowly, and in every league, checking or not.

If you are already facing the wall and can't turn, that is the last resort, not a choice. Get your stick and gloves up and spread wide on the glass, with your head off it.

From here on I will use one short version of that position, the same words every time, so you can learn it like a line. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the other player, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly.

Third idea. Get there early, then arrive under control. Get up to top speed on the way in. Early pressure is what rushes the carrier's decision. Then, in the last two or three strides, get your feet under you so you can change direction. Here is the test. Could you stop and turn right now? If not, you are going too fast. If you arrive flat out, you have one option, a straight line. Any good defenceman beats that with one turn, and now you are forty feet behind the play. That test keeps you out of the penalty box too. Building up speed into contact, even with two hard strides, can be charging. So can a long glide at someone, and so can leaving your feet. Charging can be a penalty in every league, and it can get you thrown out of the game. So arrive able to stop.

Fourth idea. Never finish into the back of a player who is facing the boards. And know that your own dump-in is what put them there.

Your team dumps the puck into their end. If you aim it well, their defenceman has to fetch it facing their own end boards, with their back to you.

But a player facing the wall, with someone closing on their back, is the exact moment the rules against hitting from behind and boarding exist for. Boarding means hitting or pushing a player dangerously into the boards. They can't see you coming, so they can't protect themselves.

So the responsibility is yours, as the player arriving. Take the puck or take the ice. Never finish into their back. If you can see the numbers on their back, pull up and play the puck.

And if they turn their back into you on purpose, don't count on that clearing you. Arrive as if it is all on you.

This is the forechecking hit most likely to end your game, so don't think of it as two minutes. In most leagues, even the lightest call for a hit from behind throws you out of the game. If you hit a player hard from behind while they stand at the boards, plan on being thrown out, in every league. And if you drive them into the boards so they can't protect themselves, in some leagues that is a match penalty, the most serious penalty there is.

And find out whether you may body check in this game at all. In a lot of hockey you can't, including younger age groups, girls' and women's hockey in many leagues, and non-check adult leagues. In at least one league it can even change from game to game. So ask before the game. Where checking is barred, you still forecheck, with your feet and your stick.

Let me pull those four ideas together, because they are the core of forechecking.

One. F one, F two and F three go by who gets there first. Say which one you are. When you are beaten, take the empty job instead of chasing.

Two. Skate at the space, not the puck, on a curve, with your stick in the lane. Steer, don't finish. Go into corners on an angle. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the other player, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly.

Three. Get there early, then arrive under control, able to stop and turn.

Four. Never finish into the back of a player facing the boards. Your own dump-in put them there. Take the puck or the ice, and if you see their numbers, pull up. And find out whether you may check in this game at all.

Everything else in this episode adds detail to one of those four. So now the systems.

Fifth idea. A system is a shape, plus a set of answers.

Forechecks are named by counting players out from their goal line. A two-one-two has two forwards deep, one in the middle, and two players high. The numbers always add up to five.

But a shape on its own isn't a system. What decides whether it works is what each of you does once their breakout starts. A well-drilled forecheck has a worked-out answer to every way they might try to get out. A badly drilled one has five players making up five different answers. If you learn only the shape, you will be standing in the right spot and still lose the puck.

Here is one answer every line needs, because it happens all the time. A wheel is when their defenceman skates the puck round the back of their own net to the other side, instead of passing it.

Say you are F one and they are going away from you. Don't follow them round the net. Chasing from behind means you arrive a stride late on the far side, with no angle left. The usual answer is to hand them on. F two, already reading it, meets the carrier coming out of the far corner. You hold the near side and take away the pass back the way they came.

Some teams do it differently, and have F one cut across in front of the net to meet the carrier at the far post. Either way, one of you owns the pass back, and one of you owns the wheel. Agree which on the bench, not on the ice. And whoever meets the carrier comes in on an angle that takes away their lane up the boards, rather than chasing from behind.

So the question for your coach isn't only what forecheck do we play. It is what do we do when they wheel it.

Sixth idea. The three jobs, in more detail.

F one makes the next play predictable. Whether F one also tries to win the puck is a coaching choice, and it is the biggest single difference between the systems. In the containing systems, F one steers and somebody else collects. In the aggressive ones, F one goes hard at the carrier to take the puck.

But hard means how early you get there, not what you do when you get there. You still come in on an angle, so F two knows where to go. Stick on the puck, not a hit. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call. And if you see their numbers, pull up.

Getting this backwards is expensive, because F two reads off F one. A steering F one with a sealing F two wins pucks. A hunting F one with a sealing F two leaves the middle wide open. So ask which one your team wants. And either way, if the carrier still has three good options when you arrive, nothing works.

Whatever F one's job, time the pressure for the moment they try to get out. One hand-tracked study found that pressuring the carrier right then took a forecheck from stopping a bit under half of breakouts to stopping nearly four in five. That is one tracker's count, so take the direction, not the exact numbers.

F two reads F one and seals. Sealing means arriving second and closing the escape route that F one has forced the carrier toward. So skate to where the puck is about to be forced, not to the puck. Take that pass away with your stick and your route. Never hit the player waiting for it.

And keep some distance from F one. If you arrive in the same square metre, one quick move beats you both.

F three is the insurance. Stay high, take away the middle, and be the first one back if the puck gets out. The first-one-back part holds in every system. The middle part doesn't. In a one-three-one, which is coming up, the middle usually belongs to a defenceman, and F three takes one of the walls.

F three is the most disciplined job, and the least rewarding. It is also the job that decides whether a failed forecheck costs you nothing or costs you a goal. So stay high until the puck is won or gone. If F three drifts low and joins in, a failed forecheck becomes a rush against.

Seventh idea. The full-ice press, the far end of the dial.

What it is. All three forwards hunt the puck, and both defencemen pinch on anything. Pinching means a defenceman stepping down from the other team's blue line along the boards to keep the puck in their end. There is no safety behind you at all. Coaches call it a full-ice press, or an all-out forecheck.

Why would a team do that? Because it is trying to keep the puck in their end at any price. One clean pass out is a breakaway, or an empty-net goal against. So teams use it almost only when they are behind late, down a goal in the last two or three minutes, or with their goalie pulled for an extra attacker. By then, giving up one more goal costs almost nothing.

Pinch on anything means the puck, not the player. Stick on the puck, not a hit. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call. And if you see their numbers, pull up.

There is one time a press is nearly free. That is a delayed penalty, when the referee has an arm up to call a penalty on them. Play doesn't stop until they actually control the puck. A deflection or a puck off a stick doesn't stop it. So keep pressing until the whistle really goes. The usual mistake here is not pressing hard enough.

The rest of the time, your team sits further back on the dial. So ask whether yours ever presses, and what the call is.

Eighth idea. The two-one-two, the aggressive standard.

What it is. Two forwards go deep. One stays high, at about the top of the circles. Both defencemen hold the other team's blue line. It is the forecheck most often taught first in North American youth and rec hockey. That is coaching agreement, not a count.

How it works. F one goes hard at the carrier and angles them to one side, usually toward the boards. Stick on the puck, not a hit. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call, and if you see their numbers, pull up. F two takes away the first pass, with the stick and the lane, never a hit on the player waiting for it. F three sits high in the middle, usually on the weak side, covering the pass across the ice.

There are two common versions. In the stacked version, F one and F two come from the same side, and F three shifts to the puck side too. All three overload one corner, and the far side is given up on purpose. In the spread version, F one and F two come from opposite sides, to cover both. Ask which yours runs.

Why a team plays it. It keeps you in their end for long spells. It forces turnovers close to their net. It wears down a defence pair over a whole game. And it pins a tired line in their own end so they can't change.

So coaches pick it when they are behind, or against a defence pair that is slow or panics. They pick it after a faceoff in the other team's end, and after a dump-in that gives your forwards a head start.

They also pick it straight after the other team ices the puck. Icing is shooting the puck from your own half all the way across the other goal line, untouched. In some leagues the team that ices it can't change lines afterwards, so their tired players are stuck out there. That is a mismatch to attack. In a lot of youth and rec hockey they can change. There, press after an icing because you like the shape, not because they're stuck. Ask which your league plays.

What it gives up. Everything behind it. If they get out cleanly, it is often three against two, or worse. And it is the most tiring system to run.

How it is beaten. A quick first pass before F two arrives. A reverse, which sends the puck back the way it came, into the space F one just left. And above all a rim, which sends the puck on round the boards, past your pinching defenceman. Coaches rank that last one as the most dangerous, because a missed pinch leaves two deep forwards out of the play. A defence pair that can make one clean pass under pressure beats a two-one-two.

The realistic alternative is a containing system, the one-two-two, which is coming up. If your team can't skate the two-one-two all game, that is usually the swap. Ask which yours plays.

Ninth idea. The two-three, and its best-known form, the left-wing lock.

What it is. Two forwards forecheck hard, and three players sit across the ice behind them. In the left-wing lock, the centre and the right winger forecheck as F one and F two. The left winger drops back to the half-wall. The half-wall is the stretch of boards about level with the faceoff dot, between the corner and the point.

How it works. If the puck isn't won, that left winger slides into line with the two defencemen, making a wall of three across the ice. So the left winger is really a third defenceman whenever the puck is going the other way.

Why a team plays it. You get two-player pressure, nearly as good as a two-one-two. But there are always three players between the puck and your net, so you are never outnumbered on the counter. It suits a team that wants pressure but can't afford to give up rushes, or one nursing a lead without going fully passive.

What it costs. It needs a left winger with real discipline, and the legs to get back every time. That player gets less of the attack. And with one fewer forward in their end, you cycle the puck less.

Hard means the same here as anywhere. Stick on the puck, not a hit. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call. And if you see their numbers, pull up.

Some teams lock whichever winger happens to be highest, rather than always the left one. Ask which yours plays.

Tenth idea. The one-two-two, the containing default.

What it is. One forward goes in on the puck. Two forwards sit across the middle of their zone. Two defencemen are high. Coaching material often calls it the most common base shape in hockey, though nobody has counted what teams really run.

How it works. F one pressures the carrier, stick on the puck, but doesn't try to win it. F one's job is to cut the ice in half. Force the play to one side, then stay on the inside so it can't come back across. F two sits on the wall on the puck's side and takes away the pass to their winger. F two is the one who actually makes the turnover, by jumping the pass F one has made predictable. F three roams the high middle and isn't tied to either side. The strong-side defenceman pinches on rims, to play the puck, and the weak-side defenceman stays home in the middle as the safety.

The clever part is the rotation. When the puck goes back across to the far side, F three slides over and becomes the new F two on that wall. The old F two takes the high middle, and F one covers the middle. The shape stays the same, and the people in it change.

Why a team plays it. You are never out of position. You let them pick the puck up, and build a wall they have to pass through instead. It is very good against teams that like to carry the puck out with speed, because there is no space to skate into.

And when it fails, your five players are already in the right shape for the neutral zone. F one becomes the lone pressure, F two and F three form the middle band, and the defencemen back off. Nobody has to sprint anywhere. That is the main reason teams use it as their base.

Coaches pick it to protect a lead, against a fast team, or when their forwards are tired. It is also the forgiving default in rec hockey.

What it gives up. Time. Their defencemen get the puck without pressure and choose their play. Against a pair who move the puck well, you will spend a lot of the night defending clean entries into your zone.

How it is beaten. Patience. Passes from one of their defencemen to the other shift your wall, and then a quick pass goes to a winger who has come underneath your F two. Or a long pass straight past your two defencemen, because there is nobody behind them.

Its more careful cousin is the one-one-three, where the third forward drops all the way back into line with the defencemen. And the more aggressive choice is the two-one-two. Ask which yours plays.

Eleventh idea. The one-three-one.

What it is. One forward pressures. Three players spread across the width of the ice in a line. One player sits behind them as the last line of defence.

How it works. The lone forechecker doesn't chase. They steer. They take away the middle and push the breakout to one side, then keep angling so the puck can't come back across. The line of three is usually two forwards and one defenceman, with the defenceman in the middle making the reads. It can't be three forwards, because F one is already a forward, and there are only three.

Some coaches put a forward in the middle of the line instead, and a defenceman on a wall. So ask which yours runs before you assume the middle isn't yours.

As the puck commits to one side, the whole line shifts with it. The player on the puck side becomes a second attacker straight away, stick on the puck, and the others cover behind. The single high player is the safety, and owns anything that gets behind the line.

Why a team plays it. It takes away passing lanes rather than players. Against a team that likes to get out with short passes, there is nowhere to put the puck. It also lets you set up rather than chase when you can't get pressure on in time, like during their line change. And it protects a lead.

What it gives up. The first touch of the puck, completely, and a lot of ice behind you if one pass beats the line. It also asks three players to skate sideways and read the play perfectly, all at once. It is not a beginner's system.

How it is beaten. Chip the puck past the flat line and beat it with speed, because anything behind it is behind all three at once. Bank a pass off the boards to someone already behind the line. Or move the puck side to side, because the line has to shift together, and gaps open as it shifts.

Coaches often drop the whole shape back and play it in the neutral zone instead. Ask which version yours plays.

Twelfth idea. The one-four, the full retreat.

What it is. One forward gives token pressure. The other four stand in a flat line across the ice, near their own blue line. So it is really a neutral-zone shape, not a forecheck in their end.

How it works. The lone forechecker steers and slows the exit, and doesn't really pressure. The four behind form a wall, and their only job is that nothing gets through it with speed. Nobody pinches. Nobody gambles.

Why a team plays it. It stops odd-man rushes and dangerous chances, and that is all. An odd-man rush is when the attackers outnumber the defenders coming back. The price is any real chance of winning the puck in their end, and so any offence.

So it is a tool for a moment, not a system for a game. A one-goal lead in the last two minutes. The last seconds of a period. The tail of a long shift with no legs left. Or a far better opponent. Nobody serious plays it all game.

How it is beaten. A hard chip past the flat line, and a race for it. Nobody in that line is deeper than anyone else, so it is a straight footrace. A long pass to a player already behind the wall doesn't work, because that player would be offside. Offside means an attacker crossing the blue line into the other team's end ahead of the puck.

Thirteenth idea. Choosing a forecheck by the game.

This is your coach's decision. But you should understand the logic, because you will be told to switch in the middle of a game, often with just a shout of back off.

Leading late, turn the dial down to a containing shape. One more goal is worth little, and giving one up costs a lot. Trailing late, turn it up, to a two-one-two and then the press. Now giving one up costs almost nothing. Tied early, play your team's normal system. There's no reason to change it.

Against a defence that panics, two-player pressure makes turnovers straight away. When your line is tired, go passive. A passive shape is far cheaper to skate, so survive to a whistle or a change. And straight after a long shift in your own end, don't chase. Get the puck deep and get off the ice.

There is one case where coaches genuinely disagree, and you will hear both answers. That is a defenceman who can really pass the puck.

The case for pressure is that a good passer needs time. If you take the time away, the skill never gets used. That suits fast, fit forwards, or a team chasing the game.

The case for containing is that pressure gives a really good passer what they want, a committed forechecker with space behind. One pass past your F one, and you are worse off than if you had never gone. That suits a team that can't be sure of getting there first, or is tired, or is protecting a lead.

The tiebreaker isn't the opponent. It is you. Pressure only beats a good passer if you actually arrive before the pass. Ask which your bench wants, because the two send F one on opposite routes.

Let me pull the systems together. A system is a shape plus answers, so ask what you do when they wheel it. F one makes the play predictable, F two seals the escape route with the stick, and F three stays high. The press and the two-one-two hunt the puck and risk what is behind them. The lock keeps three back. The one-two-two and one-three-one build a wall, and the one-four retreats. Stick on the puck, not a hit, and if you see their numbers, pull up. And know which system your team plays, and the one it switches to with a lead.

Fourteenth idea. A dump-in, a turnover and a set play behind their net are three different starts. Most players forecheck all three the same way. They shouldn't.

Off a dump-in, you control the clock. You chose when the puck went in, and you know where it is going. So you can get there early, at full speed on the way in. Then the same rule as always. Feet under you for the last two or three strides, able to stop, because the player you are arriving on has their back to you.

Leave before the puck does. Whoever will be F one should be speeding up as the dump is released, not after it. Players throw that head start away all the time by stopping to watch the puck.

Aim the dump at a person, not at an area. Put it where their defenceman has to fetch it facing their own end boards. Then remember what you have made. That player's back is to you. Take the puck or the ice, never their back. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call, and if you see their numbers, pull up.

You know where the puck is going. So F one can take the puck, and F two can go early to the passing lane instead of following into the corner. F three and the defencemen can be more aggressive too, because your team is organised and coming forward together.

There are several kinds of dump-in, and the timing of the chase changes with each one. So your line should agree which one is coming.

One more limit on a dump-in. On an obvious icing or offside, when the linesperson already has an arm up, take the puck or the ice and let them have the wall. In almost every league, contact that puts them into the boards there gets called as boarding.

Fifteenth idea. Where to aim the dump-in.

In top professional men's hockey and international hockey, there is a shape painted behind each net. It is called the trapezoid. It is narrow at the goal line and wider at the end boards. Their goalie may play the puck behind the goal line only inside it.

So dump into the corners, not straight behind the net. Behind the net is inside the trapezoid, so their goalie is allowed to play it there and will be waiting. A dump straight down the middle is a gift. The goalie stops it, sets it up for a defenceman, and your forecheck has done nothing.

A rim that dies in a corner is the best dump of all where there is a trapezoid. Their goalie may not play it there, so a defenceman has to skate over and fetch it, facing the boards. When you get there, go in on an angle, and take the puck or the ice, never their back.

A dump into the far corner, away from where their defencemen are set up, adds a second or two of travel for them. And watch the goalie's stick side. Many goalies are much better on their forehand, so a dump to their backhand side buys you time.

Many leagues have no trapezoid. Look for the lines behind the net, and ask. In Britain, in any competition, plan for their goalie to come out for the puck anyway.

Without a trapezoid, a goalie who handles the puck well becomes a third defenceman, and dump-and-chase loses most of its value. So change the plan. Carry the puck in. Or dump it where the goalie has to leave the net completely. Or forecheck the pass, not the corner, by putting F one on the defenceman waiting for the goalie's pass.

And don't forecheck expecting a whistle when their goalie falls on the puck out there. The harder you close on them, the more likely their freeze is allowed. And a freeze you think was illegal still stops play. If you skate on into that goalie, you are the one who gets penalised.

Sixteenth idea. Their goalie out of the net is someone to chase down, never someone to hit.

Chase the puck. Close the goalie down so they have to move it quickly. Put your stick in the lane they want to pass into. Then pull up before you arrive.

Here is why. A goalie who has turned to the boards for a rimmed puck can't see you, and can't protect themselves. A hit there can hurt them. It can be a penalty in every league. And charging into a goalie can get you thrown out of the game. In at least one league that is automatic, wherever on the ice it happens.

No league makes a goalie outside the crease fair game. The crease is the goalie's painted area in front of the net. Only one league's rules let you battle a goalie for a puck they hold in a corner, and only in a checking game. That is only to win the puck, never to hit them. In some leagues any deliberate contact with a goalie at all is a penalty. So the safe habit everywhere is simple. Take the pass away, and keep off the goalie.

Seventeenth idea. Off a turnover, you are recovering, not hunting.

When you lose the puck in their end, you are out of shape and they are not. Somebody is out of position, and at least one of you is skating the wrong way. For about two seconds, they have the numbers.

So the closest player pressures straight away, whoever that is. Stick on the puck, not a hit, and if you see their numbers, pull up. This is the clearest case of F one being a job, not a person. Two seconds of pressure buys the rest of your team time to sort themselves out.

Everyone else's first job is to find their place in the shape, not the puck. The instinct after a turnover is to chase and win it straight back. The right instinct is to look where your teammates are, and take the empty job. A second and third chaser after a turnover is how a lost puck becomes a goal against.

Don't pinch off a turnover, unless the puck is really dying on the wall. You don't have the shape behind you that a dump-in gives you. And accept the smaller win. Slow them down, and collect the puck in the neutral zone.

Eighteenth idea. When they set it up behind their net, your team chooses.

This is the third start, and the one nobody practises. Their defenceman simply has the puck stopped behind their own net, head up, with their forwards starting to move. It happens all the time, off line changes and whenever they win a race you thought you had.

Here your team picks one of three answers.

The first is the deep trap. All five of you back off and meet them at the blue line. You give them time, space and speed through the build-up, and take it all away in one place instead. The turnovers come at the line.

The second is to swing with them. A team breaking out with control usually sends one or two players swinging deep behind the net or through the corner, to build speed. So F one stays in to put off the pass, while F two and F three skate with the players swinging deep. Shadow them. Never hit them. If their defenceman keeps the puck and comes out, F one angles in, stick on the puck, and forces them to one side.

This kills their speed, but it opens the long pass, so your defencemen have to watch their high forward the whole time.

The third is the quick flush. Attack before they are organised, because a team that has just stopped behind its net hasn't decided who goes where yet. F one forces the carrier to one side, ideally onto their backhand. Once F one has committed, F two comes in on an angle and doubles it. F three and both defencemen drop off to take away the longer passes. This is the only one of the three aimed at winning the puck outright, and it commits the most players before the puck is won. On a flush, you are both arriving on a player facing their own end boards. So this is angling, not hitting. Stick on the puck, not a hit. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call. Never finish into their back, and if you see their numbers, pull up.

Which of the three is right is a coaching choice. It tends to follow the rest of your system. A team that runs a one-two-two is the likelier one to trap, and a team that runs a two-one-two the likelier one to flush, though nobody has counted that. Ask which yours does, because all three start with F one doing something different.

Let me pull the three starts together. Off a dump-in, you are hunting, so leave before the puck does and aim it at a person. Off a turnover, you are recovering, so the closest player pressures and everyone else finds their place. Off a set play behind their net, you are choosing, trap, swing or flush. Where there is a trapezoid, dump into the corners. Where there isn't, plan for the goalie to come out. And chase their goalie down, but never hit them.

Nineteenth idea. Defencemen, you decide whether a failed forecheck costs anything.

Forwards get most of the attention on a forecheck. But if you hold the zone, a failed forecheck just becomes a second forecheck. If you get it wrong, it becomes two against one going the other way.

Your normal spot is at or just inside their blue line, with your feet moving, on your toes. Standing still is the mistake. If you are standing still when a rim comes round, you can't reach it, and you can't get back either. Small steps forward and back keep you ready for both.

If you keep one skate touching the line, blade on the ice, you are onside in every league. That is why a defenceman can keep one heel on the line and still reach a long way into the zone.

Now when to pinch. Pinch when you can arrive on contact. That means you and the puck reach the wall at the same moment, so you can play it. The coaching test is simple. If you can't arrive on contact, don't go. Play the puck, not a hit. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call, and if you see their numbers, pull up. If nobody is contesting the puck, you need to get there clearly first, not probably first. Coming second there just means you turned your back on the play.

Pinch when the puck is on the wall and slowing down, not zipping past you. And pinch only when F three is high and ready to come back and cover the point you are leaving. That swap is what makes pinching safe. You go down, F three comes back. If F three is in the corner, the swap isn't there.

Don't pinch when you would be reaching for it, or when you would have to turn your back to the play. Don't pinch when they already have speed coming through the middle, or when you are protecting a lead late. And don't pinch when you have already been beaten once on that shift. The second gamble is how one mistake becomes two.

Twentieth idea. Only one defenceman pinches at a time.

What it is. At five on five, in every forecheck we have talked about, the usual approach is that only one of you goes. It is a very widely taught coaching choice, not a law of hockey. If nobody has told you otherwise, play on it.

How it works. The weak-side defenceman, the one away from the puck, holds the middle of the ice and doesn't join in. So look for your partner before you go. If your partner is already down the wall, you are the safety.

Why. The defenceman who stays home is the reason a failed pinch is two against one, and not a breakaway. With both of you down, there is nobody in the middle at all.

Both of you go only when the bench calls it. That means a full-ice press, a delayed penalty against them, or with your own goalie pulled. Every one of those is a decision to accept a breakaway as the price.

The realistic alternative. Some teams run a five-player rotation, or give both defencemen a standing green light in their end. Then whichever forward is highest covers the line. That is a real system, not a mistake. So ask which yours is, before you decide your partner is out of position.

And ask who covers the point you leave when you pinch. Usually F three comes back to it. If nobody does, a beaten pinch turns into a rush against, with you below the puck and the line empty. Some teams slide the other defenceman across instead, and accept a hole on the far side. Some only pinch once F three has called that they are high. Ask which of the three your team runs.

Twenty-first idea. What you do when you get to the wall.

First, find out whether you may body check in this game at all. Where checking is barred, a pinch is a play on the puck, not a check.

Second, seal the wall, not the player. Get to the spot first, in front of the winger and moving their way, and play the puck. Hold your spot with your position. No shove, no lean, no arm round them, and never your stick across them. Never into anyone's back, and if you can see their numbers, pull up. Never lift anyone off their feet. Even going slowly, a crash into the boards can hurt.

An arm wrapped round a player to pin them on the boards is holding, whether your league allows checking or not. And keep your skates out of it. In at least one league, pushing off a player with your skate gets you thrown out of the game.

Third, never cut across into the winger. Stepping down off the line, you are moving sideways, and the winger often doesn't have the puck yet. Cutting across into them can be a penalty in every league, and it can climb to getting you thrown out. So get to the spot first, and play the puck.

Fourth, if you end up behind them, play the puck. Never into their back. Even a light push from behind into the boards can be a penalty.

Fifth, don't be late. Once the puck has gone, the window is narrow, and in at least one league there is no window at all. The practical test is the gap, not the clock. If the puck was already more than an arm and a stick length from you when it left, you were late. Pull up and take the lane instead. And deciding to hit after the puck has gone is late, whatever the gap. No league caps a late hit at two minutes.

Sixth, never pinch in to check a player who is already battling your teammate. Take the puck or the ice instead. In at least one league that hit is a penalty on its own, wherever it happens.

A pinch takes you to the wall, so the wall position is yours too, in every league, checking or not. Contact still happens in non-check hockey, and the boards are just as hard. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the other player, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly.

Let me pull the defencemen together. Feet moving on the line, one skate touching it. Pinch only when you can arrive on contact, with the puck slowing on the wall and F three high. One of you at a time, unless the bench calls it, and know who covers your point. Seal the wall, not the player. No arms, no skates, never cut across, never late, and never onto a teammate's battle. And at the wall, the same position. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the other player, never duck.

Twenty-second idea. Reading the forecheck that is coming at you.

You will spend as much time breaking a forecheck as running one, so learn to spot which one you face. When your defenceman touches the puck behind your net, look at three things.

First, count their forwards below the top of the circles. Two or more means an aggressive system, so expect contact now. One means containment, so expect space now and a wall later.

Second, watch their F one. Straight at the puck at speed means they want it. They have committed, so a reverse or a quick D-to-D pass beats them. Curving to one side and slowing down means they are steering you. They want you to go one way. Going that way walks into their trap, so change sides before you commit.

Third, watch their far-side defenceman. Standing inside their blue line with feet moving means they plan to pinch. A rim past them is your best play, with lots of ice behind. Backing off toward the red line means they are giving up the zone. You have time to make a proper play.

One more tell, one pass later. After a D-to-D pass, watch what happens. If a new forechecker goes straight at the defenceman who just got it, they are running an aggressive system that rotates, and D-to-D won't save you. If their whole shape just shifts across and keeps its distance, it is containment, and D-to-D is a free reset you can use again and again.

Now what to do about each. Against an aggressive forecheck, move the puck early, before the pressure arrives, not after. A reverse beats a forechecker who has over-committed. A rim beats one who has sealed you in. A rim or chip that gets past their point is worth more than a pretty pass. And wingers, come underneath the pressure to take the pass, rather than standing still on the wall.

Against a containing forecheck, stop trying to force it. Shift their wall with D-to-D passes and calm regroups. Then chip the puck past their line and race for it, or bank a pass off the boards to a teammate already behind it. The one thing that never works is skating into the middle of three players.

Breaking a forecheck takes you down the wall too. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the other player, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly. Protecting the puck never justifies breaking any of those.

Twenty-third idea, and it is short. At rec level, simpler is better.

If you play adult rec hockey, run the forecheck everybody can remember late on a weeknight.

A one-two-two is usually the right default there. That is a suggestion for your league, not a claim about what most teams play. It is forgiving, nobody has to sprint, and when it fails you just don't get the puck back. You don't give up a breakaway. If your team can keep up a two-one-two, run it.

If your line agrees only that one goes, one supports and one stays high, you have most of what a real system gives you.

The habit that matters most isn't a shape. It is that only one defenceman goes, unless the bench has called an all-out press. Coaches say both defencemen going at once is where most rec-league odd-man rushes come from. Nobody counts that. Take it as the habit worth drilling.

And be honest about fitness. An aggressive forecheck is a fitness decision as much as a tactical one. Run the shape your legs can hold all game, keep your shifts short, and save two-player pressure for when it pays. A two-one-two for ten minutes and nothing after is worse than a one-two-two for the whole game.

Let me pull it all together.

Pressure and risk are the same dial. Know where your team sets it, and what it switches to with a lead.

F one, F two and F three go by who gets there first, and you say which one you are. When you are beaten, take the empty job instead of chasing.

Skate at the space, not the puck, on a curve, with your stick in the lane. Steer, don't finish. Get there early, then arrive under control.

Never finish into the back of a player facing the boards. Your own dump-in put them there. Take the puck or the ice, and if you see their numbers, pull up. Find out whether you may check in this game at all.

Know your system's answers, not just its shape. Off a dump-in, hunt. Off a turnover, recover. Off a set play, choose. Dump into the corners where there is a trapezoid, plan for the goalie to come out where there isn't, and never hit the goalie.

Defencemen, pinch only when you can arrive on contact, with F three high, one of you at a time. Seal the wall, not the player.

And at the wall, every time. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the other player, never duck.

Much of this depends on what your team plays. So here are three questions for your coach, and one for your league. Which forecheck do we run, and which one do we switch to with a lead? Which way does F one angle, and what do we do when they wheel it? Who covers the point when a defenceman pinches? And for the league. Which rules do we play under, and can we body check?

Go and ask. Their answers tell you how your team wants you to forecheck, and now you know why each answer makes sense.

### 9. Where this script is weakest

- **It is 50.5 minutes.** The systems part (ideas 5 to 13) runs about 17 minutes; a listener may find it a catalogue. The cut list prices the 1-4 and the puck-mover split first.
