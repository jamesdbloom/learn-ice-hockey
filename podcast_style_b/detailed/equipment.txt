===== SCRIPT START =====

This episode is about your equipment, and what you actually do with it. It takes its time. For each piece I will tell you what to do, and also why it works. If you know why, you can still make the right call when the shop, the rink or the league does things a little differently.

Hockey kit does two jobs. It keeps you out of hospital. And it decides what your body is able to do on the ice. Most players get the first job roughly right and the second one badly wrong. We will cover both.

Before any instructions, a few words you will hear all the way through. I will explain them now, so they don't get in the way later.

Face protection. There are three kinds. A cage covers your whole face with metal bars. A full shield covers your whole face with clear plastic. A half visor is a clear shield over your eyes and nose, and nothing below them.

The neck guard. This is the collar you wear round your neck. It lowers the risk of a skate or stick blade cutting your throat. You may also hear it called a neck laceration protector, or a throat protector.

The chin strap and the chin cup. The chin strap holds your helmet on. If you wear a cage, the chin cup is the padded cup at the bottom of it, where your chin sits.

The sticker. Helmets and some other kit carry a certification sticker, sometimes called a mark. It shows the item passed a safety test. Which one you need depends on where you play.

And three penalty words, because kit mistakes cost penalties more often than you might expect. A minor is two minutes in the penalty box. A misconduct is ten minutes off the ice for you. A game misconduct means you are thrown out of the game.

That is the vocabulary. Now the job.

I will start with four big ideas, because getting them wrong can hurt you or send you off the ice. If you only listen to the first part of this episode, take those four with you. After that we will go through your kit from your head down to your feet, then your stick, then everything else, and add the detail.

First idea. Your helmet is your own, you buy it new, and you never play as though it makes you safe.

Never wear a second-hand helmet. Not one you bought used, not one you borrowed, and not one handed down from an older brother or sister. And a goalie mask counts as a helmet, so the same goes for a mask.

Here is why. The foam inside a helmet protects you by crushing. That is how it soaks up a hit. And crushed foam does not spring back. So a helmet that has taken a big hit can look perfect and still protect you less. You cannot see a squashed liner from the outside, or a hairline crack in the shell. And with someone else's helmet, you cannot know what it has hit before.

No rulebook actually writes this one. It is a firm caution, not a law of hockey. But nobody can read a helmet's history off the outside of it, and that is reason enough.

That includes the loaner helmets at a taster session. If you have your own, take it. If you don't have one yet, buy one first, and ask the people running the session before you go whether you can bring your own.

Replace any helmet that has taken a serious hit, even one that looks fine. A helmet maker's own manual says a badly hit helmet should be replaced straight away. Hockey Canada says the same of a helmet that is cracked, has loose fittings, or is missing bits of liner.

Then wear it, but don't play as though you are wearing armour. There is no concussion-proof helmet. A helmet only partly protects you against the twisting forces behind most concussions. That point comes from a shop's buying guide, not from concussion research.

And never go into a collision head down. USA Hockey's safety guide warns that no equipment can protect your spine in a collision taken head down. Your helmet doesn't change that. Head up, every time.

Second idea. Do it up, and strap it down. A helmet only protects you while it is fastened to your head. A pad only protects you while it stays where you put it.

Do the chin strap up every time, before you step on the ice. Leave no more than one finger's width between the strap and your chin. Here is why. Undone, a helmet is just a hat, and it can come off at the moment you get hit. If you wear a cage, the chin cup sits right against your chin. A cage that doesn't close to your chin lets the whole helmet shift when you are hit.

Officials check this. In most British competitions, once your team has had one warning, a loose strap costs you a ten-minute misconduct. That is true even if it is your first.

Strap every pad so it cannot move. An elbow pad that spins when you fall puts its hard cap on the inside of your arm, and the bare joint onto the ice. Strap it tight enough that you cannot twist it by hand.

And buy pads that fit, not a size up. It sounds safer to go bigger. It isn't. Pads that are too big slide out of the way when you are hit, so the pad ends up somewhere other than where the puck lands. They also rub against each other, get in the way of your stride, and leave the joints open.

One more check, and do it in your skating stance, not standing up in the shop. Bend your knees as if you were skating. Your pants should still overlap the top of your shin guards. Standing up, everything looks covered. The gap opens when you bend.

Third idea. In Britain, wear a neck guard every time you step on the ice. Games, warm-ups and training, adults included.

Across England, Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland, it is required for every player at every age. It is cheap, and it lowers the risk of a skate or stick blade cutting your throat.

This is the one that catches adults. You may read that adults don't have to wear one. That is true in parts of North America. Even there, USA Hockey strongly recommends one for adults. But that exemption is not true in Britain. An adult in Britain who goes by a North American rulebook will be sent off the ice.

If you forget it in a game, in most British competitions there is no warning. It is ten minutes straight away, and a second time you are thrown out of the game. And if an official tells you to fix it and you don't, that is a separate penalty that keeps climbing. The third time, you are thrown out.

Wear it at training as well. Some British rules say so outright. Others say nothing about training. Treat that as silence, not as permission. What is missing at training is the referee, not the reason for the guard.

And if you are a junior, keep it on at the bench too. In British junior competitions, the rule covers players who are not playing that night. They may sit on the bench only in a helmet with full face protection, a neck guard and upper-body protection. Otherwise they have to leave the bench. The women's national league says the same of its under-sixteens.

Fourth idea. When a piece of kit fails during play, let go of it and get off the ice. Don't bend down for it, and don't tidy it away.

Your stick breaks. Open your hand where you stand, and let it drop. You may play on without a stick. You may not play on carrying a broken one. In British hockey, skating with a broken stick anywhere on the ice is two minutes in the penalty box, even close to your own bench.

Then leave it on the ice. If you move it at all, push it along the ice to the side of the rink, clear of the play. The instinct is to tidy it away over the boards, and that is the expensive one. Every league penalises it. In British hockey, putting it over the boards gets you thrown out of the game.

Here is the trap. Carrying a broken stick and getting rid of one are two different rules. You can obey the first and break the second in one movement, by dropping it over the boards.

If your helmet or your neck guard comes off in play, go straight to the bench. Don't stop to pick it up, and don't put it back on. Losing the kit is not the penalty. Staying on the ice without it is. In most British competitions, playing on without either one costs you ten minutes. And a second misconduct of any kind in the same game means you are thrown out. So a loose chin strap earlier, plus this, ends your night.

Some North American leagues let a player put a helmet back on and play on. You may see it on television. British hockey does not, so don't copy it.

And keep a spare stick taped and standing at your bench. Then a break costs you a few seconds without a stick, not a whole shift with a borrowed stick that doesn't suit you.

Let me pull those four ideas together, because they matter most.

One. Your own helmet, bought new, and replaced after a serious hit. A goalie mask counts. Never go into a collision head down.

Two. Chin strap done up, one finger. Pads that fit, strapped so they cannot move. Check the overlaps in your skating stance.

Three. In Britain, the neck guard on every time you step on the ice, adults and training included.

Four. When kit fails, let go and get off the ice. Drop a broken stick where you stand, never over the boards. Helmet or neck guard off, straight to the bench.

Everything else in this episode adds detail to those four, or to how your kit lets you play. So let's go through it, starting at the top, with your head and face.

Fifth idea. Protect your face and your mouth, and find out what your league asks for before you buy.

Equipment rules change from league to league far more than the playing rules do. Face protection, neck guards, mouthguards and the sticker you need all differ. So the first step is always the same. Ask before you spend.

Now the three kinds of face protection, and what each is good at. A cage gives the most protection. It doesn't fog or scratch, and air gets through. Almost everyone stops noticing the bars within a few sessions. A full shield covers your whole face too, and you can see through it more clearly. But it can fog up, and it can scratch. A half visor gives you the clearest view of all, and no protection at all for your mouth and jaw.

Here is the simple choice. For a junior, buy a cage, and that includes under-nineteen leagues, whatever your age. For anyone else, a cage or a full shield. That meets every league's face protection rule, as long as it carries the mark your league asks for.

Why is that the safe choice? In Britain every junior has to wear full face protection, under-nineteens included, even players who are eighteen. In England and Wales it has to be a cage, so a full shield doesn't count there. In North America, visors are only certified for players eighteen and over. So a younger player there needs full face too.

For women in Britain, it is full face until the season after your eighteenth birthday. From then, a half visor is allowed in British women's and senior competitions. At an international event you still need full face.

For adults in Britain, at least a half visor is required, unless you were born before nineteen seventy-five. Even then, some competitions still require one, so check yours. A half visor has to be fixed so it can't flip up, and it can't be tinted. That is so other players and the officials can see your eyes. And if your visor cracks or breaks during play, leave the ice straight away.

Now your mouth. A half visor leaves your mouth, teeth and jaw open. That is why the people who certify visors in the United States recommend full face at every age. Whatever you wear on your face, wear a mouthguard too. Some leagues require one and some only recommend one. It differs even between British competitions. In one of them, every skater needs one, even in a cage. So ask, and wear one whichever way the answer goes. A mouthguard made by a dentist is better than the kind you soften in hot water.

Sixth idea. The sticker, and how your helmet and cage should fit.

The sticker you need depends on where you play, not where the helmet was made.

In the United States the sticker is HECC. It expires six and a half years after the helmet was made. Anyone below adult level, goalies included, cannot wear a helmet with an expired sticker there, even if the helmet is physically fine. So a cheap clearance helmet is worth a close look. It may be cheap because its sticker has run out.

In Canada the sticker is CSA, and it does not expire. There, the date on it is the year the helmet was made. Canadian minor and female hockey also wants a BNQ mark on your neck guard. That certification closes at the end of July twenty twenty-seven, with nothing announced to replace it. So buy one with the mark now, keep the label readable, and ask your association what it will accept after that. The closure is not permission to skip the guard.

In the United States, check your neck guard's sticker too. A BNQ one counts there only until the end of the twenty twenty-six to twenty-seven season. After that it has to be HECC.

Neither sticker's date tells you what a helmet has been through. The American date is how long the certification lasts. The Canadian one is when the helmet was made. That is why an in-date sticker never makes a hand-me-down helmet safe.

In Britain, no rulebook names a sticker at all. Referees check something else. They check that your kit is there, unaltered, and fitted properly. So buy hockey-specific gear from a shop selling into the European market, with the CE mark where it applies. Don't modify it, and fit it the way the maker says. Ask your league what it accepts. And if you play in goal, ask before you buy a mask.

Now the fit. Open the helmet to its biggest setting, put it on, and tighten it down until it is snug. The front edge sits about half an inch above your eyebrows, flat and level. Now shake your head. The helmet should move with your head, not on it. If it shifts, it is too big. If it gives you a headache or sore spots, it is too small. Wear it for a few minutes before you decide, because sore spots show up slowly.

Fit the cage separately. A medium helmet does not mean a medium cage, even when they come as a pair. Your chin sits in the middle of the chin cup, and your eyes look between the bars, not through one. In British hockey this is a rule as well. A helmet and cage that don't match in size count as dangerous equipment, and you are off the ice until it is fixed. Keep spare screws, straps and clips in a corner of your bag.

Seventh idea. Keep your helmet on in a scuffle, and keep your hands off the other player's.

You see players take their helmets off before a fight on television. Don't copy it. If you end up in a scuffle, your helmet only protects you while it's on your head.

In some leagues, taking your own helmet off before a scuffle gets you thrown out of the game. In at least one, you are also banned until your case is heard. And in that league it covers any scuffle where someone gets a penalty, even a shove after the whistle.

Grabbing the other player's helmet, cage or chin strap costs more than it looks. In some leagues even a light grab, anywhere on the ice, gets you thrown out. And in some, a forceful or reckless grab can mean a ban for games after this one.

And say your helmet comes off in a scuffle because you had not done it up properly. At least one league throws you out for that, and you miss the next game too. That is one more reason the chin strap matters.

So do your strap up properly, keep your helmet on, and keep your hands off the other player's.

Let me pull your head and face together. Find out what your league asks for before you buy. When in doubt, a cage for a junior, and a cage or full shield for anyone else. Wear a mouthguard whatever is on your face. Buy the sticker your league wants, and check its date. Fit the helmet so it moves with your head, and fit the cage on its own. And in a scuffle, helmet on, hands off the other player's.

Eighth idea. Your skates. Fit beats price everywhere, and skates are where it matters most. A cheap skate that fits beats an expensive one a size too big.

Here is why skates come first. They are the link between you and the only surface you play on. Every skating skill you learn, the knee bend, the edges, the push, goes through the boot. A boot that lets your foot move about inside it can't pass any of that on.

Now the reason fit matters so much. You turn and stop on your edges. The edges are the two sides of the blade, the inside edge and the outside edge. You control them by tilting your ankle. If there is slack between your foot and the boot, the first part of that tilt just takes up the slack. The blade hasn't moved yet. You feel unstable, so you never commit to the edge.

Players in skates that are too big usually decide they have weak ankles. What they usually have is loose boots. And a foot sliding about inside the boot also breaks the boot down from the inside. So a size too big costs you twice.

Three checks when you try skates on.

One. Heel lock. Sit down, kick your heel firmly into the back of the boot, and lace up. When you walk, your heel should not lift or shift at all.

Two. With your heel back, your big toe should just brush the front of the boot. Not pressing into it, and not floating clear of it.

Three. No empty space anywhere else. Foot flat, snug all round, no gaps at the ankle or under the arch.

Lace properly while you check. Make it snug through the first few eyelets, then tighten well where your foot curves up into your ankle. That is what pulls your heel back into the boot. And wear the socks you actually play in.

Skates usually run about one to one and a half sizes smaller than your shoe size. That is a starting point, not a promise. One maker publishes that conversion and another deliberately does not, so get measured and try them on. For a growing child, half a size bigger is fine, and no more. A child sliding about in a loose skate can't learn their edges, and gets blisters.

If your outside edges still feel unsafe after plenty of practice, get your skate fit checked before you book another power skating session.

Ninth idea. The right boot, set up the right way.

Skates come at many prices. An entry-level boot is soft and breaks down in a season or two. That is fine for a few public skates a year, but not for learning to play. Mid-range is the first level with stiffer composite material in the boot, and the first that can be baked. For most adult recreational players, that is the best value.

Match how stiff the boot is to how often and how hard you skate, not to how good you want to be. A stiffer boot passes more of your push into the ice, but only if you are strong enough to bend it. If you are not, it stops your ankle bending. That takes away the knee bend and the edges you were paying for.

Baking means a shop heats the boot so it moulds to your foot as it cools. Have a shop do it, not your oven at home, which can cook the glue. Once they are laced on, sit still. Don't stand up and don't flex your ankle, for about ten minutes while they cool. Otherwise they set in the wrong shape. And don't bake entry-level skates. There is little in them to mould, and the heat can do more harm than good.

Expect a few uncomfortable sessions while the liner settles. If they still really hurt by your fourth time on the ice, take them back to the shop. That is a fit problem, not a break-in problem.

A sore spot on the top of your foot from the laces is called lace bite. The laces are pressing straight down onto the tendon. Try lacing the other way, with the lace going over the eyelet and back in. Then the pull goes sideways instead of straight down. A gel pad inside the tongue helps too. And if you ever need pads to fill empty space around your ankle, that tells you your skates are too big.

Tenth idea. Keep your edges sharp, and skate on one hollow you know.

Dull steel is the usual reason a player suddenly slides out of turns, or scrapes a stop instead of spraying ice.

When a shop sharpens a skate, it cuts a groove down the middle of the blade. The two sides of that groove are your edges. The hollow is how deep that groove is. It is given as a size in inches, and the smaller the number, the deeper the groove.

Here is the trade. A deeper hollow bites more. You get harder stops and tighter turns, but less glide. A shallower one glides more and bites less. Heavier players usually go shallower, because their weight already sinks the blade into the ice.

If you don't know yours, ask for half an inch. That is the usual starting point. Write it down, and ask for the same every time. If you change it, change it one step at a time.

Here is why one hollow matters so much. Your whole feel for your edges is tuned to it. If you change your hollow and your technique in the same week, you can't tell which one made the difference. And don't keep changing it from rink to rink, for the same reason.

Sharpen new skates before you first use them. They come with a factory finish, not a game-ready edge. Then sharpen roughly every twenty to thirty hours on the ice. That is a common guide, not a measurement. Sharpening too often wears your steel out early, and too rarely costs you your edges. Outdoor ice usually dulls them faster.

You need a sharpen when the blade chatters or pulls, when you slide out of turns you normally hold, or when your stops scrape instead of spray. You can also look. Hold the blade under a bright light and look straight down at the edge. A sharp edge shows as a thin line. If you can see light shining back along the edge, it has gone round, and it needs sharpening. A younger player who suddenly says their skates feel slippery or weird is usually describing dull steel.

And never walk on concrete, tile or metal in bare blades. One step can ruin an edge.

Let me pull your feet together. A skate that locks your heel, with your toe just brushing the front. No more than half a size of growing room. A boot as stiff as you can actually bend. Baked by a shop, sitting still while it cools. Sharp edges on one hollow you know, and a sharpen when your stops start to scrape.

Eleventh idea. Your stick. Use one you can bend and control.

A stick is a set of numbers, not a brand. Four of them decide almost everything about how it plays. Length, flex, curve and lie. Everything in this idea is a starting point, not a law of hockey. Two players the same size can reasonably end up on different sticks.

Length first. Stand the stick upright on its toe in front of you. In skates, the top should reach about your chin. Here is why it matters. Too long, and you stand up taller, which wrecks your knee bend, and your shot gets slower. Too short, and you lose reach, and you have to bend over more than is comfortable.

Your position pushes it a little either way. Defencemen often go a bit longer, for reach when they defend and when they poke at the puck. Forwards often go a bit shorter, for quick hands in tight and a fast shot. That is a habit, not a rule. And the longest stick allowed is not the same in every league, so if you like a very long one, check before you buy.

Flex is how stiff the shaft is. The number is roughly the pounds of force it takes to bend it an inch. Start at about half your body weight in pounds. Go up a bit if you are tall or strong for your weight, and down if you are not.

Here is why flex matters. A stiffer stick does not shoot harder. You shoot by bending the shaft like a spring, then letting it snap back into the puck. If you can't bend it, you get less power, not more, and a slower release. Too whippy is wrong as well. You overpower it, and the puck goes somewhere you didn't choose. You want a flex you can bend on every shot.

And cutting a stick down makes it stiffer, because a shorter shaft is harder to bend. So if you plan to cut more than about three inches off, buy a lower flex than your body weight says. Buying the right number and then sawing four inches off is the most common way players end up on the wrong flex.

Curve. Start with a mid curve, with the face of the blade not too open. An open face is tilted back, the way some golf clubs are, so it lifts the puck. That makes lifting the puck on your forehand easy. But it makes your backhand and your passing worse, because the back of the blade has nothing to cup the puck. For a developing player, a less open curve is better. It makes you learn real shooting and stickhandling, instead of leaning on the curve.

Lie is the angle between the blade and the shaft. With the right lie, the whole bottom of the blade sits flat on the ice in your normal stance. With the wrong lie, only the toe or the heel touches. Passes skip off, your stickhandling gets loose, and your shots go off line. To check, turn your stick over every so often and look at the bottom of the blade. If the toe or the heel is wearing much faster than the rest, the lie is wrong for you. Try a different lie on your next stick. And cutting a stick down changes the lie you need, too.

Whatever you settle on, write the numbers down, and change one thing at a time. That way you know what made the difference.

Twelfth idea. When your stick breaks, and when your goalie's does.

You already have the first part. Open your hand, leave it on the ice, and never put it over the boards. Now, getting a new one.

A teammate hands you a new stick, from the bench or on the ice. It has to be handed to you. A teammate may not throw, slide or shoot it to you. And you may not use an opponent's stick or your goalie's stick.

Your goalie is different, because a goalie can't skate to the bench for a new stick. So be the player who carries a spare out and puts it in their hand. Until then, your own stick handed to them is fine.

Never throw, shoot or slide a stick to your goalie. It can be a penalty for you. In at least one league the goalie who picks it up gets one too, and it can even risk a penalty shot.

Whether a goalie may play on with a broken stick depends on the league. In British hockey, a goalie may. In some North American leagues it is a penalty. So never tell your goalie to play on unless you know which it is.

Now the tape. Tape does four jobs. It protects the blade, and it stops water and ice building up. It gives the puck a little grip. And on the handle, it gives your hands something to find without looking. There is no right way to tape a stick. Players tape differently because they want different things.

What does matter is that the tape goes on smooth. Overlap it evenly, and don't let it wrinkle or bunch. Uneven tape gives you an uneven blade, and that shows up when you take a pass or shoot. Then wax it. Wax keeps water and ice off the tape, adds grip on the puck, and makes the tape last longer. At the top of the stick, build a knob of tape. It stops the stick sliding out of your top hand, and it helps you pick the stick up off the ice.

Let me pull your stick together. Chin height in skates. A flex near half your body weight, and lower if you will cut it. A mid curve with a face that isn't too open. Check the bottom of your blade for the lie. Write the numbers down. Carry a spare out to your goalie, and never slide it. And tape it smooth, then wax it.

Thirteenth idea. Your pads, and the gaps between them.

Pads only protect you where they overlap. So fit each piece, then check where the pieces meet.

Shoulder pads first. The tips of your shoulders sit in the middle of the shoulder caps.

Elbow pads. Try them on with your shoulder pads and gloves on. That is the only way to see whether the three pieces meet.

Gloves. The cuff should meet the bottom of your elbow pad with no gap, and your fingertips should just reach the end. If you are between two sizes, go up.

Pants. Standing up, they should overlap the knee part of your shin guards by about a third to a half.

Shin guards. Your kneecap sits in the middle of the knee cup, strapped so it can't turn.

Then the three gaps. Fitters keep coming back to them. Nobody has counted which one causes the most injuries, but each is easy to check at home.

One. The gap between your shin guards and your pants. It leaves a strip of bare leg just above the knee. A rising puck, a stick and the top of the boards all arrive there. Check it bent into your skating stance, not standing up.

Two. The elbow pad that spins. It is a strap and sizing problem, and you can stop it completely. Strap it so you can't twist it.

Three. Your wrist, between the top of your glove and the bottom of your elbow pad. Slashes land there, and you can't see that part of your arm. A glove cuff that meets your elbow pad closes it for free. A cut-resistant sleeve closes it properly, and two of the big rulebooks recommend one.

And tape your socks over your shin guards. Two or three wraps near the top of your calf, and two or three at the ankle. That holds the guard where it should be, so it can't turn during a game. Don't wrap it so tight that it cuts off your circulation.

Fourteenth idea. Spend where it counts, and know what never to buy used.

Spend on your skates first. They make the biggest difference to how you play, and a bad pair holds you back. Then your helmet, because a mistake there costs you the most. Then your stick, but spend on getting its numbers right, not on its price. A cheap stick with the right numbers beats an expensive one with the wrong ones. Then your gloves, because you feel the puck through them, and cheap ones wear through at the palm. Never spend big on a stick and cheap on a helmet.

You can save on socks, base layers, the bag and tape. Fit matters more than the model for shin guards and elbow pads. And in an adult league without body checking, light shoulder pads are fine. Where checking is part of the game, they move a long way up the list.

Plenty of kit is fine to buy used. Sticks, if the numbers suit you and there are no cracks. Pants, shin guards, shoulder pads and elbow pads, once you have checked for cracked plastic, squashed foam and broken straps, and washed them. And skates, but only if they really fit you and the boot isn't broken down. A used boot has already moulded to someone else's foot, and that is the risk.

Never buy a used helmet, goalie mask, mouthguard or jock. Buy face guards and neck guards new every time. Never buy anything with an expired sticker. Never buy a cage or visor with deep scratches or cracks.

And a sticker can be faked. Fake Canadian CSA helmets are mostly bought online, or in second-hand shops. If a helmet feels light, flimsy or modified, look the model up in CSA's own product list before you trust its sticker.

If you are starting out, ask your club first. Many clubs and rinks lend gear to new players. Some beginner programmes give you kit to keep. Whether that kit is new is a separate question, so ask them. Borrow the pads, and buy the head protection new. Get fitted in person for skates and a helmet, even if you buy online afterwards. Those are the two you can't guess. And last season's mid-range skates on clearance are usually the best value in the shop.

Fifteenth idea. Trying out in goal.

Goalie gear is a different world. It is sized differently, it costs several times what a skater's kit does, and it is harder to find second-hand in the right size. That is why many clubs own a set. So if you want to try playing in goal, borrow the club's pads, blocker, catching glove, chest protector and pants, and find out if you like it.

But get your own mask. A goalie mask is a helmet. A club mask has been on more heads and stopped more pucks than any hand-me-down, and nobody is keeping a record of what it has stopped. Ask about its history anyway. But a mask nobody can account for is one you don't put your head in. No rulebook says that about a loaned mask. It is the same caution as for any borrowed helmet.

Sixteenth idea. Look after it. Empty your bag every single time you get home.

Here is why. Wet kit zipped up in a bag is warm, dark and damp, which is exactly what bacteria and fungi like. The smell is only the sign. The foam breaks down, the stitching rots, and your blades rust. And hockey gives you plenty of small cuts and blisters, which is how skin bacteria get in. Clean, dry kit and clean skin take away most of that chance.

So take everything out, hang it up, and run a fan. Moving air does more than time. Wash your base layers after every use. Every few weeks, wash the rest, but not your skates or helmet, and never with chlorine bleach, which can break down the foam and glue. Wipe your helmet and skates by hand. Shower soon after you play, and don't share towels.

Read the care label before you wash anything, and especially your neck guard. It is meant to be worn unaltered, the way the maker says. One maker's neck guard label says wash it cold, lay it flat to dry, and never tumble dry it.

Wipe your blades and holders dry after every skate, before the skates go back in the bag. Water sitting on steel is what rusts it. Soft blade covers soak up the last of the water, but take them off once the blades are dry. Don't store your skates in them. And replace your laces before they break, because they will break five minutes before a game. Keep a spare pair in the bag.

Seventeenth idea. Get dressed, check, and step on in all of it.

Pack the same list every time, and put the neck guard on it. Every item on that list has been forgotten by somebody, in a car park, twenty minutes before a game.

Then dress in an order that works. Each layer goes on before the thing that overlaps it. And you can't bend down to lace skates once your shoulder pads are on. So base layers and jock first. Then shin guards, with socks pulled over them and taped. Then pants, then skates, while you can still reach your feet. Then shoulder pads, elbow pads, neck guard and jersey. Helmet on, strap done up. Mouthguard. Gloves last, so your hands are free for everything else.

Before you step on, check three things. Chin strap fastened. Shin guards tucked under your pants. Gloves meeting your elbow pads. Those three are what referees and injuries both notice.

And wear all of it for the warm-up as well as the game. In British hockey that is required. In most British competitions a coach or manager also has to check your helmet, cage, chin strap and neck guard before the warm-up.

Take your jewellery off before you dress. Some leagues ban it outright, even under your kit, and anywhere a referee can treat a loose ring or stud as dangerous equipment.

Don't cut your kit to make it lighter or cooler. A glove with the palm cut out is not allowed anywhere. In Britain, pants that are cut, torn or changed are not allowed either. And in Britain your jersey stays outside your pants and covers your elbow pads, so buy one big enough to do that.

Let me pull it all together.

Your own helmet, bought new, a goalie mask included, and replaced after a serious hit. Wear it, but never go into a collision head down. Chin strap done up every time, one finger. Pads that fit, strapped so they can't move, and the gaps between them closed.

In Britain, the neck guard on every time you step on the ice, adults and training included.

When kit fails in play, let go of it and get off the ice. Drop a broken stick where you stand, never over the boards. If your helmet or neck guard comes off, go to the bench. Hand your goalie a spare. And in a scuffle, keep your helmet on and your hands off the other player's.

Find out what your league asks for before you buy. A cage for a junior, a cage or full shield for anyone else, and a mouthguard. Skates that lock your heel. Sharp edges on one hollow you know. A stick you can bend. And empty the bag every time you get home.

A lot of this depends on where you play. So here are three questions to ask your coach. What face protection, neck guard and mouthguard does our league need, and which sticker? Can I bring my own helmet, and what can the club lend me? And if our goalie's stick breaks, can they play on, and who carries the spare out?

Go and ask. Their answers decide what goes in your bag, and now you know why each piece is there.
