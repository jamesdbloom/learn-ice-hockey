===== SCRIPT START =====

This episode is about playing without the puck. It takes its time. We will build the job up one piece at a time, and for each piece I will tell you what to do and also why it works. If you know why something works, you can still do the right thing when the play looks a little different from the picture in your head.

Tracking data from the NHL puts a forward at about forty seconds with the puck in every twenty minutes on the ice. That is at five-on-five, five skaters a side. It comes in bursts of just over a second each. The league calls that data unofficial, so take it as the right shape, not an exact number.

But the shape is clear. For something like ninety-six percent of your ice time, you don't have the puck. So if all your practice is stickhandling, you are training a tiny slice of your game. That work isn't wasted. A second and a bit is a very short time to be clumsy with the puck. But the rest of your ice time decides where those few seconds happen. They can come in a good spot with your head up, or in a corner with two players on you.

Before any instructions, a few words you will hear all the way through, so they don't get in the way later.

The puck carrier. That is whoever has the puck, on either team.

Your player. When the other team has the puck, this is the opponent you are covering at that moment. I will call them your player.

Goal side. Being goal side means being between your player and your own net.

The passing lane. That is the straight line a pass would travel, from the puck to a player.

The slot. This is the ice in front of the net. Its top part, between the faceoff dots and the tops of the faceoff circles, is called the high slot.

The crease. This is the blue painted area right in front of the net, where the goalie plays.

The checker. This is any player from the other team coming at you, whether they are after the puck or just coming at you.

The weak side. This is the side of the ice away from the puck.

That is the vocabulary. Now the job.

Every moment you don't have the puck, one of four things is true. Your team has it. The other team has it. It is loose, and nobody has it. Or it has just changed hands, and nobody is organised yet. Call those the four states. Each one asks you to do something different.

Here is why that matters. The state changes without warning, several times a shift. A blocked shot can change it in a tenth of a second. Most bad play without the puck isn't a player doing the wrong thing. It is a player still doing the right thing for the state before.

So keep noticing which state you are in, and change your job the moment it changes. Sometimes two seem to apply at once. Then do the more urgent job. If there is a loose puck nobody owns, treat it as a loose puck first, and that has its own rule, coming up.

Most players only have a plan for one state. Almost nobody has a plan for the loose puck or the turnover. That is why those two are where you can improve the most.

So here are four big ideas, one for each state, because they matter most. If you only listen to the first part of this episode, take those four with you. After that we will go back through them and add the detail.

First idea. When your team has the puck, be available, not just unmarked.

They sound alike, but they are not the same. Unmarked means nobody is near you. Available means the puck carrier can actually get the puck to you. The second one is the one that matters.

To be available you need three things at once.

One. Your stick on the ice, blade flat, ready to take a pass. The blade is the target, so give the passer something to hit.

Two. A straight line from the puck to your blade. If the carrier would have to bend the pass round a body, you are not available, however alone you are.

Three. Nobody's stick in that line. This is the one you can't tell from where you are standing. You can be ten feet from anyone, waving, with a defenceman's stick lying flat across the only line to you. Then you are unmarked and unavailable.

So when you get open, look at the passing lane straight away, not at your defender. If it is blocked, move. Three or four feet is often enough to open the line. A small move that opens the lane is worth more than a big burst that doesn't.

And go when the passer's head comes up, not before.

Second idea. When the other team has the puck, watch your player, not the puck.

Here is why. Five of your teammates are already looking at the puck. Your player is the one thing nobody else is looking at. Watching the puck feels responsible, but it leaves the player who scores unmarked. Coaches name puck-watching as the most common defensive mistake in amateur hockey. That is coaching agreement, not a counted ranking.

So keep your player in the corner of your eye, and take short, repeated looks at the puck. If you can't see your player and the puck in the same glance, you are in the wrong place. Move until you can.

Then use two tools, because most players only use one.

Your stick takes away the pass. Put the blade flat on the ice in the passing lane, between the puck and your player. A blade flat on the ice stops a pass that a stick held six inches up does not. The stick goes in the lane, never across a player. A stick laid across someone holds them back, and that is a penalty.

Your body takes away the shot and the drive to the net. Stay goal side, between your player and your own net, not between them and the puck. Take the ice, not the body. Get to the spot first and hold it. Don't step across into them.

To do both, face up the ice enough to see the play, stay goal side, and reach your stick across into the lane. In your own end, if you can't do both, goal side wins. Here is why. A pass caught twenty feet from your net is something your team can survive. A player alone right in front of your goal is not.

One more piece of this idea. When a teammate is already pressuring the puck carrier, don't go and pressure them too. Be the second layer instead. Sit behind your teammate and a little to the side, in the space the carrier would go into if they got past. From there you take away the pass the carrier wants. Two players on one puck leaves an attacker free somewhere, and that is the player who scores.

Third idea. When the puck is loose, one player goes hard, one supports, and nobody else joins.

A loose puck is one nobody has. It is on the boards, in a corner, or sitting in front of the net after a save. It happens dozens of times a game, and almost nobody teaches it.

Who goes depends on your team's system, and it is one of the questions to ask your coach at the end. But here is what holds in every system. One player goes hard. One supports. Nobody else joins. Why? Two players arriving at the same puck from the same angle are both committed, and neither one is free to help. That is a turnover waiting to happen.

If it isn't clear who should go, call it. I've got it. Yours. Help. The call settles it.

Now the part that keeps you safe, because a race for a puck on the wall is a race into the boards. Hitting the boards or a goal post head first, with your head down, can badly hurt your neck. And it can happen even at walking speed. So arriving safely matters more than winning the puck.

Here is how you arrive. Come to the wall on an angle, with your feet apart and your knees bent. Get your skates parallel to the boards before the contact. Forearm and hip to the boards. Never the point of your shoulder, and not the whole shoulder if you can help it. Head up, chin off your chest. Never turn your back to the checker. And never duck.

Those last two protect your head and neck. If you turn your back, you can't see the hit coming or brace for it. If you duck, your head meets the boards first.

If you really can't turn in time, and you are already facing the wall square on, that is the fallback, not the target. Get your stick and gloves up and spread wide on the glass, and keep your head off it. That is only for when things have already gone wrong.

From here on I will use one short version of that position, the same words every time, so you can learn it like a line. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the checker, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly.

And when you get there, it is stick on the puck, not a hit. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call. If you can see a player's numbers, pull up and play the puck.

Winning the puck is never a reason to arrive any other way.

Fourth idea. When the puck has just changed hands, act first.

In the two or three seconds after a turnover, neither team is organised. Whoever spots the change first usually wins it.

If your team has just won the puck, turn and go straight away. If your team has just lost it, sprint back in your lane and pick up a player, not a place. Look for the most dangerous one, and that is usually not the carrier. It is the player arriving late into the middle. That is coaching emphasis, not something anyone has counted.

And one thing matters more than anything else on the way back. Backchecking means racing back toward your own end after your team loses the puck. So you are often coming from behind. Never finish a backcheck into somebody's back. If you can see a player's numbers, pull up and play the puck. A hit from behind can cause injuries that change a player's life, and in almost every league it ends your game.

Let me pull those four ideas together, because they are the core of the job.

Before them, know which of the four states you are in, and change your job the moment it changes.

One. When your team has the puck, be available, not just unmarked. Stick flat, a straight line, nobody's stick in it. Go when the passer's head comes up.

Two. When they have it, watch your player, not the puck. Stick flat in the lane, body goal side. Layer behind the pressure, don't double up.

Three. On a loose puck, one goes, one supports, nobody else joins. Arrive safe before you arrive first. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the checker, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly.

Four. When it changes hands, act first. Turn and go, or sprint back to a player. And if you can see their numbers, pull up.

Everything else in this episode adds detail to one of those four. So let's go back through them, starting when the other team has the puck in your end.

Fifth idea. When you are the one pressuring the puck.

Somebody on your team has to go to the puck. Sometimes that is you. It might be as the first forward into their end, the first defender back on a rush, or the defenceman in your own corner.

Here is the surprising part. Your job is not to win the puck. Winning it is a bonus. Your job is to take options away and make the carrier decide early. Why? Then your four teammates have less to cover than they did a second ago.

So angle, don't chase. Come in on a curve that closes off one side of the ice. Then the only way left is the way your support is covering. If you skate straight at the carrier, you give them both sides, and you get nothing.

Steer them toward your help. Your pressure and the second layer are one play, not two. Never angle a carrier into the middle while your support sits on the boards. That is worse than not pressuring at all.

Arrive under control. Full speed with your feet stopped is how you get beaten with one move. Close the last few feet with short strides, with your stick out front, ready to change direction.

And talk, because you are the trigger. I've got the carrier tells your support where to stand. If you say nothing, four teammates have to guess.

Now the limits, and they go together with everything I just said. You steer with your angle and your stick, not your body. It is stick on the puck, not a hit. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call, and that is the next idea. No shove, no lean, and never your stick across anyone. Never lift anyone off their feet. Never into a back. If you can see their numbers, pull up and play the puck. Building up speed into contact, even with two hard strides, can be charging. And even going slowly, a crash into the boards can hurt.

Where your league does allow hitting, the body still comes second, after the stick. Use it only when it doesn't cost you the ice behind you. A missed hit takes you out of the play for about five seconds.

Who pressures, and how many of you, is a coaching choice set by your team's system. In their end, this is called the forecheck, the way your team pressures them in their own zone. Under a forecheck called the two-one-two, the nearest player pressures and the next one supports. Under a one-two-two, only one forward pressures at all, and the others hold the middle. Under a trap, almost nobody pressures deep. So ask which your team plays.

Sixth idea. Find out whether you may use your body at all, before your first shift.

Younger age groups, girls' and women's hockey, and some whole adult leagues don't allow body checking. So it is a question about your league, not only your age. In some places the answer can change from one game to the next. When a team that checks plays a team that doesn't, the whole game can be non-checking. In Britain, it is the competition you are entered in that decides, not your age. So ask your league, and get the answer in writing.

In women's hockey played under the international rules, as it is in Britain, contact is only allowed when you are clearly trying to play the puck. Never step or glide into a player, and never use the boards to pin them or push them into it. That is a penalty, and it can end your game.

Where you may not hit, a hit is a penalty. In some leagues a reckless one throws you out of the game automatically. It can even be a match penalty, which goes past being thrown out.

So where you may not hit, you defend with angle, stick and position. Take the ice, never the player. Get to the spot first, and hold it. In Canada's leagues, hold only the ice you already have. And in a Canadian adult rec league, avoid contact altogether rather than leaning into it. There, not avoiding it is a penalty, and a major penalty ends your game.

Seventh idea. The second layer, and the system behind it.

You already know the layer. Behind the teammate who is pressuring, a little to the side, in the space the carrier would go into. While you sit there, keep asking yourself one question. What is behind me, and who is it?

Whether you hold the layer depends on how your team covers in its own end. There are three systems you are likely to meet.

The first is zone coverage. The version this episode assumes is called the low zone collapse. The players who aren't pressuring sink toward the slot. Each one covers an area, not a person. In that system, sitting in the layer behind the pressure comes naturally.

The second is man-to-man. Each player follows one opponent wherever they go. So sometimes you have to leave the layer to follow your player out toward your blue line. In man-to-man, that is your job, not a mistake.

The third is a hybrid. You play man-to-man low, near your net, and zone higher up. So the answer changes with where you are standing.

There is one planned exception to the layer as well. A deliberate double-team below your own goal line. Why there? Down below the goal line, the net and the boards hem in a turnover. Two of you on the puck is still stick on the puck, not a hit. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call. If you can see their numbers, pull up.

So ask your coach which coverage you play, and what the signal is for switching. Doing any of these on your own, without the team, is the real mistake.

Eighth idea. A scramble in front of your own net.

Sometimes there is no structure left. Three players down, sticks everywhere, and you can't work out the right play. Then work down this list, in order.

First, the front of your own net. If nobody is there, go there. Nothing else matters as much. Keep your head up there, chin off your chest. The goal post can hurt you the same way the boards can.

Second, take away the shooter's stick, not the puck. Seal their blade with yours, or lift their stick as the puck arrives. Lift low on the shaft, well below their bottom hand. Lift it up, not sideways. Then let go, because hanging on is holding the stick, a penalty of its own. Never go at the hands, which can be called hooking anywhere. Never a chop. And never a two-handed shove. Keeping your blade on the ice doesn't make it legal. You don't need to win the puck to stop the shot.

Third, get it out of the danger area. Anywhere is better than the slot, as long as it stays in the rink. This is the one moment a plain, boring clear is exactly right. Fire it hard and wide off the glass, or short enough to die in the neutral zone, the middle of the ice between the blue lines. Use your stick, not your skate.

Never shoot it over the glass and out of the rink. From your own end, in some leagues, Britain's included, that is two minutes even by accident. Doing it on purpose is a penalty everywhere. And don't aim at the bench, because over the glass there still counts.

Fourth, and only then, look for the first pass out of your end. Trying to make that pass before the danger has gone is how a scramble turns into a goal against.

It works the same way at the other end, turned round. Get to the front of their net first, with your head up and your chin off your chest. Keep your stick free, and shoot from anywhere before you try anything clever.

Let me pull your own end together. When you are the one pressuring, angle, don't chase, and steer the carrier to your help. Stick on the puck, not a hit, and find out before your first shift whether you may hit at all. Where you may not, take the ice, never the player. Behind the pressure, sit in the layer and ask what is behind you. Find out which coverage your team plays. And in a scramble, net front first, then the shooter's stick, then clear it off the glass, then the pass.

Ninth idea. Transition, the moment the puck changes hands.

Why does this moment matter so much? Every organised defence is planned and practised. Against a set defence, your options are limited. But in the two or three seconds after a turnover, that structure doesn't exist yet. Players are facing the wrong way. Defencemen are up the ice. Forwards are behind the puck.

A chance you make in those seconds is made against no structure at all. That is why it is worth sprinting for. And it works against you too. Two seconds of your own team being disorganised is where you give up goals.

When your team has just won the puck, turn and go straight away. The biggest mistake here is the half-second spent checking that your team really has it. Go on the likelihood, not the certainty. If you are wrong, you have skated hard the wrong way, and you can recover from that. If you are late, the chance has gone.

Get width and depth at the same time. Somebody stretches the ice, so their defence has to back off. Somebody stays close for the short pass. If everyone does the same one, the breakout dies.

And don't all go. Transition is exactly where one forward too many joins the rush. That is how a goal against starts.

Whoever notices the change first usually wins it, and you can train that. Get your head up before the turnover happens. Then you already know where the space is when it does. If you find out about turnovers by seeing the puck go the other way, you will always be second.

Tenth idea. The backcheck, and the systems behind it.

When your team has just lost the puck, you have two decisions to make, both while you sprint.

First, don't chase the puck carrier. Take your lane. Why? The dangerous player is usually the one arriving unmarked into the middle, not the carrier. Covering a lane is worth more than catching the carrier from behind.

Some teams coach the opposite on purpose. The first forward back pressures the carrier, while the defence holds the inside. That is a real system. That pressure is stick on the puck, not a hit, and you are coming from behind, so if you can see their numbers, pull up. If it is yours, it is an instruction, not an instinct, so ask. What is wrong under any system is deciding on your own.

Second, backcheck to a player, not to a place. Pick up a player and tell your teammates you have them. Sprinting to the tops of the circles and stopping, covering nobody, uses the energy and buys nothing.

Which lane is yours is a coaching choice. The usual way is the centre in the middle lane and the wingers on the outside lanes. Some teams say the first player back takes the middle. Others pick up man-on-man, starting from the puck and working outward. Ask which yours plays.

But one thing is wrong in every system, and that is everyone sprinting to the middle. Here is why. Two wingers and a centre all in the middle leave both outside attackers free. Somebody owns the middle, and nobody assumes it is somebody else. If you are back first and the middle is empty, it is yours.

And count as you go. Know whether it is two on one, three on two or three on three. What you should do is completely different in each.

Now the safety point, and it matters more than anything else about the backcheck.

Never finish a backcheck into somebody's back.

Here is how it happens. You are late. The player you are catching is facing the boards with their head down, looking for the puck. And your last stride turns into a hit from behind. That hit can cause a serious injury, especially in the ten feet or so nearest the boards.

So when the player you are catching has their back to you, take the lane or let up. If you can see their numbers, pull up and play the puck. That holds in every league, checking or not.

A hit from behind is a penalty anywhere on the ice, not only at the boards, and it is never just two minutes. In almost every league, Britain's and Canada's included, it ends your game. Even in the one league with a lighter penalty for a push, a forceful hit at the boards throws you out. If the player can't protect themselves and you drive them into the boards or the net, in some leagues it is automatically a match penalty. That goes past being thrown out.

And don't hold them back from behind with your stick, your body or your free hand, whether they have the puck or not. Skating is the only legal way back into position.

Let me pull the middle of the ice together. When your team wins the puck, turn and go on the likelihood, get width and depth, and don't all go. When you lose it, take your lane, backcheck to a player, and count. Somebody owns the middle. And never finish into a back. If you can see their numbers, pull up.

Eleventh idea. Now your team has the puck again. Getting free with a change of speed.

Defenders can match your speed. What they struggle to match is a change of speed, because reacting costs them a moment. That is coaching know-how. Coaching manuals teach it, but nobody has measured it.

So slow down, then explode. Coast for a stride, so the player covering you closes up and settles. The moment they settle, go. It feels wrong, because slowing down feels lazy.

A hard stop works too. If the player covering you keeps going, you have space, and you are facing the play.

And never arrive at top speed with nowhere to go. Speed only helps if it gets you free at the moment the pass comes.

Twelfth idea. Getting free with a change of direction.

Two steps one way, then hard the other. Why does that work? The defender's weight has to shift before they can follow. And it costs them more time than it costs you, because you knew it was coming.

Go the way their hips aren't facing. A defender whose hips are square to the boards can't easily follow you to the middle.

If you want to keep your options, curl rather than stopping dead. A tight turn keeps your feet moving and your head up. A full stop makes you a target standing still, and it costs you three strides to get going again.

And come back toward the puck, instead of skating away and hoping. A lot of players skate away from the carrier. A player skating toward the puck arrives with speed a standing defender can't match. The pass is shorter, and the carrier has a target moving into space.

Thirteenth idea. Timing your break.

Getting open too early is the same as not being open. Say you break into space a second before your teammate is ready. A defender has that second to recover. By the time the carrier looks up, you are covered again. Then you stop, the play dies, and you blame the passer.

So watch the passer, not the space. Go at one of these moments. When their head comes up. When the puck comes to the middle of their blade, or onto their forehand. When they have just beaten a checker and have a free second. Or when their shoulders turn toward you.

Hold, hold, hold, then go on their look. Holding doesn't mean standing still. Keep moving in small ways, a curl, a drift, a change of angle, so the player covering you has to stay honest. Only the big burst waits for the passer. Think of the pass and the break as one play shared by two players.

Fourteenth idea. Your half of the pass.

A completed pass has two people behind it. If you are just waiting to be found, you are doing a quarter of your job. Here is what you owe the passer.

A target. Stick on the ice, blade flat, angled to take the pass. Give them something exact to aim at, not a general area.

A lane. Be open along a line the puck can travel. Move to make that line. Don't expect the puck to bend.

A time. Arrive as the passer is ready, not before.

A next play. Arrive facing where you mean to go, with your body open up the ice before the puck gets there. If you take a pass with your back to the play, you have turned a good pass into a battle.

Information. One word said early is worth more than a perfect route. Man on tells a teammate that an opponent they can't see is closing in. If you are the one who hears it on the boards, the same position as always. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the checker, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly.

Time tells a teammate that nobody is near, so they can lift their head and make a play. Only say time when you are sure. A wrong time sends a teammate into a hit they weren't ready for.

And a close, safe option when the carrier is under pressure. When they are pinned, they don't need a fancy option forty feet away. They need a close, safe one they can actually hit. Being the simple pass five feet away is a real job, even if it feels boring.

You can give a teammate time just by where you stand. A support option in a good spot buys the carrier the extra second they need to see the whole ice.

Fifteenth idea. Using a teammate to lose the player covering you.

Run your route close past a teammate, so the player marking you has to go round them. You aren't hitting anybody. Your teammate isn't moving into anybody. The defender just loses a stride in the traffic. In most leagues you are allowed the ice you are standing on, so that route is legal if it meets one condition. It works on almost every faceoff and every drive to the net.

There is one condition. The player you delay must not be the one pressuring your own puck carrier. Whether they were the player marking you is a useful guide, but it is not the test. In your own end, the player marking you is often that forechecker. And in Canada's leagues, your teammate has no written right to that ice. So there, only run past a teammate who is doing their own job, never one parked to catch the player marking you.

Shout as you come, and go past your teammate, not into the space they are standing in. Why? The defender is going at full speed into a teammate who is standing still and can't see them coming. That is a coaching caution, not a rule.

Never step sideways into anybody. Moving sideways into a player without the puck, without getting there first, is interference. That move is called a pick. Stopping in a defender's path to take the contact, or drifting sideways into a chaser, is the same thing.

A pick is a penalty, and two minutes is not the most it costs. A violent, reckless or injuring pick can end your game in every league. In some leagues, plan on a match penalty for a reckless one.

Taking the forechecker off your own puck carrier is a different act, even if you never touch them. It counts as interference too. Once your body meets theirs, two minutes isn't the most it costs, anywhere. In at least one league, even the version with no contact can end your game. So get open for your carrier instead. Give them a pass to make, not a body to hide behind.

One word I will keep for one job only, and that is screen. In this episode a screen means standing in front of the goalie. That brings us to their net.

Sixteenth idea. Drive the net.

Skating hard at the net without the puck does three things at once. It pulls a defender with you, because no defenceman can leave a player driving at the front of their net. It makes a rebound and tip threat, which changes where the goalie stands and what they watch. And it pulls the defence in toward the net, which opens the ice behind you, in the high slot and back toward the blue line.

That is the point of the drive. Somebody else scores, into the space the defender left.

The drive has to be real. A half-hearted glide doesn't move anybody. If you are going, go as if you expect the puck.

Now the part that keeps you safe. You are skating hard at a steel goal frame. So know where the posts are, and never arrive head first. If you are going to hit a post or the boards, take it with an arm or a leg, anything but your head. Head up, chin off your chest, and never duck. Even going slowly, a crash there can hurt. That holds in every league, checking or not.

Drive at the net, not at the goalie. Avoiding the goalie is on you. If a defender shoves you into them, that only excuses you if you were trying not to touch them. And arrive under control. Building up speed into contact, even with two hard strides, can be charging.

A defender will come with you. Hold your line with your feet. No shove, no lean, and never your stick across them. Never lift anyone off their feet, and never go into anyone's back. If you can see their numbers, pull up.

Seventeenth idea. Screening the goalie.

The job is simple to say. Stand where the goalie's eyes are, between the puck and the goalie, so they can't see the shot. Stay outside the crease, with both skates off the red line around it, not just out of the blue paint. Keep your stick on the ice, ready for a tip or a rebound. Touch nobody. And get out of the way at the last instant, but move where you can still see the puck.

Here is why each part matters. A body that moves late is harder for the goalie than one that just stands there, because they pick the puck up a fraction too late. Your stick on the ice makes you a tip and rebound threat, not just a body. And you move where you can still see the puck, because you can't time your move off a shot you never saw.

Why off the red line? Leagues disagree about whether that line counts as part of the crease. Off the line is the answer that holds wherever you play. Inside the crease, your position alone can cost your team the goal. Keep your stick blade out of the paint as well.

If a defender shoves you into the crease, get straight back out. Being pushed in covers where you are standing, not what you do next. And never stand in the goalie's way back to their net. If the goalie has come out to play the puck and you deliberately stop them getting back, that can be a penalty with no contact at all.

Never put a hand, a glove or your stick across the goalie's face. That isn't a screen. In some leagues, Britain's included, it is two minutes. Screen with your body and your position.

Now the part that keeps you safe at the net front. Know where the near post is before the shot. A defender will be working to move you off your spot, and your eyes are on the puck. So you are being moved by someone you can feel but not see. You can travel several feet without looking up once.

So take their shove braced. Wide base, knees bent, weight low, knees and hips giving rather than locked stiff. Keep your spot. Don't push back or lean on anyone, and never put your stick across someone. Head up, chin off your chest, and never duck. A post can hurt you the same way the boards can, even going slowly. None of this depends on whether your league allows checking. A box-out, where a defender holds you off the net with their body, puts you in the same place as a shove.

If the goalie leans into you, give ground straight away, then set the screen again.

Standing upright in the shot lane is the job. But know this. A puck that comes off a stick blade can go up, toward your face. When you tip a shot, your blade is in that lane, a few feet from the shooter. That is coaching know-how, not a measured risk. It is no reason to stop tipping. It is a reason to know when the puck is being released, and to have your face protection right, not nearly right.

Eighteenth idea. Keep off the goalie.

Where you stand and whether you touch the goalie are two separate questions. Being outside the crease settles the first. It settles nothing about the second. A goalie isn't fair game just because they are outside the crease.

So never start contact with the goalie, in the crease or out of it. A screen never needs to touch anybody.

Here is why. A goalie who is down or turned can't protect themselves, so contact can hurt them. It is also a penalty. Deliberately bumping a goalie, in the crease or out, is at least two minutes in every league, whether or not the puck goes in. And contact with a goalie in the crease can take the goal off the board, even when you didn't mean it.

Charging is worse. In a screen you stand still. In a charge you skate into the goalie, building up speed, even with two hard strides. If you charge a goalie in the crease, you can be thrown out of the game in every league, and in some it is automatic, even if nobody is hurt. A charge meant to hurt the goalie is a match penalty in most leagues.

Nineteenth idea. Make the defence cover you, even when the puck never comes.

A passing option the carrier looks at and doesn't use has still done its job, because a defender had to respect it. Two real options force a defender to choose. One lets them sit in the lane.

On the weak side, stand with your stick down and your feet moving. Look like you are about to get a pass. Blade down, body open to the puck, one small step toward the gap between two defenders. Why? That forces the defence to keep somebody on you. A player standing on the weak side watching forces nothing.

Width matters too. Defences want to squeeze toward the middle, and your width stops them. If you are the far winger in their end, staying out near the boards keeps a defender out there with you. If you drift into the middle to look busy, the defence closes up, and a closed-up defence is much harder to score against.

On a rush, a wide forward makes their defenceman respect the outside. That widens the gap between the two defencemen, and that gap is where the drive through the middle goes. And in the middle of the ice, a forward stretched high near the far blue line pins a defenceman deep. That shortens the ice your breakout has to cover.

How much width your team wants in their end is a coaching choice. Some coaches want the far winger high and wide, to guard against the other team breaking the other way. Others want them crashing the back post on every shot. If that is you, it is the net front again, so head up and chin off your chest. You can't do both jobs at once. Ask which your team wants.

Let me pull their end together. Drive the net as if you expect the puck, under control, and know where the posts are. Screen from outside the crease and off the red line, stick down, touching nobody. Take the shove braced, keep your spot, head up, chin off your chest, and never lean on anyone. Take the goalie's eyes, never their body. And on the weak side, stay wide and look ready, so a defender has to stay with you.

Twentieth idea. Effort without the puck.

First, the second effort. That is the play after the play. You lose a battle, and you turn and go again. You shoot, and you go to the rebound. You get beaten wide, and you take one more stride. Going again means your stick on the puck, not a hit. If you can see their numbers, pull up. Why does it work? The player who just won the battle thinks it is over, and for a moment they stop playing it. In that moment, the puck is there for whoever keeps skating.

Second, finish your route. If you drive the net, arrive at the net, not on the goalie. If you break into a lane, finish the break even after the pass has gone somewhere else.

Half-finished routes cost you twice. Within a period, defenders learn your drives aren't real and stop following you. Then you can't pull anyone anywhere. And an abandoned route leaves you in between, neither supporting nor defending. That is where odd-man rushes come from, when they attack with more players than you have back. Coaches name half-finished routes as one of the most common mistakes without the puck. That is coaching agreement, not a count.

Here is the honest part. Effort without the puck is the most visible thing about you, and the easiest thing to change. Your coach can't easily see whether you made the right read. But from the bench they can see exactly who backchecked, who finished the route, who got to the net front and who watched.

And it needs no new skill. You don't need better hands or faster feet to backcheck hard into your lane, finish your route, or take a second stride into a battle.

It just isn't a licence to skate hard in random directions. Effort in the wrong place is just being tired. Everything in the four states tells you where to spend it.

Twenty-first idea. How to train it.

Small-area games in a small space, like cross-ice or one zone, train it best. Why? In a small space you can't hide, and you are always in one of the four states.

Ask your coach for a few rules that force it. Two touches or one touch, so nobody can skate out of trouble and everyone else has to move. A goal only counts with somebody at the front of the net, which forces net drives, and the same head-up rule applies there. You must change direction before you take a pass. And three minutes with no talking, then three with everyone calling. You will hear what talking is worth.

If your games are filmed, watch a shift and never look at the puck. Watch only yourself. Look for long glides, routes given up three-quarters of the way, and standing still watching a battle. It is uncomfortable, and it is the quickest way to improve.

At a live game, pick one player and watch only them for a whole shift, not the puck. A good centre moves far more than you would think when nothing seems to be happening. Live is better than television, because the camera follows the puck and cuts out the very players you want to watch.

And use the bench. You have thirty seconds and a clear view. After each shift, ask yourself one question. Which state was I in when it went wrong? Was I unmarked but unavailable? Did I finish every route? When we lost it, how long did I take to turn? Did I double up on a teammate's pressure? And on the loose pucks, did I arrive the safe way round? Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the checker, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly.

Pick one question and use it for a whole game. One question asked six times changes what you do. Six questions asked vaguely change nothing.

Let me pull it all together.

Know which of the four states you are in, and change your job the moment it changes.

When your team has the puck, be available, not just unmarked. Stick flat, a straight line, nobody's stick in it. Get free with a change of speed or direction, come back toward the puck, and go on the passer's look. Only say time when you are sure.

When they have it, watch your player, not the puck. Stick flat in the lane, body goal side. Layer behind the pressure, don't double up. Before your first shift, find out whether you may hit at all, and where you may not, take the ice, never the player.

On a loose puck, one goes, one supports, nobody else joins. Arrive safe before you arrive first. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the checker, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly. In a scramble, net front first, and clear it off the glass, never over it.

When it changes hands, act first. Backcheck to a player, take your lane, and never finish into a back. If you can see their numbers, pull up.

And at their net, drive it for real, screen from outside the crease, head up, and never touch the goalie.

Much of this depends on what your team plays. So here are three questions to ask your coach. Who goes on a loose puck in our system? Do we play zone, man-to-man or a hybrid in our own end, and what is the signal to switch? Which backchecking lane is mine? And one for your league. Is body checking allowed in our games?

Go and ask. Their answers tell you how your team wants you to play without the puck, and now you know why each answer makes sense.

### 8. Where I departed from the source or the template

- **Structure.** The template's four big ideas are four winger jobs; here they are the source's four states, one idea each, so the spine is the document's own organising idea. The "rink one end at a time" parts become your own end (pressure, hitting, coverage, scramble), the middle (transition, backcheck), their end (getting free, the pass, routes, the drive, the screen, the goalie, width), then effort and training. Twenty-one ideas against the template's eighteen.
- **"Your man" became "your player"** throughout (plan, owner rule 3). "Man-to-man" and "man-on-man" are kept as the names a coach uses (plan decision 12). "Odd-man rushes" is kept once.
- **"Low zone collapse" is introduced as "the version this episode assumes"**, not as the usual one; the source calls it "the default throughout" (:291) and never says it is the commonest.
- **Stricter than the source in three places, never cheaper:** "Use your stick, not your skate" for a clear is stated for everyone (the source scopes it to the IIHF, :363); taking the forechecker off your carrier "counts as interference too" even with no contact (the NHL and IIHF do not name the no-contact screen and permit standing on your own ice, :561–:563); and attackers never start contact with a goalie anywhere (USA Hockey's Casebook allows engaging a goalie with the puck outside the privileged area in a checking game, :726).
- **"In most leagues you are allowed the ice you are standing on"** counts the NHL, IIHF, PWHL and USA Hockey's Preface (:512–:518), four of six; the Canada limit follows in the next paragraph.
- **The "why" for systems.** The source gives no reason a team chooses the two-one-two over the one-two-two or a trap, zone over man-to-man, or one backcheck lane system over another, so those stop after "how it works" and "ask" (plan A.2). The width choice does carry a why for "high and wide" (the counter-attack, :774) and none for "crash the back post".
- **Template close.** Three questions for the coach and one for the league, as the Faceoffs and Defender detailed scripts do.
- **The template's "Both protect your head and neck"** line is used for the boards reason, with "If you turn your back" and "If you duck" as conditionals (brief item 16).

### 9. What I found unclear in the source

- **Who goes on a loose puck under the two-one-two.** :213 says "the nearest player pressures and the second supports"; :312 says on a loose puck in their end "the nearest two forwards go and the third reads". The script uses the first wording for pressuring and leaves the loose-puck split to "ask your coach". Not a contradiction, but a listener could hear one.
- **"Take the ice, not the body" (:265) sits beside "you may use body position to lengthen their path" (:271).** The script keeps the first and omits the second (§5).
- **The facing-the-wall fallback** is only half-stated in the source (:339, "the fallback… not the target"). The glass detail comes from Body Contact and Battles. A plan row to add "stick and gloves up and spread on the glass" to the source's §State 3 would close that.

### 10. Where this script is weakest

- **The longest stretch without a recap is 54% to 86%**, about 13 minutes, across eight ideas. A recap after the fifteenth idea (routes and picks) would fix it for about 400 characters, but it would also move the seam window.
- **Limb density in three paragraphs.** The pressuring-limits paragraph (104 words) and the drive's defender paragraph carry eight limbs each in short sentences. They are accurate and required by the brief, but they read as a list.
- **"A second and a bit is a very short time to be clumsy with the puck"** paraphrases "brutally short window in which to be unskilled" (:114); it is the closest the script comes to a crafted line.
- **Man on and time are defined from On-Ice Communication**, not the source; the source names the calls but does not define them (:797).
- **The slot is defined only as "the ice in front of the net"**, because neither the source nor the two additional sources gives the standard between-the-circles definition. A listener may want more.
- Backward and forward pointers were audited by hand, by shape: "I will come back to that" (third idea, resolved in the fifth and seventh) and "that is the next idea" (fifth, resolved in the sixth) are the only forward pointers; "You already know the layer" restates its content.
