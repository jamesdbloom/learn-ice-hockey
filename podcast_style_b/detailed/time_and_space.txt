===== SCRIPT START =====

This episode is about time and space. It takes its time. We will build the idea up one piece at a time, and for each piece I will tell you what to do and also why it works. If you know why something works, you can still do the right thing when the play looks a little different from the picture in your head.

Before any instructions, a few words you will hear all the way through. I will explain them now, so they don't get in the way later.

Time and space. Space is the room you have when the puck reaches you. Time is how long you have to use it. Put together, they are the most valuable thing you can have on the ice.

The carrier. That is the player with the puck. Support means your teammates arriving to help the carrier.

The forechecker. That is an opponent pressuring you in your own end, trying to win the puck back.

The checker. That is the opponent coming at you on the boards, whether they are after the puck or just coming at you.

The gap. That is the distance between a defender and the puck carrier they are defending.

The seam. That is the gap between two defenders, the ice that neither of them has clearly claimed.

The slot. This is the ice in front of the net, between the faceoff circles. Its top part, from the faceoff dots up to the tops of the circles, is called the high slot.

The half-wall. This is the stretch of boards level with the faceoff dot.

The point. This is up at the blue line, the spot in the zone furthest from the net.

Strong side and weak side. The strong side is the side of the ice the puck is on. The weak side is the other side, away from the puck.

That is the vocabulary. Now the ideas.

Here is the one thought behind this whole episode. Every tactic in hockey does one of two things. It gets you more time and space, or it takes time and space away from the other team. And reading time and space is a habit, not a gift some players are born with. You can learn it, and you can learn it faster than you learned to skate.

I will start with four big ideas, because they matter most. If you only listen to the first part of this episode, take those four with you. After that we will go through the rest in parts. Making space with the puck. Getting open without it. Taking space away when you defend. And where the space is on the ice.

First idea. Judge a shift by the room you had when the puck arrived, not by whether your move worked.

Picture two players with the same hands, the same shot and the same top speed. They play on the same line. One keeps producing, and the other doesn't.

What is the difference? It is almost never their hands. The first player gets the puck with a metre and a half of clean ice and their head already up. The second gets it with a stick on their hip and no idea what is behind them. They have the same skill. Only one of them had room to use it.

Here is the way to think about it. Skill is what you spend. Time and space are what you earn. A player with great hands and no room has nothing to spend them on.

So change how you judge yourself. After a shift, don't ask whether your move worked. Ask how much room you had when the puck reached you. Then ask what you did to get that room.

That changes what counts as a good move, too. A move only counts if it makes room. A fancy move that beats nobody and opens no space hasn't helped you. It is a habit, not a skill. A small shoulder fake that moves a defender's hips a few inches and opens a passing lane is a skill, even though it looks like nothing.

And the cheapest ways to get better are almost all about room. Looking over your shoulder before the puck arrives costs nothing. So does turning away from pressure instead of into it, side-on to them, never your full back. Do that anywhere except your own end, where you take one look and move the puck. Putting your stick in a passing lane costs nothing too, the line a pass would travel along. None of those need faster skating or better hands.

Second idea. Read how fast they are closing, not how far away they are.

Beginners hear "you've got time" and think of seconds on a clock. That is the wrong picture, and it will get you hit.

Here is a better one. Your time is the distance to the nearest dangerous opponent, divided by how fast they are closing on you. Nobody measures that on the ice. It is a way of thinking. But it explains the moments that catch players out, and the speed half of it matters more than players expect.

Two cases show why.

First case. You can have time with an opponent three feet away. Say a forechecker has skated past you and is still travelling away from you. They are only three feet from you. But before they can do anything about you, they have to stop, turn and get going again. Coaches put that at a few tenths of a second at least. Nobody has measured it, but it is real time, and it is yours.

That is the moment for a reverse. A reverse sends the puck back the way it came, into the ice the forechecker has just left. A rim is the opposite. It sends the puck on around the boards, the same way the play is going.

Now the part that keeps you safe. You play a reverse at the boards, with that forechecker still on your hip. So how you meet the boards matters more than the pass.

Never turn your back to the checker. And never duck.

Both protect your head and neck. If you turn your back, you can't see the hit coming or brace for it. If you duck, your head meets the boards first. That is true even going slowly, so a slow checker doesn't make it safe.

So here is the position you aim for. Skates parallel to the boards. Forearm and hip to the boards. Never the point of your shoulder, and not the whole shoulder if you can help it. Head up, chin off your chest.

If you are already facing the boards and can't turn, that is the fallback, not the target. Get your stick and gloves up and spread wide on the glass, and keep your head off it. That is only for when things have already gone wrong.

From here on I will use one short version of that position, the same words every time, so you can learn it like a line. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the checker, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly.

Second case. You can have no time with an opponent twenty feet away. Picture a defenceman skating backwards at you. They are balanced, with their stick already in your lane. They are closing at maybe fifteen feet a second. That gives you a bit over a second. If the puck is on your backhand, some of that second is already gone. Twenty feet felt safe. It wasn't.

So when you look, don't just check the distance. Check three things.

Which way are their hips pointing? A stick can be anywhere. But a player speeds up in the direction their hips face. Watch the hips, and you know where they are really going.

Are their feet moving, or are they gliding? A gliding player can change direction quickly. A player in full crossovers, crossing one skate over the other to build speed, is committed. You beat them by going the other way.

How many are coming? One closing opponent, you can beat with a move. Two coming from different angles, move the puck. Whatever room you open against the first one, the second one fills.

Third idea. Look before the puck arrives, not after.

Your time with the puck starts the moment it touches your stick. Say you spend the first half second finding out where everyone is. You have used up most of your time on information you could have had for free while the pass was on its way.

So look over your shoulder while the pass is coming, not after it lands. Two looks are better than one. It feels as if you will miss the puck. You won't. You catch it with your hands and the edge of your vision, not by staring at it.

Here is why it matters so much. A player who has already looked arrives already decided. A player who looks after it lands is still deciding while the defender closes.

Knowing your next play before the puck arrives arguably buys you more time than any move you will ever learn. Nobody has measured that ranking. One study measured something simpler. Players who looked before receiving a pass succeeded more often than players who didn't.

One exception. If you are a goalie, this reverses. When you are tracking a live shot, you never look away from it.

Fourth idea. When the play ahead isn't there, turn away from pressure and wait for help.

You carry the puck into crowded ice, and the play ahead isn't there. Don't force it. Turn away from the defender closing on you, not into them. Stay side-on to them, never your full back. Make a tight turn, or curl back toward your own end. Keep the puck on the outside of the turn, with your body between the puck and the defender. Keep your feet moving. Then hold the puck for a second or two while a teammate arrives.

Never do this in your own end. If you lose the puck there, it is a scoring chance against you. In your own end, take one look and move it.

Why turn away? If you turn into the defender, you put the puck right where they can reach it. Turn away, and your body is a wall between them and the puck.

Why hold it? Because it buys your teammates the one thing they need, which is time to get there. A teammate one stride late is no use to you. Give them a second and a half, and they are a second attacker. You are not slowing the play down. You are letting everyone catch up to it.

There is a second reason. It makes the defence decide. For this to work, a teammate away from the puck has to go deep, toward their goal line. Now their defence has a question. Step up to you, or fall back? Step up, and they leave space behind them for your teammate. Fall back, and you get a clean entry and a shot from the top of the circles.

And a third. The space behind their defence is the most valuable ice there is. If you attack into it alone, you waste it. Wait until a teammate can get there with speed.

Every instinct says go forward. So this is a skill you have to practise. For a second, it will look as if you are going backwards. That is fine.

Now where to use it. In their zone, and around their blue line. And when you are first over their blue line with no support yet.

And where never to use it. Not in your own end, as I said. And don't delay when you already have more attackers than they have defenders. A two-on-one attacked at speed is worth more than a three-on-two attacked slowly.

Let me pull those four ideas together, because they are the core of everything else.

One. Judge yourself by the room you had when the puck arrived, not by your hands.

Two. Read how fast they are closing, and their hips, not how far away they are.

Three. Look before the puck arrives, while the pass is still travelling.

Four. When the play isn't there, turn away from pressure and let help arrive. But never in your own end.

Everything else in this episode adds detail to one of those four. So now let's take them into the game, starting with the puck on your stick.

Fifth idea. Change your speed.

A player skating hard in a straight line at one speed is easy to match. The defender picks the same speed and holds it. Two players going flat out at the same speed stay the same distance apart forever.

So where does separation come from? From the difference between your speed and theirs. Not from how fast you can go.

That is why slowing down can beat a defender. Glide, and the defender closing on you has to decide. Close harder, and risk you going round them. Or ease off, and give you room. Whichever they choose, the moment they change speed is the moment they are least able to change direction.

Then explode. A couple of hard crossovers out of a glide get you more separation than a long run of steady hard strides. The counts are just a picture. The reason is the difference in speed.

So the habit is glide, look, explode. The glide gives you time to look. The look tells you where to go. The burst takes you there. If you skate hard the whole shift, you never have a gear left, and you never have your head up.

It works without the puck too, and there it is worth even more. Your check is the opponent marking you. A winger who drifts, lets their check relax, and then breaks is far harder to cover than one who sprints all the time.

That a change of speed beats top speed is coaching know-how, not something anyone has measured. Learn the technique, and hold the ranking loosely.

Sixth idea. Attack space, not people.

An odd-man rush is an attack with more of you than they have defenders back. The most common way to waste one is skating straight at a defender. You have handed them a problem they can solve by standing still.

Attack the seam instead, the gap between two defenders. Now the two of them have to talk and decide who takes you. That is where teams break, because deciding under pressure is hard. If they both take you, someone else is open, two-on-one. If neither does, you are through.

The same idea works all over the ice.

On a rush, drive the ice between the two defencemen, not at the outside shoulder of one.

On a zone entry, getting the puck over their blue line, put it into the space behind a defender, not at the defender.

On a pass, aim for the ice your teammate is skating into, not where their stick is now. A pass to a stick standing still freezes your teammate. A moving player is hard to defend.

And in their zone, move to the empty ice, not to the puck. Four players chasing one puck turns five-on-five into five-on-two against yourselves.

Seventh idea. Show them the wrong option.

You can make space before it exists on the ice, in the defender's head. A defender can't cover every option, so they cover the one they think is coming. So show them the wrong one. Here are four ways.

The fake shot. Put your weight on your back leg, drop your shoulder, and start your stick back. A defender blocking shots drops a knee or turns a hip. The goalie sets. Both have stopped moving sideways, so now the pass across, or the step around the shot-blocker, is open. It only works if you sometimes really shoot from there. If you never shoot, nobody believes the fake.

The look-off. Turn your head and eyes hard to one option, then pass to another. Defenders and goalies read your eyes first, which is why it works. But you are not looking where you pass. So only use it on a pass you have already checked. Look, look away, pass.

The head and shoulder fake. A hard dip of your head and shoulder to your forehand side while you carry wide. You don't need to beat them. You need them leaning the wrong way for a fraction of a second.

And showing one side to open the other. Skate at a defender's inside shoulder, and they close the middle, which opens the outside. Skate at their outside shoulder, and they go wide, which opens the middle. You make them commit before you decide.

How do you know it worked? A defender who has been fooled turns their hips, reaches with their stick, or shifts their weight. Each one is a step out of position that you can use.

One warning. A fake costs you a moment. If you have no time, with two forecheckers closing and your back to the boards, don't fake anything. Make the simple play.

One league note. In an adult league under the CARHA rulebook, keep your backswing within fifteen inches of the puck. Sell a wrist shot, never a wind-up. A bigger backswing counts as a slap shot there, which is a penalty. If it hurts someone, you are thrown out of the game and miss at least the next one. Faking a slap shot to scare someone is a penalty there too.

Eighth idea. Use the net and the boards.

Nobody can skate through the net or the boards. So treat them both as teammates.

Behind their net is the best place on the ice to stop and think. Why? They can only come at you from two sides. You can see their whole zone in front of you. A defender who commits to one side gives you the other. And nothing behind their goal line is a scoring chance against you, so a mistake there is cheap. It is the one place where you can really stop, lift your head and choose.

Behind your own net is the opposite. If you lose the puck there, they can come round the post or walk out into the slot. So behind your own net, you are turning, not looking around. One look, one decision, move it.

The boards work the same way. Get side-on, with the puck on the far side of your body, and a defender can only come at you from one side. That halves the angles you have to protect. It is why keeping the puck on the boards is a real skill. And it is why the half-wall is such a good spot on a power play, when your team has an extra player. The boards cover one side, the corner is behind you, and the whole zone is in front.

On the boards, the same position as always. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the checker, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly. And know who is coming before the puck gets there.

Never stand square to the boards, digging at the puck with your head down. That is exactly the position a hit from behind catches you in.

But the boards take away half your options too. So use them to survive pressure, then move the puck the moment support arrives. The boards keep the puck. They don't make a play by themselves.

Let me pull the puck-carrying part together. Change your speed, glide, look, explode. Attack the seam, not the defender, and pass to the ice a teammate is skating into. Fake only when you have time, and sometimes really shoot. Behind their net, stop and look. Behind your own, one look and move it.

Now the other side of the same coin. You don't have the puck.

Ninth idea. Get open with a change of direction, not a race.

Without the puck, nobody hands you space. You make it.

You will rarely out-skate a defender in a straight line. They can match you. But you can beat them by stopping when they expect you to go, or by cutting across the way they are moving. Their weight has to change before they can follow, and you knew it was coming.

When do you go? When their head turns to look at the puck. Every defender looks away from the player they are covering eventually. That moment is yours.

Then time it to the carrier, not to how keen you are. Here is why. If you get open a second before the carrier can look up, you were open, and by the time they look, you are covered again. So break when the carrier's head comes up. If you are early, don't stand still and wait. Curl away and come again.

When you are open, show it. Your stick on the ice in a clear lane, your voice, and movement, so the carrier can find you.

And don't stand still on the half-wall or at the net front. A player standing still is covered by a defender who also gets to watch the puck.

Tenth idea. Make room for a teammate by going somewhere yourself, never by stepping into a defender's path.

This is the part that people often get wrong. Hockey is not basketball. There is no legal pick on a skater. A pick means stepping into a defender's path to block them and free a teammate.

The test is movement, not standing still. In most leagues, you are allowed the ice you are standing on, and you don't have to move to let a defender past. But moving into their path, when you weren't there first, is interference. That means getting in the way of a player who doesn't have the puck.

A pick is a penalty, and two minutes is not the most it costs. A violent or reckless pick can get you thrown out of the game. In some leagues, plan on a match penalty for a reckless one, which goes past being thrown out. And where body checking isn't allowed, a pick with your hip or shoulder counts as an illegal body check too.

So how do you get a teammate open? Three things are legal.

First, holding ice you got to first. If you are skating your route to the puck or the net, and a defender runs into you, that is generally on them. Hold your spot. Don't lean into them, and don't step into them. Moving into their path once they are coming is the pick. And in a CARHA league, it is different. There, not avoiding body contact is itself a penalty. So go round them, and don't wait for them to run into you.

Second, a rub. Skate your own line, at your own speed, to a real place you are going, through ice a defender also wants. If they have to go round you, that is the rub. If you slow down, or bend your line into their path, it becomes a pick. The referee is asking one question. Were you playing the puck, or playing the player? And never use a rub to shield your own carrier from a defender chasing them. Several rulebooks call that interference outright.

Third, screening the goalie. It is the one true screen in hockey, and it is the next idea.

Eleventh idea. Screen the goalie from outside the crease, without touching anyone.

The crease is the painted area right in front of the net. The rules protect the goalie's body there, not the goalie's view. That is why a screen is legal.

So here is the screen. Stand in the goalie's line of sight, between the puck and the goalie's eyes. Stay outside the crease, and keep your skates off the red line around it, not just out of the blue paint. Some leagues count that line as part of the crease, so off the line is the only spot that is safe everywhere. Touch nobody.

Here is why the crease matters so much. If you step inside it, your team can lose the goal, even if you never touch the goalie. Keep your stick out of the paint too.

Now the part that keeps you safe at the net. Your eyes are on the puck, as they should be. So a defender pushing you is someone you feel, not someone you see. Keep your head up and your chin off your chest, and never duck. The goal post can hurt you the same way the boards can, even going slowly.

If a defender leans on you there, keep your spot. Don't push back or lean on anyone, and never put your stick across someone.

Then the goalie. Any contact with a goalie can be a penalty, and it can get you thrown out of the game. A goalie is watching the puck, not you. They can't see you coming or protect themselves, so contact can hurt them. That is the main reason.

Contact is only let go when it was accidental and you really tried to avoid it. In some leagues even accidental contact is called. And never build up speed into a goalie. Building up speed into contact, even with two hard strides, can be charging. A charge on a goalie can end your game in every league, and in some leagues it always does.

A goalie isn't fair game just because they are outside the crease. When a goalie has come out to play the puck, an empty crease is not a free crease. Never get in the way of a goalie scrambling back, touching or not. That can be a penalty with no contact at all, and it can cost your team the goal as well. Go round them.

And if a defender shoves you into the crease, get your feet out at once, or get clear of the goalie. Being pushed in can save your team's goal. It doesn't protect you from a penalty if you made no effort to avoid the goalie. So drive to the net under control.

Let me pull the part without the puck together. Get open with a change of direction, and break when the carrier's head comes up. If you are early, curl and come again. Make room for a teammate by going somewhere yourself, never by stepping into a defender's path. Screen the goalie from outside the crease, off its red line, stick out of the paint, touching nobody. Head up, chin off your chest, at the net as on the boards. And stay off the goalie, in the crease or out of it.

Twelfth idea. Defending is the same idea, run in reverse.

Now you are taking the other team's time and space away. Every defending habit in this episode has one thing in common. It acts before the puck arrives, not after.

Why does that matter? Because acting after the puck arrives is a gamble. A blade jabbed at a pass that has already gone, or a lunge at a pass you are guessing at. If you miss it, you are out of position at the worst moment. Acting early takes away the option before anyone tries it.

Start with your gap, the distance between you and the carrier. For a defenceman, it is the biggest lever you have over an attacker's time.

And it is a trade, with no free option. A tight gap takes their time away. They can't look up, and they can't pick a corner. But every foot you take from in front of them goes behind you. That is exactly where a chip past you wants to go, or a give-and-go, a pass and a quick return pass.

A loose gap protects the space behind you. But it hands them a full look up the ice, and a shot without breaking stride.

So close your gap early, so the carrier can't look up. Closing the gap means stick on the puck, not a hit. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call. And if you can see their numbers, pull up and play the puck.

How close is close? Coaches give gap distances in stick lengths, and their figures don't agree with each other. Treat any figure as a starting point, not a law. The right gap depends on how fast you skate backwards against how fast they skate forwards, and on whether they have support coming. And read the hips, not the puck. The hips tell you where they are really going.

Thirteenth idea. Angle, don't chase.

Angling means closing on a curve that leaves the carrier only the option you want them to have. Usually, that is the boards. You are not chasing them, and you are not hitting them. You take away the middle, so the only place left is the wall.

Why does it beat chasing? A chase is a race you might lose, and if you lose it, you are behind the play. An angle is geometry. It doesn't need you to be faster. You arrive where they are going to be, on the side you chose.

And steering someone isn't touching them. But that only holds while you keep the line you picked. So pick it early, and hold it. Keep your hip, shoulder and arm out of it. Take the puck, not the player.

Here are the limits, and they go with everything I just said. It is stick on the puck, not a hit. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call. No shove, no lean, and never your stick across anyone. Never lift anyone off their feet. Never hit a player who can't see you coming, and never from behind. If you can see their numbers, pull up and play the puck.

Building up speed into contact, even with two hard strides, can be charging. And the boards are where the angle ends, not something to use. Never use them to push or pin the player. Even going slowly, a crash into the boards can hurt.

Now the leagues where body checking is barred or limited. That means younger age groups, women's hockey, and non-contact adult leagues. There, your route is part of the test. Veering off your line to make contact, stepping or gliding into them, or adding a hip, shoulder or arm when you arrive, is a penalty. So is pinning them. Contact there can be legal only if you held your line, were playing the puck, and put nothing into it.

And in a CARHA league, don't touch them at all. Not avoiding contact is itself a penalty there, and a double penalty if they go into the boards. That double penalty counts as two of the three penalties that put you out of a game. Contact you meant can get you thrown out even if nobody is hurt. If contact you meant, or contact into the boards, hurts them, expect to be thrown out, and to miss at least the next game too.

Fourteenth idea. Your stick in the lane.

Putting your stick blade in a passing lane costs nothing, and it takes an option away. Point your blade at a lane or a threat. Don't rest it on the ice between your skates. And keep it flat on the ice. A blade flat on the ice blocks the pass. A blade waving at hip height doesn't.

Here is why it works. A blade already in the lane stops passes that never even get tried. The carrier looks, sees your blade, and looks somewhere else. That costs them time.

Compare that with a stick jabbed at the puck after the pass. That is a gamble. If it misses, you have given the carrier an easy way to the net.

Coaching material calls stick position arguably the defensive habit that makes the biggest difference. It says arguably, and nobody has measured it. But it is also the first thing tired players stop doing. So when you feel tired, check your stick.

Fifteenth idea. Deny the catch, not the pass.

You will spend a lot of time trying to cut off passes, and mostly failing. Passes are fast, and you are guessing. There is a better way.

Don't lunge at the lane. Arrive as the puck does. Skate a route that puts you on the receiver just as the puck reaches them. Your stick goes in their receiving lane, or on their blade as the puck lands.

Arriving on them is stick on the puck, not a hit. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call. But never hit a player who can't see you coming, and a receiver looking down at the puck can't. If you can see their numbers, pull up and play the puck.

Your stick does a press, not a chop. Press the blade briefly, then let go. Never at the hands. Never a chop at their stick, because a chop can be called a slash. And never a two-handed shove. Keeping your blade on the ice doesn't make it legal.

The timing is what keeps it legal. If you pin the stick of a player still waiting for the pass, you are interfering with someone who doesn't have the puck. Knocking the stick out of their hands is interference too.

Two things make this work. First, defend what the receiver does next, not the pass. Take away their forward option as the puck lands, and the pass got them nothing. They have to turn back, with you on them. Second, a receiver being closed on has to look down at the puck. That is a second of blindness you created without touching anybody.

That is what taking away time really means. You are not stopping the pass. You are making sure the receiver has no room when it arrives.

Sixteenth idea. Take the middle away first.

The middle of the ice is worth more than the outside, in every zone. Why? Because more options spread out from the middle.

In your own zone, the middle is the slot. Shots from the slot go in far more often than shots from the boards. In the neutral zone, the middle of the ice between the blue lines, a carrier in the middle can attack either side. A carrier on the boards can only attack one. Even in their zone, taking away the middle forces play to the outside. There, a shot has to beat a screen, a stick, and a goalie who can see it.

So when you are beaten and have to give something up, give up the outside. Steer them to the wall and defend their inside shoulder. You are trading a wide shot for a dead pass into the slot.

Steer with your route and your stick. It is stick on the puck, not a hit, and whether you may hit at all is your league's call. No shove, no lean, and never your stick across anyone. Never lift anyone off their feet, and never go into a player's back. If you can see their numbers, pull up and play the puck. Even going slowly, a crash into the boards can hurt.

For a defenceman at their blue line, the same choice. An attacker going round you on the outside is a mistake you can fix. One going through you on the inside is a scoring chance.

This is a strong general principle, not an absolute. Some coaches want the wall taken away in certain spots. Some penalty kills, when your team is a player short, deliberately load up one side. Ask what yours wants.

Seventeenth idea. Never pressure alone.

One player pressuring the puck with nobody behind them doesn't take space away. It moves it. The player who went leaves the ice they came from. If they are beaten, that ice is now an open lane. And your team is a player short behind the puck.

Pressure only works with a second layer. That is a teammate placed to take the puck if the first pressure forces a rushed play, and to cover the ice the first player left.

Who that second layer is depends on your forecheck. The forecheck is how your forwards pressure the other team in its own end, to win the puck back. It is a coaching choice.

This episode assumes one called the two-one-two. Here is how it works. The first forward in pressures the puck. The second takes the carrier's first way out. The third holds the high middle, as the safety. And those roles go by order of arrival, not position. Whoever gets there first is the first forechecker, whatever their position.

Whoever goes first, the pressure is stick on the puck, not a hit. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call. If you can see their numbers, pull up and play the puck.

Other teams play differently. In a one-two-two, one forward goes in to steer the carrier rather than hunt the puck, and two hold back. Why would a team do that? It gives up the puck behind the other team's net, but it shuts down the neutral zone. A one-three-one trap gives up even more, and denies harder still. So ask which forecheck yours plays.

But the point is not which one is right. If you go and your teammate doesn't, you have made a hole rather than closed one. So find out what your team plays before your first shift, and go on the same trigger as everyone else.

Let me pull the defending together. Act before the puck arrives. Close your gap early, but know what you are leaving behind you. Angle, don't chase, and take the puck, not the player. Keep your blade in the lane. Arrive with the puck, not after it, with a press, not a chop. Give up the outside and keep the middle. And never pressure alone. Every time you close on someone, it is stick on the puck, not a hit, and if you can see their numbers, pull up.

Eighteenth idea. Learn where the space is, like a map.

This tells you where to go with the puck, and where to go without it.

Here are the places space usually is.

Behind their net. No shot is possible from there, so it is under-defended. And a defender who commits to one side gives you the other.

The weak side. Defences slide toward the puck, so the far side is covered last. That is why a pass across the ice so often beats a pass straight up it. You get the puck there before the defence can slide.

The point. It is defended last, because it is furthest from the net. The space there is real, but it is the least dangerous on the ice. So use the time it gives you. Don't just stand there with it.

The half-wall. There is a wall on one side, you can see the whole zone, and a defender has to come out to get you.

And the seams, between two defenders, anywhere on the ice. That space is there because neither defender is sure it is theirs.

Now the places space almost never is.

The slot. Every defensive system is built to fill it. That is why it is so valuable, and also why you should plan to arrive there with no time. One touch, a tip or a rebound. Never plan to stickhandle there.

The strong-side corner, the corner where the puck is. Two, three, sometimes four players end up there. It is a place to battle, not a place to make plays. Win it, then move the puck out.

In that battle, it is stick on the puck, not a hit, and whether you may hit at all is your league's call. If you can see their numbers, pull up. And on the boards there, the same position as always. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the checker, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly.

And the middle of the neutral zone. Both teams pass through it, and every trap, a defence that sits back and clogs the middle, is built to fill it. A carrier who slows down there gets swarmed. Cross it with speed. If you can't, don't carry the puck through it.

So, with the puck, move it toward space. Without the puck, move yourself to where the space will be. That is usually the weak side, because that is where the puck will need you next.

Nineteenth idea. Three moments where reading the space decides the play.

First moment. A breakout pass is coming to you on your own half-wall, and a forechecker is arriving. Look before it lands. If they are closing hard, chip it past them up the boards, or bump it to a teammate swinging under you. If they have skated past you, the space is back where they came from, so turn into it.

If they get to you anyway, the same position as always. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the checker, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly. Which outlet your team wants first is a breakout choice, so ask.

Second moment. You are carrying the puck through the neutral zone, and their defence is backing off. Don't slow down in the middle. Attack a seam, or go wide with speed. If nothing is there and support is coming, delay at their blue line. If support isn't coming, curl back and regroup, or chip it past them and chase it. Chase for the puck, not a hit. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call, and if you can see their numbers, pull up. Some teams want the puck in deep every time, and others want to carry it in. Find out which yours wants.

Third moment. You have the puck on their half-wall, with time. Look across before you look along. Check the weak-side seam, the back door, which is the far side of the net, and the far faceoff dot first, because that is where the space is. Only then look up the wall, because the near slot will be full. And the moment a defender commits to you, pass to the ice they just left.

Twentieth idea. Use the rules as a map of where space is allowed.

The rulebook is not just a list of things you can't do. It is a set of lines that make space, and stop teams from killing it. Read it that way, and it is easier to remember.

Offside first. Offside stops cherry-picking, which means an attacker waiting behind the defence for a long pass. You are offside if both your skates are fully over their blue line before the puck is. One skate on the line, or behind it, keeps you onside.

That gives you a technique. At their blue line, don't brake early. Braking early gives away free attacking space. Drag your trailing skate instead, keeping it on or behind the line, with its blade on the ice. Your body and stick can be ahead, at speed, and you are still onside. Keep the blade down, because in some leagues a skate held up in the air doesn't count. A blade on the ice is onside everywhere.

Sometimes the linesperson raises an arm instead of blowing the whistle. That is a delayed offside. To fix it, every attacker gets out of the zone together, each with a skate touching the blue line. That is called tagging up. Then you can all come back in. Keeping the puck is worth the few seconds.

But first, find out whether your games have a delayed offside at all. Some leagues don't give it to younger age groups, and the whistle goes straight away. A player taught to tag up there can come back in after play has stopped, into a defenceman who heard the whistle and isn't braced.

When the arm is up, get out and tag up, and leave their carrier alone. In some leagues nothing stops play just because you are about to hit them, and they are not braced for you.

Next, icing. Ice the puck only when you have to. After your own icing, the faceoff comes back to your end, and in some games your tired players can't change. Know which kind of icing your league plays.

Then the trapezoid. Some rinks have a shape painted behind each goal. Where the rule is enforced, a goalie can't play the puck behind the goal line outside that shape. Here is the tactic. Dump the puck in wide of it. Then their defenceman, not their goalie, has to go and get it, with your forechecker arriving.

The forechecker arrives stick on the puck, not a hit. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call. If you can see their numbers, pull up and play the puck. And if their goalie comes out for it anyway, stay off the goalie. Any contact with a goalie can be a penalty, and it can get you thrown out of the game. A goalie is watching the puck, not you, so contact can hurt them.

Several rulebooks have no trapezoid. Where there isn't one, expect their goalie to come out and play the puck, and wide dumps are worth much less. A goalie who comes out can play the puck, but in many leagues can't cover it out there. In Britain the published rules don't settle whether it is enforced. So plan as though their goalie may come out, keep your own goalie inside it, and ask your league.

Twenty-first idea. Space changes with the score, the clock and the rink.

The score first. A team protecting a lead, especially late, gives up space it doesn't need and takes away space that matters. It keeps fewer players below the puck, and funnels everything outside. So if you are the team behind, you will find room at the point and on the wall, and almost none in the middle.

Why not force the middle anyway? Because that is how you give them a counter-attack. Take what they offer instead. Walk the line, skating sideways along their blue line with the puck. Shoot for tips. Work below the goal line, and on the boards down there, the same position as always. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the checker, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly. Pull them out of shape, rather than trying to go through them.

The clock changes it too. When a team pulls its goalie late in a game, six attackers make a spare player and leave a lot of room at the blue line. And for the defending team, every clear becomes worth a lot, because the net at the far end is empty.

Your legs change it as well. At the end of a long shift, your legs are gone, so the gap you can really close is bigger than it looks. That is the real reason for short shifts.

Tiredness and the rink both change the same thing, which is closing speed. Anything that slows how fast defenders arrive gives everyone more time.

So does the size of the rink. The international sheet is about the same length as the North American one, but it can be wider. All the change is sideways. On wide ice, a forechecker who commits to the boards takes longer to recover, and moving the puck across the ice gains more. On narrow ice, space shrinks and the forecheck arrives sooner.

So if you move between sheets, adjust. On wide ice, delay more, move the puck across more, and be more patient before entries. On narrow ice, decide earlier, use the walls, and value the puck behind the net over the puck in the corner. On those walls, the same position as always. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the checker, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly.

In Britain, games run under the local rules are allowed rinks smaller than the international minimum, both shorter and narrower. Those rules say nothing about Elite League rinks. So in those games, expect narrow, short ice until you have seen it.

Whatever rink you play in, skate a warm-up lap and notice how far it is from the half-wall to the far dot. That distance tells you how much time you will have there.

Let me pull the map together. With the puck, move it toward space. Behind their net, the weak side, the point, the half-wall. Without it, go where the space will be. Expect no time in the slot, the strong-side corner, or the middle of the neutral zone. Drag a trailing skate at their blue line, blade on the ice. Find out whether your games have a delayed offside before you tag up. Against a team protecting a lead, take the point and the wall. And notice how big your rink is.

Last, how to build all of this. Time and space is a habit you can train.

First, play small-area games, across the ice or in half of it. Here is why they work. Less space per player means more touches, more decisions, and less time for each one. Train your decisions where it is crowded, and a full rink starts to feel roomy. If your team doesn't run them, run them yourself. Three-on-three in one zone, two nets, no offside, first to five. It is also the most fun hockey there is.

Second, when you watch hockey, don't watch the puck. Pick one player and watch only them for a full shift, including the twenty seconds they don't touch it. You will see how much of what looks like skill is really arriving at open ice before the puck does. Watch a defenceman's gap from the moment the attacker gets the puck. Most of it is decided before the attacker reaches the red line. And count how often a good centre looks over their shoulder before a breakout pass. It is more than you think.

Third, after every shift, ask yourself one question. Did I have more time than I thought? For almost every developing player, the honest answer is yes. When you panic, you spend time you actually had.

The fix is not "be braver". Nobody can act on that. Here is what you can act on. Get one more look before you receive. When you don't know, turn away from pressure rather than passing into it, side-on to them, never your full back. A turn costs a second. A pass into pressure is a turnover. The one exception is your own end. Holding the puck there is how the turnover happens. One look, one decision, move it.

And trust "time" when you hear it. If a teammate or your goalie shouts time, it means nobody is on you, and they can see what you can't. When you are the one calling it, only say time when you are sure. A wrong time sends a teammate into a hit they weren't ready for.

Track that question for ten games, and the habit takes. For almost any player, it is the fastest way to get better.

Let me pull it all together.

Judge yourself by the room you had when the puck arrived. Read how fast they are closing, and their hips. Look before the puck arrives. When the play isn't there, turn away from pressure and let help arrive, but never in your own end.

With the puck, change your speed and attack the seam. Fake only when you have time. On the boards, the same position as always. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the checker, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly.

Without the puck, change direction, and break when the carrier's head comes up. Never step into a defender's path. Screen the goalie from outside the crease, head up, touching nobody, and stay off the goalie.

Defending, act before the puck arrives. Close your gap early, angle instead of chasing, and take the puck, not the player. Blade in the lane, keep the middle, and never pressure alone. Stick on the puck, not a hit, and if you can see their numbers, pull up.

Much of this depends on what your team plays. So here are three questions for your coach, and one for your league. Which forecheck do we play? On a breakout, which outlet do you want me to look for first? When I am beaten, do you want the outside given up every time, or the wall taken away in some spots? And for the league. Which rules do we play under, and is body checking allowed?

Then, after every shift, ask yourself the one question. Did I have more time than I thought?

### 5. Safety pairings (each limb travels with its instruction)

- Reverse at the boards → never your back, never duck, one shared reason, slow-speed limb, full posture, facing-the-boards fallback, then the short form defined.
- Every later wall act (puck protection, strong-side corner battle, breakout pass with a forechecker arriving, working below the goal line, narrow-ice walls) → the short form word for word, with "Keep it up even going slowly".
- Turn away and hold → never in your own end, in the next paragraph and again in the "where never" paragraph.
- Look before you receive → the goalie exception, same idea.
- Picks → the cost, the three legal ways, CARHA "go round them", no rub to shield your carrier.
- Screen → crease line, stick out of the paint, net-front head-up and post, keep your spot without leaning or a stick across, goalie penalty and ejection then the reason, two strides, goalie scrambling back, shoved in.
- Gap, angling, steering to the wall, forecheck pressure, arriving on a receiver, corner battle, chasing a chip, trapezoid forechecker → the gate.
- Angling and steering → the full item-6 list.
- Receiving press → press the blade and let go, never the hands, never a chop, never a two-handed shove.
- Delayed offside → the age limit and the no-whistle-before-a-hit warning in the next two paragraphs.
- "Time" → only when you are sure.

### 7. Departures from the source and the template

**From the source:**
- "Any contact with a goalie can be a penalty, and it can get you thrown out of the game" is the brief's item-24 wording. The source says *deliberate* contact can eject (:293) and a *charge* ends your game in each book (:278). The script's sentence is broader, in the harsher direction.
- "Keep your stick out of the paint" generalises USA Hockey Situation 10 (:548). Stricter.
- "A goalie isn't fair game just because they are outside the crease" generalises Hockey Canada's sentence (:554). Stricter.
- "Never lift anyone off their feet" is the brief's item 6. BC :1384 states it at the net front, and the script carries it to angling and steering.
- The boards short form is voiced at five places where the source carries no posture: the strong-side corner, the first moment, below the goal line, the narrow-ice walls, and the recaps. Brief items 5 and 17 require it.
- The gate ("stick on the puck, not a hit… your league's call") is attached to the gap, steering, the forecheck, arriving on a receiver, the corner, chasing a chip and the trapezoid forechecker. The source attaches league gates to angling only (:26, :347). Brief item 13 requires it.
- The fourth idea states "never in your own end" twice, once at the act and once where the source puts it, so a seam cannot separate them.
- "Coaches put that at a few tenths of a second" softens the source's "three or four tenths" to avoid a figure. Same claim.
- The twenty-foot defenceman's "tight gap" (:94) is dropped, because "tight gap" at twenty feet would confuse a listener just told what a gap is.

- No "rink one end at a time". A hockey-IQ page is not organised by zone, so the parts are: the four big ideas, with the puck, without it, defending, and the map with the rules and the rink. Each part ends in a recap.
