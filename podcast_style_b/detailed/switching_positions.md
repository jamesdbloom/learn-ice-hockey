===== SCRIPT START =====

This episode is about switching positions. Almost everyone who sticks with hockey gets moved at some point. Wing to centre. Centre to wing. Forward to defence. Defence up to forward. Onto the other side of the ice. Now and then, into the net.

It takes its time. For each change, I will tell you what to do, and also why it works. If you know why, you can still do the right thing when the play looks different from the picture in your head. And in a new position, the play will look different a lot.

Before any instructions, a few words you will hear all the way through. I will explain them now, so they don't get in the way later.

Your lane. Picture the rink split lengthways into three strips. The strip down your side, next to the boards, is a winger's lane. It runs the whole length of the rink. The middle strip belongs to the centre.

The point. This is the area just inside the blue line, where defencemen usually stand when their team has the puck in the other end.

The slot. This is the middle of the ice in front of the net. Its top part, from the faceoff dots up to the tops of the circles, is called the high slot.

The goalmouth. This is the ice right in front of your own net.

The hash marks. These are the short lines on the outside of each faceoff circle in the end zones, roughly level with the faceoff dot.

The half-wall. This is the stretch of boards level with the faceoff dot, between the corner and the point.

Goal side. Being goal side of a player means being between that player and your own net.

And the checker. That is the player from the other team coming at you on the boards, whether they are after the puck or just coming at you.

That is the vocabulary. Now the job.

Here is the good news first. More comes with you than you expect.

Your skating comes with you. Every stride, edge, crossover and stop. The one exception is skating backwards, and that one matters a lot if you move to defence. Looking around before the puck reaches you comes with you too. So does protecting the puck with your body. So does reading whether a checker has pinned you on the boards or skated past you. And what you know about offside, icing and faceoffs comes with you. Your new spot just puts you in front of different parts of it.

So what do you lose? The map in your head. Where to look first. Where to drift when nothing is happening. Who you pick up when the puck goes to a particular place. That map took a season to build, and it belongs to your old position.

For the first few weeks, you will be thinking about things you used to do without thinking. Thinking is slow. That is normal, and it passes.

One more thing before we start. Nobody has published research on how players move between positions. So most of this episode is what experienced coaches do, not tested fact. Use it, but don't repeat it to a teammate as if it had been measured.

I will start with four big ideas, because they matter whatever switch you make. If you only listen to the first part of this episode, take those four with you. After that we will go through each switch, one at a time, and add the detail.

First idea. The habit that served you in your old position is the one that will let you down in the new one.

Here is why. Your old habits are automatic. You don't think about them, and that is what made you good. But automatic habits show up exactly when you are under pressure, with no time to think. And in a new position, several of them are exactly wrong.

Here is what that looks like. A winger learns to hold their side of the ice and not chase. Move to centre with that habit, and it makes you passive. The puck goes into the corner. Your defenceman is alone in a battle they are going to lose. And you are standing where you have always stood.

A defenceman learns to be careful and wait for the other team's mistake. Move up to forward with that habit, and it stops you attacking. You arrive late, to space that has already closed.

So here is the fix. Before the shift, name the one habit you are fighting, out loud. Something like this. I'm going to feel wrong going into that corner, and I'm going in anyway. Then go and do the thing that feels wrong.

Why name just one? Because trying harder in general doesn't fix a habit. Paying attention to that one reflex does. And unlearning is slower than learning, so be patient with it. That is coaching experience, not a measured finding.

Second idea. Find out your team's system before your first shift.

A system is the plan your team plays. In your own end, it might be a zone system, where each player covers an area. It might be man-on-man, where each player follows one opponent. Or it might be a mix of the two. Then there is the forecheck, how your forwards pressure the other team in its own end. There is the backcheck, how your forwards skate back to defend. And there is the breakout, how your team gets the puck out of its own end.

Every one of those is your coach's choice, not a law of hockey. Here is why it matters so much. Each one changes where your new position stands. It changes your job more than any technique you could work on. So ask. A new player is allowed to ask anything, as often as they like.

In this episode I will describe the common version of each job. In your own end, that is a zone system called the low zone collapse, and I will say what changes under man-on-man. If your team plays something else, your coach's answer beats mine.

Third idea. When you are lost, go to the safe spot.

For the first few weeks, the puck will go somewhere your old map never sent you. Your instinct will be to freeze, or to chase it. Don't. When you don't know where to be, go to the spot that costs your team least. Then read the play from there.

As a defenceman, that is between the puck and your net. If your partner is already on the puck, it is the goalmouth.

As a centre in your own end, it is the high slot. You go down into the corner only to support your defenceman when the puck goes in there.

As a winger, it is your lane. In your own end, that means level with the other team's defenceman on your side, at their point, between that defenceman and the slot.

Those are the spots under a low zone collapse. Under man-on-man, the safe spot is goal side of the player you are covering, wherever they go. Ask your bench which one you are playing.

Why a safe spot, and not the puck? Because getting there a beat late costs your team very little. Chasing the puck into somebody else's space is how an opponent ends up alone.

Fourth idea. Two things that don't bend, whatever position you play and whatever system your team runs.

The first is at the boards. Never turn your back to the checker. And never duck.

Both protect your head and neck. If you turn your back, the contact becomes a hit from behind, which you can't see coming or brace for. Even a light hit from behind can hurt your head or neck. If you duck, your head meets the boards first, and that can hurt your neck even going slowly. So a slow checker doesn't make it safe.

Here is the position you aim for. Skates parallel to the boards, feet apart, knees bent. Forearm and hip to the boards. Never the point of your shoulder, and not the whole shoulder if you can help it. Your shoulder faces the checker. It is the boards it should not meet. Head up, chin off your chest.

If you are already facing the boards and can't turn, that is the last resort, not a choice. Get your stick and gloves up and spread on the glass, and keep your head off it.

From here on I will use one short version of that position, the same words every time, so you can learn it like a line. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the checker, never duck.

The second thing that doesn't bend is at the faceoff dot. Never push your head out over the dot to get low. Get low from your knees and hips instead. Even two players who both get low the right way end up with their heads a few inches apart. Nothing makes that space safe. So keep your head back.

Nothing about being new to a position changes either of those.

Let me pull those four ideas together, because they are the core of any switch.

One. Your old good habits are the problem. Name the one you are fighting, out loud, before the shift.

Two. Ask your coach which system you are playing, before you play in it.

Three. When you are lost, go to the safe spot and read the play from there.

Four. At the boards, the same position every time. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the checker, never duck. At the dot, get low from your knees and hips, never with your head.

Everything else in this episode adds detail to one of those four. So now let's go through the switches, starting with the most common one.

Fifth idea. Moving from the wing to centre.

As a winger, your job had a home. You had a side, and you held it. As a centre, your job has no home. The short version is this. Go to the puck, and get underneath it.

Start in your own end. Under a low zone collapse, your job moves with the puck. When the puck goes low into a corner, go down and help your defenceman there. When the puck leaves that corner, come back to the high slot.

Going into that corner means a puck battle, so here is how you go in. Go for the puck, not the player. Stick on the puck, not a hit. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call. If you can see their numbers, pull up and play the puck. And at the boards, the same position. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the checker, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly. If you end up facing the boards, stick and gloves up and spread on the glass, head off it.

Why do teams play the collapse? Because it protects the middle of the ice just by where everyone stands. You don't need perfect reads for the front of your net to be full. It is the easiest system to learn, because the areas stay fixed even when the play is chaotic. And the wingers are already high, so they are two outlets the moment you win the puck.

Not every team plays it. Under man-on-man, you follow an assigned opponent instead, wherever they go. Teams play it because nobody is ever left unmarked. And pressure everywhere means bad passes everywhere, so it wins pucks back.

Under a hybrid, it is man-on-man below the faceoff dots and zone above them. It is a deliberate mix. You cover a player close to your net, where an unmarked opponent is most dangerous, and an area further out. The price is that it is the hardest of the three to learn, because everyone has to agree where the line is.

The job is really different in each one. So asking which your team plays is the single most useful thing a new centre can do.

Now the breakout. This is the habit that catches new centres hardest.

In the usual breakout, called the swing, the centre swings low. That means inside the faceoff dots and below the hash marks. Below the hash marks is lower than it sounds. Here is why you go that deep. It gives your defenceman a pass through the middle of the ice, not just one up the boards.

The common mistake is standing where a winger stands, out by the boards. Then one opponent can cover two of you, and your defenceman has no pass through the middle.

The swing isn't the only breakout. In a centre fly, you leave early up the middle, and a winger drops in low to support instead. In a flat or spread breakout, the wingers hold higher, and the exit uses longer passes. So find out which one you are in.

You also call the line change now. Call it earlier than you used to.

Coaches will tell you the centre skates the most. Half of that has been measured, and it goes the other way. Defencemen skate more in total, and forwards skate harder per minute. Nobody has measured centres against wingers. What a centre really does is work more of the ice, all three zones and the full width.

And you take faceoffs now. That is a whole new skill, and it can be learned. Among regular centres in the top professional league, win rates spread far too wide to be luck. When something varies that much, work moves it. Start with your stance, your grip, and the draw in your own end. And take draws at the end of every practice.

And stop worrying about scoring for now. Your value as a new centre is in being where you are supposed to be, taking the draw, and being there underneath the puck. The goals come later. And they come from the middle of the ice, which is where you are standing now.

Sixth idea. Your head at the faceoff dot, in more detail.

Getting low on a draw is right. Here is why. You react faster, and you can't be pushed off the dot. But the depth comes from your knees and hips, not from your head.

If you push your head out, the two centres can meet helmet to helmet before the puck drops. In some leagues that costs you the draw, and the referee can add a two-minute penalty. So don't plan on it being free.

The officials step in before anything touches, too. If two centres lean in until their heads are almost ear to ear, the linesperson tells both of them to move back. Move back the first time you are told.

But the penalty isn't the main reason. Your head is. Even with a safe stance, two heads end up a few inches apart on every draw. No rulebook makes that space safe. Get low from your knees and hips, every draw.

Seventh idea. Moving from centre to the wing.

It often feels like a demotion. It usually isn't one. Coaches move centres to the wing for good reasons. To put a defensively reliable player on a line that needs one. To put a good shot in a shooting position. To ease a young player's load. Or simply because they have four centres, and three of them have to play somewhere.

Your new job starts with your lane. Hold it. Here is why. Three forwards in three lanes stretch the other team's defence across the whole width of the rink. Three forwards in one lane can be covered by two defenders.

A former centre drifts toward the middle, because the middle is where the decisions are. That drift squashes your line's spacing every time. So stay out wide. This isn't about your personality. It is about spacing.

In your own end, under the low zone collapse, you cover one player. That is the other team's defenceman on your side, up at their point. Stand between that defenceman and the slot. Why there? From that spot you take away their shot and their pass into the middle at the same time. Under man-on-man, you follow that defenceman wherever they go, into the corner too.

Now watch one phrase, stay high, because it means two different things. The first meaning is covering their point while they have the puck. The second is holding up near the blue line in some breakouts. The two spots are about twenty feet apart. And the first one runs out.

It runs out the moment your team wins the puck. In the swing breakout, you then come down the boards to the outlet spot, between the hash marks and the goal line, to take the first pass. A winger who never leaves the point is as wrong as one who leaves it early.

That trip down the boards is a system, not a law. A flat or spread breakout holds you higher, toward the blue line. A stretch breakout sends one winger up the ice early. So ask which one yours plays.

And stop organising everybody. You have spent your hockey life as the connecting player, directing traffic. Still call what you can see, because talking is everyone's job. But the rotations aren't yours to manage any more. Chasing them is how a winger ends up out of their lane.

You will also touch the puck less. Across a whole game, the difference is smaller than it feels. What really changes is the shape. Fewer touches, in a narrower band of ice, and more of them at speed along the boards.

Now faceoffs, because you have a new job there too. Stand still on your own side of the dot, outside the circle, with your blade behind your hash marks. Keep your hands to yourself until the puck lands.

Here is why it matters. You have spent your hockey life in the dot. Off it, you can lose a draw before it starts, without touching the puck. Three things can do it. Moving into the circle early. Lining up offside. Or touching the player beside you. In most leagues, any of those gets your centre thrown out of the faceoff, and someone else has to take it. And in every league, a hand or a stick that gets in the other player's way can be called as a two-minute penalty.

Eighth idea, still on the wing. Winning the puck on the boards.

Winning a loose puck against the boards is the most important physical skill a winger has. That is a coaching ranking, not a measured one. And it is something centres don't practise much.

Here is how. Keep the puck on the far side of your body from the checker. Then your body is between the checker and the puck. Stand side-on, with your hip and backside to the checker, feet wide. If your league doesn't allow checking, hold your spot and don't bump or lean on them; in a Canadian adult league on the CARHA rulebook, stay out of the contact. And know where the puck is going before you get there.

If you arrive second, it is stick on the puck, not a hit. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call. If you can see their numbers, pull up and play the puck.

One thing overrides all of that. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the checker, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly. If you end up facing the boards anyway, stick and gloves up and spread on the glass, head off it.

Let me pull the forward switches together.

Moving to centre, go to the puck and get underneath it. In your own end, find out whether you are in a collapse, man-on-man or a hybrid. Swing low on the breakout, inside the dots and below the hash marks, so your defenceman has the middle. Learn faceoffs, and get low from your knees and hips.

Moving to the wing, hold your lane, because three wide forwards stretch the defence. In your own end, cover their point until your team wins the puck, then come down the boards. On the draw, stand still with your hands to yourself. And on the boards, the same position. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the checker, never duck.

Ninth idea. Moving from forward to defence. This is the biggest change in hockey short of putting on goalie pads. Two of the three things that change are about attitude, and you can fix those in a couple of weeks. The third is physical, and you can't.

The physical part is skating backwards. Your gap is the space you keep between yourself and a puck carrier skating at you. Holding a tight gap is entirely a skating problem. You can't do it against a fast forward unless you can travel backwards nearly as fast as they travel forwards.

If you can't, you do one of two things. You leave a huge gap and get walked into your zone. Or you pivot too early, turning from backwards to forwards, and get beaten by the first change of direction. Both look like bad decisions. They are really skating problems. That is why a new defenceman so often decides they can't read the game, when the real fix is in their feet.

So the first thing you need is three skating skills. The backward C-cut, one foot at a time, pushing out to full extension, with your weight onto the pushing foot. Not weakly with both feet at once, which is the slow way most beginners skate backwards. Backward crossovers, which is where backward speed really comes from. And pivots both ways, practised on purpose.

Which way do you pivot? The usual coaching instruction is toward the boards. Here is why. If the pivot goes wrong, you are beaten toward the boards, not into your own slot. That is a coaching default, not a law. Some coaches want a middle pivot on certain reads. So ask yours, and get good at both, because the right pivot depends on where the attacker is, not on which way you happen to be good.

Book a power skating session. Everything tactical about playing defence depends on this. And be patient. Coaches estimate a few months for good stopping and turning both ways, and a season for a properly rebuilt stride. Those are experienced guesses, not measurements.

Tenth idea. The two habits to take onto the ice as a new defenceman.

The first. Be goal side of your attacker, between them and your net. Stay on the defensive side of the puck. If an attacker ever gets between you and your net, it has already gone wrong.

The second. Never carry the puck, or pass it, across the front of your own net. When you need to get it to your partner, send it behind your net. Here is why. If you lose it behind the net, it is a battle behind the goal line. If you lose it in your own slot, it is a shot from point-blank range.

Some teams pass between their defencemen high up instead, above the tops of the faceoff circles. Ask which route yours uses. But never through your own slot.

Both habits come from one change. As a forward, a bad read had two layers of players behind it. As a defenceman it has one, your goalie. That should change how you weigh risk. It shouldn't change how hard you compete.

You also play as half of a pair. One defenceman goes to the puck. The other guards the goalmouth. Guarding the goalmouth means being there, between the attackers and your net. No shove, no lean, never your stick across anyone. You swap every time the puck crosses the middle of the ice, and you talk the whole time. Under a low zone collapse, the goalmouth is your job and the high slot is the centre's. Under man-on-man, the front of the net belongs to whoever's player is standing there.

When you are the one going to the puck, it is a puck battle. Stick on the puck, not a hit. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call. If you can see their numbers, pull up and play the puck. And at the boards, the same position. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the checker, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly. If you end up facing the boards, stick and gloves up and spread on the glass, head off it.

Now the forward's habit that hurts most here. A forward who moves back tends to chase the puck. Then both defencemen are on it, and nobody is in front of the net. Coaches at every level call that one of the commonest ways to give up goals, though nobody has counted it.

Eleventh idea. Your first odd-man rush.

On a two-on-one, two attackers come at you alone. The common instruction is to take away the pass and give up the shot. Sit in the passing lane, with your stick flat on the ice on the passing side. Let your goalie take the shooter.

Why that way round? Because a pass across to an open forward is a chance your goalie can't stop. The shooter is the one your goalie can play.

The realistic alternative is a timed slide across the lane, to force the shot. Slide into the lane, never into the carrier's skates. In most leagues, a slide that takes out the carrier is tripping, even if you touch the puck first. And a slide that catches them low, on the legs, can be clipping, which can get you thrown out of the game. So ask which your team plays, and make sure your goalie agrees.

Either way, don't commit to the carrier early.

Twelfth idea. Forcing the carrier outside, which is not the same as getting beaten wide.

New defencemen hear both instructions, and decide the position contradicts itself. It doesn't.

Forcing them outside means skating your route so the inside lane is shut. The carrier's only way forward runs toward the boards. There, the boards act like a second defender, and the shooting angle is poor. You are still between the carrier and your net. That is the whole point.

Getting beaten wide means the carrier gets past your outside shoulder, with open ice behind you. You are no longer between them and the net, and there is nothing left.

Here is the surprising part. In both cases, the carrier can skate exactly the same line. What is different is where you are. Being beaten wide means nobody is inside the carrier when they decide where to go next. So the habit to build isn't never let anyone get outside. It is stay on the defensive side of the puck.

How much the boards help depends on the rink. On narrow ice, they do a lot of the work. On wide ice, a carrier you have forced outside still has room to keep skating. So there, your gap and your stick matter more than steering. In Britain, assume your rink is on the narrow side, and look at the building you actually play in.

Now the half of this most converting forwards are never told. The angle is yours to take. The contact is not.

Steering means skating a line that leaves the carrier only one way to go. Pick your line early, hold it, and add nothing when you arrive. Done that way, with no contact, steering works in every league. No bump, no push, no shove, and no lean. Don't leave your lane to make contact, and don't put a hip or a shoulder out after you have angled them. Never your stick across them, and never steer them with your stick. Never into their back, and if you can see their numbers, pull up. Never take anyone's skates off the ice. And even going slowly, a crash into the boards can hurt.

If you hook a carrier and steer them dangerously into the boards, plan on being thrown out of the game.

Riding the carrier into the boards with your body is something else. That is a body check. And a lot of hockey doesn't allow body checking at all. Younger age groups, up to about twelve or thirteen. Girls' and women's hockey. And adult leagues that play without it. A local league can ban it in any age group too. So whether you may hit at all is your league's call. Find out before you hit anyone.

In a game without checking, a body check is a penalty. If it is reckless or someone gets hurt, plan on being thrown out of the game. A reckless one can even be a match penalty, which goes past being thrown out.

In the Women's NIHL, Britain's women's league, plan on no hit at all. Steer with your skating, never step or glide into the other player, and never use the boards to pin them or push them. In any other British women's competition, ask before you hit anyone.

Where checking is allowed, the ride still has a limit. The player giving the check is always the one responsible. Sending a player dangerously into the boards is boarding, even when they saw you coming. That includes a player racing for the puck on an icing. Boarding can get you thrown out of the game, and a reckless one can be a match penalty.

Now the most dangerous moment. A carrier you have steered to the boards is very often shielding the puck, half turned away, by the time you get there. If you can see their numbers, pull up. Take the puck, not the player.

Here is why, and it is the rule that matters most here. A hit from behind is never just two minutes. In almost every league, it throws you out of the game. On a player standing at the boards, a hard one throws you out in every league. Sometimes the player you hit from behind goes into the boards unable to protect themselves. Then, in several leagues, plan on a match penalty, with no choice for the referee. If the hit reaches their head as well, you take whichever penalty is harsher.

So steer with your skating. When you see their numbers, pull up, and play the puck.

And if the carrier does get past you, and you are chasing from behind, your stick goes on the puck and nothing else. A hook, a trip, a hold or a slash from behind that stops a scoring chance gives them a penalty shot. That is a free shot, alone against your goalie. And if your net is empty, the goal can simply be given to them.

Thirteenth idea. Getting the puck out, and when to go deep.

Never shoot the puck over the glass from inside your own end. Off the glass and along the boards is fine. Over it is two minutes in many leagues, including all hockey in Britain, whether you meant it or not. New defencemen take that penalty all the time. And putting the puck out of play on purpose, to stop the game, is two minutes everywhere, from anywhere on the ice.

You will also ice the puck more than you used to. In many leagues, the team that ices it can't change lines before the next faceoff, and that faceoff is in its own end. So an icing keeps a tired pair out there, facing a draw by its own net. An injured player can still come off.

In their end, you have three depths. Just inside the blue line is your default. The top of the faceoff circle is a step down, and you can still get back from there. Below the circles is a commitment you can't recover from. Only go that deep if a forward has already taken your spot at the blue line.

Only one defenceman pinches at a time. Pinching means stepping down from the blue line to keep the puck in. That is a coaching habit rather than a rule, and coaches do call exceptions. When you pinch, it is stick on the puck, not a hit. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call. If you see their numbers, pull up. And along the boards, the same position. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the checker, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly. If you end up facing the boards, stick and gloves up and spread on the glass, head off it.

And stop worrying about offence for now. Your shot and your vision will show up on their own once the defending is automatic. Jumping into the rush before you can hold a gap just gets you back to your own blue line late and out of breath.

Let me pull defence together. Book the power skating first, because a gap you can't hold is a skating problem, not a bad read. Be goal side. Never move the puck across the front of your own net. Go behind it, or high if your team does. Play as a pair, one on the puck and one at the goalmouth, and talk. On a two-on-one, find out whether you sit in the lane or slide. Force the carrier outside with your route, not your body. When you see their numbers, pull up. Never put the puck over the glass from your own end. And go deep in their end only when somebody has taken your spot.

Fourteenth idea. Moving from defence up to forward.

This is the change people overlook. It is often the harder one in your head, because you are unlearning caution you were praised for, for years.

As a defenceman, you closed space down and took options away. A forward's job is the mirror of that. Find the space nobody is defending. Arrive in it late and at speed. Make somebody decide who is taking you.

The habit that has to go is waiting until the play is obviously safe before you move. Here is why. By the time it is obviously safe, the space has closed. So go to the open ice before it looks open.

Your old job helps you on the forecheck. Forechecking means pressuring the other team in its own end, to win the puck back. As a defenceman, you read every forecheck that came at you. How many came in. Which side the first one pushed you to. Whether the pressure sealed you in or overcommitted. Now you are the one applying it, and that knowledge is worth a lot.

The forwards on a forecheck are called F one, F two and F three. They are roles, set by who gets there first. They are not positions, and they are not people. If the puck goes in deep and you are first to it, you are F one, whatever your jersey says. Go. If you hesitate, nobody is F one.

When you go, go for the puck. Stick on the puck, not a hit. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call. If you see their numbers, pull up and play the puck. And at the boards, the same position. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the checker, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly. If you end up facing the boards, stick and gloves up and spread on the glass, head off it.

How many forwards go in deep is your team's system. A two-one-two sends two in. A one-two-two sends one. And which side F three sits on is a real coaching split. The common default is the far side, away from the puck. The other version, called stacked, puts F three on the puck side. It crowds one corner on purpose, and gives up the pass out to the far side. Ask which one you are running.

On the backcheck, the usual plan is the centre in the middle and the wingers in the outside lanes. That is a coaching choice, not a law. Some teams send the first player back to the middle instead. Others pick up man-on-man, starting from the puck and working outward. Whichever you play, somebody owns the middle, and nobody assumes it is somebody else.

Crossing their blue line, keep your trailing skate down on the ice. You are only offside when both skates are completely over the line before the puck. So a trailing skate still touching the line keeps you onside. Keep the blade on the ice, and you are onside wherever you play. If you lift it, some leagues call you offside.

Work on your first three strides and your stop-and-start. Here is why. A forward's shift is one burst after another. Forecheck, reverse, backcheck, reverse again. A defenceman's skating has more gliding and more backward work. Straight-line speed matters less than how fast you get to it.

And stop worrying about staying above the puck, on your own net's side of it. That was the right instinct, and it isn't yours to have any more. Somebody is above the puck. On most shifts, it isn't you.

Fifteenth idea. Playing your off wing.

Your off wing means a left shot playing right wing, or a right shot playing left wing. The puck sits on the inside of your body, toward the middle of the ice. It isn't a demotion or a promotion, and it is more common than new players expect.

Why do coaches do it? Coming into their end with your stick toward the middle, you have more options with the puck. And you get a better forehand shooting angle from the middle of the ice. A winger on their strong side has the stick along the boards, and needs more time to get into a shooting position. On the power play, the off-wing half-wall is standard, because the puck on your inside keeps the cross-ice one-timer ready.

Here is what it costs. In your own end, a breakout pass up the boards now arrives on your backhand. That costs a beat, and it can weaken your exit.

The fix is to receive it open to the ice. As the puck comes, open your hips toward the ice, so you can see the rink and the puck at once. And take a step or two off the boards. Here is why. That space lets you skate it, pass to the centre, pass back to your defenceman, or attack the middle. Flat against the boards, you get one of those. If the puck arrives hard, angle a skate to kill it onto your stick, rather than reaching for it.

And because you are on the boards, the same position comes with you. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the checker, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly. And don't turn to face the boards to find your forehand. Looking first and seeing nobody coming doesn't make any of it safe.

A defenceman on their off side is different again. Pucks behind your own net, and passes from your partner, now come to your backhand. It is common, and it isn't a disaster.

Sixteenth idea. Going in goal.

Playing in goal is really a different sport that shares the rink. You spend the whole game in one zone, you almost never carry the puck, and you pressure nobody. It isn't a position you drift into for a few weeks to see how it goes.

The skating is different. It is short pushes sideways, in and out along your angle, post to post, and back up onto your feet. Almost all of it is on your inside edges, in a deep stance. And the technique is very hard to correct on your own, because you can't see yourself, and a goal going in doesn't tell you why. So it needs its own coaching from the start.

So before you buy anything, ask your club whether it has house goalie gear and a goalie coach. Go to a goalie session, not a team practice, where you will just get shot at. And give it a block of weeks, not one night, because the first night tells you little in either direction.

And you can't just pick up pads in the middle of a game. In most leagues, a skater isn't allowed to wear goalie gear at all, and the exceptions are narrow. Where a league doesn't ban it, a skater who goes in without being listed as the goalie gets none of a goalie's protection. Who goes in net is settled between your bench and the officials, on the scoresheet. Never by you grabbing a spare set of pads. Going back out works the same way. It is paperwork, not just a decision.

Wear a neck guard from your first time in net, whatever your age. In Britain it is compulsory for every player at every age, adults too. In England and Wales you need it at practice as well, and wear it at practice in Scotland and Northern Ireland too. The adult exemption people quote is American. And in Canadian minor and women's hockey, a throat guard hanging off your mask doesn't count on its own.

And one thing for every skater, whether or not you ever play in goal. Most goals your team gives up from the front of the net are not your goalie's fault. They come from skater habits. So learn what your goalie needs from you.

Let me pull those last three together. Moving up to forward, go to the space before it is obviously safe. First to the puck on the forecheck, you are F one, so go, stick on the puck. Keep your trailing skate on the ice at the blue line. On your off wing, take the pass open to the ice, a step off the boards, with the same boards position. And going in goal, get a goalie coach and house gear, settle it on the scoresheet, and wear a neck guard from your first time in net.

Seventeenth idea. How long it takes.

Nobody has measured how long a switch takes, so this is coaching experience, and people vary a lot. A few shifts to stop feeling lost. You will know where to stand long before you know when to move. A handful of games before the everyday reads are automatic. Who you pick up. Where you go when the puck goes to a particular place. A season before the position feels like yours, not like a job you are doing carefully. And longer, maybe much longer, for forward to defence, because that one depends on skating.

Expect a real dip in how well you play. Here is why. New techniques feel worse before they feel better, and a switch is a big version of that. The dip will feel like proof you can't play the position. It isn't.

Coaches see most people give up somewhere between week two and week five, right at the bottom of the dip. That is what coaches see, not a measurement. So judge the switch over at least a couple of months, not a couple of skates. Longer for defence, and longer again for goal. And improvement comes in steps, not a smooth line. Flat spells are normal.

Eighteenth idea. Asking for a switch, and being moved without asking.

If you want to move, ask at the right time. Not on the bench. Not straight after a game. Not in front of the group. A day or two later, in private.

Ask a specific question. What would I need to show you to get a look at centre? That is hard to dodge, and it gives you something to work on. Can I play centre just gets a shrug.

Ask to try it in practice first. That is much easier for a coach to say yes to than a change on the game sheet, and it costs them nothing. Then do the work where the coach can see it. If the answer is, I need to see you win draws, take draws at the end of every practice.

And accept that some answers are about the roster, not about you. A team with four centres and no left wingers is going to keep you on the left.

If you have been moved without being asked, it is usually not a punishment. The commonest reasons are the numbers on the roster, an injury, a line that needs a reliable defensive player, or a spot that suits you better. Some of those are compliments. Moving a forward to defence is often what happens to the player a coach trusts most with the puck in their own end.

So find out the system before you find out your feelings about it. Ask what a good shift from you looks like in that spot. Coaches rarely get asked, so the answer is more useful than you expect. And if you were told bluntly, that doesn't make the decision wrong.

Don't play the new position badly to win the old one back. It looks like sulking, even when it isn't. And it is the surest way to make a temporary move permanent.

And tell your linemates you are new to it. A line where nobody has said the centre is new will make the same mistake for three games. Thirty seconds of talk before the shift fixes most of it.

Let me pull it all together.

When you are moved, your skills come with you. What you rebuild is the map. Where to look, where to drift, and who to pick up.

Your old good habits are the problem. Name the one you are fighting, out loud, before the shift. Ask your coach which system you are playing, before you play in it. And when you are lost, go to the safe spot and read the play from there.

At the boards, it is always the same. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the checker, never duck. At the dot, get low from your knees and hips, never with your head.

Moving to centre, go to the puck and swing low. Moving to the wing, hold your lane, and stand still on the hash marks. Moving to defence, fix your backward skating first. Be goal side, and never move the puck across your own net. Steer the carrier with your route, not your body, and when you see their numbers, pull up. Moving up to forward, go to the space before it is safe. On your off wing, take the pass open to the ice. And going in goal, settle it on the scoresheet, and wear a neck guard from day one.

Then expect the dip, and keep going through it.

Much of this depends on what your team plays. So here are three questions to ask your coach. What system do we play in our own end? Which forecheck and which breakout do we run? And what does a good shift from me look like in this spot? And one for your league. Is body checking allowed in our games?

Go and ask. Their answers tell you how your team wants you to play, and now you know why each answer makes sense.

### 8. Departures from the source and the template

- **Shape.** The template's spine is four tactical ideas for one position. Here the spine is the four ideas that hold for every switch (habit, system, safe spot, the two things that don't bend). The rest is one part per switch, not one end of the rink at a time, because the source is organised by switch.
- **Three questions to ask your coach plus one for the league**, as the brief asks; the template has five plus two.
- **The boards posture is taught in full in the spine**, not where the source teaches it (:211, the centre-to-wing wall-play section), because the source's Overview (:35) makes it universal.
- **The pressure gates** (corner support, D on the puck, pinch, F one) are not in the source at those acts. They come from brief item 13. Their content traces to :282, :294 and :211.
- **The steering limbs** draw on `body_contact_and_battles.md` (allowed second source): add nothing, no lean, skates off the ice, slow speed, head off the glass, shoulder faces the checker.
- **The system whys** come from `defensive_zone_coverage.md`. `switching_positions.md` gives none for the collapse, man-on-man or the hybrid. For the breakout variants, the forecheck depth, the F3 side and the backcheck lanes the source gives no why, so the script states how each works and stops.
- **No "If you are a parent"** content (owner rule).
- **The slot** is defined by generalising :96; see §6.

### 9. What I found unclear in the source

- **The safe spot for a winger is "level with their defenceman… between them and the slot" (:96), but :182 adds "denying the shot and the seam at once".** Consistent, but :96 is the only place the safe spot is framed as a default, not as the job.
- **:211 says "contact on your forearm and hip".** The Style B standard §2 bans "take the contact on your forearm and hip" in scripts because, heard once, it is ambiguous. The source sentence has the same ambiguity. The script uses "forearm and hip to the boards". Worth a corpus row.
- **The off-wing CARHA "put your back to the boards" (:423, :438)** sits two lines from "never turn your back to the checker" (:422). The source reconciles them in prose, but a listener hearing both would not. The script voices neither the CARHA wording nor the reconciliation.
- **:326's "In every book but CARHA, a slide that catches them at or below the knees is clipping"** is unscoped as to whether a slide that reaches the puck first is also excused from clipping under Hockey Canada and CARHA. The script says "in most leagues… tripping" and "clipping… can get you thrown out" without that question.
- **Two "up to about twelve or thirteen" age bands** (USA Hockey 12U and below, Hockey Canada U13 and below, :282, :284). The script says "up to about twelve or thirteen", which does not match a British reader's U-band names.

### 10. Where this script is weakest

- **The steering unit is long and rule-heavy** (about 3,100 characters, from "Now the half of this" to the end of the chase-from-behind paragraph). It is the one place the episode sounds like a rulebook. Every sentence in it is a limb the brief or the source requires, so I did not cut it. It is also why the rules share is not lower.
- **The gate is said five times in near-identical words** ("Stick on the puck, not a hit. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call. If you see their numbers, pull up."), and the short form ten times. That is the brief's requirement, and the template's repetition principle. A listener may find it repetitive.
- **"The shooter is the one your goalie can play"** is my restatement of "let your goalie take the shooter", and is the weakest "why" in the script.
- **The defence part runs from 42% to 70%**, about twelve minutes with one recap at its end. It is the densest stretch.
- **"In Britain it is compulsory for every player at every age"** holds for the In-House Rules and the Elite League both, but the Elite League warns first. A British listener is not told that.
- Backward and forward pointers were read for shape by hand. The remaining signposts are the recap openers and "From here on I will use one short version", both from the template.
