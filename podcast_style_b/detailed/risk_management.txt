===== SCRIPT START =====

This episode is about decisions. What to do with the puck, and how to keep your mistakes cheap. It takes its time. For each idea I will tell you what to do, and also why it works. If you know why something works, you can still make the right choice when the play looks different from the picture in your head.

Before any instructions, a few words you will hear all the way through.

Your own end and their end. Your own end is the zone with your net in it. Their end is the zone with their net in it. The middle third of the rink, between the two blue lines, is the neutral zone.

The slot. This is the ice in front of the net, between the faceoff circles. It is where the most goals are scored from. Your own slot is the one in front of your own goalie.

A turnover. That is losing the puck to the other team.

D-to-D. That is a pass from one of your defencemen to the other.

The forecheck. That is the other team chasing the puck in your end, to win it back. The players doing it are the forecheckers.

An odd-man rush. That is an attack where the attackers have more players than the defenders have back. A two-on-one, for example, is two attackers against one defender.

That is the vocabulary. Now the decisions.

Every time the puck comes to you, you are making a bet. How likely is the play to work, and what do you get if it does? How likely is it to fail, and what does that cost you? You already do this, in about a third of a second, without noticing. You get better at it by doing it on purpose, in practice, on video and on the bench. A habit you never think about doesn't get better on its own. It just gets faster.

Here is why doing it on purpose matters. When a risky pass works, everyone sees it. When it fails, the goal usually comes about eight seconds later. By then it looks like somebody else's mistake. A missed cover, a screened goalie, a bad bounce. So most players never connect their decision to the goal it caused. Start connecting it yourself, and your decisions will improve faster than any skill you can practise.

I will start with four big ideas, because they matter most. If you only listen to the first part of this episode, take those four with you. After that we will go back through the rink, one end at a time, and add the detail.

First idea. Before you decide what to do, think about where you are standing.

What a mistake costs depends more on where you are than on what you try. The same pass can be a great play in the corner of their end and a disaster in your own slot. Risk belongs to the place, not the play. So change where you are before you decide. Often one stride to better ice makes every option you have better.

Here is how coaches grade the ice. The question behind it is simple. If I lose the puck here, how much time, and how many of my players, stand between that turnover and a shot on my net?

Your own slot is the worst place of all. Lose the puck there and it is very nearly a goal against. Their player has it right where goals come from. Your goalie isn't set, and your defenders are facing the wrong way.

The rest of your own end is high risk. Everyone is home, which helps. But the puck is only one pass from your slot.

The middle of the neutral zone is high risk too. A turnover there catches two or three of your players skating the wrong way at speed. Your own end is dangerous but organised. The middle of the ice is dangerous and disorganised. That is where odd-man rushes against you get made.

Their blue line is moderate. How bad a turnover there is depends on how many of your players are ahead of the puck.

And their corners, and the ice behind their goal line, are nearly free. You are a long way from your own net, with all five teammates between the puck and danger. They would need to break out, cross the middle and get into your end before they could hurt you. So that is where to be at your most creative.

That order is coaching consensus. It is worked out from where goals come from, and how long it takes to recover. Nobody has measured all five against each other. So trust the order more than the labels.

There is one thing the grading can't show you. A turnover has a direction. Losing the puck while it is travelling toward your own net is much worse than losing it while it is moving away. So is losing it to an opponent already facing your net. Here is why. Lose it that way, and their players already face your goal while yours face the other way. Their attack starts at the same moment as your turnover. Lose it deep in their end, moving away from your net, and they have the whole rink to cross. That gives your team four or five seconds to get organised. That is coaching consensus too, not a measured comparison. So a blind pass up the boards out of your own end is a bad idea. The same blind pass in their end is a nothing play.

Second idea. Get the puck out of your own slot first. And never pass the puck, or skate it, across the front of your own goal.

The front of your own net is the worst place on the ice to lose the puck. So never send it across there, by a pass or by carrying it. Take it to the boards, behind the net, or off the glass.

Don't stickhandle in your own slot either. Stickhandling buys time. In your own slot there is no time to buy, and nothing worth buying it for. Your head is down and you are surrounded. The best that can happen is that you get out, having gained nothing. The worst is that you lose it, having given away everything. So move the puck out of the slot before you do anything else with it. One stride to the boards, behind the net, or a hard shot off the glass. Change where you are first. Then make the decision.

The same goes for D-to-D. In your own end, that pass can go through the front of your net, or behind it. Go behind it. Here is why. A pass through your slot travels across the most dangerous ice there is. It goes at knee height, through the exact space where a forechecker is standing. If it is picked off, their player has the puck in your slot, facing your goalie. That is not a chance against. That is a goal. Behind the net, the pass is about eight feet longer and half a second slower. But if it goes wrong there, you get a battle behind the goal line, not a shot from twenty feet.

This is a coaching habit, not a rule. Some teams do allow a D-to-D pass high in the zone, above the tops of the faceoff circles. That is far enough out that losing it isn't a chance from the slot. So ask where your coach draws the line.

Third idea. One defenceman goes to the puck, and the other stays home. And say out loud which of you is which.

If both of you go to the same loose puck, you have put your whole defence into one fifty-fifty battle. Win it, and you have gained nothing that one of you couldn't have won. Lose it, and there is nobody between the puck and your goalie except forwards facing the wrong way.

So one goes and one supports. The partner reads where the puck is likely to squirt out, and covers that space, staying between the puck and the net. Say it out loud before either of you moves. Who has the puck, and who has the net.

The one who goes plays the puck, with the stick, not a hit. Whether you may body check at all is your league's call, so find out. Either way, take away the ice the player needs, and play the puck. And if you can see the numbers on their back, pull up.

That puck is often on the boards. So here is the position for meeting the boards, in full. I will call the player coming at you the checker, whether they are after the puck or just coming at you. Go into the boards on an angle if you can. Skates parallel to the boards, feet apart, knees bent. Forearm and hip to the boards. Never the point of your shoulder, and not the whole shoulder if you can help it. Head up, chin off your chest. Never turn your back to the checker, and never duck. Both of those protect your head and neck. Keep it up even going slowly. If the boards arrive with you already facing them and no time to turn, get your stick and gloves up on the glass, spread wide, head off it. That is only for when things have gone wrong.

From here on I will use one short version of that, word for word. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the checker, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly. If you end up facing the boards, stick and gloves up on the glass, spread wide, head off it.

Fourth idea. Play simple in your own end, and creative in theirs.

A missed chance in their end costs you a chance. A turnover in your own end costs you a goal. And a goal is worth a lot. A top professional team scores only about three goals a game. So one goal against is about a third of a night's work. One avoidable turnover in your own end can be worth more than a whole period of good attacking.

Try ten creative plays in their end and lose eight. You are still clearly ahead, because you got two chances you would never have had. Try ten in your own end and lose eight, and some of those eight end up in your net.

So in your own end, simple is usually right. That isn't because simple is good in itself. It is because the downside is huge and the upside is small. The best a fancy play in your own end can do is a clean breakout. The breakout is getting the puck out of your end under control, and the boring play usually gets you that too. In their end, creative is usually right. Failing there costs almost nothing, and a goal is the most valuable thing in hockey.

The players people call smart don't take the fewest risks. They take their risks in the right end of the rink.

Let me pull those four ideas together, because they are the core.

One. Before you decide, think about where you are, and move to better ice if you can. Your own slot is the worst place to lose the puck, and their corners are nearly free. Losing it while it travels toward your net is worse than losing it going away.

Two. Get the puck out of your own slot first, and never pass it or skate it across the front of your own goal. D-to-D goes behind the net.

Three. One defenceman to the puck, one at home, and say which. Go for the puck with your stick, not a hit, and if you see their numbers, pull up. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the checker, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly. If you end up facing the boards, stick and gloves up on the glass, spread wide, head off it.

Four. Simple in your end, creative in theirs.

Everything else in this episode adds detail to one of those four. So now let's go back through the rink, starting in your own end.

Fifth idea. Your own end, and the system behind it.

Sending one defenceman to the puck and keeping one at the net is the usual setup. It belongs to a system called the low zone collapse, where each player covers an area rather than a person. The defenceman on the puck side goes to the puck, with the stick and not a hit. The one on the far side stays home, between the puck and your net. That way, when a battle is lost, somebody is still between the puck and your goalie.

Some coaches set it up differently on purpose. When the puck is crowded into one side, they put both defencemen low along the boards, and give the slot to a forward instead. That comes from one coach's write-up, not from anything counted. Somebody must always be home. What varies is who. So ask which your team plays.

Sixth idea. Four more nevers for your own end, and the reason for each one.

Nearly every never in this episode is about your own end. That is because a turnover there costs a goal, not a chance. Most of them are coaching habits, not rules. So learn the reason, not just the never. The reason is what helps you when the play doesn't match the picture.

First. Never make a blind backhand pass up the middle of your own end. Blind means you can't see who you are passing to. Three things go wrong at once. Your backhand is your weakest side, so the pass is slow and less accurate. Blind means you don't know if it is going to a teammate or an opponent. And the middle of your own end is where losing it costs most. So turn your shoulders, and take the extra half second to look. Or put it on the boards, where a mistake becomes a battle rather than a chance. If you truly have no time, use the glass. Coaches name this as one of the commonest ways amateur teams give up goals. That is what coaches see, not a count. So let the reasoning persuade you, not the ranking.

Second. Never make a cross-ice pass in your own end, from one side of the ice to the other. It crosses every forechecker's stick. It spends longer in the air than any other pass, so they have the most time to read it. And whoever picks it off is already skating toward your net. Even when it works, your teammate usually gets it under pressure, facing the boards, because the forecheck slid across with the puck. You took a big risk to make things worse.

So move the puck up the ice, or behind the net. If the only open player is on the far side, go D-to-D behind the net first, then up. Two safe passes beat one risky one. Some teams accept a cross-ice pass above the tops of the circles. Almost nobody accepts one below them.

Third. Never change lines with the puck in your own end, or with the play coming at you. A line change is the weakest few seconds your team has. For part of it, some of your players are out of the play, and the other team can attack with five against your four.

So change when the puck is deep in their end, when your team clearly has it in the neutral zone, or at a stoppage. If you are tired and stuck in your own end, you are not tired enough to change. Get the puck out first, then change as the play moves up the ice.

Fourth. Off the glass, never over it. When you are in trouble, put the puck off the glass and out. Hard, low and along the boards, so it stays in play. The difference between a clear and a penalty is often a few degrees of your stick blade. So if you are panicking, aim lower than feels right.

This one is a rule as well as a habit. If you put it straight over the glass from your own end, in some leagues that is two minutes, even by accident. A puck that clips a stick or a body on its way out doesn't count. What counts is where the puck was when you hit it. If you do it on purpose, from anywhere, it is two minutes in every league. Don't aim at your own bench to get out of trouble, either. Over the glass there still counts. And clear it with your stick, not your skate. Even where an accidental one isn't a penalty, the faceoff comes back to your end, so it hasn't bought you anything. And if you are the goalie, don't count on an accidental one being free.

And never fire the puck away in temper after the whistle. Plan on two minutes for it, and in some leagues it can get you thrown out of the game.

Seventh idea. Your checklist for when you have just won the puck in your own end, and their forecheck is coming.

Usually your team picks its breakout by reading the forecheck in front of you. This list is for when there is no time to read anything. Hear it as a checklist for somebody under pressure, not a ranking of breakout plays. Go down it, and take the first option that is really there. And do your shoulder checks before the puck arrives. Then, by the time it is on your stick, you are already down to two options. The checklist is the decision. The look before is the information.

First, the quick-up. If your team has just won the puck, and their forwards are still below it, move it up the ice on your first touch. The two or three seconds after a team wins the puck are when the other team is least organised. That window closes fast, which is why it comes first. Look up the ice first, then down.

Second, up. Is a teammate open on your side, going up the ice, with nobody between you? Pass it. That is the fastest way out.

Third, the wheel. Do you have a clear lane to skate it out yourself? Round behind the net and up the far side, or straight up your own side. Then go. Carrying it out and passing it out both keep the puck, so carry it only when the lane is really clear.

Fourth, D-to-D, across to your partner, if your partner is open with time. Behind the goal line, never through the slot. It changes the side of the ice, and the forecheck has to start again.

Fifth, the reverse. Has the forechecker come in too hard and skated past you? Send the puck back the way it came, into the space they just left, to a teammate arriving behind you.

Sixth, the rim. A rim is a hard pass onward round the boards, to your winger's side. The reverse beats a forechecker who has gone past you. The rim beats a forechecker who is pinning you against the boards. They are opposite plays, chosen by the same read. Getting that read wrong is one of the commonest turnovers in your own end. A loose puck on the boards is fine. It is a battle, not a chance against.

Seventh, off the glass and out. No shame in it at all.

And if there's nothing? Take the hit, protect the puck against the boards, and accept the faceoff. Take the hit properly. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the checker, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly. If you end up facing the boards, stick and gloves up on the glass, spread wide, head off it.

Or ice it. Icing means shooting the puck from your own half all the way across their goal line, with nobody touching it. Play stops, and the faceoff comes back to your end. In some leagues your tired players can't change after it either, unless someone is hurt. Icing here is bad. But a penalty is worse, because it gives them a power play, an extra player while one of yours sits in the penalty box. So never solve it by putting the puck over the glass.

Eighth idea. Five plays that are almost always right.

None of these will make you the story of the game, and that is the point. Each one has a small upside and a very small downside.

One. Off the glass and out when you are in trouble. But know what it buys you. The puck lands in the neutral zone, which is still high risk, because odd-man rushes get made there. So you have moved from the worst ice to bad ice, not to safe ice. What you have bought is distance, and the two or three seconds your team needs to get set. So use them. Get organised. Don't assume the danger has passed.

Two. Shoot on net with traffic, meaning bodies in front of their goalie. A shot that reaches the net with a body in front makes rebounds, tips and scrambles. The worst that usually happens is a faceoff in their end, or a puck dug out of the corner. A pass instead of that shot can be picked off and go the other way.

Three. The simple play under pressure. When you are being closed down, every option gets harder. But the simple option gets harder the least. A give-and-go that works when you have three seconds becomes a giveaway when you have one.

Four. Put the puck deep when your line is tired. Tired players make late decisions, and late decisions in your own end are how goals happen. That is reasoning, not a measured effect. There is a bonus too, in some leagues. Shoot it in from your own side of the centre red line, and if their goalie freezes it, the faceoff is in their end, and their players can't change. Only an injury, or a penalty that changes the numbers on the ice, lets them. So their tired players are stuck out there while you get fresh legs on.

Five. The safe option in the last minute of a period. A goal against then leaves you no time to answer before the break, and they walk off with it. In that last minute, a cross-ice pass in your own end goes from a bad idea to unforgivable.

Underneath all five, here is the honest part. Safe is what you do under pressure. It isn't a way to play the whole game. One tracker coded about half the games of two seasons of top professional hockey. Carrying or passing the puck out of your end led to an attack into their zone nearly nine times in ten. Dumping it out worked about one time in five. But when a team kept the puck after a dump-out, it did just as well. So what matters is keeping the puck, not how you got it out. When you have time, make the possession play. When you don't, get it out.

Let me pull your own end together. Learn the reason for each never. No blind backhand up the middle. No cross-ice pass. No line change with the play coming at you. Off the glass, never over it. With the forecheck coming, go down the checklist. Quick-up, up, wheel, D-to-D, reverse, rim, glass. Take the first one that is really there. If nothing is, take the hit properly. And with time, keep the puck. Without it, get it out.

Ninth idea. The middle of the ice.

Carrying the puck through the neutral zone, here is the order to look in.

First, is anyone behind their defence? A pass into that space has the biggest upside here, and it travels away from your own net.

Second, is a winger with speed going up the boards? Pass it and follow. Passes up the ice to moving teammates are the safest useful play in this zone. That is coaching consensus, not a counted ranking.

Third, is the middle lane open, with a teammate supporting you? Then take it, but only with support. You are choosing the riskiest ice on the grading. A turnover in the middle hands them an odd-man rush going the other way.

Fourth, can you gain their blue line with control, with a teammate arriving? Carry wide, protect the puck on the outside, and drive the line.

And if none of that is there, chip it past their defenceman into space and race for it. Or dump it into the far corner and go after it.

Two things never, anywhere in this list. Never turn back into the middle at your own blue line with the puck on your stick. And never pass side to side across centre ice. Coaches name both as big makers of two-on-ones against, though nobody has counted it.

That is not a ban on going backwards. A regroup is the same retreat done properly. Give the puck back to a defenceman, and keep the D-to-D pass flat and hard. Never send it blind across the middle with a forechecker between you.

Tenth idea. Their blue line, and the forced pass.

Never force a risky pass at their blue line with nobody back. Here is why. At their blue line your attack is at its most stretched. Your forwards are ahead of the puck, and your defencemen have often stepped up to hold the zone. Lose it there, and your five-man attack becomes their three-man rush in one touch. The real cost isn't losing the puck. It is the odd-man rush that follows.

So run one test before that pass. If this pass is picked off right now, how many of my teammates are behind the puck? Fewer than two, don't make it. Chip it past the defender into the space behind them and race for it, or dump it into the far corner and forecheck.

Those aren't free either. Tracking in one Swiss league found that a dump-in so often hands the puck straight back that it gives up a little more than a carry-in does. So dump it when the pass isn't on. Don't dump it instead of a carry you could have made.

This one is a coaching habit, and it bends. A team that is trailing late, or built to attack in waves, may take this risk on purpose.

Let me pull the middle of the ice together. Look behind their defence first, then for a winger with speed. Take the middle lane only with support. Never turn back at your own blue line, and never pass across centre ice. Regroup instead. At their blue line, if fewer than two teammates are behind the puck, don't force the pass. Chip it or dump it, but only when you can't carry it in.

Eleventh idea. In their end, take your chances.

Their end is where you get to be dangerous. Failing there is cheap, and success there is worth more than anywhere else. Here is the order.

First, is there a lane to the net, with a body in front of their goalie? Shoot. Now.

If the body in front is you, stay outside the crease, the goalie's painted area, and off the goalie. Keep your spot. Don't push back or lean on anyone, and never your stick across someone. Any contact with a goalie can be a penalty, and it can get you thrown out of the game. A goalie who is down or turned can't protect themselves, so contact can hurt them. And keep your head up, chin off your chest. The goal post can hurt you the same way the boards can.

Second, is there a pass into the middle? Into the slot, to the back door, or across the middle of the ice. Make it. A pass across the middle makes their goalie move sideways and reset. Tracking from two European pro leagues found it the most valuable play they measured. It comes second only because a shooting lane is there far more often. A missed shot costs almost nothing, and a missed middle pass is the commonest way an attack turns into a rush against you. But when that pass is really there, it beats everything.

Third, can you get the puck to the net for a rebound or a tip, with traffic? Do it. A weak shot with a body in front beats a great pass that never happens.

Fourth, are you under pressure, with support below you? Take it behind the net or into the corner and cycle, keeping it with short passes low in their end. You keep the puck, you keep them in their end, and you tire them out.

Fifth, is the point open? The point is the area just inside their blue line, where your defencemen stand. Is there a clear lane from it, and a screen set? A screen is a player standing in their goalie's line of sight. Then pass back to the point. But open means a lane to the net, not just a lane to your defenceman.

And if there is nothing at all, chip it back deep and forecheck again. Don't force it out through their blue line. Losing the puck in their corner is free. Losing it at their blue line, with your defence pinched in, is a rush against.

Twelfth idea. Before you take a chance, ask whether you are anticipating or gambling.

Jumping a passing lane, pinching, stepping up and activating are all risks. A pinch is a defenceman stepping down from their blue line to keep the puck in their zone. Stepping up is moving up to challenge an attacker, in the neutral zone or at your own blue line. Activating is a defenceman joining the attack low in their zone. You need all of these, because hockey with no risk never scores. The question is never whether to take risks. It is whether this one is anticipation or a gamble.

Anticipating means acting on information. Gambling means acting on hope. When they work, they look exactly the same from the bench. But they are completely different decisions.

You have information if you can say why before you move. The carrier's hips and shoulders have turned toward a teammate. The carrier has looked at that teammate twice. That teammate's stick is out as a target. You have counted the carrier's other options, and there aren't any. Or you have seen this player make this exact pass already tonight. If the honest answer is that it felt like a pass was coming, you are gambling.

Then the second question. Not only, am I right? But, what happens if I am right and I still miss it? Even a well-read risk is a bad one if nobody covers the space you left.

Here is the picture. A pinch with your centre high in the zone behind you is a good pinch, even if you lose the puck. Your centre becomes the third defender, and the worst case is two against two. The same pinch with your centre down below their goal line is a bad pinch, even if you win the puck. You were one bounce from a two-on-one, and you didn't know it.

So here is the full test, three questions you can run in half a second. Do I have information, not just a feeling? If I miss, is someone behind me? And what does the scoreboard say I can afford? Two yeses, and a green light from the scoreboard, then go. Otherwise hold your position. Holding is a decision too, and often the right one.

When you do pinch or step up, go for the puck with your stick, not a hit. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call. If you can see their numbers, pull up. A pinch often meets the boards. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the checker, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly. If you end up facing the boards, stick and gloves up on the glass, spread wide, head off it.

Thirteenth idea. Stay on your skates.

When you close down a player with the puck, skate at them. Stay on your feet, and keep your stick in their passing lane. Take away their options rather than the puck. A defender on their feet with a good stick beats a defender sliding with a great one.

Here is why. You can't change direction in the air. A carrier who sees you leave your feet makes you miss with one move. Then you are on the ice behind the play for four or five seconds, just when your team needs all five players. And the upside is small. At best you break up one play. That is true even of a leap no referee would call.

Whether closing down can end in a body check depends on your league. Where checking isn't allowed, this is still how you close a carrier down, with your feet on the ice, your stick in the lane, and no hit. Where it is allowed, the check is made on your skates, with the same limits every time. Never a run-up. Building up speed into contact, even with two hard strides, can be charging. Never your stick across them. Never into their back. If you can see their numbers, pull up. Never lift anyone off their feet. Keep it off the head. Never drive or push anyone into the boards. Even going slowly, a hit into the boards can hurt. And never finish a check once the puck has gone.

Leaving your feet to hit is a rule, not just a habit. Jumping into a check is charging, in every league. A charge can get you thrown out of the game in every league, and if someone is hurt, some leagues make that automatic. A charge meant to hurt someone is a match penalty in most leagues, which goes past being thrown out.

On a two-on-one, whether you slide across the passing lane depends on your team's system. Where your job is to hold the lane, sliding early gives it away. Some teams coach a timed slide across the lane as the plan itself. So ask which yours plays. Either way, slide at the pass, never sprawl at the shot. And never slide into a player's legs. In most leagues that is a penalty even if you touched the puck first, and it can take them off their feet with nothing to break the fall.

Let me pull the attack together. In their end, shoot when there is a lane and a body in front. A middle pass, when it is really there, beats everything. Cycle under pressure, and never force it out through their blue line. Before a pinch, a step up or jumping a passing lane, ask the three questions. Information or a feeling? Who is behind me? What does the score allow? Go for the puck with your stick, not a hit. And stay on your skates.

Fourteenth idea. Let the scoreboard set your risk, not your personality.

The right amount of risk isn't part of who you are. It moves with the score and the clock.

Leading by one in the third period, take risk down. Reach for the safe options earlier than usual. Here is why. A goal against gives them the game back. A goal for only adds to a lead you already have.

Leading by three, take it right down. Nothing a creative play could win is worth what a comeback would cost. Manage the puck, forecheck, and get your changes.

Tied and early, play it straight. Run the checklists as they are.

Trailing late, take risk up. Pinch, activate, force your way into their zone, and try the riskier pass. When you are two down late, a goal against costs you almost nothing, and a goal for is worth everything. So a player who doesn't raise their risk when trailing isn't being safe. They are being unhelpful. When you pinch, go for the puck with your stick, not a hit. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call. If you can see their numbers, pull up.

Shorthanded, when you have fewer skaters because of a penalty, take risk down. A turnover in your own end is worse than usual. Clear it, and clear it hard. The one exception is a real two-on-one the other way, one you have actually read and not just hoped for. That is one of the best chances in hockey.

On the power play, when you have the extra player, be creative inside their end and careful at their blue line. A shorthanded rush against you is the worst thing that can happen on a power play.

If your league plays three-on-three overtime, be patient. In all that space, every turnover is an odd-man rush, and there is no next shift to fix it. The usual approach is to attack only with numbers, and to regroup rather than force your way in.

In the last minute of a period, take risk down. Give them nothing to walk off with.

When you are trailing and your goalie is pulled for an extra attacker, take risk all the way up. The only bad decision then is a slow one. When they have pulled theirs and you are leading, take risk all the way down, but shoot at the empty net rather than hunting for a better play.

Your level matters too. In a team that doesn't practise much, the give-and-go you can see is one your teammate can't. In rec hockey, a turnover often meets nobody coming back at all. And the goaltending varies more, both ways. So your own turnovers cost more, not less. Here is the blunt version. Most amateur teams lose far more goals to unforced turnovers than they win with creative plays. Tighten up nothing but your own end, and you will win more games. Nobody is asking you to stop trying things. Try them in their end.

So risk is a dial, not a switch. A big lead, a tired bench, or a backup goalie in your net all turn it toward the simple play. A team that is being outplayed turns it the other way.

Fifteenth idea. A penalty is a decision too, and it has a price.

In the NHL's last regular season, the twenty twenty-five to twenty-six season, about one power play in five ended in a goal. So taking a minor penalty hands the other team roughly a one-in-five chance of scoring. Against about three goals a game, that is a real piece of a game, given away.

So when is a penalty worth taking? Less often than players think. The test is unforgiving. Is the thing you are stopping more likely to become a goal than that one-in-five chance? Three cases pass.

One. To stop a certain goal. A one-in-five chance beats a sure thing every time, in the first minute as much as the last.

Two. Late in a game, to break up a rush that would tie it or win it. With thirty seconds left and a one-goal lead, they only get part of a power play.

Three. Taking a penalty on purpose to stop play when you are pinned in and exhausted, with no change possible. It is a real option and it works, but it is cynical, and your teammates won't thank you for it.

What is not on that list? Retaliation. Sending a message. Sticking up for a teammate after the whistle. And in the first period, nothing at all except stopping a certain goal. There are forty minutes left to pay for it.

Even when you are stopping a certain goal, the foul has to be one that costs a minor. Never haul a player down. And never take their legs. A player who falls at full speed can't protect themselves. Here is why. Say a player is clear on a breakaway, alone with only your goalie to beat. If you foul them from behind, or from the side, that isn't two minutes. In every league it gives them a penalty shot, a free shot alone on your goalie. Over eleven seasons in the NHL, penalty shots went in a bit more often than power plays do. And if you are the goalie, coming out to foul a breakaway from the front doesn't save you either. In some leagues that is a penalty shot too.

With your own goalie pulled, it is worse. A foul from behind then gives them the goal in every league, and in some leagues a foul from the front does too. There is no shot left to save.

There is one more reason a desperate foul is a bad trade. No league promises that a hook that drags someone down stays a minor. If it hurts them, most leagues make it a major and throw you out of the game. A trip can cost you your game in several leagues. In some, a reckless hook or trip gets you thrown out even if nobody is hurt. And one meant to hurt someone is a match penalty in most leagues.

So the practical rule is this. If you are chasing from behind, and the only way to stop them is a foul, you are probably saving nothing. With your net empty, you are saving nothing at all. The time to stop the breakaway is four seconds earlier, with your positioning.

Never slew-foot. That means using your leg or foot to knock a player's feet out from under them, sometimes while pushing their upper body back. It puts them down on their back with nothing to break the fall. Once it is called, it ends your game in most leagues, and it can in every league. Touching the puck first doesn't excuse it.

One more in front of your own net, and this one has a free option. If the puck is in the air, you may catch it, but put it straight down to the ice, or just knock it down. That costs nothing, in any league. If you hold on to it, skate with it, turn with it or throw it, that is what costs you. And if you are not the goalie, never fall on the puck, hold it, or cover it with your hand. If you do it in your own crease, plan on a penalty shot. With your own goalie pulled, treat it as a goal against.

Most penalties in amateur hockey aren't trades at all. Nobody weighed the odds. They look more like habits, which is what coaches see rather than anything measured. Two habits cover most of it. A stick foul that is really a skating foul, like a hook because you stopped skating twenty feet ago. And retaliation, like a slash after losing a battle, or a cross-check after the whistle. Fix the first with your skating, and the second with your composure. Use that to fix your own game, not as a fact about everyone else's.

One penalty is a decision, and a bad one. In most leagues, diving to draw a call is a penalty of its own, and it can hand them the power play you hoped to get.

Let me pull the scoreboard and penalties together. Take risk down when you lead, right down by three, and up when you trail late. Shorthanded, clear it unless you have really read a two-on-one. A minor gives them about a one-in-five chance, so take one only to stop a certain goal, and never from behind on a breakaway. Stop the breakaway earlier, with your positioning. Never slew-foot. And catch a puck in the air only to put it straight down.

Sixteenth idea. The four seconds after you give the puck away.

Everybody makes bad decisions, at every level. What separates good players is almost entirely what happens in the next four seconds.

One. Turn and skate. Not toward the puck. Skate to the middle of the ice, between the puck and your net, and become the extra defender. Chasing the carrier from behind gets you nothing. If they are clear on a breakaway, fouling them gives them a penalty shot.

That is the usual approach where your team defends lanes and players. Some teams coach the opposite on purpose. The first forward back pressures the carrier, while the defence holds the inside. If that is your job, pressure with your feet and your stick, not a hit, and if you can see their numbers, pull up. Find out which yours plays, rather than making it up on the ice.

Two. Get to the inside. Your job now is to take away the middle, not to win the puck back. Stop the pass into the slot, and a scoring chance becomes a shot from the outside.

Three. Talk. One word does it. Turnover. Back. I'm high. That one word reorganises four teammates who are still reading the play. Saying nothing is the second mistake.

Four. Finish the shift. Don't change while the play is live because you are embarrassed. That turns a giveaway into a giveaway plus five against four.

And never solve the chase with your body. Never hit a player who can't see you coming, and never from behind. If you can see their numbers, the numbers on the back of their jersey, pull up and play the puck. Even where your team pressures the carrier, that pressure is your feet and your stick. A hit on a player's back ends your game in most leagues once it is called, and it can in every league. If you meant to hurt them, it is a match penalty in most leagues. Driving them from behind into the boards when they can't protect themselves is a match penalty in several.

Then don't try to make up for it. The worst part of a bad decision is often the next one, the one meant to fix it. You give the puck away, you feel it, and thirty seconds later you force something. A pinch you haven't read. A cross-ice pass. A hit you leave your feet for. That second mistake is nearly always worse. The first was a play that didn't come off. The second is a bad choice made on purpose.

The next play is a new play. If the simple option was right before your turnover, it is still right after it.

After the game, watch the video if you have it. Ask yourself, what did I see, and what was actually there? The gap between those two answers is what to work on. Bad decisions are the most coachable thing in hockey. Unlike a shot or a stride, they can be fixed in a conversation.

Let me pull it all together.

Before you decide, think about where you are, and move to better ice. Your own slot is the worst place to lose the puck. The middle of the neutral zone is high risk too, and their corners are nearly free. Losing it while it travels toward your net is worse than losing it going away.

Get the puck out of your own slot first, and never pass it or skate it across the front of your own goal. D-to-D goes behind the net. One defenceman to the puck, one at home, and say which. Then the boards. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the checker, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly. If you end up facing the boards, stick and gloves up on the glass, spread wide, head off it.

Simple in your end, creative in theirs. Off the glass, never over it. Under pressure in your own end, take the first option on the checklist that is really there. With time, keep the puck.

Let the scoreboard set your risk. Before a chance, ask three things. Information or a feeling? Who is behind me? What does the score allow? Stay on your skates, and go for the puck with your stick, not a hit. Take a penalty only to stop a certain goal, and stop the breakaway four seconds earlier instead. After a turnover, get to the inside, talk, finish the shift, and never solve it with your body. If you can see their numbers, pull up.

Much of this is your team's choice. So here are three questions to ask your coach. Where do you draw the line on a D-to-D or a cross-ice pass high in our end? After a turnover, do we get to the inside, or pressure the carrier? And on a two-on-one, do you want us holding the lane, or sliding? And one for your league. Is body checking allowed?

Go and ask. Their answers tell you where your team wants you to take your risks, and now you know why each answer makes sense.

Voiced, that is a contradiction a listener can't resolve, so the script describes direction in plain words and never names it (§8).
- **The spine's system beats moved out of the spine.** The collapse and the overload are now idea 5, after the spine recap, so that the spine ends inside the first quarter. Idea 3 keeps the instruction and its reason.
- **The net-front role in the collapse is reworded** from "holds the net front" (:252) to "stays home, between the puck and your net". Naming a net-front job without its limbs would teach an act half.
- **One D to the puck: the stricter gate.** The source permits "the body, only where your league permits checking" (:240). The script follows brief item 13: "the stick, not a hit", with checking as the league's call.
- **"Stepping up" is defined as "moving up to challenge an attacker"**, not the source's "challenging an attacker forward" (:556). That avoids a word that sounds like contact.
- **"A shorthanded rush against you"** replaces the source's "against four attackers" (:517, :534), which a listener can't parse (§8).
- **Two goalie lines were added from the source:** the accidental clear (:392, :396, CM :911) and the breakaway foul from the front (:687). The remake carried both, and both are penalisable.

### 8. What I found unclear in the source

- **North is defined two ways (:101 against :163-164).** "North — toward the opponent's net", then "A 'north' turnover — you lose the puck while it is travelling toward your own net." Read as written, a north turnover is a puck moving south. The facts block at :156 repeats it. This is a plan-row candidate: name the turnover by the direction of play from the opponent's side, or drop the labels.
- **"A shorthanded rush against four attackers" (:517, :534)** reads as though the rush is against the attackers. It means a rush by the penalty-killing team while four of yours are caught up ice. Worth rewording in the source.
- **The Overview says "Two are rules"** (:38) and KT5 says "Two are rules everywhere" (:968). But the glass rule's strict-liability limb is in three books, not everywhere. KT5 does go on to scope it correctly.
- **KF :16 and the body text disagree on wording, not substance.** KF says the leap is charging "by name, in all six books". The facts line :297 and the body :313 agree. No defect, noted only because the "by name" phrasing is stronger than CARHA's "runs or jumps into".
