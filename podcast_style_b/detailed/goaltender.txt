===== SCRIPT START =====

This episode is about playing in goal. It takes its time. We will build the job up one piece at a time, and for each piece I will tell you what to do and also why it works. If you know why something works, you can still do the right thing when the play looks a little different from the picture in your head.

Here is the idea everything else is built on. Goaltending is not mainly about fast reactions. It is about being in the right place, so that the save hardly needs a reaction at all.

Before any instructions, a few words you will hear all the way through. I will explain them now, so they don't get in the way later.

The crease. This is the blue paint in front of your net.

The posts. These are the two upright sides of your net, part of its steel frame.

The slot. This is the area right in front of your net, where the other team's best players stand. It is the most dangerous ice on the rink.

Depth. This is how far out of your net you stand.

Square. This means your chest, hips and shoulders are facing the puck.

The five-hole. This is the gap between your legs.

A rebound. This is the puck bouncing back off you into play after a save.

The butterfly. This is dropping to your knees with both leg pads spread out sideways, and the five-hole closed.

That is the vocabulary. Now the job.

I will start with four big ideas, because they matter most. If you only listen to the first part of this episode, take those four with you. After that we will go back through your job one part at a time, and add the detail.

First idea. Stand on the line, and be out, square and set when the shot leaves.

Picture a line drawn from the puck to the exact middle of your goal line. Your job is to stand on that line, facing along it, with the middle of your body on it.

Here is why that matters so much. When you are on that line, you are covering the middle of the net. The shooter can only score by hitting the edges.

One well-known way to say it is three steps. Be out, be square, be set. Out means at the right depth for this situation. Square means your chest and hips pointing straight at the puck, on that line. Set means still, or as close to still as the play allows, at the moment the shot leaves the stick.

Here is a coaching judgement, and not every coach shares it. Out and square but still moving when the shot comes is worse than a little closer to your net and completely set.

Here is why. A goalie who is moving at the release can't push in any direction, because their weight is already going one way.

Some coaches give up depth for one reason only, a player who could get behind you. That means a pass across to the far side of your net, or a deke, which is a fake to go round you. So ask your coach which way they want it.

Your stance is what makes all of this possible.

Feet a little wider than your shoulders. Weight on the inside edges of the balls of your feet. Here is why. With your weight on your heels you can't push, and you will fall backwards on your first move sideways.

Get low by bending your knees, not your back. Bending at the waist drops your head and pushes your shoulders forward. That is how goalies fall through their saves.

Hands out in front, where you can see them at the edge of your vision. A surprising number of pucks just hit hands that are already in the right place. Hands back at your hips have to travel forward before they can travel sideways.

Stick flat on the ice, about a blade's length in front of your toes. Angle it so a puck off it goes down and out, not up into you.

And keep your head still and level. Your eyes do the work. Everything else is there to keep them steady.

When the puck moves across the ice, stay square by turning your shoulders and hips and taking small shuffles. A shuffle is a push sideways without turning your skates. Keep them small. Two big shuffles instead of four small ones open your five-hole halfway through, and you can't change direction.

As the puck goes wide toward the boards, you move along a gentle curve. Out in the middle, and back toward the posts. Here is why. As the puck goes wide, the angle closes by itself. Being out there buys you nothing, and just means you can't get back.

Finish each move right against the post, not near it. A gap between your pad and the post at ice level leaves the low shot on the near side. Goalie coaches call that the cheapest goal in hockey. That is coaching emphasis, not a counted ranking.

And while the puck is live, you can't look back at your net. The puck moves faster than your head can turn. So rebuild your sense of where the net is at every whistle.

Skate your arc, the curved path you move along in front of the goal. Tap both posts with your stick or your glove. Find your spot at the top of the crease, and feel where your skates are on the paint. Then you don't have to think about where the net is during play.

Second idea. Decide how far out you are before the shot.

Every save starts with a decision you made before the shot. How far out of my net am I?

Think of it from the shooter's side. The net is six feet wide, and you are a wall in front of it. Move a foot toward the shooter, and you cover noticeably more of the net from where they stand. Coming out toward the shooter like that is called challenging.

So why not come out every time? Because depth costs you three things.

First, time. The further out you are, the less time you have to react.

Second, getting across. The further out you are, the further you have to travel when they pass.

Third, knowing where your net is. Far out past the crease, it gets hard to know where the goal is behind you. That is how goalies end up making a save while standing beside the post.

So it is always a trade, and coaches give the two sides names. The shooter in front of you is the front door. The space you open behind you, usually toward the far post, is the back door. Coming out closes the front door and opens the back one.

There is no one right depth. There is a right depth for each situation. Here are the rough defaults, and every goalie coach adjusts them.

One shooter, nobody open for a pass, and the shot from far out. Come out to the top of the crease or beyond. Take the shot away, and make them beat you with a shot placed exactly right.

The puck down low or in tight. Get back. The angle is already bad for the shooter, so depth buys them nothing, and you need to be able to move.

A pass open across the slot. Back off. Being slightly less square to a shot you can still see beats being perfectly square to a shot that never comes, with the back door wide open.

Most coaching today teaches angle before depth. Get on the line first, then add depth if the play allows. Here is why. Deep but off your angle is a goal. On your angle but a little deep is a save you have to make.

There is a known exception. The short side is the side of the net nearest the shooter. When you step off the post to face a shot from the short side, add depth first. Pushing out along the short side closes the gap by the near post that opens the moment you leave it. So ask your coach whether they teach angle first.

Against a shot through a screen from far out, the coaching schools really do disagree. A screen is a player standing in your line of sight.

One school wants you deeper, so you have more time to find the puck. The other calls going deep a common mistake. It says going deep opens too much net and doesn't help you see more of the puck. So it wants you close to the shooter, which leaves less room for the puck to move, and for tips. Both sides are trading the same two things, net covered against time to see. Ask which your coach teaches.

And watch for two habits that are really about confidence, not technique. Hiding deep in your net, where the posts feel safe. Or running out at shooters and getting beaten by the first pass. If you see either in your own game, treat it as a head problem before you rebuild your technique.

Third idea. Watch the puck all the way into your body.

Goalie coaches put almost every soft goal down to one cause. At some point, you stopped seeing the puck. Nobody has counted that. It is the coaching view, and the rest of this idea is built on it.

Follow the puck with your head, not just your eyes. A small turn of your head keeps the puck in the middle of your vision, where you see best.

Watch it all the way into your pad, your glove or your chest. The common mistake is looking at where the puck is going instead of where it is.

Keep a quiet head and quiet hands. A goalie whose arms fly and whose head snaps back loses the puck right as it hits them. Then they are guessing where the rebound went.

And don't be a spectator on a pass. Start moving when the pass is released, not when it arrives.

Watching it all the way in does a second job. If you saw the puck hit you, you know where the rebound went. A rebound into the slot turns a shot from far away into a shot from six feet.

So treat the rebound as part of the save, in this order. First, stop it happening. Second, cover the puck or clear it. Third, if you can't, send it somewhere safe. Fourth, get ready for the next shot.

Stopping it happening means killing the puck's speed. Let your stick give a little when the puck hits it. A stiff stick sends it straight back out. A soft one drops it at your feet, where you can cover it.

The same goes for your body and pads. Relax into the shot instead of bracing against it. A tense goalie is like a trampoline.

Somewhere safe means the corners, wide and low toward the boards. Never back into the slot, where their best players are standing.

On a shot to your blocker, the flat pad on your stick hand, turn it so its face points at the corner, not back up the ice. A blocker save always makes a rebound, so where it goes is the whole skill.

And know where their players are before the shot. Glance off the puck before the release and notice who is where. Be specific. Not just that somebody is open, but that a left-handed shot is open at the back post. That tells you which save to choose, and where the rebound must not go.

Fourth idea. Stay on your feet until going down is the answer.

The butterfly covers most of the bottom of the net. That is why it is the base save of modern goaltending. But dropping into it on every shot, going down first and hoping the puck hits you, beats you from above while you are still on the way down. It fails for three reasons.

First, it gives away the top of the net, and once you are down you can't get back up in time.

Second, it is worse the smaller you are. A tall professional in a wide butterfly covers most of the net. A twelve-year-old doesn't, and there is a lot of open net above them.

Third, it takes away your choice of save. If your answer to every shot is the same, you are not reading anything.

So the butterfly is a save you choose, not who you are. The half butterfly, with one pad down and the other leg still under you, is probably the save you will use most. It handles a low shot to one side and leaves you balanced.

Read the play first, then choose the save that fits it. A glove save is the best answer to a high shot, because a clean catch leaves no rebound at all.

When you do go down, get back up the right way. Keep the leg on the puck side down, lift the far leg first, then push toward the puck. Standing up away from the puck is how goalies end up out of position for the second shot.

Play the puck you can see. Stay up until going down is the answer.

Let me pull those four ideas together, because they are the core of the job.

One. Stand on the line from the puck to the middle of your goal, out, square and set when the shot leaves. Hands in front, small shuffles, and rebuild your picture of the net at every whistle.

Two. Decide your depth before the shot. Out against a lone shooter from far away. Back when the puck is in tight or a pass is open. Every depth trades the front door against the back door.

Three. Watch the puck all the way into your body. Kill the rebound if you can, and if you can't, send it to the corners, never the slot.

Four. Stay on your feet until going down is the answer, and get up toward the puck.

Everything else in this episode adds detail to those four. So now let's go through your job one part at a time, starting right in front of your net.

Fifth idea. Talk.

Talking is the most valuable thing you do that isn't a save.

Here is why. You are the only player on the ice who isn't deciding while sprinting. You are still, and you are facing the play. Your defenceman going back for a puck in your corner is facing the end boards. A forechecker, an opponent chasing the puck, is coming from behind them. You can see that, and they can't.

If you don't tell them, they guess. And a guess in your own end is a turnover in the most dangerous part of the ice.

That moment, with their back to the play, is also when a defenceman is most exposed to a hit from behind. So your call does more than save the puck. It is the warning that helps keep a teammate safe on the boards.

Here are the usual calls. The words vary by team, so agree yours in the dressing room, not on the ice.

Time means nobody is near you, so lift your head and make the good play, not just the safe one. It matters as much as its opposite. A defenceman who fears pressure that isn't there throws the puck away. Only say time when you are sure. A wrong time sends a teammate into a hit they weren't ready for.

Man on means someone is on you right now, so get rid of it.

Leave it means I will stop the puck and leave it behind the net for you. I've got it means I am playing it myself, so go to your breakout position. That is the spot you skate to when your team gets the puck back.

Back door means an opponent is open at the far post, and the nearest player takes them, right now. Here is why it needs a call. The person who can see that open player is almost never the person who can cover them.

Screen means I can't see. Get out of my line of sight, or get beside the player who is in it and hold your ground. Make it your first call, because nobody else can know you can't see.

And one word to be careful with. In this episode, shot means you have the shooter, so your defenceman should take away the pass. Plenty of teams use shot as a warning that a shot is coming. That is the opposite instruction.

Here is why the meaning matters. A warning that a shot is coming tells a skater nothing they can act on. I have the shooter tells them exactly where to put their stick. If your team already uses shot as a warning, pick a different word for the warning.

Now how to call well. Call early, while the play is still developing. A call that arrives with the puck is too late to use. Keep it to one or two syllables, because nobody can follow a sentence at full speed. Say the same word every time.

And call every time. A goalie who calls only sometimes trains their defence to ignore them. Then the one urgent call gets ignored with the rest. Ask your defencemen to answer you. One word back is enough.

Sixth idea. Playing the post.

When the puck goes below the goal line or very tight to your post, standing normally leaves a gap at ice level between your pad and the post. That is exactly where a player coming out from behind your net tries to stuff the puck in. So you seal the post.

There are two common seals. In the VH, the pad nearest the post goes up, flat against the post, and the other pad lies along the ice. That puts a wall up the post. In the reverse VH, the pad nearest the post lies along the goal line, and the other leg stays up, ready to push out. That puts a wall along the ice.

Each has a reason. The reverse VH kills the low shot on the near side, and the player coming round from behind. The VH is better against a shot that can go up the post. Your coach will teach you one, and which part of you makes the seal.

The detail most people leave out is the pad away from the post. Dig it into the ice. Here is why. When the first shot hits your post-side pad, that anchor is what stops it driving you back into your own net.

The reverse VH has three honest weaknesses, and goalie coaches argue about all of them. You can get stuck on the post and be late for a pass back into the slot. The top of the net over your shoulder is open. And dropping to the post early can make you stop reading the play.

That is why the usual North American teaching is to go to the post late and come off it early. Save seals for pucks that really are below the goal line, or right on top of you. Stay on your feet while the puck is still in front of the goal line.

That is one school. The Swedish tradition, where the reverse VH started, teaches an earlier seal with a set trigger. Both are reasonable, so ask your coach what your trigger is.

What nobody defends is dropping to the post out of habit, without reading the play.

Seventh idea. Screens and tips.

A screen set outside your crease is normally legal. Nobody will blow a whistle for it, and nobody will help you unless you make them. So dealing with it is a skill you own.

Here is what every coaching school agrees on. Pick one side of the screen and see the puck clearly there. Half-seeing it on both sides is the one thing nobody defends.

Move a step or a small shuffle to open a lane if you need to. Being slightly off your perfect angle with a clear view beats being perfectly square and blind.

Whether you look around the screen or over it is a real coaching split. One school says look around. Drop your head to one side and find the puck through legs, under arms, around a hip. Here is why. Standing tall to see over means you can't move.

The other school says look over shoulders, to keep your size. It thinks giving up size for a clean look is the worse trade. Ask which your coach teaches.

And when you can't see the release, make yourself big. Hands out and wide, stick down and active, chest up. You are partly blocking now rather than reacting. When you can't see, that is the right trade.

Call screen, loudly, early and every time. What you want is a teammate beside the screener, holding their ground. Never pushing the screener, and never walking them off their spot, which can be called interference in any game.

Who that teammate should be is a coaching choice. In some systems it is a defenceman. In others it can just as easily be a forward. So find out before you need to know who you are shouting at.

A tip is a shot that changes direction off a stick a few feet in front of you. No reflex is fast enough to follow one, so don't try.

Watch the sticks, not just the puck. If a forward stands at the edge of your crease with their blade in the shooting lane, treat every shot from the blue line as a likely tip.

Stay tall and patient for as long as you can. Here is why. A tip that stays low will hit you if you are big and set. One that goes up will go over you if you are already down. Your best chance on a tip is being in the right place with a wide body and a still head, not a heroic reaction.

Eighth idea. Freezing the puck.

Freezing the puck means covering it to stop play. It is a real tool, and it is controlled more tightly than most players realise.

In your crease, freeze a rebound when an attacker is on you and you have the puck clean. Here is why. A whistle beats a scramble. And when your team is pinned in its own end, that freeze buys tired teammates a line change and a breath.

But the attacker on you is what earns the freeze, not the change you want. A puck you had time to play with your stick, and covered anyway, can be a penalty in every league. Some referees warn you once first. Don't count on it.

If nobody is on you, put the puck down and play it. Don't hold on to it.

Outside your crease, play it or clear it. Covering it out there can be a penalty in every league. A glove half over a loose puck out there hasn't stopped play either, and an attacker can still poke it in. If you can't play it or clear it, get back into your stance. The instinct that is right in your crease can cost you two minutes outside it.

One cover out there is usually allowed. If you came out to cut down the angle, made the save, and covered it straight away, that is fine in most leagues. But not behind your goal line, and ask your league how far it goes.

Now two times to leave the puck alone.

First, when your defenceman is arriving with time. It matters much less that you have the puck than that they don't. And every stoppage is a faceoff in your end that you might lose.

Second, a puck shot in from their side of the centre red line. In many leagues, if you freeze that one, your own team can't change before the faceoff. Your tired players are stuck out there. So on that puck, if your defenceman can reach it, leave it.

When in doubt, with an attacker on you in your crease, freeze it. A faceoff you might lose is a smaller problem than a scramble you are already losing.

A few habits go with this. If a puck lands on the back of your net, let it fall off. Never drop the puck onto your net or into your pads to get a stoppage. That is a penalty.

Never race out to jump on a loose puck, either. That is a penalty in most leagues too.

And when you clear snow, scrape it, don't pile it up. Banking snow at your net is a penalty, and keeping that area clear is your job.

Ninth idea. Your crease, and the traffic in it.

Get to your spot in the crease early, and hold it. Play through traffic instead of appealing it.

Here is why. You have less protection than a lot of goalies think, and it differs from league to league. So know what you can rely on.

Inside your crease, an attacker who runs into you usually costs their team the goal. Outside it, you are not fair game, and contact can still cost them the goal. But contact out there can still happen, so don't count on being left alone. And a screen is not a foul. A screen set outside your paint, with no contact, almost never costs them the goal.

In a scramble for a rebound, go and win the puck, not the player. In many leagues, no whistle is coming. Contact that just happens when you both go for a loose puck is allowed. But that excuses the attacker, not you.

So keep your stick and your body to yourself, in your crease and out of it. Never push, lean on or body check an attacker, even one standing in your way. Contact you start can be a penalty in every league, even with no force at all. Take your ground by getting there first, not by moving someone off it.

Never chop at an attacker's stick, and never cross-check anyone, at any height. A cross-check is using the shaft of your stick, held in both hands, to hit or shove a player. Either one is two minutes at the least. A hard or reckless one can end your game. And never punch anyone with your blocker. That can end your game too.

Here is why this matters even more for you. A teammate serves your minor penalties. So your retaliation becomes your team's penalty kill.

And don't fake being hit. That is a penalty in its own right. And a goalie who sells contact gets fewer calls when it really matters.

Outside your crease, play the puck, not the attacker. Deliberately getting in their way out there can be a penalty too.

If a player is standing in your crease, make it visible and say so out loud, before the puck goes in. Shout for the teammate whose job the front of the net is, rather than dropping to the ice.

Many games have no video review. There, the referee's live call is the end of it. So say it at the time or not at all, and expect to eat some goals a review would have taken off.

If someone runs you, get back and get set again. Then tell the referee what happened, not what to call. They drove through me. Nobody pushed them. They hit me from behind. Those are the facts the rules turn on. Saying that's a penalty tells the referee nothing. And if your head took any of it, say something at the next stoppage.

Tenth idea. When a fight or a scrum breaks out.

Gloves come off in front of you, and the instinct is to go. Don't. Stay in your crease. If you need to move, ask the referee first. If the fight is in your crease, wait for the referee to send you somewhere.

Here is why. Leaving your crease during a fight is a penalty in most leagues. Going in, to fight or even to break it up, gets you thrown out of the game in many leagues. Then your team is short a goalie and a player, and you are watching from the dressing room.

Let me pull the front of your net together. Talk early, short and every time, and only say time when you are sure. Go to the post late, come off it early, and dig the far pad in. Through a screen, pick one side. Freeze because an attacker is on you in your crease, and outside it, play it or clear it. Keep your stick and your body to yourself. And in a fight, stay in your net.

Now behind your net.

Eleventh idea. Stop the rim, and know how far back you may go.

Behind your own net, you are your defenceman's first option, and usually not by passing.

A puck sent hard round the boards is called a rim. Left alone, it ends up in the far corner with a forechecker on it. Stop it dead behind your net and leave it there for your defenceman.

Here is why. You have turned a race into an easy pick-up. Call leave it early, while they are still skating back, not as they arrive. A late call is one they can't use.

How far behind your net you may go to do that depends on your league, so find out before your first shift.

Some leagues paint a trapezoid behind the net. That is a shape that is narrow at the goal line and wider at the end boards. Where it counts, you may play the puck behind your goal line only inside it. Play it outside, and it is a penalty.

Blocking a rim with your pads out there counts as playing it. So outside the trapezoid, get out of the way and let your defenceman have it. What decides it is where the puck is, not where you are.

If you aren't sure whether your league uses one, play as though it does. And look behind the net in warm-up to see whether the lines are painted.

Other leagues have no trapezoid. There, you may play the puck anywhere behind your net, so go and get it. But play it, don't smother it. And out there you are not protected the way you are in your crease. In some games where checking is allowed, you can be hit for a puck you are holding. So arrive with your head up, expecting contact. Leagues with no trapezoid control the freeze instead. Covering the puck behind your goal line, with your body out of your crease, is a penalty in all of them.

Whether your goalie plays the puck at all is also a coaching choice. Some teams want the goalie handling everything they legally can. Others want the goalie to touch nothing and stay in the net. Both are reasonable. Ask, and then be consistent.

Twelfth idea. Moving the puck, in order of risk.

When you do move the puck, go in order of how risky it is.

First, leave it for a defenceman with time. That is the lowest risk, and it starts a controlled breakout, your team carrying the puck out of its own end.

Second, a short pass to a defenceman on the boards on the puck side. If it is picked off, it is picked off below the goal line, where it matters less.

Third, a rim round the boards to your far winger. That gives up the puck but takes the pressure off.

Fourth, off the glass and out. It is rarely wrong when you are in trouble, and goalies are allowed to be boring.

And never up the middle. If you give it away there, it is a shot at an empty net, and you are the one standing in it. But an option that is plainly open still beats a safer one that is covered.

When you clear it, put it along the glass, never over it. In most leagues a clear that goes straight out of the rink from your own end is a penalty, whether you meant it or not. If the puck isn't settled on your blade, send it down the boards instead of lifting it.

A save that deflects over the glass is different. That is not a clear, so don't start flinching at pucks you should stop.

Once the whistle has gone, put the puck down. Firing it out of the rink after the whistle is a penalty, and in some leagues it climbs until you are thrown out of the game. Then your team needs another goalie.

And move the puck with your stick. Don't pick it up and throw it with your glove. Throwing it can cost you a penalty, or stop play.

Thirteenth idea. The discipline of leaving it alone.

The best puck-handling decision a goalie makes is usually to leave the puck alone.

Don't leave your net for a fifty-fifty puck, one you might not win. Lose the race and the net is empty, with you behind the play.

Don't handle the puck just because you can. Every touch is a chance for a turnover, and playing the puck takes you out of your net.

And if you do commit, commit. The worst outcome is starting to play it, changing your mind, and leaving you and your defenceman both out of position. Call I've got it, and mean it.

If you win a race to the boards, you arrive facing the end boards with a forechecker coming from behind. So arrive with your head up and your chin off your chest. Never duck. If you do go into the boards or a post, make sure it is anything but your head first. That protects your head and neck, and it holds even going slowly.

That advice is written for every player on the ice. This episode can't give you a full position for a goalie in pads taking a hit at the boards. The one taught to skaters may not carry over, so ask your goalie coach. Better still, don't race for a puck you might not win.

Fourteenth idea. Icing, and the puck dumped in at you.

Icing is when a team shoots the puck from its own half all the way past your goal line, with nobody touching it. When the other team ices it at you, decide before it arrives whether you want the whistle.

Usually you do. The faceoff goes back to their end. And in many leagues the team that iced it can't change players, so their tired five are stuck out there.

In many leagues, you can wave an icing off without touching the puck. Just skating toward it, faking at it, or reaching for it with your stick is enough. Your defencemen can't do that. Only you can.

So if you want the whistle, stand still. Do nothing, on purpose. Drifting toward the corner just in case throws the whistle away, and hands them a live puck in your end.

In other leagues only touching the puck counts. There, drifting out gets you nothing, and you have left your net for no return.

Wave it off on purpose only when the counterattack is worth more than the whistle. That means your defenceman is arriving with time, and their forwards have already turned for the bench. Two strides toward the puck is the whole action, but decide before it arrives.

Then talk either way. Leave it, or I've got it. Your defenceman is skating back with their head down and has no idea which you chose. If nobody says anything, you get two players and one puck.

Let me pull behind your net together. Stop the rim dead and call leave it early. Know how far back your league lets you play. Never pass up the middle, and clear along the glass, not over. Stay in your net for a fifty-fifty puck, and if you do race to the boards, expect contact, head up, chin off your chest, never duck. On a puck iced at you, decide first, then say which.

Now the rush, and the special situations.

Fifteenth idea. The breakaway.

A breakaway is one attacker alone against you. You and a lot of time to think, which is the real difficulty.

Meet them well out, then back up on C-cuts, matching their speed, so the angle stays closed the whole way. A C-cut is a curved push with one skate that moves you backwards while your chest keeps facing the play.

Here is why you don't just stand still or charge. Standing still at the top of the crease lets them pick a corner. Charging out lets them go round you.

Keep a little backward motion, called flow, so you can react either way. Flat-footed, you can react to nothing. Too much flow, though, and you back into your own net.

Stay on your feet as long as you can. The shooter's whole plan is to make you move first. If you move first, they go where you aren't. The last one to move wins.

What to watch is a real coaching split. One school says watch the puck, not the body. Everything above the puck is acting, and a body fake only exists to move you.

The other school says the puck is exactly what the shooter fakes with. So it reads the part of them that can't lie, their chest and hips.

Ask your goalie coach which they teach, and stick to one. Switching in the middle of a play is how you end up reading neither.

Take away what they show you. If they have the puck out in front and are winding up, expect the shot and stay big. If they pull it to their forehand with their head up, expect a deke and stay patient.

The poke check, jabbing your stick out at the puck, works when they let it get too far in front of them. It is a commitment. Miss, and you have no reach and no balance left.

And never knock your own mask off to stop the play. That is a penalty in every league. Against a breakaway it is worse, because the other team can be given the goal or a penalty shot. If your mask comes off by itself, play stops for it anyway, though not always at once, so there is nothing to fish for.

Sixteenth idea. Penalty shots and shootouts.

A penalty shot is one attacker against you, starting from centre ice, with no defenders.

Stay in your crease until the shooter touches the puck. Leave early, and the shot is taken again. Keep doing it and the price climbs until you are thrown out, and in at least one league the goal is simply given.

There is no clock on a penalty shot. Any number of seconds you hear is a myth. So be patient, and let them run out of ideas.

Play the shot once. A rebound can't score, except one that goes off the post or the crossbar, then off you, and in.

A shootout is a run of these, one shooter after another. Patience is the whole thing. Each shooter's move is built to make you go first, so make them decide before you do.

Seventeenth idea. Two-on-ones and three-on-twos.

A two-on-one is two attackers against one of your defencemen. The usual arrangement is that your defenceman takes away the pass and you take the shooter.

Your defenceman stays in the passing lane, with their stick flat on the ice. You play the puck carrier as if nobody else were there. Square up, come out to your normal depth, and make the save.

Here is why it is set up that way. The pass across to an open forward is the chance you can't stop. The shot is the one you can.

That is a convention, not a law. Some teams play a different system on purpose. The defenceman times a slide flat across the passing lane, to take the pass away and force a shot. Where that is your team's plan, the slide is right, and your job doesn't change. You still have the shooter, square and set.

What must not happen is the defenceman sliding early when your team plays the passing lane. That opens both options at once. Worse than either is the two of you arriving with different answers.

So get it said out loud. Your defenceman says I've got the pass, or you say shot. And ask your coach and your defencemen what your team does, before the game.

A three-on-two is three attackers against two defenders. From your side it is the same read, with a wider back door. Don't over-challenge. Stay a little deeper than usual, so you can still get across. Moving sideways matters more than the last foot of angle.

And call odd man. An odd-man rush is any attack where the attackers outnumber the defenders who got back. The call tells everyone to stop watching the puck and start counting.

How your two defencemen split the three is your coach's choice. The common way is each defenceman taking an outside attacker, with the middle one left to whoever is skating back. The versions go wrong in different ways, so find out which your team plays.

Eighteenth idea. Faceoffs in your own end.

A faceoff in your end is the one moment the play comes to you on a schedule, so use it.

Get set before the puck drops, on your angle to the dot, square and at your normal depth. Here is why. A draw lost straight back to a shooter can be a shot before you have moved.

Talk before the drop, not after it. Check the front of your net is covered and every attacker has someone, and say so if not.

If the draw is lost cleanly, expect a quick shot first and a tip second. If it is won to the boards, be ready to stop a rim behind your net.

Nineteenth idea. The power play and the penalty kill.

When your team has the extra player, the power play, you see very few shots. Then now and again comes a breakaway or a two-on-one the other way, against tired defencemen.

The danger is being cold and thinking about something else when it comes. So follow the puck at the far end, move your feet at every stoppage, and talk more, not less. Your players at the blue line have their backs to the play coming at you, and you have the clearest view of it.

On the penalty kill, it is the opposite. Expect traffic, shots you can't see, and passing side to side to make you move. So put seeing the puck first. Whether that means playing deeper is a coaching split, as it is for any screened shot, so follow your goalie coach.

Know which shape your team kills in, and which version of it. Here is why. The shape tells you which lanes your team is giving away, and so where the shots will come from.

The usual shapes are a box, a diamond, and a wedge plus one. A team may use a diamond against a power play with a player in the middle of the slot, because a box leaves that player unmarked. Ask which your team runs.

A freeze on the kill gets tired players a change and makes the power play set up again from a faceoff. It takes no time off the penalty, because the clock stops too. And it is still only a freeze you were entitled to make. Being on the kill is no excuse for making a stoppage you didn't need.

Twentieth idea. Empty nets, and the delayed penalty.

When your coach pulls you for an extra attacker, go hard, and go the moment you are called. A slow change risks a penalty for too many players, or a goal with you halfway up the ice.

Take your stick with you, and leave nothing near the net. A stick, a piece of equipment or piled snow left there can hand the other team an awarded goal or a penalty shot. In at least one league that counts even if you were knocked out of your stick.

On a delayed penalty, the referee's arm goes up against the other team, and play stops as soon as they get the puck. So your bench may want you off for an extra skater.

Whether you go is the bench's call, not yours, and plenty of recreational goalies stay in. Here is why. The trip is longer than it looks, and an extra skater is only worth having if someone is ready to come on. Find out what your team does before it happens.

If you go, wait until your team has the puck, take the shortest route, and keep your eyes on the play. While you are off, the goal to fear is your own team's pass sliding back into your empty net. So agree beforehand that nothing goes back toward it.

When play stops, get back in fast, and rebuild your picture of posts, paint and depth before the faceoff.

When their net is empty and they have the extra attacker, play a little deeper, and expect passes to the back door rather than clean shots. Freeze every puck you are entitled to freeze, and only those. This is when you will want a whistle most, and wanting one is exactly what the freeze rules are written against.

If their net is empty and you get the chance, you may score. Shoot it with your stick, never throw it. And keep your skates and the puck on your own side of the centre red line. Playing the puck beyond it is a penalty in every league, and leagues measure it differently. Keeping both on your side is safe everywhere.

Let me pull the situations together. On a breakaway, meet them well out, back up at their speed, and be the last one to move. On a penalty shot, stay in your crease until they touch the puck. On a two-on-one, you usually have the shooter, and you say so out loud. Be set before a faceoff in your end. On the kill, see the puck, and freeze only what you are entitled to. And when you are pulled, go fast, and take your stick.

Now the last part. Your gear, your head, and what you need from your teammates.

Twenty-first idea. Your gear.

Clubs often lend goalie gear, and borrowing is a fine way in. But borrow the pads, the blocker, the catching glove and the chest protector, and buy the mask.

Here is why. A certification sticker is a clock, not a report. Even an in-date one tells you nothing about how many pucks that mask has stopped, or the state of the padding inside. A mask nobody can account for is not one to put your head in.

Check the sticker on any mask you are handed. In some leagues an out-of-date one counts as no certification at all. And if there is no sticker, ask your league what it accepts.

Wear a neck guard, a neck laceration protector, at every age. A throat flap hanging from your mask is an addition, not a substitute. In Britain it is required at every age, adults included. Some North American rules let adults go without one. Wear one anyway.

Replace gear you have outgrown. Here is why. Gear that hurts you teaches you to flinch, and a flinch can't be coached out. Fit and comfort beat brand and looks.

If your stick breaks, drop it and call for a teammate's. That is safe in every league. Some leagues let a goalie play on with a broken one, and in others playing on is a penalty. Never go to the bench for a new one during play. A teammate hands you one on the ice, and a skater's stick is legal in your hands until then.

At a whistle, ask the referee before you head to the bench for a stick, a repair or anything else. Going without asking can cost your team a penalty, or get you replaced on the spot.

Twenty-second idea. A shot off your mask.

If you take a hard shot off your mask, say something at the next stoppage. Tell the nearest official, your bench and your captain.

Here is why it has to be you. Your face is behind a cage, and the rest of you looks fine. Nobody else can see that you are hurt. And don't wait for a whistle, because one may never come. In most leagues nothing stops play just for a shot off the mask. Play stops for a hurt goalie only once an official sees that you are hurt, which a cage hides.

A concussion is an injury to the brain. It doesn't need you to be knocked out, or a body check. And it affects your judgement, so this isn't a call to make on how you feel.

If a concussion is suspected, come off, and don't play again that day. A qualified medical professional who has assessed you decides when you play again, not you. Afterwards, someone should stay with you. Don't drive, and don't go home alone.

Some signs after a knock to the head mean an ambulance straight away. Neck pain, a seizure, a severe or worsening headache, and repeated vomiting are among them, and there are others. The Conditioning and Recovery episode lists them all. And if anyone might have hurt their neck or back, phone for help first. Don't move them, and leave the helmet on.

Think of it this way. A goalie who comes off and turns out fine has cost the team a goalie change. A goalie who stays on and isn't fine risks a second knock on an injured brain.

Twenty-third idea. Warm up, and look after your hips.

Get a sweat on before you face live shots. Here is why. Every butterfly drop, slide and post seal works the far end of your hip's range, over and over.

One goalie coaching manual warns that going in cold invites groin and hip pulls. That is its judgement, not a measured rate. Warm up by moving, not by holding stretches.

Groin and hip pain gets treated as normal in this position. Don't. Pain that keeps going or keeps coming back needs a physiotherapist or a sports physician, and when you play again is their call.

Twenty-fourth idea. After a goal goes in.

Every goalie lets in goals, and some will be bad. The skill is not avoiding that. It is what you do in the next ninety seconds.

Have a reset routine and do it the same way every time. Skate a small circle, get a drink, scrape your crease, tap both posts. Here is why it works. Doing the same thing every time tells your body that the last shift is over.

Save the question of what you could have done for tomorrow. During the game it takes attention you need for the next play. Forget the goal for the rest of the game, and remember the read that caused it in the week after.

And never point at a teammate. Not with your hands, your body or a look. It is the fastest way to lose a dressing room.

Know the signs that your confidence is slipping, because they look like other problems. Suddenly blaming your equipment, or your defence. Hiding deep or running at shooters. Going quiet and sorry for yourself, or acting cocky. Guessing, and dropping before the shot. Treat those as signs about your head, not as the real problem. They often show up plainly in warm-ups.

And remember why you're there. Goalies need to enjoy making saves. If stopping the puck is the part you love, the goals you let in are just part of the price.

Twenty-fifth idea, and the last. What to tell your skaters.

Many goals at the front of your net are not goalie mistakes at all. They come from skater habits. That is a coaching judgement, not a count. So here is what to ask of them.

Shoot at the net, never at my head, in warm-ups and drills. And don't fire pucks at me from ten feet. Here is why. A shot off the mask may never bring a whistle.

Don't screen me. Standing in front of the net with your back to the shooter, in my line of sight, causes goals.

And don't back into my crease when you are being pushed. Hold your ground early instead, without pushing back. Here is why. You land backwards on a goalie who is usually down, square to the shooter, and can't see you coming. A goalie in that spot can't protect themselves, so it is a collision as well as a screen.

Don't block a shot from in front of me. A puck you half-block there is one I never saw. Get out of my line instead.

At the front of the net, win the spot by position, never by moving the screener. Get your inside shoulder ahead of theirs and hold it. No push, no lean, and never a stick across them. Never walk them off their spot, which can be called interference in any game.

As the puck arrives, you can lift their stick, then let go. Go low on the shaft, well below their hands, and lift up, not sideways. Never chop at the hands. And never a two-handed shove, which is a cross-check whatever your blade is doing.

On a rebound, box out. Take the inside position between that player and the puck, and hold it. No lean, and never use it to hit them.

Where your game allows checking, a battle or a legal check can move a body at the front of the net. Take it toward the corner, never across the front of the net. Here is why. That path has a steel post at one edge and your own goalie at the other, and the player being moved can see neither.

In all of this, at the front of the net, these limits hold. No shove, no lean, and never a stick across anyone. Keep that player's skates on the ice. Never lift anyone off their feet, because then they can't brace or choose what hits the post. Never into anyone's back. If you can see their numbers, pull up. If you hit a player from behind so they go head first into the post, plan on being thrown out of the game, whatever league you play in. A crash there can hurt even going slowly. And if you are the one being moved, head up, chin off your chest.

If my stick breaks, bring me yours and put it in my hand. Never throw it or slide it.

Let me play the puck when I call for it. I've got it means go to your breakout position, not to the puck.

And answer my calls. One word back is enough.

Let me pull it all together.

Stand on the line from the puck to the middle of your goal, and be out, square and set when the shot leaves. Decide your depth before the shot. Watch the puck all the way in, and send rebounds to the corners, never the slot. Stay on your feet until going down is the answer.

In front of your net, talk early, short and every time, and only say time when you are sure. Go to the post on a read. Through a screen, pick one side. Freeze because an attacker is on you in your crease, never because you want a whistle. Keep your stick and your body to yourself, and in a fight, stay in your net.

Behind your net, stop the rim and call leave it early. Know how far back your league lets you play, never pass up the middle, and clear along the glass, not over it.

On the rush, be the last one to move, and agree with your defenceman who has the pass.

And look after yourself. Buy your own mask, wear a neck guard, and warm up before live shots. After a hard shot off the mask, say something.

Much of this depends on your team and your league. So here are three questions to ask your coach. Do you want me deeper or closer against a screened shot? When do you want me sealing the post? On a two-on-one, does our defenceman take the pass, or slide? And one for your league. Is there a trapezoid behind the net, and how far back may I play the puck?

Go and ask. Their answers tell you how your team wants you to play, and now you know why each answer makes sense.

### 7. Departures from the source or the template

- **Boards short form for the goalie** (brief limb 5): not voiced; see §4 item 1. This is the main departure.
- **Close:** three coach questions **plus one league question** (trapezoid), as the remake did. The brief says "three questions to ask your coach"; the league question is extra because the trapezoid answer cannot come from a coach in every league.
- **Structure:** the template's "rink one end at a time" does not fit a goalie, who stays in one end. The parts are: the spine, then in front of your net, behind your net, the rush and special situations, and your gear, your head and your skaters. A recap closes each part.
- **Twenty-five numbered ideas** against the template's eighteen. Each is short; the alternative was fewer, denser ideas.
- **Two additional sources** (C&R and Body Contact) carry: someone stays with you / don't drive / don't go home alone; phone for help, don't move them, helmet on; pull up on the numbers; never chop at the hands; the two-handed shove is a cross-check. The brief allows two.
- **Slot definition** is taken from the source's own words ("where the other team's best players are standing", "the most dangerous ice on the rink") rather than the glossary's "between the faceoff circles", to stay inside the two-extra-source limit.
- **"A rebound can't score, except off the post or the crossbar, then off you, and in"** is said for every league. The source notes CARHA's text does not carry that exception (:967). The error is in the safe direction for a goalie (it keeps them playing the puck), but it is not exact.
- **"The coach who made the three steps famous"** (Korn) is not named; the three steps are.

### 8. What I found unclear or contradictory in the source

- **The goalie at the boards.** The source sends the goalie into a corner race (:775-778) and then says no source gives them a posture. That leaves a taught act with half a limb set, which brief limb 5 treats as a defect. It belongs in `goaltender.md` (or a goalie coaching source) before any script can fix it.
- **Shorthanded icing** is told to the goalie as a shout to make (:1076), but the condition is long and level-dependent. A plainer one-sentence version in the page would let the scripts carry it.
- **The 3-on-2 systems** (:1010) and the **wedge+1** (:1073) have no "why" in this page, so the script stops at "how" for both, per decision 6.

### 9. Where this script is weakest

- **Length.** 51 minutes. The middle third (behind the net and the situations) is the longest stretch between recaps (about 19 minutes from the 62% recap to the 81% one).
- **Rules density in the crease idea.** The Ninth idea is the most rules-shaped stretch, with seven "can be a penalty" style sentences in a row. Each is an instruction with its consequence, but it reads as a list of prohibitions.
- **The skaters' limb paragraph** (105 words) is the least plain unit, because every limb the brief requires sits in it.
- **"Here is why" appears 29 times.** It is the template's device, but at this length it may grate.
- Backward pointers were audited by hand for shape. One was rewritten ("the screen question again"). The remaining signposts are "Everything else in this episode adds detail to those four" and the recap openers. "The Conditioning and Recovery episode lists them all" points at another episode, as the remake did.
