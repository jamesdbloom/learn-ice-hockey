===== SCRIPT START =====

This episode is about special teams, and what you actually do on them. It takes its time. We will build it up one piece at a time, and for each piece I will tell you what to do and also why it works. If you know why something works, you can still do the right thing when the play looks a little different from the picture in your head.

Before any instructions, a few words you will hear all the way through. I will explain them now, so they don't get in the way later.

Special teams. This means any time the two teams don't have the same number of skaters on the ice. It happens when somebody is sent to the penalty box.

The power play. That is when one of their players is in the penalty box, so your team has an extra skater.

The penalty kill. That is the other way round. Your team is a skater short. A player on the penalty kill is called a killer.

Five-on-four. This is how people write the numbers. It counts skaters only, not goalies. So five-on-four means five skaters against four.

The slot. This is the ice right in front of the net, between the two faceoff circles, out to the tops of the circles. It is the most dangerous ice on the rink.

The point. This is the area just inside the blue line in the other team's end. It is an area, not the line itself.

The half-wall. This is the stretch of boards roughly level with the faceoff dot, between the corner and the point. The point and the half-wall are two different places.

The crease. This is the goalie's area, the blue paint in front of the net. It has a red line around its edge.

That is the vocabulary. Now the job.

The two halves of special teams are the same problem, seen from opposite sides. On the power play, you are trying to leave one of their players stuck covering two of yours. On the kill, you are trying to make sure that never happens to one of yours.

I will start with four big ideas, because they matter most. If you only listen to the first part of this episode, take those four with you. After that we will go back through it in detail. First the power play, then the penalty kill, then the moments that belong to both.

First idea. On the power play, make one of them cover two of you.

Four killers can cover four attackers. If nobody on your team moves, it is really four against four, with your spare player standing somewhere useless. The extra skater is worth nothing on its own.

It only turns into a goal when one killer has two attackers to worry about, and only one body to do it with. That is a two-on-one, somewhere on the ice. A two-on-one means two attackers against one defender.

Two-on-ones aren't all worth the same, though. A two-on-one at the front of the net, your net-front player and a teammate at the far post against one defender, is close to a sure chance. A two-on-one at the top of the zone is usually just a shot from far out, into traffic. So make them wherever the kill lets you, because each one pulls the kill further out of shape. But cash them in as close to the net as you can. That ranking is coaching judgement, not a rule. Coaches broadly agree on it, but nobody has counted it.

Now, how do you make a two-on-one? With passes. Not by skating, and not by shooting.

Here is why. A penalty kill is a shape, and a shape has to move to cover the puck. Every pass makes at least one killer skate, and a killer who is skating is arriving late. The puck travels much faster than any person can skate. So a kill can cover any one option, but it can't cover three options in two seconds. Two quick passes pull a kill further out of shape than thirty seconds of one player stickhandling on the boards.

And the pass that beats a kill is usually the second one. The first pass makes them move. The second pass goes into the space they have just left.

So when do you shoot? The usual default is to shoot when the kill is out of shape, not into four killers who are already set. A shot into four set bodies is a coin flip. They block it, clear it, and you have to come back in against a kill that is ready for you. That is a coaching philosophy, not a law.

The real alternative is a shot-volume power play. You shoot from the moment you are set, and you count on tips, rebounds and winning the loose pucks. Why would a team play that way? It suits a team with a heavy shot from the point, a brave player at the net and good puck hunters. Waiting for the kill to break suits a team with a good passer and a player with a strong one-timer. So ask your coach which one your unit plays.

Second idea. At their net, screen from just outside the crease, and never touch the goalie.

If you are the net-front player, you live just outside the crease. Keep both feet clear of its red line, not just out of the blue paint. In most leagues that line counts as part of the crease, and standing a boot's width further out satisfies every league.

From there you have three jobs. You screen the goalie, which means standing in their line of sight so they can't see the shot. You get your stick blade on shots from the point to tip them. And you hunt the rebounds. Keep two hands on your stick, with the blade on the ice, because all three jobs need it.

A screen from outside the crease, with no contact and without crowding the goalie so they can't move, is legal. It is the most valuable thing a net-front player does. Keep your hands and your blade down. A screen is your body in their sightline, nothing else. Keep your stick out of the crease too, even with both your skates outside, or it can cost you the goal.

Now the part that keeps you safe there. At the front of the net your eyes are on the puck, and they should be. So a defender pushing you sideways is someone you feel, not someone you see. And the path they move you along has a steel goal post at one end and the goalie at the other.

So know where the near post is before you plant. Take the shove braced, with a wide base, knees bent, weight low, and knees and hips giving rather than locked. Head up, chin off your chest, and never duck, so you never meet a post head first. That protects your head and neck. Keep it up even going slowly, and in every league, because a defender can push you there whether the push is allowed or not.

Hold your spot with your feet. Don't push back, don't lean, and never put your stick across anyone. Never put your stick or your body into anyone's back, and if you can see their numbers, pull up. Never lift anyone off their feet. Even going slowly, a crash there can hurt.

Never brace against the goalie the way you brace against a defender. Never put a hand, a shoulder or a hip into the goalie to make room, however much of your power play is ticking away. If a goalie leans into you, give ground straight away, then set the screen again with both feet off the red line.

Here is why. A goalie who is down or turned can't protect themselves, so contact can hurt them. It can also be a penalty, and it can cost you the goal. And arrive under control. Building up speed into contact, even with two hard strides, can be charging. If contact is coming anyway, don't speed up through it. Be the player who clearly tried to avoid it. A charge on a goalie can get you thrown out of the game in every league, and in some it is automatic. In some leagues a reckless one is a match penalty, so plan on that.

In a scramble, play the puck, not the goalie. If you push the goalie into the net with the puck after a save, the goal comes off, and expect a penalty too. And get out of the way of a goalie skating back to their crease. Blocking that path can cost you the goal, and it can be a penalty even with no contact. On a power play, any of those penalties ends your advantage.

There is one more job near the net, and it belongs to whoever is on the far side. It is called the backdoor, the far post on the side of the net away from the puck.

Whenever the kill's low defender commits to the puck side, or to your net-front player, a teammate arriving at the far post is a two-on-one that nobody is covering. The pass usually crosses the middle of the ice to get there, which is why it so often goes in.

So arrive late. A player who stands at the back post all shift gets marked. A player who arrives as the shot or the pass is released doesn't. Time it. Don't camp there.

Third idea. On the penalty kill, choose what you give up, and hold your own job.

Four of you cannot cover five of them. So stop trying to cover everything. A penalty kill is a decision about which shot you are willing to give up. Then you spend two minutes making sure it is the only shot they get.

Every kill shape gives something away on purpose. One shape leaves the middle of the slot. Another leaves the outside and the far post. Another leaves the ice around the edge of the zone.

So here is how a kill breaks. One killer sees the open area and leaves their own job to cover it. The thing that was theirs is now open, and that becomes the goal. So hold your own job, even when the open area looks dangerous. Giving it up is the plan. On a penalty kill, staying in your own job matters more than working hard.

And remember that time is on your side. A kill that gives up three shots from the point and never breaks is better than one that gives up nothing for forty seconds and then breaks.

Fourth idea. On the kill, clear it, then change. And a skater short, fire the long clear where your league allows it. When you win the puck on a kill, get it out first, and change tired players second. Change when you can, not when you must. A tired killer starts chasing, and a killer who chases opens holes.

Now the rule that is the kill's biggest advantage. Icing is shooting the puck from your own half across the other team's goal line, with nobody touching it. Normally that stops play for a faceoff in your end. But in most leagues, a team that is a skater short can fire the puck the length of the ice without icing being called. Where you have that, fire it the moment you have it. You don't need to find a teammate. Getting the puck two hundred feet away buys time, and a chance to change.

Check your league first, because not every level gets this. At some younger age groups a team that is a skater short is called for icing like anyone else.

And it only works one way. On the power play, never fire a loose puck the length of the ice. You get a faceoff in your own end, and in many leagues your tired players can't change.

Let me pull those four ideas together, because they are the core of special teams.

Three. On the kill, choose what you give up, and hold your own job even when the open area looks dangerous.

Four. On the kill, clear it, then change. A skater short, fire the long clear where your league allows it. A player up, never.

Everything else in this episode adds detail to one of those four. So now let's go back through it, starting with the power play.

Fifth idea. Wherever you stand on the power play, be ready to shoot or pass the moment the puck reaches you.

A player who needs two touches to become dangerous isn't an option at all. The kill can ignore that player and cover the other three. So get your feet set and your stick blade on the ice before the puck arrives. And look inside, toward the middle of the ice, before you look up.

If you play on a half-wall, the usual advice is to play your off-wing. That means a left-handed shooter on the right side, or the other way round. Why? It puts the puck on your inside hand. That opens your body to the middle of the ice, so you can shoot or pass without turning. It also makes the one-timer possible.

A one-timer is a shot taken straight off a pass, without stopping the puck first. Keep the backswing short. In at least one adult recreational league, a backswing over fifteen inches makes it a slap shot, which is a penalty there. If it hurts someone, you are thrown out of the game, with a suspension after it.

The threat of the one-timer is worth as much as the shot. If you are a live one-timer on the far side, one killer has to stay wide to cover you. That wide killer is exactly how your teammates in the slot and at the net get free.

Sixth idea. Know your power-play shape, and your spot in it, before your first shift.

Which shape your power play uses is your coach's choice, not a rule of hockey. So is who stands where. If you are guessing where to stand, you are the reason the pass gets covered. So find out before you are on the ice. Here are the five you are likely to meet.

The first is the one-three-one. One player at the point. Two on the half-walls. One in the middle of the slot, called the bumper. And one at the net front. That is one point player, not two.

Here is how it works. The point player walks the line, which means sliding sideways along the blue line. Each slide changes the angle of every passing lane, so keep sliding until a killer turns their head or shifts across to follow you. The two half-wall players make most of the decisions. From there they can reach the point, the bumper, the net front, and the far half-wall. The bumper keeps their blade on the ice for a shot straight off a pass, and holds the kill's high players down so they can't step out at the point.

Why do teams play it? Because it fills all five dangerous areas at once, and there is always a short pass available. And the bumper stands in the one place a box kill doesn't cover. The cost is that every player must be comfortable under pressure. With only one player back, a lost puck at the blue line is an odd-man rush the other way, more of them coming than you have back.

The bumper is often the centre, because it suits a centre's skills, soft hands in traffic and quick passes. But plenty of teams put a winger there. So ask who has it.

The second is the umbrella. Three players high, in a curve, not a flat line. One sits highest, in the middle near the blue line. The other two sit a little lower on either side. Two more play low, around the net. There is no bumper. The middle of the slot is left empty on purpose, so the high players have clean shooting lanes into it.

The three high players move the puck side to side, looking for a shot with a clear lane. The two low players screen, tip and collect rebounds. Why play it? It gives you space and time up high, and three angles to shoot from. And with players always above the puck, the kill rarely breaks out the other way. It suits a team with heavy shots from the point and a brave net-front player.

The cost is that the puck stays far from the net, where goalies and killers are comfortable.

The third is the overload. Four players on one side of the ice, and the fifth on the far side as a release. The four work the puck round in a small area until a killer commits too far. Then you attack the gap, or swing it across to the far side for a shot while the goalie is moving. Why play it? It outnumbers the kill in one corner, and it beats a kill that won't come out to the boards. It suits a patient, skilled unit. The cost is fewer shots, and short passes a sharp kill can read and pick off.

The fourth is the spread. Two low, just outside the posts. Two high and wide. One in the middle of the slot. The idea is the spacing. Stand far enough apart that one killer can't cover two of you. Why play it? It stretches a small kill as far as it will go, and it makes chances at the far post almost by itself. The cost is long passes, which a well-placed stick can pick off.

The fifth is the older two-three. Two defencemen at the points, three forwards low. The points shoot and keep pucks in, and the three low players screen, tip and hunt rebounds. Why play it? It is safe and easy to teach. With two defencemen back, the kill rarely breaks out the other way. It suits a team protecting a lead, or a group still learning to hold the blue line. The cost is that it ignores the middle of the slot. Against a good box, it is close to harmless.

Your coach picks the shape. Ask which one you run, and exactly where you stand in it.

Seventh idea. The plays those shapes are there to make.

The first play is the seam pass. A seam is a passing lane that goes through or across the middle of the kill, not around it. Why does it work? Because a kill's shape is built to defend the outside. Any puck that crosses the middle makes every killer and the goalie turn at once. But if the seam isn't there, don't force it. Picked off in the middle, it is a breakaway the other way, with four of your teammates on the wrong side of the puck.

The second play is the royal road pass. The royal road is an imaginary line straight down the middle of the ice, net to net. A pass that crosses it just before a shot is the most dangerous kind of pass that has been measured, in tracking of even-strength play.

Here is why. A goalie who has to follow the puck across the middle has to push hard sideways, and while they push they are off their angle. The shot arrives before they are set. So before you shoot from a half-wall, look at the far side. A pass across beats a shot from the same spot almost every time.

The third play is the point shot for tips and rebounds. The shot from the point isn't an attempt to score from the point. It is a way of delivering a tip or a rebound. Make it low, hard and on net, with a body in front. Keep it a wrist or snap shot if your league limits slap shots. In at least one adult league a big wind-up counts as a slap shot, a penalty there, and if it hurts someone you are thrown out.

Shoot when your net-front player is set and the goalie can't see, not before. And change the angle first. Step sideways along the line until the shot-blocker has to move their feet, usually a step or two. Then the lane the kill set is no longer the lane you are in. A shot that misses the net is a free clear off the end boards, and twenty seconds gone.

The fourth play is the walk-down. That is the half-wall player skating the puck down toward the goal line and inside, instead of passing. It forces the kill to decide. If the kill's low defender steps out to you, the net front is free. If they stay, you have a shot from the top of the circle with the goalie moving. Walk toward the middle, not the corner. Below the goal line and wide, you are no threat. Head up, and keep your feet moving toward the net, because once your feet stop, the kill resets. If it is shut down, take it back to the point and start again.

A walk-down starts on the boards with a killer stepping out at you. So this is the place to learn how to meet the boards, and I will teach it in full once.

First, who it is about. The checker means the other player coming at you along the boards, whether they are after the puck or just coming at you.

Never turn your back to the checker, and never duck. Both protect your head and neck. If you turn your back, you can't see the hit coming or brace for it. If you duck, your head meets the boards first.

So here is the position you aim for. Get your skates parallel to the boards, feet apart, knees bent. Forearm and hip to the boards. Never the point of your shoulder, and not the whole shoulder if you can help it. Head up, chin off your chest.

If you are already facing the boards and can't turn parallel, that is damage control, not a choice. Get your stick and gloves up and spread on the glass, and keep your head off it.

Being a player up doesn't protect you here, and neither does a league with no body checking. The boards don't need an opponent to hurt you. Even where a killer isn't allowed to push or pin you into the boards, they can still arrive while you are against them. Keep it up even going slowly.

So far on the power play. Know your shape and your spot in it, and look for the pass across the middle before you shoot. On a walk-down, head for the middle, not the corner.

Eighth idea. Getting into their zone.

Most amateur power plays die at the blue line, not in the zone. Here is why. A kill defending its blue line is compact, with four players between you and the net. You have to bring five players through a narrow gate without losing the puck.

So a clean entry with the puck is worth far more than a fast one. Get in with control and set up, and you have ninety seconds of pressure. Dump it in and lose the race, and you have ten, and then you do it all again.

The drop pass is the classic power-play entry. The carrier leaves the puck for a teammate coming behind them. The attack keeps its speed, and their line commits to the wrong player. But every kill expects it now, and a drop pass stolen at the red line is a breakaway against you.

The swing helps too. One or two players curl back to pick up speed, so they take the pass already moving forward. That solves the hardest part of an entry, getting to the line with speed and the puck at the same time.

Ninth idea. When the kill clears it.

Tenth idea. Five-on-three.

Two players up is a different game, not a better power play. It should almost always score. When it fails, it is nearly always because the unit played it like five-on-four.

Eleventh idea. The shapes on the kill.

Again, which shape you run is your coach's choice, and the same four players can run all three in one game. So know which one is called before the faceoff.

The first is the box. It is the one almost everyone learns first. Two forwards high, one on each point. Two defencemen low, covering the corners and the front of the net. A box has no single top player. Two forwards, side by side, is the whole point.

Here is how it works. The high forwards stay in the shooting lanes and never both drift to the same side. Turn your inside shoulder so you can see your point player and the middle at once.

So against a one-three-one, the bumper stands in that hole, uncovered by design.

The second is the diamond. One player at the top. Two in the middle, one on each side. One low.

Why does it exist? Because a box leaves the bumper unmarked. In a diamond, the two middle players each take a half-wall, and both squeeze onto the bumper when the puck goes to the middle. The top player pressures the point and takes away the middle shooting lane. Pressure with your stick on the puck, not a hit. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call, and if you can see their numbers, pull up. Don't get walked around up there, because you are the only one. The low player covers the front of the net, alone, the same way, with no lean, no push, and head up.

The price is that the low player is alone against a net-front player and a backdoor arriver at once. That is a two-on-one you are choosing to give up. And a confused diamond is worse than a good box.

The third is the wedge plus one. Three killers hold a tight triangle around the net, two defencemen just off the edges of the crease and one forward higher up in the slot. The fourth player, the plus one, pressures whoever has the puck.

The triangle stays tight and protects the slot. It doesn't chase. The two near the crease hold their spots with no lean and no push, head up, chin off their chest. The plus one's job is to make every possession uncomfortable, not to win the puck. Pressure with your stick on the puck, not a hit. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call, and if you can see their numbers, pull up. When the puck moves, the nearest triangle player steps out as the new plus one, and the old one drops back in. That handover is the hard part, so everybody has to know who is out and who is in, every second.

Why do teams play it? It protects the most dangerous ice and still puts pressure on the puck, and it makes turnovers. The cost is that everyone has to keep track of a job that keeps moving, so it is a poor choice for a young or thrown-together unit. And a plus one who just chases the puck around the outside leaves you killing with three. Coaches teach the plus one's route in different ways, so ask how yours moves.

Twelfth idea. The pressure setting.

Every shape also has a pressure setting, passive or aggressive, and that is your coach's choice too. It is a separate decision from the shape.

Passive means holding your shape, giving up the outside, blocking lanes and waiting for a clear. Why? It is the lowest-risk way to play. It is the usual choice when you are protecting a lead, when you are tired, late in a kill, or when their power play is much better than you.

Aggressive means going at the puck carrier to force mistakes. Why? Because it can create chances for your team going the other way. But it also creates chaos, and chaos with four against five is not usually your friend. Pressure spends a killer to buy a mistake, and the mistake doesn't always come.

Pressure means your stick on the puck, not a hit. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call. And if you can see their numbers, pull up and play the puck.

Whichever setting it is, pressure as a unit or not at all. One player pressuring while three sit back is how the passing lanes open.

Thirteenth idea. Kill it before it gets in.

The best place to kill a penalty is before the puck enters your zone. Here is why. A power play that never gets set up never gets to use its extra skater. In the middle of the ice you can make them play your way. Once they are set up in your end, you are only limiting damage.

Whatever the plan, here is what you do. Take away the middle and push them to one side. A power play forced to come in along one side of the ice has lost half its options. Angle them with your skating, not your stick.

Stand up at your blue line as a pair. Both defencemen step up, or both back off. One of each leaves a hole. Stepping up means your stick on the puck, not a hit. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call, and if you can see their numbers, pull up.

Go after the receiver, not just the carrier. The dangerous player on the entry is usually the one arriving with speed off a drop pass or a swing. Taking away the target is easier than taking away the puck. So put your stick in that player's passing lane. When the puck reaches them, go for the puck, not the player. Stick on the puck, not a hit. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call. Where hitting isn't allowed, even a step or a glide into them can be a penalty. And contact before the puck arrives is interference, a penalty in every league.

Make them dump it. A dump-in with your defencemen already turned is a win. Make them fail to get in three times, and the penalty can be over without you ever defending your own end.

And don't lunge or dive at your blue line. A missed poke at the puck there is a clean entry, with you out of the play.

So when do you pressure, and when do you back off? Go through it in this order.

Can you actually get there? Pressure that arrives a stride late is worse than none, because you have spent a player for nothing.

Where is the puck? Pressure the puck when the carrier is looking down, receiving it badly, or facing the boards. Never go at one with their head up and help on both sides.

Now a safety point that goes with that. A carrier who is looking down, facing the boards, or has their back turned is telling you the puck can be won. That is all it tells you. It is not a reason to arrive harder. A player facing the boards is exactly the picture the checking-from-behind rule is written about.

So angle to the puck, never through their back. If you can see the numbers on their jersey, pull up and play the puck. If the only way to the puck is through their back, you were never winning it, so leave it.

Here is what a hit from behind costs. It is never just two minutes. In most leagues any call for it throws you out of the game, and where it doesn't, you get a ten-minute misconduct on top of your penalty. If you drive a player who can't protect themselves into the boards or the net from behind, plan on a match penalty, which goes past being thrown out. In some leagues the referee has no choice. And you don't need the boards for the worst case. In most leagues a hit from behind in open ice can also be a match penalty.

What is the clock? Pressure early, while you are fresh. In the last thirty seconds, or when you are out of gas, back off and make them beat your shape.

And what is the score? Protecting a one-goal lead late is not the time to gamble.

So far on the kill. Know your shape, and hold your own job. Pick a pressure setting as a team, and pressure with your stick on the puck. Kill it at your blue line if you can. And go for the puck, never through their back.

Fourteenth idea. Once they are set up in your end. Your stick is the most useful thing you have when you are a player short. Blade flat on the ice, out in front of your body, in the passing lane, not on the puck carrier. Sticks in lanes stop far more passes by putting players off than by picking them off.

Take away the seam first. Point your stick at the most dangerous pass, not at the puck. The puck is already being handled by a teammate of yours. The pass around the outside is the one you are happy to give up. And stay low with your knees bent, because you can't move sideways quickly from standing up.

The power play wants you to chase. Them having the puck on the outside isn't dangerous. You leaving your spot to chase it is. If you chase and miss, you have made the seam yourself, because four players can't cover five with one of them out of place.

So chase only a puck you can actually win. A bobbled pass. A player with their back turned. A puck on the boards with no support. And a turned back still tells you the puck is winnable and nothing else. Stick on the puck, not a hit. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call. If you can see their numbers, pull up and play the puck.

Talk all the time. Call switches, call the bumper, call the backdoor. Most breakdowns on a kill are two players working to different ideas, not two players being lazy. And tell your goalie what you are giving up. If the plan is to give up shots from the point, your goalie will set up for a shot through traffic, instead of waiting for a pass across.

Change when you can, not when you must. Clear first, then change, in that order. And when your unit changes, count before you go. Too many players on the ice is a penalty, and on a kill that is two players short.

Fifteenth idea. Blocking shots.

Blocking shots is a skill you learn, not just bravery. And how much your team blocks at all is a coaching choice. Some kills want players collapsing in front of the goalie and blocking a lot. Others want the lane taken away on your feet, with the goalie seeing everything. A kill that blocks less is not a soft kill. So ask your coach which they want, and ask your goalie.

If you block, here is the whole of it, and it goes together.

Be in the shooting lane before the shot, not sliding into it. Sliding gets you a puck off the ankle, and a shooter who has already changed their angle.

Block from close, square to the shooter, with your head out of the lane. Hands behind your body, with the backs of your gloves facing out. Never lift a leg. Never turn your back to the shot or turn sideways to it, and never lunge. Stay on your feet until the puck has actually left the stick. Tucking your hands behind your shin pads is for a block when you are already down on the ice, not a standing one.

Block with your body, not your stick. Keep the stick flat on the ice beside you, or out of the lane. A puck off a stick held out in front tends to go upward, often into your own face. That is widely taught coaching know-how, not something anyone has measured.

And never block from in front of your own goalie's eyes. Half-deflecting a shot your goalie couldn't see is worse than letting it through cleanly. If you can't get properly into the lane, get out of it.

Sixteenth idea. Clearing the puck.

A skater short, where your league allows it, fire the long clear the moment you have it. Aim it to make their fetch awkward, into the corner away from their goalie, or hard around the boards. Keep it hard and flat, and keep the backswing short. In at least one adult league a big wind-up counts as a slap shot, a penalty there, and if it hurts someone you are thrown out.

It only lasts while a penalty leaves you with fewer skaters than them. Once you are level again, even at four-on-four, icing is called. But if you are killing two penalties and the first one ends, you are still a skater short, so you can still fire it. And it counts from when the puck leaves your stick. So with two seconds left on the kill, fire it rather than hold on to it.

Seventeenth idea. The two penalties a tired kill talks itself into.

Both cost two minutes instead of a faceoff, and both happen when you are desperate to get the puck out.

The first is putting it over the glass. In many leagues, shooting the puck straight out of the rink from your own end is a two-minute penalty, even when you are short, whether you meant it or not. And flipping it out on purpose is a penalty from anywhere on the ice, in every league. So never flip it out to buy a whistle.

A clear played hard off the glass that stays in the rink is exactly what you want. But where that rule applies, a clean flip over the glass from your end is a second penalty, and that makes it five-on-three. So aim your clears hard and flat, not lofted. Goalies too, because in some leagues a goalie's clear out of the rink can cost two minutes even by accident. Keep it under the glass.

The second penalty costs more. It is smothering the puck to get a whistle. A skater, not the goalie, who deliberately falls on the puck or gathers it into their body takes a two-minute penalty. If the puck is inside your own crease, or you are, plan on a penalty shot instead, a free shot alone on your goalie. If your own goalie has been pulled, plan on it being a goal for them. And in at least one league they can choose to take a second player off you instead of the shot, which makes it five-on-three.

The free option is to knock the puck down with your hand, or catch it and drop it straight back to the ice. If you hold on to it, it is the same penalty. A shot that goes under you while you are down blocking isn't a penalty, but using your hand to cover it is.

And don't leave it for your goalie to freeze. A goalie who freezes the puck when nobody is checking them can take two minutes too. Get a stick on the puck and clear it.

Eighteenth idea. The shorthanded rush.

When you win the puck, the default is always to get it out, then change. A shorthanded rush is the exception, and you take it only when it is free.

Take it when all three of these hold. The turnover has handed you a real two-on-one or a breakaway, not one you would have to make with speed. Their players are behind you. And you can get there before their point player gets back. If it is a race you will lose, it isn't a rush.

Don't take it if any one of these is true. It is a two-on-two or worse. Your partner hasn't seen it. You are protecting a lead late. Or you are the last player back. Why so strict? Lose it, and you are four against five again, with two of you out of the play and them coming back at speed. If you have to think about whether it is on, it is not on.

Let me pull the kill together. Know your shape, and hold your own job. Pressure as a unit, with your stick on the puck, or not at all. Go for the puck, never through their back, and pull up when you see their numbers. Sticks in the lanes, seam first. Block from the lane, on your feet, head out of it. Never lift a leg, turn your back or lunge, and never block from in front of your goalie's eyes. Clear, then change. Keep every clear in the rink, and never smother the puck.

Now the moments that belong to both sides.

Nineteenth idea. When a penalty ends.

On the power play, know the clock. The last ten seconds are your best chance. Why? The kill is watching the clock too, and their concentration dips.

On the kill, watch for the player coming out of the box. That player is a fresh attacker, arriving from the side at full speed, with nobody assigned to them. So whoever is nearest calls it out, loud, and picks that player up.

And if you are the one in the box, watch the play, not the clock. Come out facing the ice, and sprint. For about three seconds, you are the most dangerous player on the ice.

Twentieth idea. Faceoffs on special teams.

Faceoffs matter more on special teams than anywhere else. A player up, winning the draw skips the zone entry completely, and the entry is the hardest part. A player short, losing it means defending a set power play from the first second.

So agree the play before you line up. On the power play, are you winning it back to the point, to the half-wall, or forward? On the kill, are you winning it clean for a clear, or tying up the other centre so a winger can sweep it? And where is that clear going? A tie-up is stick on stick only. Lift or press their stick with yours, over or under the blade, for an instant, then play the puck. Never a hand on their stick, and never your stick on their hands. And on the kill, never aim the draw out of the rink. A draw steered out on purpose can be a penalty, and on a kill that is five-on-three.

In some leagues, the team given the power play picks which faceoff dot in their end the first draw is on. Pick the side that suits your one-timer.

Win the draw with your stick, not by leaning on theirs. Contact with the other centre before the puck drops can be a penalty on its own, the very first time, and in some leagues the referee has no choice.

And wingers, stay off the hash marks, the short lines on the outside of the circle, until the puck drops. If a winger creeps in early, it is your centre who gets thrown out of the circle, not the winger. If your team does it twice on the same draw, in most leagues it is a penalty. On a kill, either of those makes it five-on-three.

Twenty-first idea. Four against four, and three against three. At four-on-four, each player has about a quarter more ice, mostly in the middle. So skill and skating matter more. But you have one fewer layer of help behind you, and a two-on-one comes at you faster. So defencemen should join the rush less, not more. Get caught up the ice and it is an odd-man rush the other way.

Change with real care. A change with the puck loose hands them a rush against three. So change when you have the puck in their zone, or after a clear. And don't spread out just because there is space. Support the puck with short passes.

Three-on-three overtime is about keeping the puck. They can't score while you have it. So if the attack isn't there, carry it back out to the middle of the ice and start again. That is standard practice at the top level, and it is allowed there.

Attack only when you have the extra player. A two-on-one at three-on-three is nearly a goal, and a one-on-one is nothing. Try to get one defender alone in space and go at them. And don't all chase the puck together.

Never change with the puck loose, because a bad change at three-on-three is a breakaway. Change while you hold the puck, often by passing it back to your goalie. The goalie settles it and plays it back out once the change is made. They don't freeze it. Freezing it with nobody checking them can be a two-minute penalty, and the stoppage hands the other team the faceoff anyway.

Not every league plays overtime this way, and some don't play it at all. So check how yours works.

Twenty-second idea. Pulling the goalie on a power play.

Late in a game, a team on the power play can pull its goalie for an extra skater and make it six-on-four. That is the biggest advantage in hockey.

If you are the power play, put two at the net front, both feet off the red line, and neither one touching the goalie. Hold your spot, don't push back. Head up, chin off your chest, because a goal post can hurt you the same way the boards can. Send the extra player where they force a decision, to the bumper spot or the backdoor, never out on the outside. Move the puck quickly, and accept that the shooting lanes will be crowded.

If you are the kill, their empty net is your weapon. Win the puck and you can fire it the whole length of the ice into that net, and it counts. So clear with your head up, looking for the empty net. Keep the clear low and under the glass. Keep the backswing short too, because in at least one adult league a big wind-up is a slap shot and a penalty. A puck over the glass from your own end is still a penalty in many leagues.

Blocking shots and sticks in lanes matter more than ever. Block the same way as always. Be in the lane before the shot, square to the shooter, head out of the lane, hands behind you, stick flat beside you. Stay on your feet until the puck has left the stick. Never lift a leg, never turn your back or go sideways, never lunge, and never block from in front of your goalie's eyes.

There is a related moment. When the referee raises an arm for a penalty against the other team, play goes on until they get control of the puck. Your team will often pull its goalie for an extra skater then. In that window, the danger isn't a shot against you. It is one of your own players sending the puck back into your own empty net. So be careful how you move it backwards.

Twenty-third idea. Your goalie, on both.

On the kill, agree who fetches the puck behind your net before it starts, too. In some leagues your goalie can't play the puck in the corners, but they can still stop a rim behind the net and leave it for you. Either way, your goalie moves a puck on, never sits on it. Freezing it out there is a penalty.

Let me pull it all together.

On the power play, make one of them cover two of you. Move the puck, look for the second pass, and cash it in close to the net. Be ready the instant it reaches you. Know your shape and your spot in it. Get in with control, and when they clear it, one player chases.

At their net, screen from just outside the crease, both feet off the red line. Know where the post is, head up, chin off your chest, never duck. Never touch the goalie, and arrive at the far post late.

On the boards. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the checker, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly.

On the kill, choose what you give up, and hold your own job. Pressure as a unit, with your stick on the puck. Kill it at the blue line if you can. Go for the puck, never through their back, and pull up when you see their numbers. Sticks in the lanes. Block from the lane, on your feet, head out of it. Never lift a leg or turn your back, and never block from in front of your goalie's eyes. Clear, then change. Fire the long clear where your league allows it, keep it in the rink, and never smother it. A player up, never ice it.

And when the numbers change again, at four-on-four, in overtime, or with a goalie pulled, keep the puck. Change only when it is safe, and attack with the extra player.

Here are three questions for your coach.

Which shape do we run on the power play and on the kill, and where do I stand in each?

Do you want us shooting a lot, or waiting for the kill to break, and how much do you want us blocking shots?

And at our level, can a team that is a skater short ice the puck, and is body checking allowed?

Go and ask. Their answers tell you how your team wants to play special teams, and now you know why each answer makes sense.
