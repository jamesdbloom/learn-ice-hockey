This episode is about the rules of hockey. Not every rule. Just the ones that change how you play, so that you do something differently on the ice because the rule is there. We will take it slowly. For each rule I will tell you what to do, and also why. If you know why a rule shapes the play, you can still make the right choice when the play looks different from the picture in your head.

Before anything else, a few words you will hear all the way through. I will explain them now, so they don't get in the way later.

The rulebook. Hockey has more than one. The professional leagues, the international federation, and the national bodies each publish their own. When I say your book, I mean the one your league plays under.

The zones. Two blue lines split the rink into three parts. Your own end is the zone with your net in it. The middle part is the neutral zone. The other team's end, the one you attack, is called the attacking zone.

The red lines. The centre red line runs across the middle of the rink, halfway between the blue lines. The goal line is the red line each net sits on.

The crease. This is the painted blue area in front of each net. It is the goalie's area. The red line around its edge counts as part of it.

The officials. The referee calls penalties. The linesperson calls offside and icing, and drops the puck at most faceoffs. A faceoff is how play starts again after a whistle, with the puck dropped on one of the painted dots.

Penalties. A minor is usually two minutes in the penalty box, and your team plays a player short. A major is five minutes. Thrown out means you are sent off for the rest of the game. The team with the extra player is on the power play. The team that is a player short is on the penalty kill.

The checker. Near the boards, that is the other player coming at you, whether they are after the puck or just coming at you.

That is the vocabulary. Now the rules that matter most.

I will start with four big ideas. They are the ones that keep you on the ice, and keep you from getting hurt. If you only listen to the first part of this episode, take those four with you. After that we will follow a shift, from the bench to the whistle, and add the rest.

First idea. Before your first game, find out which rulebook you play under, and whether your game allows body checking. Here is why this comes first. The rulebooks don't always agree. The same act can be ordinary play under one book and get you thrown out under another. The second question matters just as much. Body checking means using your hips and shoulders to hit a player who has the puck, to win it. Where your game doesn't allow it, even a perfect check is a penalty.

A lot of hockey doesn't allow it. In North America that includes the younger age groups, girls' and women's hockey, and adult leagues set up as no-check. In Britain, junior hockey is no-check at under-ten and under-twelve, and so is the women's national league. And your age group doesn't settle it on its own. Why does this change how you play? Because in a no-check game, your answer to a puck carrier is your skating, your stick on the puck and your position. Never your body. A plain check there is a penalty. A hard or reckless one can get you thrown out of the game.

Second idea. Protect heads, theirs and yours. Never hit anyone in the head or neck, and never duck. Start with their head. Where hitting is allowed, keep it below the shoulders. Don't learn this rule from watching the NHL. It is the most lenient league there is on hits to the head. Now your own head. Carry the puck with your head up. Here is why, and it is a rule as well as a habit. In Britain's book, skating head down at a checker takes away some of your protection. Don't give anyone the chance.

And never duck. If you tuck your chin to your chest, your neck lines up straight. Then your head meets the boards or a goal post first, and that can badly hurt your neck. It can happen even at walking speed. In some leagues, crouching down to avoid a check is a penalty as well.

A wide stance with your knees bent isn't ducking. It's how you take a hit. So near the boards, here is the position the rules and your neck both want. Get your skates parallel to the boards, feet apart, knees bent. Take the contact along your side. Forearm and hip to the boards. Never the point of your shoulder, and not the whole shoulder if you can help it. Your shoulder and hip turn toward the checker. Head up, chin off your chest. Never turn your back to the checker. That position matters more than anything else in this idea. From here on I will use one short version of it, the same words every time, so you can learn it like a line. And if a hit lands on your head, come off and say so. After a knock to the head you are off for the rest of the day, and a medical professional decides when you play again. Third idea. If you can see their numbers, don't finish the check. Pull up and play the puck. The numbers are on the back of a jersey. If you can see them, you are about to hit a player from behind. Here is why this idea makes the top four. These are the penalties to expect most in recreational hockey. That is coaching know-how, not a count. And they don't usually come from bad intent. They are what a tired or beaten player does instead of skating.

Seventh idea. The delayed offside, and tagging up. Sometimes the linesperson raises an arm instead of blowing the whistle. That's a delayed offside. It means one of your team is offside, but the other team can bring the puck out, so play carries on.

Go for a puck the goalie is playing outside the crease with your stick, never with your body. In Britain it isn't clear whether the trapezoid is enforced everywhere. So assume your own goalie is bound by it, and assume theirs might come for the puck anyway. Then ask your league.

Freeze the puck only under real pressure. In many leagues a soft freeze on a routine dump-in from beyond the red line costs you like an icing. The faceoff is in your end, they pick the dot, and your team can't change.

Keep both your skates and the puck on your own side of the centre red line. That's legal in every book.

If your stick breaks, don't go to the bench. Call for one from a teammate on the ice, and play with your blocker, glove and body until it arrives.

And when a fight breaks out near your crease, stay in your net. If you need to move, go where the referee sends you, with permission. Never join in.

Sixteenth idea. Broken sticks, for skaters. When your stick breaks, open your hand. Drop it straight away. You can keep playing without a stick, but playing on with a broken one is a minor. Don't poke at the puck with what's left.

Don't carry the broken one to the bench, either. Drop it where you are. Get a new one handed to you, from your bench or by a teammate. Never have one thrown or slid to you.

And here is a habit the rule creates. If you're a defenceman who loses your stick in your own end, drop it, get your body into the passing lane, and let a forward hand you theirs. Forwards, hand yours over. You can get to the bench far more easily than a defenceman can.

Seventeenth idea. Faceoffs. If you're not taking the draw, keep both skates outside the circle and off its line. At a dot in either end, stay on your own side of the hash marks, the short lines beside the circle, until the puck drops. Touching the line is fine. Crossing it isn't.

Here is why it matters so much. If you move in early, your centre gets thrown out of the draw, not you. Your team then takes the draw with its second choice. A second violation by your team on the same draw can cost you two minutes. Losing your best faceoff player because a winger leaned in early is one of the most costly rules mistakes there is, and it's completely avoidable.

Centres, win the draw with your stick. Closing your hand on the puck can be a penalty, and batting it with your hand to win the draw is a minor. In Britain's book, winning the draw with your skates is a violation as well.

Two more things change how your team plays faceoffs. After your opponents take a penalty, or ice the puck, your team picks which dot in their end. So somebody on your team should be making that choice on purpose. And if your team has just earned a draw in the other end, don't go and join a scrum there. If players from the bench or the points get stuck in, the draw can be moved out to the neutral zone.

Let me pull the two nets together. Screen from outside the paint, off the red line, head up, without pushing. Never into the goalie, in or out of the crease, and never in their way back. Shoved in, get out and go around. In your own crease, never close your hand on the puck. Goalies, know whether you have a trapezoid, freeze only under pressure, and stay in your net in a fight. When your stick breaks, drop it. And on the draw, skates outside the circle and behind the hash marks until the puck is down.

The last part is the one that happens when play stops.

Eighteenth idea. Play as though one major ends your game. First, the sizes of penalty, all in one place. A minor is two minutes, with your team a player short. A major is five. A misconduct is ten minutes for you, but a teammate comes straight on, so on its own it doesn't put your team a player short. Being thrown out is the rest of the game, and sometimes more.

In a lot of hockey, one major ends your game. In many leagues, every major comes with being thrown out. In Britain's book, a first major for a long list of fouls throws you out straight away. Hits from behind, cross-checks, slashes, hooks, high sticks and hits to the head are all on that list. And a second major, or a second misconduct, in one game throws you out too. So play as though one is enough.

Why does that change how you play? Because a major leaves your team a player short for five full minutes, and a goal against doesn't end it. Then you're gone for the rest of the game. And it costs more than tonight. Many leagues suspend you for your next game, or report it so you can be banned for longer. In some, you can be banned for something the referee didn't even call.

Penalties add up as well. In some leagues, as few as three penalties of any kind in one game throw you out.

A misconduct often comes with a minor, and then your team is short. So once you're given one, add nothing to it. No word to the referee, and no shove on the way to the box.

In Britain, wear your neck guard every shift. Outside the Elite League, going on without one is a ten-minute misconduct straight away, with no warning. A second one throws you out.

And if you play adult recreational hockey in North America, ask whether your league belongs to CARHA, the adult recreational body. Where it does, the rules change what you do. The slap shot is banned outright. Deliberate body contact is a penalty in every game. And almost every major throws you out. Many adult leagues don't belong, so ask.

Nineteenth idea. After the whistle. The whistle doesn't end your shift. The scrum around the net and the faceoff both carry on after it, and that's where undisciplined players give away penalties.

Leave the puck where it is, or pass it gently to the official. Never shoot it away. That's usually two minutes, and in some leagues it can be more. Shooting it toward an official or the crowd can get you thrown out.

Don't throw a punch, and don't answer one. Turn away, get to an official or your bench, and let the referee sort it out. A punch with your glove on isn't a mutual two minutes. In some leagues even a punch that misses counts as fighting. Assume a punch gets you thrown out. And if a punch lands on your head, come off and say so. You're off for the rest of the day, and a medical professional decides when you play again. Someone should stay with you, and you shouldn't drive or go home alone. If you get worse, or your neck hurts, get medical help straight away, even though you got up.

Assume a fight gets you thrown out. And stay out of anyone else's fight, even to break it up. In some leagues the first player to join a fight that's already going is thrown out, peacemakers included.

Do your chin strap up properly. If more than one finger fits between your jaw and the strap, it's too loose. And keep your hands off anybody's helmet, your own included. In some leagues, taking a helmet off in a scuffle, yours or theirs, gets you thrown out. And a scuffle only needs one penalty, so a shove after the whistle can count.

Racial slurs, taunts and sexual remarks can get you thrown out in every book, and can mean a ban too. In most leagues an obscene gesture gets you thrown out the first time.

Don't dive, or make a fall look worse than it was. That's a penalty too. In British hockey outside the Elite League it now comes with a ten-minute misconduct as well, and in Britain's book a second misconduct in a game throws you out.

Leave the talking to your captain. Only the captain, or an alternate when the captain isn't on the ice, can discuss a rule with the referee, and only when invited. Coming off the bench to protest is two minutes. Disputing a call from the penalty box after play restarts gets you ten minutes. Here is why to keep quiet. Complaining never gets a call reversed. If your captain is invited to talk, ask, don't tell.

And never put a hand on an official. Deliberate force against one gets you thrown out in every book, and expect a ban on top.

Twentieth idea. Delayed penalties. Sometimes the referee raises an arm and lets play carry on. That means a penalty is coming against the other team. Play stops when they get control of the puck. Control, not a touch, so a puck off one of their skates doesn't end it. Remember whose arm it is. A linesperson's arm at the blue line means offside. The referee's arm means a penalty.

Because play stops as soon as they control the puck, they can't score against you from open play while the arm is up. So many teams pull their goalie for an extra skater. Here is how it works. The goalie skates to the bench, and a sixth attacker jumps on. Why do it? Because you're sure to get the puck or a whistle, there's no risk of a goal against from open play.

It's a tactic, not a rule. In recreational hockey plenty of goalies stay in their net, because the trip is longer than it looks. And a sixth attacker only helps if somebody comes off for the goalie. So agree in the room what your team does. The thing that goes wrong is a goalie setting off while nobody on the ice expects it. Ask which yours plays.

When the arm goes up and you're the closest player to the bench, and not in the play, you're the one coming off. Then keep the puck. No risky passes across the middle, and no forced shot from a bad angle. A miss that they collect ends your six-on-five, and you get a five-on-four instead. If the zone isn't open, take it back and regroup. They can't force a whistle without taking it from you.

One way it goes badly wrong is your own team putting the puck into your empty net. That's a goal against you, and the penalty is still given. So with your net empty, no blind passes.

If you score during the delay, a minor that was coming is wiped out. A major still goes on. And if your team is the one about to be penalised, get control of the puck as fast as you can. In your own end, a hand pass is legal, and it counts.

Let me pull it all together.

Before your first game, find out which rulebook your league plays under, and whether your game allows body checking. Play as though one major ends your game, and as though being thrown out costs you more than tonight.

Never hit anyone in the head or neck. Carry the puck with your head up, and never duck. At the boards, it's the same every time. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the checker, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly.

If you can see a player's numbers, whether they have the puck or not, pull up and play the puck. In any chase, go for the puck, not the player, and never hit anyone from behind. Keep your stick on the puck, never on the player, and below your shoulders. Never shove with both hands on it, and never chop.

Drag your back skate on the blue line. Get the puck past the red line on your stick before you send it away. Don't ice it, or knock the net off, to buy a rest. Screen the goalie from outside the paint, and never go into the goalie. After the whistle, leave the puck alone, keep your hands to yourself, and let your captain do the talking. And if a hit lands on your head, come off and say so, and let a medical professional decide when you play again.

Many of these rules change from league to league. So here are three questions for your coach. Which rulebook do we play under, and is this a checking game? Is our icing hybrid or automatic, and can we ice the puck on the kill at our level? And on a delayed penalty, does our goalie come off, and where does the extra skater go?

Go and ask. Their answers tell you which rules you play under, and now you know why each one changes how you play.

### 7. Departures from the source or the template

- **"Never lift anyone off theirs [their feet]"** (eleventh idea) is brief item 6's limb and is in neither the source nor the two additional pages. It is conservative and makes nothing cheaper, but it is not traceable. Cut it if traceability outranks the brief.
- **"A goalie who is down or turned can't protect themselves"** is the brief's wording (item 24). The source supports it only indirectly (:797, a goalie who could not protect themselves).
- **"Keep your head off it"** in the facing-the-boards fallback is brief item 5's wording. BC :899 says spread the load and put the stick and gloves on the glass.
- **"Get medical help straight away, even though you got up"** is brief item 34's wording, supported by CR :27 and :338.
- **"Even a light hit from behind can cause a serious head or neck injury"** is USA Hockey's warning as quoted in BC :861 ("could inflict a severe…"), softened to "can cause a serious", per the owner's "aware, not freaked out".
- **"Never low, on the legs"** replaces the source's "above their knees" (:529) in the legal-check definition, per brief item 17, because Hockey Canada sets the clipping line at the hips (:478). Stricter, not cheaper.
- **"Don't carry the broken one to the bench"** is stated for everyone. The source's flat rule is the IHUK In-House one (:589) and Hockey Canada's; USA Hockey allows a short carry near the bench (:589), and NHL/IIHF :587 say only not to carry it while play is live. Stricter, not cheaper.
- **The trapezoid's purpose** comes from the Sources trailer (:1171), the IIHF's own announcement, not from the body.
- **The template's "three questions to ask your coach"** is followed. The template itself had seven; the brief asked for three.
- **Structure.** The template goes "through the rink, one end at a time". A rules page has no natural ends, so after the spine the script follows a shift: the bench and the trip up the ice, contact, the two nets, and what happens when play stops. Each part ends with a recap.
- **Idea headings are joined to their first paragraphs** (spoken words unchanged) so no synthesis request can end on a bare heading.
- **Body checking is defined as "using your hips and shoulders to hit a player who has the puck, to win it"**, taken from the remake and :529. The source has no single definition.

### 8. What I found unclear or contradictory in the source

- :461 says checking from behind "can end your game in every book, and most do at the first tier", while :468 says USA Hockey's lowest tier "keeps you in the game". These agree, but a listener can only be given "most", so the script says "in most leagues even the smallest penalty for it does".
- :25's IHUK U12 minor (three or four minutes) sits in the Key focus but nowhere in the penalty-types table (:357). A reader of §4 alone would believe a minor is always two minutes.
- The source's own facing-the-boards fallback is missing. :33 and :510 teach the posture without it, so the script imports it from BC (:899). That is a gap in `rules_primer.md`, worth a plan row.
- :805 calls a screen "normally legal" and :811 says it is "not something you will get to argue from the ice". The script says "a screen is legal, and referees allow it all the time", which is :1102's wording. That is slightly more confident than :811.

### 9. Where this script is weakest

- **"Britain" is spoken twelve times.** Each one changes what a British listener does (the book, the U12 formats, the neck guard, diving, the trapezoid, the skate win at the draw). A North American listener may hear it as a British episode.
- **It is still a rules episode.** The broad regex share is 33.7%, lower than the remake but well over the "two in ten" guide for rule-shaped pages. The standard exempts the rules pages from that guide, but the owner may still find the eighth and twelfth ideas tariff-heavy.
- **The twelfth idea is the densest**: seven fouls and the lift in about 3,200 characters. It is where the "one plain consequence per act" discipline is most strained.
- **The goalie idea is short for a goalie listener.** It keeps the five rules that change what a goalie does and drops the bench-at-a-stoppage, crease-in-a-fight and pressure-test detail.
- **Seams fall inside two recaps.** That is safe, but a listener may hear a voice-continuity change between "Two." and "Three.".
- Backward and forward pointers were audited by hand for shape. Kept signposts: "From here on I will use one short version of it", "Everything else in this episode adds detail to one of those four", and the recap openers.
