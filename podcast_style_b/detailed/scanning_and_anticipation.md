===== SCRIPT START =====

This episode is about seeing the ice. Where to look, when to look, and how to use what you see to know what happens next. It takes its time. For each piece, I will tell you what to do and also why it works. If you know why something works, you can still do the right thing when the play looks a little different from the picture in your head.

Before any instructions, a few words you will hear all the way through. I will explain them now, so they don't get in the way later.

A look. In this episode, a look means turning your head away from the puck to see what is around you. Coaches also call it a scan, or a shoulder check.

The forecheck. That is the other team pressing you in your own end, to win the puck back. The players doing it are the forecheckers. The backcheck is the opposite. It is racing back toward your own end after your team loses the puck.

The half-wall. This is the stretch of boards roughly level with the faceoff dot, about halfway between the goal line and the blue line.

The slot and the net front. The slot is the dangerous scoring area in front of the net. Its top part, from the faceoff dots up to the tops of the circles, is called the high slot. The net front is the ice right in front of the goal.

That is the vocabulary. Now the job.

Players who seem to see the whole ice are not magic. Almost always, they have simply looked already, while the puck was somewhere else. That is a habit, not a gift.

I will start with four big ideas, because they matter most. If you only listen to the first part of this episode, take those four with you. After that we will go through the rest one piece at a time.

First idea. Take at least one look before the puck reaches you. Then have your eyes back on the puck when it arrives.

Here is why the timing matters. A pass takes one to three seconds to reach you. During those seconds, your eyes have no other job. So anything you see then is free. Now say you look only after the puck is on your stick. Then you pay for that look with the time you have on the puck. And the picture changes while you are looking.

Picture two players taking the same pass on the half-wall.

The first player watches the puck the whole way. The puck arrives. Only now do they look up. A forechecker is two feet away. They panic, and throw the puck up the boards to nobody.

The second player looked over their inside shoulder while the puck was being picked up behind the net. They looked again as the passer's head came up. And they took one last glance just before the puck arrived. So they knew about the forechecker before the puck was even passed. They took the puck already turning away from the pressure, toward open ice. Then they moved it to a teammate they had seen three seconds earlier.

From the bench, the second player looks quicker, calmer and more skilled. But their hands are no better. They just took two or three quick looks, at moments when looking was free. It costs no fitness, no ice time and no talent. All it takes is remembering to turn your head.

There is a hockey study behind this. Researchers in Sweden watched professional men's and women's games. They studied more than two and a half thousand times a player took a pass. When the player had looked first, their next play worked about seventy-nine times in a hundred. Without a look, it worked about seventy times.

What the study could not tell is whether more looks beat fewer. The researchers say that plainly. So treat the first look as a bar to clear. Once you clear it every time, work on what you see, not on how many times you look.

One exception is the goalie. A goalie looks off the puck before a shot, to see who is where. But once the shot is released, a goalie never looks away from it, all the way in.

Second idea. Decide while the puck is still on its way.

The puck's arrival should set off an action. It should not start a decision. Most players get the puck, then look, then decide, then act. That takes well over a second. In that time, a forechecker can close the space you thought you had. Good players look, then decide, then get the puck, then act. Same actions, in a different order. And the new order costs you nothing.

So while the puck is coming, name your first option in your head. One or two words is enough. Up the wall. D-to-D, a pass across to your defence partner. Reverse. Off the glass. Naming it is what commits you. A vague idea is not a decision.

Name your bail-out too, your second choice. Here is why. Your first option will often vanish in the last half-second. If you already have a plan B, you just switch to it. If you don't, you are starting from nothing. Panic is what happens when you run out of options in the middle of a play.

Then let your last look overrule you. That is the whole reason the last look exists. It is there to cancel a plan that has gone out of date.

And accept being wrong sometimes. A second-best play made straight away beats the perfect play made a second late, almost every time. Below the professional game, a quick decision seems to matter more than a perfect one. Coaches widely believe that, but nobody has measured it.

Third idea. Know what you are looking for, and in what order.

Start with the puck. It is the easiest thing on the ice to find, because everyone is skating toward it. So it does not need a look of its own. Keep it as your anchor, the thing your eyes keep coming back to. Then spend every extra look on people and space. They are the things that will not announce themselves.

One honest point. The idea of the puck as your anchor comes from an eye-tracking study of five footballers, not hockey players. Take it as a strong hint, not as proof.

Now take in four things, in the order they can hurt you or help you.

First, the pressure on you. Who is closest, from which side, and how fast? The side matters more than the distance. A forechecker coming at your forehand side takes away your best play. One coming at your backhand side leaves your strong hand free.

Speed beats distance too. A forechecker fifteen feet away at full speed is more urgent than one eight feet away and gliding.

Second, your outlets. An outlet is the pass that gets the puck away from pressure and up the ice. Know three things before the puck arrives. Your first option, the play you would most like to make. Your support option, a teammate close enough to take the puck under pressure, usually beside you or behind you. And your safe out.

The safe out is a rim around the boards, or the puck lifted off the glass and out. Here is why you want one ready. If you know your safe out before the puck comes, you are never trapped. You are only choosing.

Third, the danger you are responsible for. Everything so far was about you. This one is about your goal. In your own end, ask one question. If the puck goes to the net right now, who scores? Usually it is a player at your net front. Or it is someone sneaking into the high slot.

Last, the space you could move into. Where is the open ice now, and where will it be in two seconds? It comes last because it is the one you can afford to be wrong about. Seeing space is what makes a good player dangerous.

Fourth idea. When you are defending, look for bodies, not the puck.

Here is why. The puck does not score against you. A player does.

On the backcheck, look over your inside shoulder. That is the shoulder nearer the middle of the ice. Do it at least twice on the way back, and count opponents, not pucks. If you skate back watching only the puck, you arrive with no idea who came with you. Then you pick up nobody.

On the way back, you are there to find players and the puck, not to hit. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call. Never hit a player who can't see you coming, and never from behind.

In your own end, every time the puck moves, ask the question again. If it goes to the net right now, who scores? And every time the puck goes into a corner, check the front of your net. The easy tap-in at the back door, while everyone watched the corner, is not bad luck. It is a look nobody took.

Where your eyes go also depends on how your team defends its own end. There are three common ways.

The first is a zone collapse. It is the standard this episode assumes. Each player sags toward the net and takes whoever comes into their area. So your eyes switch back and forth, between the puck and the most dangerous player near you.

The second is strict man-to-man. Here you follow one opponent wherever they go. So that player gets far more of your looks. You still take the look that asks who scores.

The third is a hybrid. You play man-to-man down low, near the net, and cover an area up high. So where your eyes go depends on where you are standing.

Which one your team plays is your coach's call. So ask which yours plays, because your eyes follow your system.

Let me pull those four ideas together.

One. Take at least one look while the puck travels to you, and have your eyes back on it when it lands.

Two. Decide while it travels. Name a first option and a bail-out, and let your last look overrule you.

Three. The puck is your anchor. Then look for pressure, your outlets, the danger, and space, in that order.

Four. Defending, look for bodies, not the puck. Look over your inside shoulder on the backcheck, and keep asking who scores if the puck goes to the net right now.

Everything else in this episode adds detail to one of those four. So now let's go deeper, starting with how and when to look.

Fifth idea. Taking a pass on the boards.

Coaches single this one out as a pass to look hard before. That is coaching emphasis, not something anyone has counted. But the reason is plain. Half your ways out are boards and glass. And one of your blind sides is right behind you.

So take two looks at least. One early, and one late, just before the puck arrives. The early look tells you your options. The late look tells you whether they are still there.

Now one new word for this part. The checker is the other player coming at you near the boards, whether they are after the puck or just coming at you.

And looking is only half of it. Your eyes do not protect you if your body is in the wrong position. Taking a pass on the boards means contact with the boards. And the position you arrive in decides how much that contact costs you. This matters more than any tactic in this episode.

Never turn your back to the checker. And never duck.

Both protect your head and neck. If you turn your back, you can't see the hit coming or brace for it. If you duck, your head meets the boards first. That is true even at walking speed, so a slow checker does not make it safe.

So here is the position to aim for. Come to the wall on an angle, with your feet apart and your knees bent. Skates parallel to the boards. Forearm and hip to the boards. Never the point of your shoulder, and not the whole shoulder if you can help it. Head up. Chin off your chest.

If you are already facing the wall and can't turn, that is damage limitation, not a position to aim for. Get your stick and gloves up and spread wide on the glass. Spread the impact as wide as you can, with your head off it. That is only for when things have already gone wrong.

From here on, I will use one short version of that position, the same words every time, so you can learn it like a line. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the checker, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly.

Sixth idea. How to actually take a look.

A look is a head turn. Not a flick of the eyes, and not a dip of the chin. Turn far enough that your chin passes over your shoulder.

Here is why it has to be that far. If you only move your eyes, you see your own shoulder pad and the edge of your cage or visor. Your gear cuts down what you can see out of the side of your eye, more than people admit. So your head has to do the work.

A look is a snapshot, not a stare. Take the picture, and think about it afterwards. Nobody has measured how long a good hockey look should last. But here is a test you can use. If you are still looking a second later, you have stopped playing.

Check both shoulders, not just your favourite. Most players look toward the side they plan to pass to. The surprise is usually on the other side, the one you were not planning to use.

Keep skating while you look. Look while you glide, while you cross over, and while you pivot. Here is why. A look that costs you a stride makes you slower. And a look that makes you slower is one you will soon stop taking. All of this can be trained.

And the last look is always back at the puck. If you are still looking away when it arrives, you can miss it. Then a good decision is wasted on a missed catch.

Seventh idea. Use the moments nobody else uses.

There are three of them. In all three, your hands have nothing to do.

The first is while the puck is on the far side of the ice. That is the most wasted time in hockey. Look at your own side. Who is near you? Where is the seam behind you, the gap between two defenders that a pass can go through? Who is at your net front? Players who do this always seem to be in the right place. Now you know why.

The second is the instant your own pass leaves your stick. Your eyes want to follow it. Break that habit. The moment the puck leaves you, you are a player without the puck. Your next job is to support the puck, or to get to the net. Which one depends on what you see right now.

The third is every whistle. It is free information, several times a period, and almost nobody takes it.

Check who they have on the ice, and whether it is their top line. Check who is taking the faceoff, and which way they usually turn. Check whether their defence pair is the quick, mobile one or the stay-at-home one. That decides whether you can beat them wide. Check where your bench is, because that is where you will change. And check the score, the clock and the penalty clock. They change how much risk is worth taking.

The same free look comes when you are a defenceman going back for a puck dumped into your end. Look over both shoulders before you reach it. Where the forechecker is coming from decides your play. You take it behind the net, reverse it to your partner, or move it up the boards.

Forechecker or not, set your body for the boards. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the checker, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly.

Now use your ears too. A teammate shouting time means nobody is close to you. A teammate shouting man on means an opponent is closing on you. Either call is a look a teammate took for you. That is why teams that talk look faster.

If you are the one calling, only say time when you are sure. A wrong time sends a teammate into a hit they weren't ready for. Man on is the other way round. Call it early, even if you are not certain.

Man on or not, near the boards get your body right. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the checker, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly.

Let me pull the looking together. Make every look a real head turn, chin over your shoulder, on both sides, without losing a stride. Finish every set of looks on the puck. Use the far side, the moment after your pass, and every whistle. Listen for time and man on, and only call time when you are sure. On the boards, look early and late.

And hold the same position on the boards every time. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the checker, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly.

Now the second half of the job. Looking tells you where things are. Reading tells you where they are going. And almost all of it comes down to reading bodies.

Eighth idea. Reading the puck carrier, the player carrying the puck at you.

Look at the middle of their body. Put your eyes on the numbers on their chest, or on their belt. Not the puck, not the stick, not the head. You will still see the puck out of the corner of your eye.

Here is why. A head, a shoulder, the hands or a stick can all be thrown one way without the player going anywhere. Those fakes cost the carrier nothing. And they are aimed straight at you. But shifting their body weight sideways is part of how a player really changes direction. So the middle of the body is the more honest part. It is a bias in your favour, not a law.

Be honest about the evidence too. It comes from rugby, not hockey. And it says the middle of the body is where to put most of your attention. It is not one magic cue you can read on its own.

Watch the feet as a second cue. If the hips have turned and the outside foot is planted, the carrier is going that way.

Do not bite on a head fake, a shoulder dip or a puck fake.

The very best carriers can lie with their hips too. They sell a real change of direction with their whole body, then reverse it. If one beats you that way, you were simply beaten. Get back into position instead of chasing.

And when you have the puck, flip it round. Your head and hands are your fakes, and they cost you nothing. A look-off, a shoulder drop, a puck fake.

Ninth idea. Reading a passer.

A pass is easier to read than a carry. Here is why. A pass has to be aimed.

Start with the shoulders. Most passes go roughly where the passer's front shoulder points. A passer who has not turned square can't make a hard, accurate pass across their body.

Watch the stick blade too. An open blade, with its face rolled back, lifts the puck off the ice, into a saucer pass or a chip. A closed blade keeps the puck flat. And a closed blade pointed at a lane means that lane is about to be used.

Watch where their eyes have been. At most levels, players look at their target before they pass, often a full second before. If they looked at the far winger while taking the puck, the far winger is the pass.

But some players stare at one option and pass to another. That is called a look-off. A player who does it has already looked around, and knows where everyone is. Against that player, stop reading the eyes. Read the shoulders and the blade.

Now the read that wins you the puck. Say a player has just taken a pass without looking first. They have to look before they can pass. So pressure them straight away. Here is why it works. They need an extra moment, and they will show you the pass when they take that look.

Pressure here means your stick on the puck, not a hit. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call. And if you can see their numbers, pull up and play the puck.

Tenth idea. Reading a shooter.

Goalies and defenders who block shots read the same cues, so this is for both.

The hands are the loudest cue. They tell you what kind of shot is coming. Hands wide apart, with the bottom hand well down the stick, means a slap shot or a heavy wrist shot. That takes time to load. Hands close together means a quick release, a snap shot, or a pass.

The weight tells you whether the shot is coming at all. A shot needs the weight moving onto the front leg. If the weight is still sitting back, the shot is not coming yet, whatever the hands are doing. The shooter is waiting for a lane, a screen or a pass.

The blade at release tells you where. An open blade lifts the puck. A closed one keeps it low. For a goalie, that is the difference between a low save and getting beaten high.

And do not read the head. Any shooter worth worrying about is looking somewhere else.

If you are a defender, watch for a shooter walking the line. That means sliding sideways along the blue line to change the angle. A shooter doing that is hunting for a lane, not shooting yet. So get your feet into the lane they are trying to open. Square to it, with your head out of it.

Do not lunge at the shooter. Over-committing is how a point shot turns into a two-on-one behind you, two attackers against one defender.

And if you are a goalie, move your head to look around a screen, not over it.

Eleventh idea. Reading a defenceman, when you are the one attacking on the rush.

One new word first. Your gap is the distance between the defender and you. Reading a defender is mostly reading their gap and their feet.

Say the defender is closing hard, with a tight gap, about one to one and a half stick lengths. They mean to end the play at the blue line. And their momentum is a commitment. So change your pace. Slow down and let them skate past you. Or cut underneath them. Or chip the puck past them and race them to it.

That race is for the puck, not the player. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call. If you can see their numbers, pull up.

Do not skate straight into a defender who is closing hard. That is exactly the collision they set up.

Now say the defender is backing off, with a soft gap. They are giving you the blue line to protect the slot. You will get in with the puck, but not to the slot. Beat that with a delay, curling back to wait for the teammates coming in behind you. Or a drop pass, leaving the puck for a teammate behind you. Or a shot aimed for a rebound, with a teammate arriving at the net.

Watch their feet. Skates parallel and square means they can still turn either way. Skates angled, or hips opened to one side, means they have committed. Then attack the side they opened, or the side they left.

Watch whether they stand up at their line or back off. Standing up takes the way in away from you. Then chip it in and chase it, pass across to a teammate coming in another lane, or regroup. Don't force it. If you chase, you are chasing the puck, not the player. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call. If you can see their numbers, pull up. Backing off gives you the line and takes away the middle.

Their stick is the weakest cue, because a stick is easy to move. Trust the feet over the stick.

That gap distance is coaching convention, not measurement, and coaches disagree on the exact numbers.

Let me pull the reading together. A carrier, read the chest and hips, and don't bite on the head, the stick or the puck. A passer, read the front shoulder and the blade, and pressure one who took the puck without looking, with your stick on the puck. A shooter, the hands for what, the weight for whether, and the blade for where. A defender on the rush, the gap and the feet, and if they close hard, change your pace.

Twelfth idea. Read the shape, not just the players.

A forecheck has a shape. The shape tells you where the pressure will come from, before it arrives. So you can pick your breakout early. Your breakout is your way out of your own end with the puck.

The forecheckers are called F one, F two and F three. These are roles, set by the order players arrive. They are not positions, and not particular people. Their left winger can be F one this shift and F three the next.

The standard shape this episode assumes is the two-one-two. Two forwards press in deep. One stays higher. Their two defencemen hold their blue line.

Here is how it comes at you. Two players arrive fast, from two directions. F one goes for the puck. F two goes for your most obvious pass. They seal off the boards early, so a pass up the boards is the one most likely to be picked off.

The way out is a reverse to your partner, or a hard rim. A rim sends the puck on round the boards, the way it was already going. A reverse sends it back the way it came. If you mix them up, you give the puck away.

Here is why those two work against a two-one-two. Both forecheckers have committed. A reverse punishes their momentum. They are skating one way, and the puck goes the other. Skating it round the back of your net is the dangerous one here. F two is holding the far side, on purpose.

Other shapes change the read, and each one has its own purpose.

In a one-two-two, one forechecker comes in deep, two sit across the middle, and two stay back. You get time in the corner. But they are giving it to you on purpose. They want you to carry the puck into a wall of three. So beat it with a hard, early pass up the boards, before their second layer is set. Or regroup, back toward your own goal line, and come again.

In a trap, such as a one-three-one, there is almost no pressure in your corner. That is bait. The boards and the middle are filled. So skate the puck up with speed and your head up, and make someone commit. Or chip it past their layer, instead of passing into it.

Against an all-out press, everything is pressured at once. Take the safe out, off the glass or a rim, and live to play the next shift.

And off the glass means off it, never over it. From your own end, a puck that flies over the glass and out of the rink is a penalty in some leagues, even if you didn't mean it. If you put it out on purpose, it is a penalty in every league.

Here is a job almost nobody does. On your first shift, find out which shape they are running, and tell your bench.

One time to expect pressure straight away is right after your own team ices the puck. The faceoff comes back to your end. And in some leagues, the tired line that iced it can't change. An injured player can still be replaced. So decide your safe out before the puck is dropped.

Also watch each forechecker's angle. The angle tells you where they want you to go. One cutting off the middle is steering you to the boards, to seal you there. One coming from outside is steering you into the middle, usually toward a teammate who is waiting. Go where they don't want you.

Thirteenth idea. Play where the puck will be next.

The best read is not about a player at all. It is about where the puck is about to be.

Start with the boards. A hard rim follows the curve of the boards, and arrives where you would expect. A soft one dies in the corner. A puck flipped off the glass comes down somewhere else entirely. Rinks differ too. Some have lively glass, and some have dead spots behind the net. So learn your home rink on purpose. And at an away rink, bounce a couple off the end boards in the warm-up.

Now rebounds. The rule of thumb is that a rebound comes off the way the goalie's pad or blocker was facing. So a shot from an angle tends to kick out to the far side, and the place to arrive is the far post. That is a coaching rule of thumb, not a measured finding. And a goalie who steers rebounds on purpose will break it.

The part you can rely on is this. Arrive at the net moving, expecting the puck, not standing there watching for it. Arrive just outside the crease, the goalie's painted area, and off the goalie. Keep your stick on the ice, with the blade out of the paint.

Moving does not mean flying in. Arrive under control. Building up speed into contact, even with two hard strides, can be charging.

Keep your head up and your chin off your chest at the net. A defender can push you while your eyes are on the puck. And the goal post can hurt you the same way the boards can.

And never go into the goalie, in or out of the crease. Any contact with a goalie can be a penalty, and it can get you thrown out of the game. Here is why it matters. A goalie who is down or turned can't protect themselves, so contact can hurt them. And it can cost your team the goal.

Keep your skates and your stick out of the crease too. In some leagues, a goal doesn't count if even your stick or a skate was in the crease while the goalie was in it.

Standing in the goalie's line of sight, so they can't see the shot, is called a screen. From outside the crease, with no contact, it is legal, and the goal stands. But leave the goalie room to move inside the crease, or the goal can still be taken away. And stay off the crease line too. Most leagues count the line as part of the crease.

Never get in the way of a goalie who is trying to get back to the crease. That is a penalty in every league. In some, it costs your team the goal as well. A reckless block, or one that hurts someone, can get you thrown out of the game. In some leagues, hurting someone that way always does.

A goalie outside the crease is not fair game either. Any unnecessary contact with one is a penalty. In some leagues, even accidental contact is.

So never charge a goalie, in the crease or out of it. Charging a goalie can get you thrown out of the game in every league, and in some it always does. And trying to hurt a goalie is a match penalty in most leagues, the most serious penalty there is.

Fourteenth idea. Know which battles you are going to lose.

Reading the next play also tells you which battles you will lose. This read saves goals.

Say you are arriving second. Or you are off balance, or on your backhand, or up against a bigger player who already has position. Then you will probably lose that battle.

So stop trying to win it. Play for what happens next instead. Get to the spot where the loose puck will squirt out.

Or pin the puck against the boards with your skate or your stick, just for a moment, for a teammate to dig it out. Here is why it works. The player who gives up the battle and takes the loose puck ends up with it.

Pin the puck, not the player. Your stick goes on the puck, never on the player and never across them. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call. If you can see their numbers, pull up. Never use your skate to push a player, even without a kick. Never use your hands on the puck.

And never hold the puck there to get a whistle. Holding the puck to force a stoppage is a penalty in every league. In some, it is called after three seconds, even if someone is pressuring you.

You are on the boards, so the same every time. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the checker, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly.

Let me pull the shape and the next play together. Read the forecheck's shape early. Against a two-one-two behind your net, reverse it or rim it hard, rather than skating it round. Off the glass, never over it. Find out what they are running on your first shift, and tell the bench. Learn how the puck comes off the boards in your rink. Arrive at the net moving but under control, outside the crease, head up, and never into the goalie. And in a battle you will lose, go for the loose puck, and pin the puck, never the player.

Fifteenth idea. Anticipate, do not guess.

The two look the same when they work, and the same when they fail. But they are not the same thing. Anticipating is acting on what you have seen. Guessing is acting on hope.

Here is the test, and it is not whether you were right. Ask yourself this. Would my position still be useful if I turned out to be wrong?

Picture it. An anticipating player leans toward the likely pass, while staying between the puck and the net. That is useful either way. A guessing player commits to a spot that is worthless unless the guess comes off. That is a guess, even when it works.

Jumping a passing lane means leaving your spot to step into the path of a pass and take it. Do it only when all of these are true.

You have a real cue. The passer's shoulders, a closed blade pointed at that lane, or their eyes have already been there.

Someone is behind you, covering the ice you leave.

The puck is in the neutral zone, the middle of the ice between the blue lines, or in their end. Here is why. Being wrong there costs a rush against, not a goal against.

You can get there with your feet, not by reaching with your stick. Reaching is the giveaway that you guessed. It leaves your body in the wrong place and your feet stopped.

And the game situation allows it. Tied in the second period, fine. Protecting a one-goal lead with two minutes left, no.

When you jump, you are there for the puck, not the player. Your stick goes on the puck, not a hit. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call. And if you can see their numbers, pull up.

Now when not to. Never jump a lane when you are the last player back. Never in your own end, especially the middle of it, where a miss gives them a shot from point-blank range. Not when you made up your mind before the passer even had the puck. That is a plan looking for a reason, not a read. And not again after you have already missed once this shift. Chasing one gamble with another is how one mistake becomes a goal.

Here is why the bar is so high. The costs are not equal. A jump that works usually makes a scoring chance. One that fails in your own end usually gives one up. So you need to be well over fifty-fifty sure, and more than that in your own end.

Sixteenth idea. Get your head up off the puck.

None of this works if you have to look at the puck to control it. Here is why. A player who has to watch their own stick can only look around when they don't have the puck. That takes away exactly the moments when looking is worth most. So head-up puck control is the way in to everything in this episode. It is why keep your head up is the most repeated instruction in hockey.

Keep the puck where you can see it out of the corner of your eye. Be aware of it the way you are aware of your own hands.

Keep your hands away from your body, so the puck is out in front of you. Tucked in at your feet, you can't see it without looking down.

Hold the stick with a soft grip. Tense hands can't feel the puck. So you look down to check on it.

Train it with a drill that fails if you look down, not with willpower. Stickhandle while a partner holds up fingers or coloured cards for you to call out. If you look down, you fail the drill. Ten to fifteen minutes a day off the ice, with a ball or a puck, moves this along faster than almost any other off-ice work.

Seventeenth idea. How experience turns into instinct, and how to speed it up.

Experienced players do not have faster eyes. They have fewer things to think about.

Here is what that means. A new player sees ten people in ten places, and has to work out what each one means. An experienced player sees one picture they recognise, with an answer already attached. The puck is about to go back up to the blue line, and nobody is marking the net front. That is why instinct feels instant. Nothing is worked out. Something is recognised.

It takes repetitions. But you can get far more out of the ones you already have. Here is how.

Name patterns out loud after they happen. On the bench, say it. That was an overload, three of them on the puck side, and their player on the far side of the blue line was wide open. If you don't name it, most of that experience just evaporates.

Predict out loud, then check. Watching a game, say what happens next. They will reverse it. They are going to walk the line and shoot for a tip. A wrong prediction you notice is worth more than ten right ones you never made. And you can do this from the stands.

Work on one pattern a week. This week, I only watch what their far-side defenceman does on the breakout. Trying to notice everything means noticing nothing.

Get more decisions per minute. A three-on-three game in one third of the ice gives you many more reads than a full-ice drill. Decisions build recognition, not ice time.

Now the training itself. On the ice, use drills you fail if you don't look. A shoulder-check drill, where the coach changes something behind you before the pass. Number or colour calls while you handle the puck. Small games with rules, like calling your target's name before you get the pass. And talk in every drill. A call is a look you didn't have to take yourself.

With video, watch your own shifts and really count your head turns. That turns I should look more into a number you can move. Pause a second before you get a pass, and name your three options. Then play it, and see what you did. The gap is what you are missing. And count the head turns of a teammate whose vision you rate.

At a live game, watch one player for a whole shift, away from the puck. Live is best, because the TV camera follows the puck and cuts everyone else out. Watch a defenceman without the puck too. Most of the interesting reading happens where the camera isn't pointing.

One caution. Off-ice reaction tools, like light trainers, train your eyes, not your hockey. Use them as an extra. Never use them instead of playing, and being watched by someone who knows what to look for.

Let me pull it all together.

Take at least one look while the puck travels to you, and have your eyes back on it when it lands. Decide while it travels, with a first option and a bail-out, and let the last look overrule you. The puck is your anchor. Then pressure, outlets, danger and space. When you are defending, look for bodies, and keep asking who scores if the puck goes to the net right now.

Make every look a real head turn, both shoulders, without losing a stride. Use the far side, the moment after your pass, and every whistle.

On the boards, the same every time. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the checker, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly.

Read the carrier's chest and hips, the passer's shoulder and blade, and the shooter's hands and weight. Read the forecheck's shape. Play where the puck will be next. Arrive at the net moving but under control, head up, and never into the goalie. Anticipate, do not guess. And get your head up off the puck, because everything else depends on it.

Much of this depends on how your team plays. So here are three questions to ask your coach. Do we defend our end with a zone collapse, man-to-man, or a hybrid? When do you want me jumping passing lanes, and when not? And who on the bench wants to hear what their forecheck is, after my first shift?

Go and ask. And next time a pass is coming to you, turn your head before it gets there.
