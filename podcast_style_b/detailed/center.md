This episode is about playing centre. It takes its time. We will build the job up one piece at a time, and for each piece I will tell you what to do and also why it works. If you know why something works, you can still get it right when the play looks a little different from the picture in your head.

Before any instructions, a few words you will hear all the way through. I will explain them now, so they don't get in the way later.

The lanes. Picture the rink split lengthways into three strips. The strips down each side, next to the boards, are your wingers' lanes. The middle strip is yours, in all three zones.

High and low. In whichever end you are in, high means toward the blue line, and low means toward the goal line, the line the net sits on.

The hash marks. These are the short lines on the outside of each faceoff circle, roughly level with the faceoff dot. Below them means between them and the goal line. You will be told to swing low more than anyone, and that means getting below the hash marks, not just below the puck.

Strong side and weak side. The strong side is the side of the ice the puck is on. The weak side is the other side. Yours switches every time the puck does.

The slot. This is the ice in front of the net, between the two faceoff circles, from the tops of the circles down to the net. Its top part, between the faceoff dots and the tops of the circles, is called the high slot.

The point. This is the area just inside the blue line where defencemen usually stand.

The half-wall. This is the stretch of boards roughly level with the faceoff dot, between the corner and the point. It is your wingers' ice, not yours.

The crease. This is the goalie's area, the blue paint in front of the net. Treat the red line around its edge as part of it.

The checker. This is the player coming at you on the boards, whether they are after the puck or just coming at you.

That is the vocabulary. Now the job.

A winger owns a lane. A defenceman owns the back of the play. You own the space between them. You play the middle of the ice in all three zones, and your job moves with the puck. That makes you the link between the other four skaters.

It also means a centre's job changes from team to team more than anyone else's. So you will often hear three things. The common way, the realistic alternative, and the question to ask your coach.

I will start with four big ideas, because they matter most. If you only listen to the first part of this episode, take those four with you. After that we will go back through the rink, one end at a time, and add the detail.

First idea. In your own end, get below the puck.

When your defenceman picks up the puck in your end, they need someone underneath them to pass to. That is normally you. Get inside the faceoff dots and below the hash marks. In practice, that is the inside half of the faceoff circle on the puck side. Keep moving, stay open, and keep talking.

Here is why it matters. Too high, and your defenceman has nobody safe to pass to. They get pressured straight away, and the whole shift starts in your own end. Too wide, out on the boards, and one opponent can cover you and your winger at once. Then your defenceman loses the middle option altogether.

This low job belongs to the swing breakout. It is the one the coaching guides describe, but it is a coaching choice, and nobody has counted what teams actually run. In a centre fly, you leave early up the middle, and the far-side winger drops in to do the low job instead. In a flat breakout, both wingers hold higher and the team gets out with longer passes.

The commonest breakdown is a centre flying while the wingers hold high. Then nobody is under the puck at all. So ask your coach which breakout you play. And whichever it is, if nobody is underneath the puck, be the one who is.

Second idea. In your own end, never leave the slot empty.

The slot is where most goals are scored from. Of the three forwards, you usually have the most defending to do, and you spend it on that ice.

Your job moves with the puck. When the puck is in the corner or behind your net, go in and help your defenceman win it. Two of you on the puck usually wins it. Go for the puck, not the player. Here is why it matters. Stay up in the slot when the puck is in the corner, and your defenceman is alone against the carrier and will probably lose. But go to the corner and leave the slot empty, and you have swapped a battle you might win for a chance nobody can stop.

So never leave both empty at once. When you go to the corner, call for the weak-side winger to drop into the high slot behind you. Call it early. Anyone with a clear view can make that call, so speak up.

That rotation belongs to a system called the low zone collapse, where each player covers an area. It is a coaching choice. Under man-on-man, each player follows one opponent, and nobody rotates in behind you. We will come back to that.

Third idea. Own the middle of the ice, going forward and coming back.

Going forward, the middle is the best place on the ice to decide from. From there you can see both wingers and both defencemen. So carry through the middle and give the puck to the best option. Don't carry it for the sake of carrying it. If a teammate is better placed, move it.

Do it with your head up. You can't brace for a hit you can't see. So the view you carry the puck for is the same thing that keeps you safe.

Coming back, when your team loses the puck, the most dangerous attacker is usually not the carrier. It is the forward arriving late and unmarked behind the carrier, heading for the slot. Get back on that forward's inside, between them and your net. Don't chase behind their shoulder. If you are trailing them, you can't defend anything. Take the ice, not the body. Your stick goes in the passing lane, not on them.

Who takes the middle lane on the way back is a coaching choice. This episode assumes it is the centre. Some teams give it to whoever gets back first. Others go man-on-man, starting from the puck. So ask. And if you can't tell who has called it, take it.

Sixth idea. Under pressure, put the puck to the outside.

The middle of your own end is the worst place on the ice to lose the puck. A turnover there is a shot from close in. That is coaching agreement, reasoned from how dangerous shots there are, not something anyone has counted.

So if you have the puck there with players closing in, don't try to carry it through them. Get to the boards or behind your net first, then move it. Give it to a winger on the boards, or rim it. Getting the puck out of danger beats making a great play.

A rim is a hard pass along the boards that follows the curve of the rink, usually from one corner, round behind the net, to the far corner. A reverse sends the puck back the way it came, against the flow, usually to a defenceman arriving behind you.

They are opposite plays, and the forechecker tells you which. A forechecker is an opponent hunting the puck in your end. A rim beats a forechecker who is sealing you against the boards, because it goes past them. A reverse beats a forechecker who has come in too hard, because it goes into the space they just left.

Make that choice while the puck is still coming to you. While your defenceman is fetching it, look over your shoulder to see which side the forecheck is sealing. Then you can move it the instant it arrives, instead of finding out with a forechecker already on you.

Now the part that keeps you safe. Both plays put a forechecker behind you, by design, almost every shift.

Two things override everything I have just said, at any speed.

Never turn your back to the checker. And never duck.

Both protect your head and neck. Turn your back, and you can't see the hit coming or brace for it. Duck, and your head meets the boards first. That is true even going slowly, so a slow checker doesn't make it safe.

So arrive with your skates already parallel to the boards. Forearm and hip to the boards. Never the point of your shoulder, and not the whole shoulder if you can help it. Your shoulder faces the checker. It is the boards it should not meet. Head up, chin off your chest.

This holds even where body checking isn't allowed. The checker may not hit you there, but you can still end up in the boards while you both chase the puck. So take this position every shift.

Seventh idea. Taking the first pass.

Your team is winning the puck, and you have swung low. Now be the first and most reliable pass for your defencemen. Move toward where the puck is going, so you are a moving target.

When the pass comes, look to get out at once. If the middle is clearly open, carry it up the middle. If it isn't, give it to a winger on the boards. Your movement down low is what makes the breakout work. Stand still, and your defenceman's option disappears.

If your team plays a centre fly, the picture changes. Here is why a team plays it. You leave the zone early up the middle to stretch the ice, and the far-side winger takes the low pass instead. The main job, once more. Sprint back through the middle, pick up the late forward, and get on their inside. Now the detail.

Skate first, then look. Don't decide in the middle of a rush to chase the carrier. Your defencemen have the carrier. If you swing wide to help them, you leave the exact ice they are protecting.

Some coaches do want the first forward back, usually you, to pressure the carrier while the defence holds the middle. That is a system, not something to decide yourself, so ask which yours plays. How close can you get to the late forward? Take the ice, not the body. Get to the spot ahead of them with your skating. Don't step across their path, don't hold them, and don't hit them. Keep your stick in the passing lane and your free hand off them. That works whatever your league's rules turn out to be.

If you are the last forward back and your defence is outnumbered, take away the pass. Don't go for a hit.

And once you are set, become the centre again. Cover the high slot and support the corner.

Ninth idea. Whether you may check at all, and the hit you never make.

Before you plan any hit, find out whether body checking is allowed in your game. It usually isn't in younger age groups, in girls' and women's hockey, and in non-checking adult leagues. And it is the game that counts, not just your age group. A local league can ban it, and some leagues ban it whenever a non-checking team plays a checking team. Never count on a hit from behind costing only two minutes. In most leagues it gets you thrown out of the game. If you drive them into the boards or the net when they can't protect themselves, it can be a match penalty too.

Tenth idea. The neutral zone, with the puck.

The neutral zone is the middle of the rink, between the two blue lines. Here you are the link between your defence and your attack.

With space, carry it with pace through the middle. Read the play. If a winger is open, pass and let them go into the zone at full speed. If you have space, carry it through yourself. Keep the play moving.

Under pressure in the middle of the neutral zone, it is your own end all over again. Move the puck to a winger, or chip it off the boards. Don't carry it through heavy traffic.

Here is why. That is where odd-man rushes get made. An odd-man rush is an attack with more of them coming than you have back, like a two-on-one. Coaches agree on that, though nobody has counted it. A quick, simple decision is worth more than skill here.

Eleventh idea. Without the puck through the middle, you set the pace.

Stay in the middle, level with the puck or a little behind it. Then you are a moving target for a pass. Stay connected to both wingers.

When your defence regroups, which means passing it back to start again, curl back with them. Swing low through the middle so you meet the pass already skating forward. Don't stand at the red line waiting. A pass taken standing still gives the other team time to set up in front of you. Teams differ on whether the centre or a winger curls back, so ask.

Now their blue line. Offside means both of your skates have completely crossed their blue line before the puck has. So one skate still on the line keeps you onside. Keep that trailing blade down on the ice, because in some leagues a skate held in the air counts as offside.

Coming into their zone, it is tempting to slow down so you don't go offside. Don't. Time your stride so the puck crosses first, and arrive at full speed.

Here is why. If you coast, both wingers arrive slowly with you. A line that comes in slowly is the one a defence stops at the blue line. That costs your team more than an offside whistle would.

If you do go in too early, there is often a second chance, called a delayed offside. Play carries on, and your team fixes it by tagging up. That means all five of you clear the zone and touch the blue line before anyone goes back in. So count your linemates out. Not every level plays delayed offside, so ask how it works in your games.

Do all of this with your head up. Arriving at speed, and checking on your linemates, both tempt you to drop your eyes. And you can't brace for a hit you can't see.

Twelfth idea. When they have the puck in the neutral zone.

You are usually the closest forward, so you start the pressure. Drive at the carrier, or cut off the middle and force them wide. Step up when the chance is clear. Stepping up means getting your stick on the puck and taking the ice, not a hit. Arrive under control, and add nothing when you get there. Otherwise, take the middle away and force the play to the boards.

Here is why. You are your line's first wave of defence. If you don't start the pressure, they get a free run at your defencemen. And with the middle blocked, they are forced wide, where your wingers can help.

How hard you press depends on your team's system, and there are two very different kinds.

The first is containment. Shapes you may hear named are the one-two-two, the one-two-two trap and the one-three-one. You are the lone forward taking the middle away and steering the carrier to a side. The wingers hold their lanes. Getting the turnover is a bonus, not the aim.

The second is an aggressive press. Two forwards hunt the puck, and the wingers do leave their lanes to pressure. Why would a team do that? Because the point of a press is to take the puck back, not to wait for a mistake.

Most teams use more than one, depending on the score and the shift. So ask which you play, and what the call is for switching.

Thirteenth idea. The forecheck.

Forechecking means pressuring the other team in their own end, to win the puck back.

The forecheck has three roles, called F one, F two and F three. The first forward in is F one, the second is F two, and the highest is F three. They are roles taken in the order you arrive, not people. In the common two-one-two, two forwards go in on the puck, one stays in the middle, and both defencemen hold the blue line. The first one in is usually you.

If a winger gets there first, they are F one, and you take the support role straight away. Don't swap back.

As F one, drive at the carrier, so they have to decide quickly. Come in on an angle that pushes them toward one set of boards. Here is why. Then your F two winger knows where the puck is going, and can get there to seal it. Your job is to steer them, not to hit them.

Steering means skating a line that leaves the carrier only one way to go. Pick your line early, hold it, and add nothing when you arrive. That works in every league. No bump, no push, no shove, and no lean. Don't veer into them at the last moment. Never your stick across them, and never into their back. If you can see their numbers, pull up and play the puck. Never take anyone's skates off the ice. And even going slowly, a crash into the boards can hurt.

Where checking isn't allowed, even a push on its own is a penalty, and a violent one can get you thrown out of the game. So find out whether your game allows checking before you ever hit on a forecheck. Steering still comes first.

And arrive under control. Building up speed into contact, even with two hard strides, can be charging. So take the angle with your feet, not with a run-up.

In the usual setup, F three stays higher, covering the pass out and anyone slipping up the middle. Some teams play it differently. In the stacked version, F three shades to the puck side, and all three forwards overload one corner. They give up the far-side pass out on purpose. And a one-two-two sends only one forward in. Ask which your team runs.

Fourteenth idea. The dump-in, and their goalie.

A dump-in means putting the puck deep into their end to chase it. Aim it at the corners, away from their goalie. Here is why. Then a defenceman has to fetch it, with you arriving on them.

In some leagues their goalie may only play the puck behind the goal line inside a marked shape behind the net, called the trapezoid. That is exactly why dump and chase works there. In other leagues there is no trapezoid, and their goalie can come out to the corner. Then a dump-in is worth much less. So look behind the net, and ask your league.

Even then, keep coming. In some leagues a goalie who has left the crease may not fall on the puck, so they have to move it.

And a goalie who has come out is never someone to hit. Unnecessary contact with a goalie out of the crease is a penalty in every league. Whether their cover of the puck was legal is the official's call, not yours. So never play on into a goalie because you think it wasn't.

Let me pull the forecheck together. Whoever arrives first is F one. Take the angle and steer, with nothing added, and arrive under control. Dump it to the corners, away from their goalie, and never into a goalie who has come out.

Fifteenth idea. The slot, and the pass across the middle.

You already know to shoot from the slot when you have space. Before you shoot, look for the one pass that is worth more. It is the pass across the middle.

Picture an imaginary line down the middle of the ice, from one net to the other. It is called the royal road. A pass that crosses it just before a shot makes the goalie push hard sideways, so the puck arrives before they are set. A pass kept on your own side barely moves them.

As a centre, you are at both ends of that pass more than anyone. You are the far-side target when a winger has the puck on the boards. And you are the passer when you come out from behind the net, because a pass from there up to the slot crosses the road almost every time. So the pass to refuse is the extra one along your own side.

If defenders close in, find a winger on the half-wall or in the circle, or a defenceman at the point. Keep moving, and keep the puck moving.

When your defenceman pinches, stepping down the boards to keep the puck in, somebody has to fill the point they left. In many systems that is you. Slide up to the blue line on their side and play defence until they are back. Here is why. Stay in the slot instead, and you leave an odd-man rush going the other way. Some teams give that job to a winger, so ask who covers the pinch.

Sixteenth idea. Under pressure down low, and the cycle.

Under pressure in their end, protect the puck with your body. Look for a quick pass to a winger on the half-wall, or to the defenceman at the point. Or go behind their net and use it as a pivot. Come out the other side, or find a teammate arriving late. Here is why behind the net works. From there you can see the whole ice, and a turnover there is not a shot against you.

Down here, the same position. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the checker, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly. If you end up facing the boards anyway, stick and gloves up and spread on the glass, head off it. Behind the net it matters twice over, because the goal post is right there.

Cycling is how you keep the puck low in their end until the slot opens. Your line rotates around the corner, the half-wall and behind the net, passing short and protecting the puck. The usual version is to leave the puck on the boards behind you for a teammate coming in low, then rotate up into the space they just left.

Here is why it works. The defenders are skating without the puck. They can't change, and as they tire they shrink in toward it. That opens the slot you want to arrive in.

Four reads make it work.

First, rim or reverse, and the checker tells you which. If the checker is still in front of you, rim it on past them. If they have gone past you, reverse it into the space they left.

Second, bump it off the boards rather than onto a stick. Fire the puck at a spot on the boards so it bounces to your teammate. A stick in the passing lane can't block that. But call it, because a bump nobody expects is a turnover.

Third, cut back when you run out of boards. Skate back up the way you came. A defender whose weight is going toward the corner can't turn as fast as you can. Make the turn while they're still too far away to reach you. Once they can reach you, it's too late. Go side-on to the boards instead. Never turn so you're facing the wall with them behind you.

Fourth, the scissor. You and a teammate skate at each other and cross, with the support player going on the outside. Drop it to them or keep it. Defenders in the middle of swapping who they cover are briefly covering nobody.

And the cycle has to end in something. A shot from in front, a pass across the middle, or a drive to the net. Three tidy laps and no shot is a win for the defence.

Seventeenth idea. The front of their net, in more detail.

The net front is three separate skills, not one place to stand. Screening, tips and rebounds.

Screening means standing where the goalie can't see the shot. Stand between the goalie's eyes and the puck, not between the goalie and the net. Those are two different lines. When your defenceman slides along the blue line, that line moves, so slide with them. Chest up and square to the shooter, hips low and knees bent. Stand inside the faceoff dots, about six to eight feet out from the goal line. The crease reaches about six, so live at the far end of that.

Be still at the moment the shot is taken. If you keep shuffling, the goalie gets flashes of the puck through the gaps. Between shots, keep moving.

Track the puck, not the shot. Your eyes move, but your chest doesn't. Turning your body to follow the shot un-screens the goalie, and takes you out of the tip and the rebound.

For tips, keep your blade on the ice. Most pucks you can tip come in low, and a stick off the ice at the net front is doing nothing. You are only changing the puck's direction a little, not shooting it. Angle the blade and give with the puck a touch. A big swing usually misses. Hold your blade out where you want the puck, and shout for it.

For rebounds, decide to go before the shot, not after. Time it by your job. If the net front is your job, be planted there before the shot. If you are driving in from further out, arrive as the shot is released, not well before it. Go early, and a defender has time to tie you up and turn you away from the puck. That split is coaching know-how, not measured.

When the rebound comes out, shoot it straight back. Don't settle it and don't look for a pass. The window is a second or two, while the goalie is still down. If you get tied up, go to the far post. Nobody is covering it.

Now keeping yourself safe there. You are standing still with your eyes on the puck. So a defender pushing you sideways is someone you feel, not someone you see. And that path has a steel post at one edge and the goalie at the other.

So know where the near post is before you plant. Keep your head up and your chin off your chest, and never duck. If you meet a post, meet it with an arm or a leg, never your head first. Even going slowly, a post met head first can hurt your neck. A box-out, where a defender holds you away from the net with their body, walks you along the same path, whatever your league allows.

When a defender pushes you, keep your spot, but don't push back or lean on them, and never put your stick across someone. A shove with your stick held in both hands is a cross-check, whatever the blade is doing. And if the goalie moves into you, give ground, even when it costs you the screen.

Eighteenth idea. The crease, and the goalie.

Work just outside the blue paint, and off the red line around it too. In most leagues that line counts as part of the crease. Keep your stick out of the paint as well. In some leagues your stick in the crease, with their goalie in it, takes the goal off, whoever scored.

Never crowd the goalie so tightly they can't move. Take the goalie's eyes, never the goalie's movement.

When their goalie comes out to play a puck, leave them their path back. Blocking it can be a penalty with no contact at all, and it can take the goal off. Screen them once they are home.

If a defender shoves you into the crease or into the goalie, get out at once, around the goalie, not through. Being pushed in only helps you if you make a real effort to avoid the goalie. Every league penalises a player who doesn't try. Inside the blue paint, assume any contact with the goalie costs the goal.

So never go into the goalie, in or out of the crease, slow or fast. Here is why. A goalie who is down or turned can't protect themselves. Going into one can hurt them. It is a penalty whether or not the puck goes in, and it can cost your team the goal. Arrive into a goalie with speed, which is charging, and you can be thrown out of the game in every league.

Let me pull the rest of their end together. In the slot, shoot, or pass across the middle. Cover the point when your defenceman pinches. Cycle to open the slot, and make it end in a shot. At the net, screen from outside the paint and off the red line, be still at the release, keep your blade on the ice, and decide on the rebound early. Head up, never duck, and never into the goalie.

Nineteenth idea. Faceoffs.

You are your team's main faceoff taker. Here is something worth knowing first. Most draws barely matter. In top-level hockey, a team's faceoff record explains only a small part of whether it wins.

The ones that do matter are in your own end, on special teams, in the last minute, and after an icing. Coaches put that at ten or fifteen a game. Prepare for those, and agree with your linemates beforehand where you are trying to win each one.

In your own end, win it back to a defenceman, ideally the one on the boards side, for a quick rim out. Here is why it matters there. A clean loss puts a set team running a prepared play into your slot, while your five are standing still and facing the wrong way.

In their end, win it to a defenceman for a one-timer, or back to a winger in the circle for a quick chance. In the neutral zone, win it clean if you can. If not, a tie-up your wingers can chase down is fine.

If you can't win it clean, lift or press their blade with yours, over or under it, for an instant. Then play the puck so your wingers can get to it. Stick on stick only. Never on their hands, never a chop, never hook it or hold it down, and never grab it. And keep your hand off the puck altogether until a third player has touched it.

If you lose a draw in your own end, stay with their centre. Tie up their stick if you can, stick on stick, for an instant. Then go with them toward the slot, rather than chasing the puck. Take the ice, not the body. That is the usual job, but some teams give the centre a different player, so ask.

Study the other centre. Most favour one hand or one way of taking the draw. And vary your own.

Now staying in the dot, which is uniquely your problem. Get low by bending your knees and dropping your hips, not by pushing your head out over the dot. Two centres getting low the wrong way end up helmet to helmet. That can get you thrown off the draw and given a penalty. If the linesperson tells you to move back, move back the first time.

Your wingers can get you thrown out of the draw without realising it. If a linemate creeps into the circle or over the hash marks before the drop, you are the one removed, not them. In some leagues a winger's stick over the line counts too. So warn them before every draw that matters. Be set when the linesperson is ready, and never time your arrival to beat the clock.

And agree two back-up takers, not one. In some leagues the player who caused the violation can't take the draw. So if your only back-up is the winger who just crept in, you have no plan.

Twentieth idea. A puck in the air near your own net.

If you catch a puck out of the air, knock it straight down where you caught it, and play it with your stick or your skate. Catching it is fine, except at your own faceoff, where your hand stays off the puck until a third player has touched it. Holding it, carrying it, or dropping it somewhere better can be a penalty.

In your own crease, it is worse. The crease goes up into the air, as high as the top of the net. So a puck caught above your paint counts as in the crease. Close your hand on it there and it is usually a penalty shot, a free shot alone against your goalie. And with your net empty, it can be a goal given to them. So never cover a puck in your own crease with your hand or your body.

Twenty-first idea. The power play, when your team has an extra player.

The setup most often described as the modern default is the one-three-one. There you are usually the bumper. That is the middle of the three players across the slot, between the player at the point and the player in front of the net. Be the passing target for both half-walls, shoot quickly, screen the goalie and tip point shots.

Here is why the bumper is such a good job. A penalty kill playing a box has nobody in the middle. So the bumper is the hardest player for them to cover, and that is exactly why a kill often switches to a diamond against you.

In an umbrella or an overload you will usually be low, or on the boards on the puck side. There is no bumper in a true umbrella, so don't go looking for the spot. Ask which your team runs.

Getting into their zone, you often drop back for the breakout pass and carry it through the neutral zone. Don't skate straight into their defence, and don't dump it into traffic. Both just hand them the puck. Come in with control, carrying it into space or passing it in across the line. Passing in is not better than carrying in. Pick whichever lane is open.

Twenty-second idea. The penalty kill, when your team is a player short.

A kill is four skaters, usually two forwards and two defencemen. Know your shape before the puck drops, and hold it. A box and a diamond ask very different things of you.

In a box, the standard shape, you are one of the two high players, each covering one point. There is no single top spot, so don't drift into the middle looking for one.

In a diamond, used against a one-three-one, there is a single top spot, and it is usually you. Pressure the point, while the middle two take the half-walls and someone accounts for the bumper.

In a wedge plus one, you are often the plus one, the player working outside the triangle. That job is pressure, not chasing. Get at whoever has the puck. When the puck moves, you stop and drop into the triangle, and the nearest player steps out as the new plus one. Here is why. Following the puck around the outside is how that system gets beaten. The route you take out to the puck is your coach's choice, so ask.

Whatever the shape, don't get pulled out of position chasing the puck around the outside. Clear it whenever you get it. In most leagues a team a player short may ice it. But not at every age, and not once the numbers on the ice are even again. So check before you build your kill on it.

When their defenceman winds up from the point, you may have to get in the shooting lane. How much your team blocks shots at all is a coaching choice. So ask your coach, and ask your goalie what they want.

If you block, here is the whole of it, and it goes together. Get into the lane before the shot, not sliding in as it comes. Block from close and on your feet, square to the shooter. Head out of the lane. Hands behind your body, with the backs of your gloves out. Stick flat on the ice beside you, never out in front, because a puck off a blade held out in front tends to ride up toward your face. That last part is coaching know-how, not measured. If you do go down, go down toward the shooter, not sideways, with your hands behind you or tucked in behind your shin pads.

Two places you never block from. Never in front of your own goalie's eyes, where all you do is deflect a puck they never saw. And never on a two-on-one, when you are the only defender back. Here is why. Your job there is the pass, and your goalie already has the shooter.

If a blocked shot ends up under you, that's fine. Reach for it with your hand to hold it there, and it is a penalty.

Twenty-third idea. Icing, and line changes.

Icing is shooting the puck from your own half across their goal line without anyone touching it. Play stops, and the faceoff comes back to your end.

The icing a centre gives away is the lazy dump from just behind the centre red line. The fix is cheap. Carry one more stride, so the puck is past the red line on your stick before you let it go. In most leagues the dump is then fine however far it goes. A few leagues split the ice at the blue lines instead, so ask where your league's line is. Or make the dump stop short of their goal line. That works everywhere.

Here is why icing is aimed at you. After your own icing, the faceoff is in your end, and in some leagues your line can't change for it. So a lazy dump means a tired centre taking a draw in their own end that they badly need to win.

If your legs are gone, put the puck off the glass and out of your zone, not down the ice. Off the glass, never over it. From your own zone, a puck that flies straight out over the glass is a penalty in some leagues even when you didn't mean it. Put it over on purpose and it is a penalty everywhere.

When the other team ices it, it can be one of your best draws of the night. In some leagues they can't change. Then you are taking a draw in their end against five tired players. Have a set play ready.

Now line changes. Some games, especially for younger players, change lines in a set way, and then your coach and the officials tell you how. In games where your line chooses when to change, and it changes as a group, you go off first. Call it, go, and the wingers follow you off.

Here is why. Your route covers more of the ice than either winger's, and the centre is reckoned to tire first. That is coaching know-how, not a measured finding. So the line changes on your legs. Change when your legs go, not when the play happens to stop.

Don't change when there is a faceoff in their end that you are needed to take. And after your own icing, where your line can't change, don't start drifting toward the bench.

Let me pull it all together.

In your own end, get below the puck, inside the dots and below the hash marks, unless your team plays a centre fly. Never leave the slot empty. Go to the corner or hold the high slot, and call the weak-side winger in when you go. Under pressure, get the puck to the outside. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the checker, never duck.

Own the middle. Head up going forward. On the backcheck, get inside the late forward, take the ice, not the body, and never finish a check on a player's back.

On the forecheck, take the angle and steer, and arrive under control. In their slot, shoot, or pass across the middle. At their net, screen from outside the paint and off the red line, head up, and go around the goalie, never into them.

At the dot, get low with your knees, warn your wingers, and agree two back-up takers. On the kill, know your shape and hold it. And change on your legs.

Much of this depends on what your team plays. So here are three questions for your coach. Do we play a swing breakout or a centre fly? In our own end, is it a low zone collapse, man-on-man, or a hybrid? And on the backcheck, who takes the middle lane? And one for your league. Does my division allow body checking?

Go and ask. Their answers tell you how your team wants you to play, and now you know why each answer makes sense.
