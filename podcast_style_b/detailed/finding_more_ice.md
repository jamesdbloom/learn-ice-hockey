===== SCRIPT START =====

This episode is about finding more ice. Most players get far less time on the ice than they need to get better. If your team practises once a week, that is not much. The good news is that most of the extra ice out there is found by asking the right people, not by searching online. So this episode is about who to ask, what to ask them, and why.

Before anything else, a few words you will hear all the way through.

Public skating. These are the sessions anyone can pay for and skate at, with no hockey game.

Stick-and-puck. This is open ice for practising on your own with a stick and a puck. There is no game.

Drop-in. This is a pick-up game you pay for on the night.

Those names are loose, and rinks use them in different ways. So read the rink's own description of a session, because that tells you what it really is.

Learn-to-play. This is a block of beginner sessions that teaches the basics of playing hockey.

Rec hockey. Rec is short for recreational. It means hockey played for fun, rather than in the top leagues.

Body checking. This means using your body, on purpose, to knock an opponent off the puck. Some leagues allow it and some don't.

That is the vocabulary. Now the job.

I will start with four big ideas, because they matter most. If you only hear the first part of this episode, take those four with you. After that, we go through each place to find ice, closest first.

First idea. Ask your own club for more ice before you look anywhere else.

Here is why. Your club is the easiest source of extra ice you have, and it is the one players forget to ask. A common mistake is waiting for your team to give you more ice. Ask your club, and add a session yourself.

So start close and work outwards. Your club first. Then your rink. Then the national governing body, the organisation that runs hockey in your country. It can tell you which clubs exist. It can't tell you when they skate. For that, you go back to the rink.

Second idea. Add one more session a week if you can, and pick the right kind of ice for the job.

Match the ice to what you want to work on.

Public skating is for your skating. It is the cheapest ice you can buy, but it isn't for hockey.

Stick-and-puck is for working with the puck. For most players it is the most useful extra ice there is.

A drop-in game, or a place on a second team, is for reading the game. That means learning what to do in a real game, with real players around you.

And a learn-to-play block is for anyone still learning to stop.

Third idea. Ring the rink, and ask your teammates.

Here is why. Lots of sessions are never listed anywhere central. If you only search online, you will miss them. The rink usually knows every session and every club that uses its ice. And your teammates know about the sessions that players run themselves. Between the rink and your teammates, they know almost every session there is.

Fourth idea. Before you join anything, ask four things. What level is it? What gear do I need? What does it cost? And is there contact?

Here is why. Turning up to the wrong session is an easy mistake. And turning up to a session with contact when you expected none is how people get hurt.

For a league, there is one more question, and it matters most. Does this league allow body checking? Ask your coach or your league secretary. The league secretary is the person who runs the league's admin. Don't ask the rink desk. Whether checking is allowed is set by your age group, your league and your country, and the desk can't answer that. And being a beginner does not put you in a non-checking league. Some adult leagues allow checking. So ask before your first game.

Let me pull those four ideas together.

One. Ask your own club first. Two. Add one session a week, and match the ice to the job. Three. Ring the rink and ask your teammates, because most extra ice is found by asking. Four. Before you join anything, ask the level, the gear, the cost and whether there is contact. And for a league, ask your coach or league secretary whether it allows checking.

Now we will go through each place to find ice, starting with your own club.

Fifth idea. The four questions to ask your club.

Ask your coach, or your club secretary. Here are the four questions.

The first question. Can I join another team's practice? A second team, or a development session, often has room for one more player. If it is a child moving up an age group to get the extra ice, ask whether that age group allows body checking. Ice Hockey UK's junior leagues allow checking from the under-fourteen age group upwards. So ask before the child moves up.

The second question. Is there a train-only place? In England, England Ice Hockey describes a train-only option. It is for players who want to train with a club without the pressure of having to play in matches. It is aimed at older children who start late.

The third question. Does the club book any extra ice? Some clubs run a skills hour, or book their own stick-and-puck.

The fourth question. Is there a spare list? That is a list of players a team rings when it is short on the night. Get on it, and a team can ring you when they are short.

In Britain, there is one thing to sort out first. Get registered to that club, and to that team, before the day of the game. It can't be done on the night. And if a team plays someone who isn't registered, it can lose the result. So register early, and then you are ready when the call comes.

Sixth idea. If you might play goal, say so.

Goalies are scarce at every level of amateur hockey. So a player who will go in goal gets offered more ice. Clubs often lend goal pads. But buy your own mask. A goalie mask protects your head the way a helmet does. You can't see damage inside a used one, and you can't know what it has been hit by.

Seventh idea. Public skating.

Public skating is the cheapest ice you can buy. Use it for your skating, not for hockey.

Rinks list their public sessions on their websites, usually under public skating, or what's on. Before you go, do two things.

First, check the posted rules on sticks and pucks. Assume they are not allowed unless the rules say they are. If in doubt, leave the stick at home.

Second, ask the desk which sessions are the quietest. A quiet session gives you room to work.

Here is why the quiet session matters. A public session is full of children and new skaters. So keep fast starts, hard stops and skating backwards to a clear patch of ice, and only if the posted rules allow them at all.

Eighth idea. Stick-and-puck and drop-in.

This is the most useful extra ice for most players. Stick-and-puck gives you room to work on your puck handling on your own. A drop-in gives you a real game to read. Find them through the rink and through your teammates.

Here is how to find one, in order.

First, look at the rink's timetable. Look under hockey, or under ice hire, not on the public skating page.

Second, if nothing is listed, ring the desk and ask.

Third, ask your teammates about sessions players run themselves. Some drop-ins are booked by a club, or by a group of players, and they fill up by word of mouth. You won't find those online.

Fourth, try other rinks within reach. A rink a little further away might run sessions where yours runs none.

And before your first drop-in, ask the level, the gear and whether there is contact. Turning up to the wrong drop-in is an easy mistake, and one phone call avoids it.

Let me pull that part together. Ask your club about another team's practice, a train-only place, extra ice and the spare list. In Britain, register with the club and team before the day of any game. If you might play goal, say so, and buy your own mask. Use public skating for skating, check the rules on sticks, and keep the hard work to a clear patch. And find stick-and-puck and drop-in on the rink's hockey timetable, by ringing the desk, and through your teammates.

Ninth idea. Joining a rec or adult team when you don't have one.

The answer is simple. Ask to be placed as an individual. And ask about checking in the same call.

To find clubs, ask the rink which clubs and leagues use its ice. Then ask these five questions.

The first question. What level is it? Judge yourself on your skating, not your shot. Skating is what sorts players into levels. And if you are unsure, take the lower level. That is experience, not a rule, but here is the reason for it. Moving up part way through a season is easy, and people welcome it. Moving down is awkward. So lean low.

The second question. Is it a checking league? Ask your coach or the league secretary, not the rink desk. Being a beginner does not put you in a non-checking league.

The third question. Do you place individual players? Some leagues do. Others only take whole teams. In Canada, the Adult Safe Hockey League has a sign-up form for individual players.

If you are still on a junior team in Britain, ask before you register with an adult team. Under Ice Hockey UK's junior rules, a player who registers for senior membership can't play junior hockey for the rest of that season. So check first, or you could lose the rest of your junior season.

The fourth question. Is it sanctioned? Sanctioned means the national governing body has approved it. This one matters more than it sounds. In Britain, hockey that isn't sanctioned isn't insured. If you get hurt, the national body's insurance doesn't cover you. And under England Ice Hockey's last published rules, a registered player who joins a league it doesn't recognise faces a suspension and fines. In Canada, Hockey Canada can't insure players in a league that isn't sanctioned. And a child registered in one loses all their Hockey Canada membership privileges for the rest of that season. So know which kind of hockey it is before you sign up.

The fifth question. What does it cost, and do you need a goalie? If you might play goal, this is the moment to say so.

Tenth idea. Learning to skate and learning to play, for adults and for children.

The order is simple. Learn to skate first, or alongside. Then learn to play. Then join a club team. And tell the club you are coming.

Here is why you tell the club. A learn-to-play programme and the club may not be connected at all. England Ice Hockey says learn-to-play programmes are usually run by the rink, or sometimes by the club. It also says the rink's programmes are not directly linked to junior clubs. So a player can finish a learn-to-play block at the rink, and no club knows they exist. Contact the club as well, so the block leads somewhere.

Skate first if you can. England Ice Hockey advises working up to level five skating before starting learn to play. Rinks run their own skating levels, so ask the rink what its level five means.

For a child, ask two more things. Are the coaches background-checked? In Britain, that is a DBS or a PVG check. And who is the club's safeguarding lead? That is the person at the club whose job is keeping children safe.

Eleventh idea. Camps and clinics.

Camps and clinics are extra coaching that you book on top of your team's practice. No camp finder turned up on the national websites. So ask your club, your rink and your coach.

Before you book, ask four things. Who coaches it? What level does it suit? How many players are there for each coach? And is there contact? Don't book a camp just because of its name. Those four answers tell you more.

In Britain, also ask whether the camp is sanctioned by Ice Hockey UK. Its insurance covers a camp only if the camp is sanctioned and you are registered. For a child, ask the same two safeguarding questions as before. Are the coaches background-checked, and who is the safeguarding lead?

One kind of session worth looking for is power skating. Power skating means coaching on skating technique alone. One power-skating session a season is worth it, if you can.

Let me pull that part together. With no team, ask to be placed as an individual, and ask the level, whether it checks, and whether it is sanctioned. Judge your level on your skating, and lean low. Still on a junior team in Britain? Ask before you register with an adult team. Learn to skate, then learn to play, then join a club, and tell the club you are coming. For a child, ask about background checks and the safeguarding lead. And for a camp in Britain, ask whether it is sanctioned, because you are only insured at a sanctioned camp, and only if you are registered.

Last part. Where to look, depending on where you live.

These lists tell you which clubs exist. They don't tell you when those clubs skate. For that, ring the rink. And if a page has moved, go to the organisation's home page and look for play, or clubs.

Across Britain. Ice Hockey UK now governs the sport in all four home nations, but its website has no club finder. To find a rink and its learn-to-skate classes, use British Ice Skating's rink finder.

England. England Ice Hockey's Play page explains how to get into hockey in England. Its club finder isn't live. For adult rec hockey, it links to its Rec section. That has a team list naming each team and its home rink. Some older pages still carry the EIHA name. They are out of date, so use the current England Ice Hockey site.

Scotland. Scottish Ice Hockey is now part of Ice Hockey UK. It lists junior clubs, recreational clubs and women's clubs. Its recreational page says some clubs run Hockey For All sessions for beginners.

Wales. Welsh clubs play in the English leagues. So use the England pages. The Rec team list includes the Cardiff and Deeside teams. No separate Welsh ice hockey body was found.

Northern Ireland. Scottish Ice Hockey's junior and recreational lists include Northern Ireland clubs. The Irish Ice Hockey Association describes itself as the governing body for ice hockey in Ireland. Its clubs page lists the Belfast clubs and their training times. Ask the club you join which body it registers with.

The USA. USA Hockey's Find Hockey Near You finds local youth programmes by zip code. Its Come Play Youth Hockey page links to Try Hockey For Free, to Learn to Skate USA, and to the NHL and NHLPA Learn to Play programme. That programme's locations are on each NHL team's own page. For adult hockey, ring the rink and ask which adult leagues and drop-ins use its ice.

Canada. Hockey Canada's Pathway to Minor Hockey page links to three things. Its association finder. The First Shift, which is a learn-to-play programme. And Esso Fun Days, a free taster for girls and women. Hockey Canada calls its finder a starting point, so check with the local association that your child can play there. For learning to skate, use Skate Canada's Find a Club, or your local parks and recreation department. For adults, look at Hockey Canada's Adult Recreational Hockey page, and the Adult Safe Hockey League's page for individual players.

Anywhere else. The IIHF has a page listing every national body it recognises. Find yours there. Then ring the rink.

Let me pull it all together.

Most of the extra ice you need is found by asking, not searching. Ask your own club first. Ask for another team's practice, a train-only place, extra ice, or a place on the spare list. In Britain, get registered with the club and team before the day of any game. Add one session a week if you can. Use public skating for your skating, and stick-and-puck or drop-in for your hockey. Ring the rink, and ask your teammates.

Before you join anything, ask the level, the gear, the cost and whether there is contact. For a league, ask your coach or league secretary whether it allows body checking. Being a beginner doesn't put you in a non-checking league. In Britain, ask whether it is sanctioned, because unsanctioned hockey isn't insured.

No team? Ask to be placed as an individual. Might you play goal? Say so, and buy your own mask. For a child, ask about background checks and the safeguarding lead. And learn to skate first or alongside, then learn to play, then join a club, and tell the club you are coming.

Here are three questions to take to your coach this week. Can I join another team's practice? Is there a spare list I can go on? And is our league, or the one I want to join, a checking league?

Then ring the rink, and ask what hockey it runs that you could join.
