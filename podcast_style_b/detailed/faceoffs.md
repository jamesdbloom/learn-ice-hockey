===== SCRIPT START =====

This episode is about faceoffs, and what each of the five of you actually does at one. For each part I will tell you what to do, and also why it works. If you know why something works, you can still do the right thing when a draw doesn't go the way you planned.

First, a few words you will hear all the way through.

A faceoff, or a draw. This is how play starts and restarts. An official drops the puck between the sticks of two players, usually the two centres.

The linesperson. This is the official who drops the puck.

The dot. This is the painted spot where the puck is dropped. The dots in the two ends each sit inside a big painted circle, called the faceoff circle.

The hash marks. These are short red lines on the outside edges of each circle in the two ends. The wingers line up at them, and they are a hard boundary. Before the puck drops, nobody but the two centres may cross them.

The slot. This is the ice straight out in front of the net, between the two circles. Its top part, from the dots up to the tops of the circles, is called the high slot.

The point. This is the area just inside the blue line, where defencemen usually stand. And the half-wall is the stretch of boards level with the faceoff dot, between the corner and the point.

Boards-side and inside. Left and right change depending on which end you are in, so this episode uses two words that don't. Boards-side means nearer the boards, the side you will usually send the puck out. Inside means nearer the middle of the ice. Learn those, and the same setup works at either circle.

That is the vocabulary. Now one thing before the ideas.

Most draws matter much less than people think, and a few matter a lot. Over eleven NHL seasons, how often a team won its faceoffs barely predicted how many points it took. Why so little? Because a won draw buys you a head start of a few seconds, not a goal. After about ten seconds, the shots even out.

So don't chase your faceoff percentage. Prepare the ten or fifteen draws a game that change something. That number is a coaching estimate, not a count.

Which ones? The draw in your own end. The draw when you are a player short. The draw protecting a late lead. And the draw after an icing. Icing is when a team shoots the puck from its own half across the other team's goal line. Play stops, and the draw comes back to that team's end.

Above all, the one in your own end. If you lose that one cleanly, the other team runs a rehearsed play into the most dangerous ice on the rink. And your five are standing still, facing the wrong way.

I will start with four big ideas, because they matter most. If you only listen to the first part of this episode, take those four with you. After that we will go round the rink, one zone at a time, and then through the special situations.

First idea. Winning the draw is a reaction, not a fight. Watch the linesperson's hand, not the other centre.

Here is why. If you react to the puck leaving the hand, you are training a reaction, and a reaction gets faster with practice. If you react to the other centre, you will always be a moment behind them. And they can fool you with a fake.

Now the stance. Get low. Bend your knees, drop your hips, and get your chest over the puck. Keep your weight on the balls of your feet. Put your bottom hand right down near the blade, because that is where your speed and power come from. Stand with your feet staggered, not square, and load your back foot, the one you push off.

Why low? You react faster from a loaded position, and you are much harder to push off the dot.

But get that depth from your knees and hips, not by pushing your head out over the dot. Two centres who both lean in end up with their heads side by side, almost touching. If your helmets touch before the drop, that can cost you the draw. The referee can add a penalty as well, and in some leagues that penalty is automatic. So keep your head back. And if the linesperson tells you both to move back, move back the first time.

There is no single right way to win a draw. Good centres own three or four. The backhand pull, where you turn and pull the puck back on your backhand. The forehand pull, the same on your forehand. The tie-up, which is the second idea. And the stick lift. Mix them.

Here is why. If you win three in a row the same way to the same defenceman, the other team will expect the fourth. They will rush that defenceman the moment the puck drops. So change the technique, not just where you send it. Pull one back, tie up the next, sweep the third.

Second idea. When the other centre keeps beating you clean, tie them up, and let your wingers win it.

A tie-up works like this. As the puck drops, lift their stick or press on it with yours, over or under the blade, for an instant. Then play the puck. Your aim is not to win the puck yourself. It is to make sure nobody wins it cleanly.

Why does that help? A straight contest against a better centre is one you will mostly lose. A tie-up turns it into a loose puck, a scramble, and your wingers are already leaning toward it. So in your own end, and when you are a player short, a tie-up counts as a win.

Stick on stick only. Never put a hand on their stick. Never put your stick on their hands. And never hook their stick or hold it down. Each of those can be a penalty. In some leagues, a hold on their stick that hurts them gets you thrown out of the game. That is why you play the puck straight after the lift.

Tell your wingers before you line up that it is a tie-up draw. Then the two nearest players go for the puck the moment it drops, instead of waiting to see.

That is the common answer to a centre who beats you. The other common answer is to change who takes the draw. Maybe a second centre who shoots the other way, or a winger who reads this centre better. Ask your bench which your team reaches for first.

Third idea. The four players not in the dot decide what the draw is worth. Move on the drop, to places agreed before it.

Most draws don't end in a clean win or a clean loss. They end in a scramble. That is a coaching estimate, not a counted figure. So the draw is usually settled a second after the drop, by whoever moves first.

Most faceoff goals against come from four players not knowing what to do once the puck moves, not from the centre losing it. So your job starts on the drop, not after you see where the puck went.

Here is the common setup in your own end. The boards-side winger stands at the outside hash mark, nearer the boards. The inside winger stands at the inside hash mark, facing the slot. The boards-side defenceman stands behind and outside that boards-side winger. The other defenceman, the slot defenceman, stands in front of your goalie.

On a clean win, the centre pulls it back to the boards-side defenceman. The wingers go out to the boards, between the hash marks and the goal line. That is about level with the dot, not thirty feet up the wall. Head up there, never your back to anyone coming at you, and never duck.

Why there? A pass to a winger up near the blue line is long and flat. It gets picked off, and it arrives at your back. Lower down, the pass is short.

On a clean loss, both wingers go straight to the points, the areas just inside your blue line where their defencemen set up. Go on the drop. You are all that stands between their defenceman and a shot from the top of the circle with your goalie screened.

Your centre picks up the other centre, who will head for the high slot or the front of the net. The boards-side defenceman takes the corner or the half-wall. The slot defenceman stays in front of your goalie, between the puck and the net, and doesn't chase the puck out to the point. Slot defenceman, hold that spot with your feet. Play their stick, lift or press then let go, never their hands, never a chop. No shove, no lean, and never your stick across anyone.

On a tie-up, everyone jumps. A tied-up puck is loose in a tiny space with eight players around it, and it belongs to whoever moves first. Usually the inside winger and the boards-side defenceman are closest, so those two go for it. Stick on the puck, not a hit. Whoever sees it shouts, Loose.

All of that is a coaching choice, not a rule. It assumes a zone defence, where each player covers an area rather than a person. Under man-to-man, each winger follows the player they were given instead, which may mean staying low. So ask your bench what your team runs, before your first shift.

And one more piece of the own-end job. Win it to the boards, never into the middle. A puck pulled into your own slot is barely better than a loss, because the slot is the most dangerous ice there is. The boards are the safe half of the ice.

Fourth idea. Tell your linemates where to stand. It is the cheapest improvement any team can make.

Keep your skates and your stick behind your own hash mark, and keep your skates off the circle line. That is legal in every league.

Here is why. If a teammate creeps into the circle or over the hash marks before the puck drops, it is your centre who is thrown out of the draw, not the teammate. So you lose your best draw-taker, on the draw you can least afford to lose them.

And your team is now one more mistake from a penalty. A second violation on the same draw can cost two minutes, and in most leagues it will.

Let me pull those four ideas together, because they are the core of the job. One. Watch the linesperson's hand, not the other centre. Get low from your knees and hips, not your head. Own three or four draws, and mix them.

Two. When a centre keeps beating you, tie them up. Stick on stick, over or under the blade, for an instant, then play the puck. Never a hand on their stick, never your stick on their hands. Or change the taker. Ask your bench.

Three. The four players off the dot move on the drop, to places agreed before it. In your own end, a win goes to the boards, never the middle. On a loss the wingers take the points. On a tie-up the two nearest go for it, stick on the puck.

Four. Tell your linemates. Skates and stick behind your own hash mark, skates off the line. A teammate who creeps in gets your centre thrown out.

Everything else in this episode adds detail to one of those four. So now let's go round the rink, starting in your own end.

Fifth idea. Your strong side, and why your shooting hand matters.

In this episode, a centre's strong side means the circle where their natural backhand pull sends the puck toward the boards. That is your most powerful pull, going to the safest part of the ice.

Coaching sources say a right-shot centre is strong on the right side, and a left-shot centre on the left. But they don't say which way you are facing when they call it right. So test it yourself. Take ten draws in each circle, and you will know within a minute.

On your weak side, the pull toward the boards has to be a forehand. For most players that is slower and weaker. Don't fix that by pulling to your backhand instead. In your own end that sends the puck into the middle, the worst place it can go.

You have three better answers. Learn the forehand pull. Or flip your hands and hold the stick the other way round. That is a whole second skill, worth it if you take a lot of draws. Or put a centre who shoots the other way on that dot.

A team can put a left-shot and a right-shot centre on the ice together and swap the taker by circle. That costs nothing, and almost no recreational team does it.

It is also why some leagues let a team choose which dot a draw is at, and why that choice is worth more than it looks. It lets you put your centre on their strong side.

Sixth idea. Your own-end setup, in more detail.

This is the highest-stakes setup in hockey. It has to work on a win, a loss and a tie-up, because you will get all three.

The centre's job is simple. Don't lose it cleanly. A tie-up is fine. A clean loss to their defenceman at the point is not.

The boards-side winger is usually the winger whose natural side matches the circle. You are the first outlet on a win, and the first player out to their point on a loss.

The inside winger has the harder job. On a loss you have the longer trip out to the far point. And on a scramble you are the closest body to the most dangerous ice. Stick on the puck. That scramble is where players most often stand and wait to see what happens. Coaches call it the most under-coached moment in hockey. Don't wait. Go.

The boards-side defenceman sits near the hash marks. You are ready for anything pulled back to the wall, or to get behind the net.

The slot defenceman guards the front of the net, between the puck and your goalie, with feet and stick, never a shove.

Man-to-man is not the only alternative to the zone setup. There is also a hybrid, where the wingers still go to the points on a loss, but the low defender stays on one player.

Another common variation is to set your defencemen on their off sides for own-end draws. That means the right-shot defenceman on the left boards, and the left-shot one on the right. Why? A puck pulled to the wall then arrives on their forehand, and the breakout pass is a forehand pass. It costs a little in coverage, and gains a lot on the way out. Ask which your team does.

The setup doesn't change from one circle to the other. It is a mirror image. What changes is whether your centre is on their strong side.

Seventh idea. Getting it out after an own-end win, and the rim that dies.

Here are four common ways out.

The quick rim. The boards-side defenceman fires the puck hard round the boards straight away. No holding on to it, no risk, and you are out.

The direct exit. The defenceman passes to the boards-side winger, who is already breaking up the wall. You keep the puck, and the play is done before the other team's forecheck is organised. A forechecker is an opponent pressuring you in your own end.

The reverse. A defenceman takes it behind your net to change the angle. That is good against a team that loads up the boards side.

And off the glass and out. This should be the call when you are tired, pinned, or a player short. Off the glass, not over it, because a puck over the glass can cost you two minutes.

Why does the quick rim work so well off a draw? Because at the whistle every forechecker is standing still. A hard rim is past them before anyone moves.

But rim it all the way, or keep it tight. Hard enough to carry round to your winger, or tight enough that your goalie can stop it behind the net and leave it there. Never halfway. A rim that dies in your own corner hands the puck back. And it sends a defenceman to fetch it under pressure.

Who fetches a dead rim depends on your league. In some leagues your goalie may only play the puck behind the net inside the trapezoid, the shape painted behind the goal. There, a puck dead in the corner is a defenceman's to fetch.

Where there is no trapezoid, your goalie can go and get it. But they must play it with their stick, not cover it, because freezing it out there is a penalty. If you aren't sure, play as though the trapezoid counts.

Now the defenceman who goes to get it. The boards are a safe place for the puck. They are not always safe for the player fetching it. You are facing the wall, with a forechecker coming in behind you. And this is worse than an ordinary trip to the boards. That forechecker was standing still at the whistle, and arrives at full speed.

In this episode, the checker means any player from the other team coming at you, whether they are after the puck or just coming at you.

So here is how you meet the wall, and it is not a matter of style. Never turn your back to the checker. And never duck.

Both protect your head and neck. If you turn your back, you can't see the hit coming or brace for it. If you duck, your head meets the boards first. That is true even at walking speed, so a slow checker doesn't make it safe.

Go to the wall on an angle. Get your skates parallel to the boards, feet apart, knees bent. Forearm and hip to the boards. Never the point of your shoulder, and not the whole shoulder if you can help it. Head up. Chin off your chest.

If you are caught already facing the wall, turn parallel if you possibly can. Only if you can't, get your stick and gloves up and spread on the glass, with your head up and off it. That is the last resort when things have gone wrong, not a way to play.

From here on I will use one short version of that, the same words every time. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the checker, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly. That goes for a winger taking the puck on the wall, too.

Now say you are the forechecker chasing that rim. Know your game before you hit anybody. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call. In a lot of youth hockey, in girls' and women's hockey, and in non-checking adult hockey, checking isn't allowed. There, a hit is a penalty, and one that hurts the player can get you thrown out of the game. So ask your league, and until you have an answer, don't hit anyone.

And wherever you play, go after the puck with your stick, not the player. If the player is facing the wall when you get there, or you can see their numbers, pull up. Take the puck, or angle them off it.

A hit on a player facing the wall is checking from behind. In nearly every league, plan on being thrown out of the game for it. In some it is a match penalty, the most serious penalty there is.

In women's hockey, pushing a player into the boards, pinning them there, or stepping or gliding into them is a penalty.

And if you are the defenceman, none of that takes anything off your posture. Even where checking isn't allowed, two players racing for the puck along the boards still meet. The wall still arrives. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the checker, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly.

Let me pull your own end together before we move up the ice.

Find out whether your team plays zone or man-to-man off the draw. Learn your strong side by testing it, and on your weak side never pull it to your backhand. Win it to the boards. On a win the wingers go to the wall, low. On a loss they go to the points, on the drop. The two nearest jump a tie-up, stick on the puck. Rim it all the way or keep it tight, never halfway, and know whose corner puck it is in your league.

Whoever fetches it, or takes it on the wall, remember. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the checker, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly. And whoever chases it, stick on the puck, pull up if you see their numbers, and know whether your game allows checking.

Now the dot itself.

Eighth idea. Scout the centre opposite, and keep them guessing.

Most centres have one favourite draw and one strong side. Two shifts of watching is usually enough to find them. And most of your chances to watch come while you are sitting on the bench. So watch the other lines' draws, not just your own. It is free, and almost nobody does it.

Here are the tells. A bottom hand rolled over, knuckles down, means a backhand pull is coming. That grip gives a lot of power on the backhand, and almost nothing on the forehand. Hands high on the stick mean a sweep or a tie-up, not a yank.

The foot they have loaded tells you which way they mean to turn. The angle of their blade tells you where they want the puck to go. A tight grip and a low, coiled stance usually mean they are going for a clean win. Relaxed and upright often means they plan to tie you up.

Linespersons have habits too. How high they hold the puck, how fast they drop it, and how long they wait. Notice them. Some coaches even watch the official's elbow instead of the hand, because it moves a fraction earlier. Try both.

Then keep the other centre guessing, for the same reason as before. Even a draw you are worse at is worth using if it stops them getting ready for your best one.

You can try a small fake, too. A shoulder, a hip, or a slightly delayed stick can draw the other centre into moving early. In some leagues that only means a re-drop. In others, the centre who moved early is taken out of the dot. So it can pay, but don't build your draw on it.

The fake can land on you instead. If your own shoulder goes early, that is your violation. And it uses up one of the two violations that can cost your team a penalty. A fake that touches the other centre is contact before the drop, and that can be a penalty too. So fake with your shoulder, never into them. Bait them once, then take a clean draw.

And when the other centre is the one cheating the drop, moving early or leaning in, don't cheat back. Hold your timing, watch the hand, and press their stick as the puck lands, so their early jump wins nothing.

Never answer a lean with a lean. Contact before the drop is a violation whoever makes it. The centre who makes it can get a penalty as well, and in some leagues that penalty is automatic.

Ninth idea. Your stick, your skates and your hands at the dot.

Some younger age groups use a quicker, simpler draw than the one in this episode. If that is your game, ask how it works.

Start with the stick lift, the bolder version of the tie-up. You lift their blade clean off the ice and take the puck yourself. It wins more when it works, and it fails badly when you miss. The same limits hold. Stick on stick, for an instant, never their hands, never a hook or a hold.

Put your whole blade flat on the ice in the white part of the dot, and leave it there until the puck lands. Leagues ask for different amounts of blade. The whole blade in the white satisfies all of them.

Who puts their stick down first changes from league to league. It matters, because whoever goes first shows their blade angle first. So when you have to go first, vary the angle you show. When you go second, take your look.

And never refuse to put your stick down to wait the other centre out. Every league can take you out of the dot for that.

A word for your wingers too. You will see NHL players reach in with their sticks and rest a skate on the line. Plenty of leagues don't allow either, so don't copy it. Keeping everything behind the line gives up a reach that is worth nothing next to what a violation costs your centre.

Now your feet. Some centres kick the puck back with a skate, or open a skate so a tied-up puck squirts the right way. Whether you may win a draw with your skate depends on your league. In some leagues, including all hockey in Britain, it is a violation, so don't.

Where it is allowed, your skate goes at the puck, never at the other player. At a faceoff, two players' skates are only inches apart.

Here is why that matters so much. If you kick at an opponent, or even try to, plan on being thrown out of the game. In most leagues it is a match penalty. And in at least one league you don't even need a kick. Using your skate to push off an opponent gets you thrown out too.

And your hands. If you took the draw, keep your hand off the puck until a third player has touched it. Trying to win the draw by batting it with your hand can be a penalty.

Everyone else may catch a puck out of the air, but drop it straight down to the ice. Better still, keep your hands off it in the scrum altogether.

Don't fall on the puck or gather it into your body. That is a penalty, and covering it with your hand usually is too. If the puck is in your own crease, the goalie's painted area, either one can be a penalty shot, a free shot alone on your goalie. In at least one league it counts if you are in the crease, even if the puck isn't. And with your net empty, treat it as a goal given to them. So win the puck with your stick.

Tenth idea. When your centre gets thrown out of the draw.

First, don't give the officials the chance. Go straight to the dot. Stalling on the way to buy your bench a rest is a penalty, not a tactic, and in some leagues there is no warning first. Being on time is free.

And never time your arrival to land just as the clock to line up runs out. That can get the centre taken off the draw.

If your centre is thrown out, someone else takes it. Nobody can come over the boards for it unless a penalty changes the numbers. The replacement comes from the players already on the ice, usually a winger.

So agree before the game who that is. In some leagues the player who caused the violation isn't allowed to take it. So a line needs two names, not one, and every winger should know whether they are one of them.

If you are the replacement, tie it up and take it straight. Lift or press their stick with yours, over or under the blade, for an instant, then play the puck. Never a hand on their stick, never your stick on their hands, and never hook it or hold it down.

Why so simple? Your team is now one violation from a penalty. So nobody moves early, nobody leans, nobody reaches. It is no time for a set play.

Let the linesperson get clear before you commit. In at least one league, a second violation that runs into the official is an automatic penalty. And don't count on a time-out to save you. In many leagues you can't take one after a faceoff violation.

Let me pull the dot together. Scout from the bench, and change your technique. Fake with your shoulder, never into them, and don't cheat back. Whole blade in the white, and leave it there. Vary your blade angle if you go first. Your skate goes at the puck, never the player. Hands off the puck. Get to the dot on time. Agree two backup takers. And if you are the backup, tie it up and take it straight.

Now up the ice.

Eleventh idea. Neutral-zone draws, where you can experiment.

The neutral zone is the middle of the ice, between the two blue lines. Its four dots have no circles. Draws there matter least, which makes them the ones to take risks on.

The common default puts both wingers wide in their lanes, level with or just behind the dot. Both defencemen sit back toward your own blue line, one each side of the middle, ready for a rush if it goes wrong. Your team's may differ, so ask.

On a win, move the puck up straight away. Carry it or pass it into their zone with control. Why not just shoot it in and chase it? Because a won draw gives you the puck and a standing start, and a dump-in throws that away.

Tracking from one season of a European league found that possessions with a dump-in made far fewer shots than those without. That was not the NHL and not your league, so take the direction, not the numbers.

On a loss, your defencemen are the whole plan. They close the space on the carrier straight away and defend the rush. Stick on the puck, not a hit. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call. And if you see their numbers, pull up.

This is the place to try things. A draw you are still learning. Your flipped grip on your weak side. Or a draw you want the other centre to have in their head when you meet them in your own end later. A lost neutral-zone draw costs you almost nothing.

It is also the place for the lose-it-forward play. That means pushing the puck forward past the other centre and going after it. It works here because there is space behind them, and little danger if it fails. In their end it can sometimes beat a centre who is set on pulling back. In your own end, never. Go round the other centre, never through them. They are now between you and the puck, so chase the puck, not the ice they are standing on. Driving through them to reach it is interference, a penalty in any game. In women's hockey, stepping or gliding into a player who is standing still is a penalty too, and a major there gets you thrown out of the game.

Twelfth idea. Draws in their end. An offensive-zone draw is the one place in hockey where you get to run a real set play, from a known start, against defenders who are standing still. Here is a common default. Your team's may differ, so ask.

The centre tries to win it back, not forward. The defenceman on the draw side sits at the point, feet already set, stick already loaded. That is the shooter. The other defenceman holds the far point, as the safety against a counterattack. The inside winger is the usual target for a quick pull-back shot, and drives the net if the draw is lost. The boards-side winger is ready to attack the wall, stick on the puck, or spin off it.

Why win it back to a set shooter? Because it is one of very few shots in hockey where you don't have to take a pass, settle it and then set your feet. The feet are already planted. That is worth more than a slightly better angle.

In at least one adult recreational league, a big backswing counts as a slap shot, which is a penalty there. If that is your league, keep the backswing short.

Now the player driving the net. Get to the front of the net, but outside the crease, the goalie's painted area. Keep your skates off its red edge too. Arrive under control. Building up speed into contact, even with two hard strides, can be charging.

Stay off the goalie and out of their way. Here is why. A goalie who is down or turned can't protect themselves, so contact can hurt them. It can also be a penalty, and it can cost you the goal. Even standing so the goalie can't move freely in the crease can cost the goal.

At the net, hold your spot with your feet. No shove, no lean, never your stick across anyone, and never into anyone's back. If you can see a player's numbers, pull up. Never lift anyone off their feet. Know where the post is, keep your head up and your chin off your chest, and never duck. Even going slowly, a crash there can hurt.

On a clean loss, the job flips at once to not giving up a counterattack. The far defenceman holds. The near one gets back to the line. And the wingers pressure the puck rather than drop back.

Why pressure? Because you have four players above the puck, and their ways out are limited. Your forecheck is never better placed than it is right now. Pressure means stick on the puck, not a hit. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call. And if you see their numbers, pull up.

On a tie-up, attack the puck with the two nearest players, sticks first, and keep both defencemen at the line. A tie-up in their end is a free loose puck in their zone. That is a good result, not a failure.

After the whistle, stay out of shoving matches. In their end there is a cost on top. If you join one inside the edge of the circle, the draw moves out to the neutral zone, and you have thrown away the draw in their end.

Thirteenth idea. Set plays, and a small playbook.

Three set plays are common, and all of them are coaching choices.

The first is the D one-timer. The centre pulls it straight back to the draw-side defenceman, who shoots it first time, without stopping it. That is what a one-timer means. Its weakness is that every defence expects it. So somebody has to clear the shooting lane, usually the player at the net, who steps across to screen it, never a push.

The second is the winger walk-out. The centre pulls it to the inside winger, who takes a stride toward the middle and shoots, or passes into the slot. Use it against a team that rushes straight out at your point, because you are attacking the space they just left.

The third is the tip play. The pull goes to the point. Someone screens the goalie, standing in their line of sight, outside the crease. A screen is your body, nothing else, so keep your hands and your blade down. Someone else tips the shot. Each shot is worth a bit less, but it makes rebounds and chaos. And it is the safest of the three, because a point shot into traffic rarely turns into a rush the other way.

Some teams also loop the boards-side winger round to the inside, or swap a winger and a defenceman before the drop. Those tend to work about once a game against a team that is paying attention.

Now, how many plays? Two or three per zone, not six. Here is why. Teams that put in six plays run them all badly, and the players spend the drop trying to remember which one is on. One play run very well beats six run fairly well.

The centre calls the play in one word at the dot, and everyone acknowledges. A play nobody heard is not a play.

And give every play a failure branch. If we lose it, this player does this. Most plays are drawn up only for the win. That is why teams that lose an offensive-zone draw so often look lost.

When there is confusion, no call, or a replacement taking the draw, use the simple default. Tie it up in your own end. Point shot in theirs.

Let me pull the middle and their end together. In the neutral zone, carry or pass it in on a win. On a loss, close the gap with your stick on the puck. Experiment there, and go round the other centre, never through. In their end, win it back to a set shooter. At the net, outside the crease, off the goalie, no shove and no lean. On a loss, pressure with your stick, not a hit. Keep two or three plays, one-word calls, and a plan for the loss. And after the whistle, stay out of shoving matches.

Now the special situations.

Fourteenth idea. Draws on special teams.

On the power play your team has an extra player, because the other team took a penalty. In some leagues you get to choose which of the two dots in their end the draw is at. If so, pick your centre's strong side, or the side your one-timer shoots from. Ask for it early, not at the dot. In other leagues the official picks, so be ready for either side.

Then win the draw, or at least don't lose it cleanly. A tie-up that keeps the puck in their zone is fine. A clever set play that ends with them clearing it is not.

Here is why. If they clear it, you have to break out, regroup, and carry it back in against a penalty kill already set on its blue line. That can cost a third of your two minutes. The entry you avoid is worth more than the draw you win.

On the penalty kill, when your team is a player short, everything flips. You are not trying to create anything. You are trying to end their possession.

A draw in your own end a player short is where the disadvantages pile up. You are a player down, in your own end, against a set unit with a rehearsed play and a shooter whose feet are planted. So a tie-up is a win here too.

The common choice is to put your best draw-taker out, even if they are not a natural penalty killer. Why? The draw is worth more than twenty seconds of coverage. Ask whether your bench does.

A clean win can be time off the clock. In most leagues, while you have fewer players on the ice than they do, you can shoot the puck the length of the ice without icing being called. Some youth levels don't allow that, so check yours. And it ends when your penalty time runs out. In at least one league that is so even before your player is back on the ice.

Fifteenth idea. The draws that come with a situation.

When the other team ices it, win the draw and attack at once. Here is why. They iced it because they were pinned in their end, so they are tired. That tiredness is their weakness, and it disappears the moment they get to breathe.

In some leagues, the team that iced it can't change players or take a time-out, and the other team chooses the dot. In others they can change, so check yours before you build a plan on it. Where that freeze applies, your coach can send out whoever they like against their tired line. That edge only lasts one shift, so use it.

When your team iced it, it is the other way round. You are tired, stuck in your end, and can't rest. Get the puck off the glass and out of your zone at the first chance. Nothing clever.

Late in a game, protecting a one-goal lead in your own end, that draw is worth more than all the draws before it. There is no time to recover from losing it. So this is where you accept the tie-up. A scramble that burns time beats a fifty-fifty try at a clean win.

The other common approach is to put your best taker out with a plan for the win and for the loss. Many coaches do both, so ask which yours stresses.

And get the alignment right before the play. Late in a game, more draws are lost because players are unsure of their places than because the other centre was better.

Chasing a goal with seconds left, win it straight back to a shooter whose feet are already set. Put a forward at the net for the screen and the rebound, outside the crease and off the goalie. There is no time for a second pass. Another option is for the centre to shoot straight at the net off the draw. That gives the goalie no time to set, but it is a weak shot. Ask your bench which, and settle it at the bench, not at the dot.

Defending that draw, all you need is time. A tie-up is a win. The wingers get into the shooting lanes rather than chasing the puck. Get close to the shooter. Stay on your feet, square to the shot, hands behind your body, and head out of the lane. Keep your stick flat on the ice beside you, never out in front. Commit fully, still on your feet, or get out of the lane. And a won puck goes off the glass and out. Every second of a scramble is a second off their last attack.

After a time-out, expect the other team to have a plan too, and expect it to be different from their usual one.

With your goalie pulled for an extra skater, the extra player usually goes to the front of the net or to the high slot. At the net, they stay outside the crease and off the goalie. Both defencemen go to the points. The centre has to win it back cleanly. Why? There is no safe outlet, and a lost draw lets them clear it and run down the clock.

Defending against a pulled goalie, win the draw and get the puck out of your zone under control. Shoot at the empty net only with a clean lane. Six against six is not a player short. So a miss from your own half is icing, and the draw comes straight back to your end.

At four on four, your coach might send out two forwards and two defencemen, or three forwards and one. Either way the ice is huge, and a lost draw can quickly become two of them against one of you. So keep both of your back players back. At three on three in overtime, a lost draw is often a scoring chance within ten seconds, and the game ends the moment anyone scores. Get that one exactly right.

Sixteenth idea. How to practise.

A draw is the nearest thing hockey has to a fixed skill. Same start, same cue, same distance, dozens of times a game. That is exactly the kind of thing that gets better with reps.

In one recent NHL season, among centres who took a lot of draws, the best won about six in ten, and the worst about four in ten. Coaches read that gap as a skill that rewards work.

Practise in pairs with a real drop, from the right height, with unpredictable timing, because you are training a reaction. End practice with ten minutes of draws, both circles, both grips. Spend the reps on your weak side and the draw you are worst at, because that is where the gains are.

Off the ice, coaches recommend grip and wrist strength and quick hands, because a draw is about hand speed before it is about strength. That is coaching craft. Nobody has published a measurement of faceoff reaction times.

Rehearse with all five players, and run the win, the loss and the tie-up. Have every winger take draws, because every winger will take one in a game when the centre is thrown out. And teach your wingers the rule about the hash marks. It takes thirty seconds.

Let me pull it all together.

Prepare the draws that matter. Your own end, the penalty kill, a late lead, and after an icing.

Watch the linesperson's hand, not the other centre. Get low from your knees and hips, never your head. Own three or four draws, and change them. When a centre beats you, tie them up, stick on stick, never a hand on it, never their hands.

The four off the dot move on the drop, to places you agreed. In your own end, win it to the boards, never the middle, and rim it all the way or keep it tight. Whoever fetches it, or takes it on the wall, remember. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the checker, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly. And if you are chasing it, stick on the puck, pull up if you see their numbers, and know whether your game allows checking.

Tell your linemates. Skates and stick behind the hash mark, skates off the line. Whole blade in the white. Your skate goes at the puck, never the player. Your hands stay off the puck.

In their end, win it back to a set shooter, and at the net stay off the goalie. Keep the playbook small, and always plan for the loss.

Three questions to ask your coach. Do we play zone or man-to-man off our own-end draws? Who takes the draw if our centre is thrown out, and who is second? What are our two or three set plays, and what happens if we lose the draw? And one for your league. Does our game allow checking?

Go and ask. Their answers tell you how your team wants you to play its faceoffs, and now you know why each answer makes sense.
