===== SCRIPT START =====

This episode is about puck handling. It takes its time. For each piece I will tell you what to do and why it works. Then you can still choose well when the play looks different from the picture in your head.

Puck handling means moving the puck on your stick, skating with it, and keeping it from the other team. Good hands buy you about half a second before you have to decide. This episode is about spending it well.

First, a few words you will hear all the way through.

Stickhandling and carrying. Stickhandling means moving the puck on your blade, touch after touch. Carrying means skating with the puck pushed a little ahead of you, touching it only now and then.

Top hand and bottom hand. Your top hand is the one at the end of the stick. It steers. Your bottom hand is lower down the shaft. It guides and steadies.

The checker. That is any opponent coming at you, whether they are after the puck or just coming at you.

The far side. This is the side of your body away from the checker.

A poke check. That is an opponent jabbing their stick blade straight out along the ice to knock the puck off yours.

That is the vocabulary. Now the job.

I will start with four big ideas, because they matter most. If you only listen to the first part of this episode, take these four with you. After that we will add the detail, one situation at a time.

First idea. Play with your head up.

Whenever you have the puck, your eyes should be up, not on it. If you take one habit from this episode, take this one. That is a coaching priority, not a measured ranking.

Here is why. Finding the open teammate, reading the pressure, timing your entry over the blue line, hitting a teammate who is moving. Every one of those needs your eyes. A player staring at the puck can't do any of it, however well they understand the game.

And a player looking down can't see the checker coming. So they get hit hard.

So how do you get your head up? If you just order yourself to look up, you look up, lose the puck, and look straight back down.

Build the feel first, and your eyes come up on their own. That means learning the blade by touch. Move the puck slowly and notice how it feels on the toe, the tip of the blade, in the middle, and on the heel, the end nearest the shaft. Do it on your forehand and your backhand.

Then peek, don't stare. Start with your eyes up, and glance down only when you have really lost track of the puck. Then make the glances shorter and rarer.

And head up doesn't mean chin to the ceiling. Eyes up and forward is enough. The puck sits at the bottom edge of what you can see.

Second idea. Keep your body between the checker and the puck.

Coaches rank this above every move, because a move decides a handful of plays in a game and protecting the puck decides dozens. That is how coaches see it, not a count.

Start with where the puck is. Put it on the far side, away from the checker. Then your body is a wall between the checker and the puck.

Here is why. If the puck is on the checker's side, you are relying on your stick to beat their stick. And their stick has less distance to travel than yours. You have already lost it. Keep your blade on the far side of the puck too, so a poke has to go through the puck and your blade.

That needs your top hand out in front of your chest, not stuck to your hip. A hand stuck to your hip keeps the puck right in front of you, which is right in front of the checker too.

Next, get low and wide. Feet wider than your shoulders. Knees bent, hips down, weight low. Chest up, so you can still see. Standing tall and narrow is why players get knocked off pucks.

Keep your feet moving, with small, quick steps. A player standing still gets pinned. A player who is moving can slip away along the boards or spin out to the middle. Only spin off someone who's already leaning on you — never off someone still coming at you, however slowly.

Now say a checker is already on you, in a slow battle for the puck. Turn side-on, so your hip and backside face the checker, not your chest. Here is why. Your hip and backside are the strongest, widest part of you, and side-on the checker can't reach through the front of you. Your shoulder, arm, hip and leg are all in the way at once. If you turn to face the checker instead, you get pinned on the boards.

What you may do with your body depends on your league, so find out before you play. Either way, you take contact, you don't give it. Hold your position and let the checker come to you. Don't lean, bump or drive into them. In a game without body checking, doing that is a penalty. If it hurts them, or it is reckless, you can be thrown out of the game, and in some leagues that is automatic. In some, a reckless one is a match penalty, so plan on that.

And in a Canadian adult league that plays the CARHA rulebook, go further. Shield the puck with your position and your feet, and stay out of the contact altogether, because not avoiding it is a penalty there.

Now the part that keeps you safe on the boards.

Side-on is for a checker already on you in a slow battle, not for a race to the boards. But two things hold at every speed, and they beat everything else in this episode.

Never turn your back to the checker. And never duck.

Both protect your head and neck. If you turn your back, the contact becomes a hit from behind, which you can't see coming or brace for. If you duck, your head meets the boards first. That is true even going slowly, so a checker coming slowly doesn't make it safe.

So here is the position you aim for. Skates parallel to the boards, feet apart, knees bent. Forearm and hip to the boards. Never the point of your shoulder, and not the whole shoulder if you can help it. Head up, chin off your chest.

If you are already facing the boards and can't turn, that is damage limitation, not a position to aim for. Get your stick and gloves up and spread on the glass, arms out, and keep your head off the glass.

From here on I will use one short version of that position, the same words every time, so you can learn it like a line. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the checker, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly.

Third idea. Know your out before you get to the puck.

Protecting the puck buys you a second, not a minute. Every second you hold it, a second checker is on the way. If your plan is to shield it until something happens, what happens is a turnover.

So while you are still skating to the puck, answer three questions.

Who can I pass to? Usually that is your low support player, or the next teammate arriving.

And where is the pressure coming from? That tells you which side of your body the puck needs to be on.

If nobody is open, you have four outs. A rim, which is a hard pass sent on round the curve of the boards. A reverse, which sends the puck back against the way the pressure is coming. A chip off the boards past the checker into space. Or a spin into the middle, but only off someone who's already leaning on you, never off someone still coming at you.

Here is why answering early works. A player with a plan moves the puck in half a second. A player without one protects it beautifully for three seconds, and loses it.

Fourth idea. Look for the pass first, and know where not to stickhandle.

With a defender in front of you, look for the pass before the move. Here is why. A pass moves the puck faster than anyone can skate. It can't be poked off your blade. And it moves the defence further than any move does. Even a pass to a teammate in a worse spot usually beats a move against a defender in a good spot.

Then there are three places where you don't stickhandle at all.

The first is your own end. A turnover there is a scoring chance against you, with no time to recover. So pass it, rim it, reverse it, or put it off the glass and out. Off the glass, never over it. In many leagues, a puck sent straight over the glass from your own end is two minutes, even by accident. A rim or a reverse is played on the boards, often with an opponent arriving, so the same position. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the checker, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly.

The second is the middle of the ice, in your own end or between the blue lines. Most of their players are there, there are no boards to help you, and a lost puck goes straight to the slot, the ice in front of your net. So don't dangle, which means stickhandling to beat players, through the middle. If it is really open, carry the puck through. Carrying is not stickhandling.

The third is when you are the last player back. If losing the puck means an odd-man rush against you, with more of them coming than you have back, the move is off. Always.

Let me pull those four together.

One. Head up. Build the feel, so your eyes can come up.

Two. Body between the checker and the puck. Far side, low and wide, feet moving. Take contact, don't give it. On the boards, skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the checker, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly.

Three. Know your out before you get there, because protection buys a second, not a minute.

Four. Pass first. And in your own end, through the middle, or as the last player back, don't stickhandle. Move it.

Everything else adds detail to one of those four. Now the battles in tight.

Fifth idea. Getting out of pressure.

Most of your puck touches in a real game happen in a space about the size of a phone box. There is a stick in your feet and a body on your back. Open-ice hands are the fun part. Tight hands are the useful part.

In tight, shorten everything. Small touches, close to your skates, blade always tilted over the puck. A big pull in traffic just gives the puck away. Handle it in the space you own. In traffic that is the small triangle between your two skates and the tip of your blade, plus whatever your body is shielding.

Your hands need room for this. Watch a new player, and their top hand is often stuck to their hip, with the puck rattling in a narrow strip right in front of their skates. That is called hip lock. It feels safe, and it is the opposite, because there is no far side to move the puck to. Get your top hand out in front of your chest, about a fist from your body, elbow slightly bent. From there the blade can reach all the way round you.

When pressure comes from one side, turn away from it, not into it. Your first move should be to take the puck round behind your body, not to try to beat the stick on that side.

And remember, protection is a way to buy time, not a place to stay. Once it has bought you that time, get out. There are two ways.

The first is the spin-off. From side-on, hip toward the checker and weight low, turn away from them and take the puck with you. You push off the outside edge of your outside skate. That is the skate further from the way you are turning, on its little-toe side. So turning left, you push off the little-toe side of your right skate. The checker's weight is going into where you were. You are already leaving.

The second is the tight turn. Cut across the checker's path instead of away from it, so your body stays between them and the puck the whole way round.

The timing is the hard part. Spin the moment you feel the checker lean in. Only spin off someone who's already leaning on you — never off someone still coming at you, however slowly. If you spin before they commit, they just follow you. Both moves are skating moves before they are hand moves, so the work you do on your edges pays off here.

Now the boards. The boards are like an extra defender on your side. With your hip and forearm against them, the checker can only come from one direction. You have halved the problem. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the checker, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly. If you are caught facing the boards anyway, get your stick and gloves up and spread on the glass, and keep your head off the glass.

But don't stand square to the boards with your feet planted. Facing straight into the wall and standing still is how you get pinned, and how you lose the puck to the next player in. Angle yourself so you always have a way out along the boards, and keep your feet moving so you can take it. And remember the boards are an out as well as a shield. A rim or a reverse both count as winning the battle.

Now a choice your coach makes. How long you hold the puck on the boards is a team decision, not a law. Some teams want a quick chip or rim out straight away, to take no risk at all. Others want you to hang on, draw two checkers to you, and wait for support. The reason there is that your team comes out of the battle still holding the puck. These are really different jobs, so ask which one your coach wants.

Sixth idea. Your free hand.

When a checker is on your hip, or their stick is across your hands, it is tempting to grab them or their stick with your free hand. Don't.

Keep your free hand high on your own stick, and use the bar made by your forearm and the shaft as a brace. Here is why that is the habit to build. You get the same physical help as a hand on the checker, with none of the penalty risk. And both hands are ready to move the puck the instant the pressure lets go.

Brace, don't push. Hold the ground you already have, and don't lean the bar into them. Never lay the shaft across them, and never drive it, held between your two hands, into a player. That is a cross-check, and it is a penalty in every league. In a CARHA league, keep the bar off them altogether.

Never hold. Closing your hand on a player or their stick is holding. That is at least two minutes everywhere, and in some leagues it can get you thrown out of the game.

Keeping a player or their stick off you for a moment with your free hand is called fending off. Some leagues allow it, some allow it only on strict conditions, and some don't allow it at all. So find out before you rely on it. Wherever you do it, only use it to keep a position you already have, never to push off and make space. The free hand high on your own stick works in every league.

And keep your hand off the other player's cage, the face guard on their helmet. Grabbing it is a penalty in every league, and in most leagues it costs more than ordinary holding. In some leagues even a mild grab throws you out of the game, every time.

Seventh idea. A loose puck in your feet.

A puck that comes loose into your skates is not lost. It is in one of the hardest places on the ice for an opponent to reach. The checker has to reach past your skates, your legs and your turned body, and doing that at speed usually costs them their balance. Use your skates as a wall, and your hips and backside as the barrier, so your stick is free to handle.

But that protection comes from your body, not from a rule. No rule keeps a stick out of the space between your feet. A checker can get their stick in there. It only becomes a penalty if it trips you or holds you up, and if they are clearly going for the puck, the referee may let it go. So don't wait for a whistle.

Trap it, then play it. Trap the puck against the inside of your skate and pull it straight back out to your blade. Do it fast. While you dig at your feet your head is down, and that is when the checker arrives.

Don't park on it. Holding the puck in your feet to force a whistle is a penalty in every league. The trap is a half-second move, not a hiding place.

You can also kick the puck to yourself. Pull it into your feet, kick it forward, and catch it on your blade. That is allowed anywhere on the ice. It is worth drilling, because it turns a puck you had lost into a puck back on your blade.

But only ever at the puck, never at a player. Keep the kick small. And if you can't see the puck, don't swing your skate at where you think it is. Kicking or even trying to kick a player is never a two-minute mistake. In almost every league it is a match penalty, which throws you out and keeps you out until your case has been dealt with. In the rest, the referee can still throw you out. And never use your skate to push a player, even without a kick. In some leagues that throws you out too.

Now the other way round, when you are the one trying to take the puck. Play it with your stick, not a hit. Whether you may hit anyone at all is your league's call. And if you can see the other player's numbers, pull up.

Stick on the puck, never on their hands or body, and never a chop. Keep it out from between their legs. A stick in there that trips them or holds them up can still be a penalty, even when you are going for the puck. A reckless one can get you thrown out, and in some leagues, plan on a match penalty.

Eighth idea. A puck that pops up at your own net.

One new word first. The crease is the painted area in front of each goal.

A puck that pops up off a skate, a stick or the boards tempts you to catch it. Catching it is allowed. Keeping hold of it is not.

So knock it straight down to the ice, in one movement, and move it with your stick at once. Don't take a stride with it, throw it, or fake the drop.

Why be so strict? Because keeping hold of the puck, covering a loose one with your hand, or falling on it on purpose can be a penalty. And in your own crease it is far worse. A skater who holds, covers or gathers in the puck in their own crease gives the other team a penalty shot, a free shot alone on your goalie. If your goalie has been pulled for an extra attacker, in most leagues the other team is just given a goal. Your goalie is allowed to cover the puck there. You are not.

One thing catches players out. The crease isn't only the paint on the ice. It runs up to the top of the net. So a puck you catch in the air over your crease, below the crossbar, counts as in the crease.

So at your own net, never close your hand on the puck, and never trap it in your skates. Knock it down and move it.

Let me pull the battles in tight together. Shorten everything, and turn away from pressure, not into it. Protection buys time, so spin off or turn tight only once the checker is already leaning on you, never while they're still coming at you, however slowly. On the boards, keep an angle and your feet moving, never square and planted. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the checker, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly. Free hand high on your own stick, braced, never pushed, never holding, never on the cage. A puck in your feet, trap it and play it, never park on it. Kick the puck, never a player. And a puck that pops up at your own net, knock it down at once.

Ninth idea. Don't slow down when the puck arrives.

Here is the most common sight in amateur hockey. A player takes a pass in full stride, slows down to a comfortable speed to handle it, and hands the defence two free seconds.

Why does it happen? Handling and skating fight for your attention, and your stride is what gets dropped. Three things go. Your arms stop driving your stride, because they are working the stick. Your head drops, which tips your weight forward and shortens your stride. And your feet get narrower, which weakens your edges.

Your speed is the most valuable thing you have. The whole point of good hands is that they cost you none of it.

So at full speed, carry the puck. Don't stickhandle every step. Push it out a little ahead of you and a little to the side, usually on your forehand, where your blade sits as you stride. Ahead means you skate toward it instead of dragging it. To the side means it isn't in the way of your feet. Take two or three hard strides, catch up to it, and push it out again. A few big pushes and real strides beat fussy handling every time. Touch it just enough to keep the defender honest, a small dribble every few strides, so they can't time a poke check.

Carrying has a second benefit. It doesn't take much attention, so your head can stay up. Every stride you spend looking at the puck is a stride you didn't spend reading the defence. If you can only look up when you slow down, you will only make decisions when you are slow.

Where this matters most is the blue line. If you cross it with speed, the defenders have to back off. If you slow down at the line, you are easy to defend.

One habit helps. Do your crossover and tight-turn drills with a puck at least half the time, because that is how you will use them in a game.

Tenth idea. You and the puck don't have to travel together.

This is the version that keeps your speed highest. Push the puck into the space you are about to be in, then skate onto it. If a defender's stick is blocking your way, push the puck past the stick and go round the other way. The puck goes past it, and so do you, separately. Or chip it off the boards past a defender and skate round them to collect it. The boards make the pass for you. Or slip it between or past their skates and take the other route round.

That is the honest answer to a lot of one-on-ones. You don't need fancy hands to beat a defender if the puck can go one side and you can go the other.

There is a cost, and it depends on where you are. A failed slip usually leaves a loose puck, not a clean steal. In their end or the middle of the ice, a loose puck behind a defender is often a race you can still win, so the play is cheap. In your own end, it leaves a loose puck in front of your own net, with you on the wrong side of the defender. That is a scoring chance against you. So slip forward. Never slip to get out of trouble.

Eleventh idea. You can beat one player, not two.

When a second defender arrives, trying to beat both is how a two-on-two turns into a rush the other way. You have three better choices. Pass to the teammate the second defender has just left open. Chip the puck into the space behind them. Or protect it and wait for support. Take the contact side-on where your game allows checking, hold your spot where it doesn't, and stay out of the contact in a CARHA league.

And one convincing move beats four fancy ones. Every extra move is another moment the puck is exposed, and another chance for a stick to arrive. If you find yourself on your third move, the play has already gone wrong.

Now a choice your coach makes. How much carrying a team wants varies a lot. Some coaches want defencemen to skate the puck out of their end whenever the lane is there. Others want the first available pass, every time. Some want wingers taking players on along the boards. Others want everything chipped in behind the defence, for the forwards to chase down. This episode assumes a team that likes to keep the puck as it comes out of its end. But this is your coach's call, so ask what yours wants.

Your position changes this too. A defenceman in their own end should protect the puck rather than try to beat a forechecker, because if the move fails it can be a goal against. A winger needs wall protection above everything, which is coaching craft, not a measurement. And a centre almost always has teammates around, so the job is to hold the puck just long enough to move a defender, then give it up.

Twelfth idea. When you take a defender on, make them turn.

Start with what never works. Don't skate straight at the defender's chest. They stay square, keep both ways past them closed, and stand you up. Skate at one of their shoulders instead, so they have to turn. The moment their hips turn, there is a way past that wasn't there before.

Which shoulder is a real choice, not a settled rule, and the two do different things.

The outside shoulder is the one nearer the boards. If you attack it, the defender opens their hips toward the boards. That opens a way back underneath them, into the middle. And from that angle, a change of direction is a small move for you and a big one for them. The catch is that a well-coached defender wants you out there, because out wide you are less of a threat.

The inside shoulder means driving at the middle of the ice. The defender has to believe that threat, because the middle is what they are taught to protect first. The cost is that you are skating into help. The other defender and the forward racing back are in the middle.

So how do you choose? Take the outside when you have more speed than the defender, and want to beat them wide or cut back underneath. Take the inside when you don't have the speed to win wide. Take it too when support is coming through the middle, or when the defender is already pushing you wide. Either way, the point is to make them turn, and then take the way past that their turn opens, not the one you planned before the rush. Neither choice is settled coaching doctrine, so ask your coach which they want from your position.

Next, change your speed, not just your direction. Here is why it works. Defenders are good at defending direction. They are coached on it, and their backward skating is built for it. What they defend badly is timing. A defender keeping their distance has to match your speed. So slow down to bait them, then explode. Once they have slowed down, they can't speed up backwards again quickly. Go the instant they settle. Or attack the moment they turn from skating backwards to forwards, or move sideways. For that moment they can't change direction again. If you come at the same speed in a straight line every time, the defender has only one problem to solve, and that is easy.

Use the defender's weight too. A defender who has committed their weight one way can't take it back. If they lunge or reach with their stick, go round them, not through them. If they are skating backwards hard, stop suddenly, and they keep going back into space you have left.

And if you have a step on them, just skate. Coaches see this all the time. A player with a clear lane skates right at the defender to try a move, and gets stood up. If you have speed, use it.

Last, remember what the move is for. Beating the defender is not the goal. Making a scoring chance is. A move that beats them but leaves you no speed and no angle has done nothing. A simple drive wide that beats nobody, but pulls two defenders and opens a pass to the middle, has done a lot. So win one-on-ones with a change of speed and body position more than with your hands.

Thirteenth idea. Fakes, and how they really work.

A defender can't wait to see what you actually do. By then it is too late for them. So they read your signals instead. Your head, your eyes, your shoulders, your hips, your weight, and the angle of your blade. They commit on what those tell them.

That is the whole idea of a deke, a move to get past someone. It is a false signal. You show one thing, they commit to it, and you do the other. A defender who commits the wrong way is already beaten.

Three things follow from that. First, commit fully. A lazy head turn fools nobody. The fake has to look exactly like the real thing until the last moment. If you wouldn't have believed it, neither will they. Second, a fake with only your hands isn't a fake. If you move the puck while your body stays still, you have told the defender nothing, because they are reading your body. Third, speed up out of it. The fake makes the gap, and your legs use it. If you don't burst away, the defender has time to recover.

If you learn one move, learn the head and shoulder fake. A sharp head turn one way, your shoulder dips with it, and your weight shifts. Then push off and go the other way. It costs nothing, it can't be poke-checked, and it works at every level.

Two more moves, and when each one fits.

The fake shot. Load up exactly as for a real shot, weight shifting and blade drawn back, then don't shoot. Defenders drop to block or turn their hips, and goalies commit to a save. Then pull the puck round them, or pass. It only works if your real shot is a threat. In a CARHA league, fake a wrist shot, never a slap shot, because a big slap-shot backswing is a penalty there.

The toe drag. Push the puck out on your forehand, offering it. Roll the blade over the puck so the toe is on the far side of it, and drag it back toward your feet. Then roll the blade back to catch it. That last part is the hard part, and it is where the move is usually lost. It works best against a defender who reaches early with their stick. And here is why it is a fairly safe move. Even a failed toe drag usually ends with the puck in your skates, where you can kick it back to your stick. That is a much better way to fail than most moves. But don't use it in a game until you can do it cleanly several times in a row in practice.

Let me pull open ice together. When the puck arrives in stride, keep striding. Push it ahead and to the side, and carry it rather than stickhandle. Let the puck and you take separate routes when a stick is in the way, but slip forward, never out of trouble. You can beat one player, not two. Use one move, not four. If you take a defender on, make them turn, change your speed, and sell one fake with your whole body. And remember the goal is a scoring chance, not beating the defender.

Fourteenth idea. Down low in their end.

Holding the puck deep in their end, behind their net or in their corner, has a different purpose. In your own end, you protect the puck to get it out. Down here, you protect it to make their defence move. Every second you keep it is a second they spend chasing instead of covering.

Keep your feet moving and the puck on your far side. A player who stops behind the net or in the corner gives the defender a still target to pin. Moving, you can turn either way.

Before anything else, look at the front of their net. A teammate there who has got free of their check is a better option than anything you can do on the boards.

If nobody is free there, the common approach is the cycle. Cut back up the boards you came down. That works because a defender who has committed toward the corner can't change direction as fast as you can. Make the turn while they're still too far away to reach you. Once they can reach you, it's too late. Go side-on to the boards instead. Never turn so you're facing the wall with them behind you. Or pass the puck back and down the boards to the teammate arriving behind you, and then get away to a new spot rather than stand and watch.

The alternative is to walk out. Say the defender turns their back to the play, or commits too far toward the boards. Then turn off the boards into the space they gave up, and take it to the net. Play the puck there, never the goalie. A goalie watching the puck can't see you coming or brace for you, and contact can hurt them. It can also be a penalty in every league, and cost you far more than two minutes. That is the moment a cycle is meant to create, and it doesn't last.

It is still a battle on the boards, so the position doesn't change. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the checker, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly.

How long your team keeps the puck low before it goes to the net or the point is a team choice. Some teams want it at the net at the first chance. Others want to wear the defence down first. Ask your bench which yours plays.

Fifteenth idea. Bad pucks.

Most pucks you get in a game aren't perfect, and handling them well does more for keeping the puck than any deke. Two things to know.

First, a bad puck still needs the blade tilted over the top of it and a soft give as it arrives, whether it is bouncing, on its edge or flat. It is an open blade that a puck jumps over. Let a bouncing or rolling puck settle before you do anything with it. One extra moment to flatten it is quicker than chasing your own bad touch.

Second, a puck on your backhand under pressure is often the right puck. If the checker is on your forehand side, your backhand is the far side, and the far side is where the puck belongs. So tilt the blade over the puck on your backhand as hard as on your forehand.

Sixteenth idea. How to practise, and the hands to build.

Puck handling is the one hockey skill you can really improve without ice. Take that chance.

You need a smooth surface, like a shooting pad or plastic tiles, and something to handle. Get the surface first, or the puck won't slide. A golf ball or a tennis ball costs nothing and builds fast hands. Never train only with a weighted puck or stick, or you build a stroke that only works with weight.

A little and often beats a lot once in a while. One coaching publisher's advice is ten to fifteen focused minutes every day, rather than two hours once a week. That is one source's guidance, not a measured comparison, but it fits how skills are generally thought to settle in, so it is worth following. Leave the pad and a ball where you walk past them. The practice you actually do beats the practice you planned.

Now, what to work on. Start with your grip. Hold the stick firmly enough that it can't escape, and softly enough that you aren't crushing it. Here is why. A squeezed hand can't feel, and if you can't feel the puck, you can't play with your head up. If your hands feel dead, check your bottom hand first. That is one coach's tip, not a count.

Let your top hand do the steering. It opens and closes the blade over the puck. Your bottom hand stays loose, so the shaft slides through it.

Tilt the blade over the top of the puck, on your forehand and your backhand, so the puck sits in the pocket instead of rolling up the blade and over. Rolling your top wrist is what changes the tilt from forehand to backhand, and that wrist roll is the heart of stickhandling. And let the blade give as the puck arrives, so it deadens instead of bouncing off. Catch it like an egg.

Move the puck with your wrists, not big arm swings. Here is why. Arm swings are slow, a defender can see them coming, and they pull you off balance. And you can stop a wrist move halfway, but you can't stop an arm swing halfway.

Practise both wide and tight. Wide means big sweeping pulls outside your feet, for open ice and for setting up a move. Tight means small, quick touches near your skates, for traffic, the boards and the slot. Players who only handle wide get poke-checked in traffic. Players who only handle tight can never move a defender.

And go front to back, not just side to side. Here is why. Side to side alone is a rhythm, and a defender who has watched it for three seconds knows exactly when the puck will be on your backhand. So work the whole circle round you, in front, both sides, and behind your heels. Front to back is also what makes the toe drag possible. Keep the puck moving, too. A puck sitting still on a still blade is the easiest thing in hockey to take.

Then do all of it with your eyes up. Stickhandle facing a wall with words or numbers on it, and read them out. If you can't read them, your head is down. Scatter a dozen pucks or balls and handle through them without touching any. And play keep-away against another person. Nothing shows up a player who watches the puck faster than someone trying to take it.

Last, bring it to the ice. Off-ice hands aren't on-ice hands until you have used them under pressure. The first time you take them onto the ice, expect a small step backwards while you get used to skates and a puck that slides much more freely. That is normal. In every practice, do the same movements on skates, and get into small-area games, like keep-away or cross-ice, anything in a tight space. Small games force the tight handling and protection that matter, at game speed, against real sticks.

Let me pull it all together.

Play with your head up, every time you have the puck. Build the feel, so your eyes can come up on their own.

Keep your body between the checker and the puck. Puck on the far side, hands away from your body, low and wide, feet moving. Take contact, don't give it. In a game without body checking, hold your spot and never lean, bump or drive into anyone. In a CARHA league, stay out of the contact altogether.

On the boards, skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the checker, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly.

Know your out before you get to the puck, because protection buys a second, not a minute.

Look for the pass first. In your own end, through the middle, or as the last player back, don't stickhandle. Off the glass, never over it.

Free hand high on your own stick, braced, never holding, never on the cage. Trap a loose puck and play it. Kick the puck, never a player. And a puck that pops up at your own net, knock it straight down.

In open ice, keep your speed and carry the puck. If you take a defender on, make them turn, change your speed, and sell one fake with your whole body. And remember the goal is a scoring chance, not beating the defender.

Much of this depends on what your team plays. So here are three questions to ask your coach. How long do you want me holding the puck on the boards? How much do you want me carrying the puck, and how much passing? And on a one-on-one, which shoulder do you want me to attack? And one for your league. Is body checking allowed in our games?

Go and ask. Their answers tell you how your team wants you to handle the puck, and now you know why each answer makes sense.

### 7. Departures from the source, the template and the remake

- **Stricter than the source, in two contact places.** (a) The free-hand brace: the source lets you lean the bar in to hold ground in a checking game (PH :511). The script says "don't lean the bar into them" everywhere and adds "never lay the shaft across them", for brief items 2, 6 and 7. (b) Side-on: the source says "hip and backside into the pressure" and "absorb the contact" in a checking game (PH :449, :468). The script says "you take contact, you don't give it… don't lean, bump or drive into them" unconditionally, so that no condition licenses a lean (brief items 1 and 2).
- **"Can be a penalty"** for holding a caught puck, covering it or smothering it. USA Hockey's held catch is a stoppage, not a penalty (PH :338), so "is a penalty" would have been harsher than the source.
- **Two terms defined without a PH line**: "dangling" and "the crease" (PH :359 delegates the crease to Rink Map).
- **One additional source, BC**, for four limbs the brief requires that the source page does not carry: the league gate as a standalone instruction, stick on the puck not hands or body, the numbers limb, and the goalie limb on the walk-out.
- **The source gives no "why" for how much carrying a team wants** (PH :917). The script says what and how, names the default, and stops with "ask what yours wants", as the plan's A.2 requires.
- **Template shape, adapted for a technique page.** Four big ideas and a recap, then three parts by situation rather than by rink zone: battles in tight, open ice at speed, and their end plus practice. Recaps follow the spine, the battles in tight and open ice. The last two parts have no recap of their own; the close covers them. Sixteen ideas. The close asks three coach questions and one league question, not the template's five and two.
- **Spine at 25.3%**, against the template's 23% and the plan's "within the first quarter". Hip lock and the hold-time choice were moved out of the spine into the fifth idea to get it there.
- **Against the remake:** "he/his" replaced throughout; the CARHA slap-shot injury tariff, the skate push-off, the shot-blocker and pin limbs gone; the "USA Hockey safety guide" attribution and the spine mechanism gone (owner rule on aware-not-frightened); hip lock, the hold-time reasons and the checker gate added.

### 8. What I found unclear in the source

- ~~KT3 (PH :1129) is broader than the body under CARHA.~~ **Withdrawn. The 21 October reviewer ruled that this is not a source defect:** CARHA 49(a) is itself an offence of intentional contact, so a bump penalised under it that injures is intentional contact that injures, and the mandatory major applies. KT3 is consistent with PH :478.
- **PH :302 and KT8 say "five of the six books throw you out and keep you out until a hearing"**, while PH :317 lists the match penalty under five books including CARHA, scoped to CARHA-affiliated leagues only. Consistent, but a reader of the takeaway alone does not see the CARHA scope.
- **The brace (PH :496, :511)** says "lean it in to hold the ground you have" and also "never a push". The line between leaning a two-handed shaft into a player and a cross-check is the source's thinnest safety line, and Hockey Canada and CARHA write no force into the cross-check (PH :526). The script resolves it the strict way; the page may want to as well.
