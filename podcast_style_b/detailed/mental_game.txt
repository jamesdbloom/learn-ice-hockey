===== SCRIPT START =====

Before anything else. If you are in crisis right now, any thought of harming yourself or of suicide, stop listening to this and get help now, tonight.

Call your local emergency number. That is nine nine nine in the UK and Ireland, one one two across the EU, and nine one one in the US and Canada. In the UK and Ireland you can also call Samaritans, free, at any hour, on one one six, one two three. In the US and Canada, call or text nine eight eight. This is not something to weigh up. Call someone tonight.

Now, the hockey. This episode is about the mental side of the game. It takes its time. We will build it up one piece at a time, and for each piece I will tell you what to do and also why it works. If you know why something works, you can still use it on a night that feels different from the last one.

Start with what the mental side can and can't do. Your top speed and your shot barely change between Saturday and Sunday. Your attention, and how you handle a mistake, can change a lot. So on a bad night when you are healthy and rested, the cause is more likely to be in that second list. That is a sensible guess, not a measured finding. But it is why the mental side is worth training.

It has limits, though. It won't make you faster. It won't fix a bad first step, or a wrist shot with no weight behind it. It closes the gap between your worst game and your best. It does not raise your best, and it is no replacement for skill work.

Why does it matter so much in hockey? Because the game never stops, and the play changes every two or three seconds. If you spend four seconds on the pass you just missed, you have missed about two more decisions. And you are tired when it matters. The last ten seconds of a shift, when your legs are gone, test your decisions hardest, and that is when a trained habit has to do the thinking for you.

Before any instructions, a few words you will hear all the way through. I will explain them now, so they don't get in the way later.

A shift. The short spell you spend on the ice before your line changes and you sit on the bench.

A turnover. Losing the puck to the other team.

The forechecker. A player pressuring the other team in that team's end, to win the puck back. Backchecking is the opposite. It means skating back hard to defend.

The slot. The ice right in front of the net, between the faceoff circles.

Your gap. The space between you and a puck carrier skating at you.

The boards. The wall around the rink. The glass is the clear panels on top of it. And the checker is the other player coming at you along the boards, whether they are after the puck or just coming at you.

A shoulder check. A quick look over your shoulder, to see what is behind you.

That is the vocabulary. Now the job.

I will start with four big ideas, because they matter most. If you only listen to the first part of this episode, take those four with you. After that we will go through the rest, from the night before a game to the end of a season.

First idea. After every mistake, run the same reset, and name your next job before your skates hit the ice.

Picture it. You lose a battle for the puck, or fan on a shot, or get beaten wide, and the play doesn't stop. Within two seconds the puck is somewhere else and you have a new job. If you are still holding on to the last two seconds, you aren't doing the new one.

Then you sit down on the bench, and the real cost lands. There is a theory about why. Worry eats into the part of your mind you use to block out distractions and to move your attention from one thing to the next. So a mind still re-running a turnover has less left for what the bench is for. That means watching their defence under pressure, hearing your coach's change, and seeing who you are out against next. So one bad shift becomes two.

That is a theory, not a measured finding, and it wasn't tested on hockey players.

A mistake you carry costs you twice. Once when it happens, and again while you replay it and the play moves on. The reset is how you put it down.

Build it with four steps, always in the same order, until it runs without you thinking about it.

Step one. A physical trigger, one small movement that is always the same. Tap both shin pads with your stick, squeeze your stick once, or adjust your right glove. It doesn't matter which. It matters that it is always the same, and that it is something you do. That makes the reset an action, not a mood you wait for.

Step two. One long breath out. Not a big breath in, but a long, slow breath out.

Here is why. A slow breath out is what calms your body down after a spike. The research backs that effect on the body, from a very large review of studies. It is less clear that it calms worry. And it hasn't been shown to make your next pass better. So use it to change how you feel, not to play better.

Step three. A cue word. One or two words, said in your head, pointing at what you are about to do. Not at what you just did. Next. Feet. Inside. Wall. Talking to yourself like this does help, by a moderate amount. The research backs that too.

Step four, and this is the one that matters. Name your next job in one plain sentence, before your skates hit the ice. Get above the puck. Gap early. First pass up the wall.

The first three steps are there to make the fourth one possible. A plan pushes the replay out of your head.

That exact four-step order is coaching know-how. No study has tested it as a sequence. Each step has some support of its own, and almost every coach teaches something like it.

Now one thing not to do. Don't try to force the thought out. Telling yourself not to think about the mistake tends to backfire. Pushing a thought away takes effort. That effort uses up attention, and the thought often comes straight back anyway.

So instead, notice it. Let it be there. And move your attention to your next job anyway. Something like, I made a bad read, and now I'm going to win this wall battle. That works better than, stop thinking about the bad read.

The research on this kind of acceptance is thin. The studies are few and small, and none of the effects is clearly bigger than nothing. But it still beats fighting the thought, so use it.

Second idea. On the bench, recover, watch, and plan one thing. Never replay the mistake.

You have three jobs on the bench, and none of them is going over what went wrong.

Recover. Get your breathing back down.

Watch. The bench is the best seat in the rink for reading the game. Look for which of their defencemen panics when your team forechecks, whether their centre is cheating up the ice for offence, and which of their forwards has stopped backchecking.

Plan one thing. Decide the single thing you'll do next shift, in that same plain sentence. And as your change comes, glance at the clock and the score. Protecting a one-goal lead late in a game asks for different risks from chasing one.

Working out your mistake is a job for after the game. It helps then, and it hurts during. If something won't let go, park it on purpose. Tell yourself, I'll look at that after.

Third idea. Decide early and commit. Simple in your own end, creative in theirs.

This idea is really about the fear of making mistakes. It is the most damaging fear in the game, because it doesn't look like fear. It looks like a careful, quiet, tidy player who never does anything wrong.

Here is what actually happens. You are scared of a mistake, so you play safe. And in practice, safe means late. You hold the puck an extra half-second to be sure. You turn down the pass in a tight spot. Then you chip the puck out anyway, once the forechecker has arrived.

Hesitating is a mistake too, and hockey punishes it fast. That half-second you take to be sure is all a forechecker needs to get to you. And a late chip, with a stick already on you, comes straight back.

So the careful player makes more turnovers, not fewer. And every one of them feeds the fear. That is the loop.

There is a slower cost too. A player who plays not to lose the puck stops trying the plays that would make them better. So they stop improving, they fall further behind, and the fear grows.

The chip itself is not the problem. Off the glass and out, chosen early when you are under pressure in your own end, is a good play. Off the glass, not over it. Simple in your own end is sound hockey. The same chip half a second late is a turnover.

So what do you do? The answer is not, be brave. Turn the vague fear into a specific target you can actually hit. Take your first touch away from pressure. Shoot within half a second when you get the puck in the slot. A target gives the fear something to do.

And accept some mistakes as the price of playing properly. Good coaches want mistakes from trying something, not from doing nothing. If yours doesn't, that tells you about your coach, not about you.

Fourth idea. Judge your night on the things you control.

There are two kinds of goal. An outcome goal is a result you don't fully control, like scoring tonight or winning. A process goal is something you do control. Keep your gap through the neutral zone, the middle of the rink. Take your first touch away from pressure. Shoot within half a second in the slot.

Here is why you score yourself on the second kind. You can't control whether the puck goes in. The screen, the deflection and the save aren't yours. You can control your route, your gap, your first touch, which shots you take, and whether your feet are moving.

And results are noisy. Say you judge yourself on, did I score. Then a night with four good chances and two posts feels like a failure. Your confidence takes a hit it hasn't earned. It was a good game. Judging what you did is steadier, because what you did is steadier.

There is a second reason. Worrying about the result makes you watch yourself. And watching yourself breaks skills that normally run on their own. Under pressure, a smooth skill turns slow and deliberate. That is the leading explanation of choking, and the research supports it. The main experiment was in golf putting, and it is one experiment, not a big pile of them. If you worry about the result, you end up watching yourself shoot. That is how you shoot badly.

But don't throw outcome goals away. Keep one on the list. Only one study has tested all three kinds of goal against each other. It was small, it wasn't in hockey, and it found that a mix of goals did best. So score yourself on your process goals, and keep the outcome goal in view. Just don't make it the verdict on your night.

Let me pull those four ideas together, because they are the core of it.

One. After every mistake, run the reset. A physical trigger, one long breath out, a cue word, and name your next job before your skates hit the ice. If the thought won't go, notice it and move on anyway.

Two. On the bench, recover, watch, and plan one thing. Leave the mistake for after the game.

Three. Decide early and commit. A simple play made on time beats a careful one made late.

Four. Judge your night on the things you control, and keep an outcome goal on the list.

Everything else in this episode adds to one of those four. So now let's build on them, starting with the goals themselves.

Fifth idea. Write your three process goals, and write them for your team's system.

Pick three process goals for your role, for the season. Three, and no more. Then score yourself on them after every game.

Here are some examples. A defenceman might pick keeping a good gap through the neutral zone. Or getting shots through from the point, rather than waiting for the perfect lane. The point is the area just inside the other team's blue line, where defencemen usually stand. A winger might pick making the first pass out of their own end up the boards. A centre might pick being the lowest forward in their own end, and getting above the puck on every turnover.

Now the part that varies. Those examples assume one particular way of playing. In your own end, that is a system called the low zone collapse. Up the ice, it is a forecheck that sends two forwards in deep.

Under man-on-man in your own end, you follow the player you are covering. So a centre may follow their player right up to the point. A winger's goal then becomes staying with their player, not holding the high slot, the top part of the slot. And under a forecheck that sends only one forward in deep, a winger holds their spot on the boards instead of arriving at the front of the net.

So ask your coach which system you play, and write your three after that.

Never put finish every check on that list. Finishing a check means carrying on with a hit on a player even after they have got rid of the puck. It doesn't belong, for two reasons.

The first reason. You may not be allowed to check at all. Body checking means using your body to separate a player from the puck. Whether you may do it is your league's call. Your age can rule it out completely. And being old enough doesn't settle it either, because some categories don't allow it at any age. In Britain, the answer is in your own competition's rules, not the main rulebook. So ask your league, and get the answer in writing.

The second reason. Even where you may check, hitting a player who has already passed or shot is a late hit. Never start a check after the puck has gone. And never hit a player who can't see you coming, because even a light hit from behind could cause a serious head or neck injury. If you can see their numbers, the numbers on their back, pull up and play the puck.

So what can go on the list? Angling. Angling means steering a puck carrier with the route you skate, not by hitting them. You take away the middle of the ice and leave them only the boards side. It is allowed even where checking is not.

Here is how it stays legal, and safe. Pick your line early, hold it, and add nothing when you arrive. Stick on the puck. No bump, no lean, no shove, and never your stick across them. If you leave your skating lane to make contact, some leagues count that as a body check. In some, just not avoiding contact counts too. Never into anyone's back, and if you can see their numbers, pull up. Never take a player's skates off the ice. And even going slowly, a crash into the boards can hurt.

So, angle every carrier off the middle works as a process goal in any league. Ride them off the puck does not.

Sixth idea. In a slump, shoot more, not less.

A slump is a run of games where nothing goes in for you. This trap is hard to see from the inside. A forward stops scoring, so they get careful. They wait for a better chance. They don't want to waste shots. So they shoot less.

Here is why that makes it worse. Fewer shots means fewer rebounds, fewer deflections, and fewer of the ugly goals that end a slump. It also means fewer chances in total, so the drought lasts longer, even if they are scoring at the same rate. And they start carrying the puck into trouble, looking for the perfect play. That leads to turnovers, and the turnovers hurt their confidence even more.

To be straight with you, no study has shown that players in a slump really do shoot less. That part is coaching know-how. But the advice holds either way.

So set a target for shot attempts. Five a game, or whatever suits your role. Hit it whether or not anything goes in. You are rebuilding your chances on purpose.

Then change what counts as success. If a goal is the only thing that counts, you'll collect almost nothing. Add things you can actually collect. A clean entry into their zone. A shift that ends in their end of the rink.

And help in other ways. A forward who isn't scoring can work hardest on the forecheck, or be the first one back. That helps the team, and it rebuilds confidence where the feedback comes straight away. On the forecheck and the backcheck, that work goes on the puck, not the player. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call. Never hit a player who can't see you coming, and if you can see their numbers, pull up.

Now, where confidence actually comes from, because it tells you how to rebuild it. There are four sources, and the order matters more than the list.

The strongest, by a distance, is having actually done the thing. Not imagining it, and not being told you can, but doing it.

Second, watching someone like you do it. A teammate at your level doing it helps more than the best player on the ice doing it.

Third, being told. That is the weakest of the three. And there is a catch. Being put down knocks confidence further than being encouraged lifts it. So one dismissive comment can outweigh five compliments. What you avoid saying to a struggling teammate matters most.

Fourth, how you read your own body. A racing heart before a big game doesn't set your confidence. Whether you read it as, I'm ready, or as, I'm falling apart, does.

So confidence comes after preparation and evidence. Talking yourself up does help, by a moderate amount. But with no real successes behind it, it is a thin layer over nothing.

Here is how confidence falls apart. It rarely fades evenly. It collapses in one narrow spot and leaks outward. You fan on a shot, so you stop shooting, so you handle the puck less, so your touch goes. Now you feel bad at hockey, not just at one shot. Being tired makes that worse, and so does comparing yourself with the player who took your spot.

So rebuild the thing you lost. In practice, do reps of the exact thing that went. Not general shooting. The exact shot you've been missing, until you have twenty good ones. That is the strongest lever you have, because it gives you the strongest kind of confidence. You have done it.

And don't add more analysis. Thinking hard about a skill you already know makes it worse, because you start watching yourself. Do the technical work in practice, and play on instinct in games.

Seventh idea. Switch your focus on triggers. Nobody concentrates for sixty minutes.

Here is a way to picture attention. It can be wide or narrow. And it can point outward at the game, or inward at your own thoughts. That gives four kinds of focus, and hockey needs all four in a single shift.

Wide and outward is taking in the whole ice, which you need before the puck comes to you or when a rush is coming at you. Narrow and outward is locking onto one thing, like a pass you are catching or the spot you are shooting at. Wide and inward is thinking and planning, which is for the bench, a whistle, or between periods. Narrow and inward is one cue word, or the feel of one movement, in the last half-second before a faceoff or a shot.

So the skill isn't being focused. The skill is switching. A defenceman who stays locked on the puck carrier through a three-on-two, three attackers against two defenders, stops seeing the trailer, the extra attacker arriving late. A forward who keeps looking around as the pass arrives won't catch it. The switches are the game.

That is why nobody concentrates for a whole game, and nobody needs to. What good players have is reliable moments when their focus snaps back. Tie those moments to triggers. Going over the boards for your shift. A whistle. The puck going behind your net. Each time, snap back up, take in the whole ice, and check over your shoulder.

Now, puck-watching. Puck-watching is standing still, eyes locked on the puck, while an opponent nobody is covering sits behind you. Coaches name it as the most common defensive mistake below the professional level, though nobody has counted it. It usually gets blamed on effort. It isn't an effort problem. It's an attention problem, and seeing that is what lets you fix it.

Here is why it happens. The puck is the brightest, fastest thing on the ice, and it is where the danger seems to be. So your attention locks onto it. In the four kinds of focus, you are stuck in narrow and outward when you need wide and outward. And nerves make it worse, because worry eats the very switching you need to pull your eyes away. A nervous player puck-watches more.

So the fix isn't, try harder. It's a shoulder check tied to those triggers, practised until it's a habit.

Now cue words, in a bit more detail. A cue is a very short instruction that forces the right focus at the right moment. Keep it to one or two words. Anything longer arrives too late.

Point it at the ice, the puck, the target or the other player, rather than at your own body. Drive the ice away, rather than straighten your leg.

That is the usual advice, but it is contested. A recent re-check of the research found the benefit close to nothing, once biased studies were allowed for. It costs nothing, and there is no sign it hurts, so do it. But if a cue about your body is the only way you can feel a correction, use it.

And match the cue to the job. For precise things, like placing a shot or your grip on a faceoff, use an instruction. Far post. Stick down. For hard, effortful things, like a battle for a loose puck or the end of a long shift, a fired-up word is a sensible choice. Go. Work. The research shows instructions help on precise jobs. On the effortful ones it doesn't name a winner. And self-talk did more for new skills than for ones you already do without thinking.

Let me pull that part together. Write three process goals for your role, once you know your team's system. Never put finish every check on the list. Angle carriers off the middle with your route. Stick on the puck, no bump, no lean, no shove, never into anyone's back, and if you see their numbers, pull up. In a slump, shoot more, and do reps of the exact thing you lost. And switch your focus on triggers, with a shoulder check.

Now, before the game.

Eighth idea. Nerves and worry are different things. Use the first, and deal with the second.

Nerves are your body getting ready. Heart rate up, quick breathing, a hollow stomach, needing the toilet. They fit the size of the game, and they usually settle inside your first shift. Nerves are a good sign. A player with no nerves before a big game is often flat. So nerves need managing, not getting rid of.

Anxiety is the worry. Going over everything that could go wrong. What if I'm terrible tonight. It doesn't go away when the puck drops. And it is the thing that eats your ability to block out distractions and switch your focus. Anxiety needs dealing with.

If the worry is there on Wednesday about Saturday's game, or has stopped being about hockey at all, that isn't a hockey problem. It needs someone qualified, not a mental skill.

So, the nerves. First, label them. Standing on the blue line for the anthem with your heart going, say to yourself, that's my body getting ready. Not, I'm nervous. Same feeling, different label.

Here is why. Telling people that the signs of stress are their body getting ready improves how they perform. That is one of the sturdier findings in this topic, though most of it comes from exams rather than sport. It costs nothing, and it fits the fourth source of confidence. How you read the feeling matters more than the feeling.

Second, breathe out longer than you breathe in. In for four, out for six, six to ten times. The long breath out is the part that does the work. It calms your body. That it calms your worry is less well shown, and it hasn't been shown to make you play better.

Third, know that there is no single best level for everyone. You will hear that performance is an upside-down U, with too little energy making you flat, too much making you fall apart, and the best in the middle. The picture is useful, but the original work said it depends on the task. Simple, well-learned tasks tended to get better as energy went up, and only hard or unfamiliar ones showed the upside-down U.

So instead of aiming for the middle, think about two things. Your best level is your own, and it may not be calm. Some players play best fired up and a bit angry. Some play best calm. Don't copy a teammate. Find yours.

And it depends on the job. Simple, physical jobs, like a battle for a loose puck or a hard shift, cope fine with you fired up. Being fired up changes your energy, not what you may do. It is still the puck, not the player. Precise or new jobs, like a shootout attempt, or a set play your team only learned on Tuesday, want you calmer.

Here is how to find your level. It takes about eight games. After each one, write two lines. How you felt beforehand. Flat, edgy, calm, angry or distracted. And how you actually played. Patterns show up fast, and they often surprise people. Some players play best a little irritated. Some find their calm, focused nights are their worst.

Then use the warm-up to get there, in either direction. If you're flat, short hard bursts, quick feet, loud talk, hard shots. If you're over the top, long smooth skating, easy passes, slow breaths out, less talking.

One more picture, for when a game goes badly wrong. Under heavy worry, performance doesn't fade gently. It can fall off a cliff. That is the spell where a team gives up three goals in six minutes. And getting back takes more than calming down a little. You have to come right down, well below the point where you fell.

That is the case for a coach calling a timeout, changing lines, or having the goalie freeze the puck. And it is the case for you taking a full, deliberate reset, not just calming down a bit. That picture comes from a very small study of eight basketball players. So treat it as a useful way to think, not a proven fact.

Ninth idea. A pre-game routine that runs in any rink.

A routine is there to make your state the same every week, so the only thing that changes is the game.

Routines work. The research supports that. A large review of studies found that routines before a performance help, and they helped under pressure too. It didn't find that age or skill level made a difference, which is a reason not to think routines are only for experts. What nobody can tell you is which parts of a routine do the work. So build one, and don't believe anyone who says exactly which steps are essential.

Routines and superstitions look the same from outside. Here is the test. If you couldn't do it, would it wreck you?

A routine is what you do for its effect. A warm-up that gets your body warm. Breathing that settles you. If the room is different or you arrive late, you squeeze it down, and you're fine.

A superstition is what you do because of what you fear will happen if you don't. Same socks. Same tape job. Tapping the goalie's pads a set number of times. It is harmless on its own. But it makes your calm depend on something you don't control, and one day the socks will be in the wash.

So keep the routine, and cut anything that would break you if you couldn't do it. In an away rink, a different room, or on an odd schedule, your routine still has to run.

Here is a timeline that works. The exact times are coaching know-how, so adapt them to your league and your travel.

The night before, pack and check your kit, and get your skates sharpened if they need it. Sleep. Sleep has better evidence behind it than anything else in your routine. And don't cram tactics late at night. Going over systems at eleven at night doesn't make them stick.

Your last full meal is about three hours before.

Arrive an hour to an hour and a half before, at the same point every week if you can. Rushing is an easy way to arrive wound up. Get dressed in the same order. That isn't a superstition. It is one less decision.

Twenty to thirty minutes before, warm up off the ice. Get your body warm with moving exercises, not holding stretches. This is where you push your level up or down.

In the on-ice warm-up, skate at full game speed at least twice. A warm-up done entirely at sixty percent teaches your body that tonight is a sixty percent night. Goalies, track the puck properly from the first shot.

In the last minute before the game, two or three slow breaths out. And one thought about the game, about something you'll do. Not, we need this one. Something like, first pass up the wall, or, gap early.

Between periods, the same thing, shorter. Recover, make one change, take one cue. And don't spend the break on the referee.

Let me pull the before-the-game part together. Nerves are your body getting ready, so call them that. Breathe out longer than you breathe in. Your best level is your own, so track it over about eight games, and use the warm-up to push yourself up or down. Keep a routine that runs in any rink, and cut anything that would wreck you if it were missing. And skate at game speed in the warm-up.

Now the harder parts.

Tenth idea. Fear, and how to work on it.

Fear is common in hockey and rarely talked about. Nobody has measured how common, so don't trust any figure you hear. If you're scared of something on the ice, you aren't soft, and it isn't a flaw in your character. It is a normal reaction to a fast game with hard surfaces, and you can work on it.

There are four kinds, and they are four different problems.

Fear of contact. Being hit, or hitting. It is common in players who started late, players coming back after time away, and anyone who has just moved up a level. Body checking isn't allowed in non-checking hockey, so ask your league which kind it plays. But body contact, like battles, pins and collisions, is in every version of the game.

Fear of injury. This one is usually specific, and usually earned. Someone with a bad shoulder is afraid of that shoulder, not of hockey, most of all in the first few games after it heals.

Fear of the puck. Flinching when you block a shot, turning away from a shot from the point, not wanting to be in the slot when the puck is coming.

Fear of making a mistake. That is the careful, tidy player from the third idea. It is the most damaging of the four, and the least visible.

For physical fear, the approach that works is called graded steps. Do the thing you're afraid of, a little at a time, at a level where you stay in control. Only move up when that level feels boring.

The evidence for this with fear and anxiety in general is strong. Using it for fear of contact in hockey is coaching know-how. But it does follow the order Canadian and American hockey use to teach checking. Positioning and angling first, then stick checks, then body contact, and body checking last. Those are ways to teach a skill, not tested treatments for fear.

Technique lowers fear more than courage does. A lot of the fear of contact is a fair fear of getting hurt, because you don't know how to take a hit. So learn it. This is the part that keeps you safe, so I'll go slowly.

See the hit coming. Get your weight low, keep your feet moving, and keep your head up with your chin off your chest. Come into the boards on an angle, not straight on.

If the contact is coming anyway, get your skates parallel to the boards, feet apart and knees bent, with your forearm and hip to the boards. Your shoulder can turn toward the checker. The boards are what it should not meet. Never the point of your shoulder into the boards or the glass, and not the whole shoulder if you can help it.

Head up and chin off your chest is the part you never give up. It is the half of getting low that players drop. Never turn your back to the checker. And never duck.

Both protect your head and neck. If you duck, your head meets the boards first, and a head-first hit like that can injure your neck even at walking speed. If you turn your back, the contact becomes a hit from behind. You can't see it coming or brace for it, and even a light hit from behind could cause a serious head or neck injury. So a slow checker doesn't make it safe.

If you are already facing the boards and can't turn parallel, that is the last resort, not a choice. Get your stick and gloves up and spread wide on the glass, with your head off it. And still never turn your back to the checker.

That position matters more than anything else in this part. From here on I'll use one short version, the same words every time. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the checker, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly.

Learn that, and the fear drops for a good reason.

Now the steps for contact. They might go like this. Leaning on a teammate who is standing still and ready for it, in a drill your coach runs. Then angling along the boards at half speed. Then one-on-one battles on the wall. Then the same at game speed. Your coach sets these up. Stay at each step until it bores you.

Once there is an opponent, in the angling and the battles, win with your feet, and your stick on the puck. No bump, no lean, no shove, and never your stick across anyone. Never into anyone's back, and if you can see their numbers, pull up. Never take a player's skates off the ice. Whether body checking is part of your game at all is your league's call. And on the boards, the short version. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the checker, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly. A crash into the boards can hurt even then.

For the puck, the same idea. Tennis balls first, then slow pucks, then real shots from a distance, then closer. And learn to block a shot properly, because it is close to the opposite of what instinct tells you.

How much your team blocks shots at all is a coaching choice. Ask your coach, and ask your goalie.

If you block, here is the whole of it, and it goes together. Head up and out of the shooting lane, the line the puck travels. Square to the shooter. On your feet, and close. Shin pads facing the shot. Hands tucked in, with the backs of your gloves facing out. Never turn your back. Never turn sideways. Never lift a leg. Each of those shows the puck a part of you with no protection, and turns you away from it. Head up and out of the lane is the one that never bends, whatever else your team asks of you.

Going down on the ice at a shot takes you out of the play, whether the block works or not. A timed slide to block a pass on a two-on-one is a different thing. If that is your team's plan, ask your coach how they want it done, rather than working it out at full speed. A player who was never taught to block a shot is right to be afraid of it.

And check your kit. Pads that don't fit, or are too thin, make you flinch, and you're right to flinch. A player flinching at shots from the point in thin shin pads isn't being silly.

Not all fear should be pushed through. Fear that says, this is really dangerous, is doing its job. A player out of control. A hit from behind toward the boards. Going into a corner with your head down while someone is arriving. The goal isn't to switch fear off. It's to stop it running in situations that are actually safe.

Coming back from an injury, all of this assumes you have been medically cleared to play. If you haven't, that comes first, and the decision isn't yours to make. Your mind usually takes longer to come back than your body. It's normal to be cleared and still be protecting the injury three weeks later. Come back through the same graded steps, not straight into a game. And mental health symptoms raise the risk of injury and slow recovery. So a fear that hangs on isn't just uncomfortable. It's a physical risk too, which is one more reason to come back slowly, through the steps.

Eleventh idea. Goalies, and how the team treats them.

Everything in this part is coaching know-how with a sensible reason behind it. It isn't research. The sources behind it are the weakest in the whole topic. Even for soccer goalkeepers, who have been studied more, researchers looked and found almost nothing tested.

If you play out, listen anyway, because most of it is about how you treat your goalie.

Here is why the position is different. A goalie's mistakes are the scoreboard, with nobody behind them to cover. After a goal, play stops and everyone looks at them, and they stand there with nothing to do but think about it. They can be idle for eleven minutes and then need a huge save. And they are often alone, training apart, with the only other goalie on the team competing for the net. Blame lands on them by default.

So, goalies, build a short memory. It's a routine, not a personality. Run the same routine after every goal, including the ones that weren't your fault. If you only run it after bad goals, it becomes a sign that you failed.

One way to do it. Take the puck out of the net yourself, or don't, but pick one and always do it. Skate a short loop out to the top of your crease, the painted area in front of your net, or to the corner. Move. Moving gives the reset a shape and breaks the frozen stance.

Water bottle, one long breath out, wipe your mask. Then say one plain sentence about the goal. Screen, didn't find it. And stop there. No judging, no blaming yourself. Go back to your crease, tap both posts, say your cue word, and set for the faceoff.

That one plain sentence, and then setting for the faceoff, are the whole thing. Saying it once, plainly, satisfies the urge to go over it, so your mind stops replaying the goal all period. The rest is just a frame for it.

A goalie's confidence comes from saves made. That is the strongest source of confidence again, having done it. After a bad night, a goalie should face lots of saveable shots in the next practice, before the hard ones. Lots of shots matters more than hard shots.

And if you play out, this part is mostly about you. Most teams get it wrong in one of two directions.

Don't pile on. A head shake, a stick slam, skating away after a goal, all of that reads as blame to your goalie, whether you meant it or not. And it doesn't stay between the two of you.

But don't over-console either. Skating over after every goal to say it wasn't their fault turns each goal into a big event. That is the opposite of what the short memory is for. The best default is normal. A tap on the pads on your way past, one word, and back to your job.

Include your goalie off the ice. In the dressing room, before the game, and socially. It costs nothing. And talk to them during play. The goalie is in the best spot on the ice to talk to the team, and giving them a job keeps them in the game through the quiet spells.

Twelfth idea. Keep your frustration to yourself, and off the officials.

After a bad call, skate away and run your reset.

Here is why. Your frustration, shown, hands the other team a power play, a spell where they have an extra player. And when they score on it, everyone blames the bad call, not you. The point isn't that you have to be nice. It's that your frustration costs your team.

Now what it costs, in plain words. Arguing a call costs you at least a penalty, and that usually becomes their power play. If you keep at the official after that, the penalty gets longer. If you keep going after that, you're thrown out of the game. In some leagues the referee doesn't have to go step by step, and can throw you out much sooner.

Some things skip the climb. In most leagues an obscene gesture ends your game on the spot. Swearing at or abusing an official can get you thrown out too. So can a racist or other hateful slur at anyone. Spitting at anyone, or threatening an official, can get you thrown out and suspended. You don't have to touch an official to be thrown out.

Banging the boards or the glass with your stick in protest at a call is a penalty, and in some leagues it ends your game. Throwing anything onto the ice from the bench is a penalty too, and in some leagues that ends your game as well. A water bottle, a towel, even a stick thrown to a teammate who has lost theirs. Swearing at or abusing anyone, an opponent included, is a penalty. And never hit back physically.

Never put a hand on an official, however wrong the call. Not to argue, and not to pull free of a linesperson breaking up a fight. In every league that can end your game, and in many it brings a suspension as well. Treat any hand on an official as ending your game.

Let me pull the harder parts together. Beat physical fear with technique and graded steps, not courage. On the boards, the short version. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the checker, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly. Already facing the boards, stick and gloves up on the glass, head off it, as a last resort. And when you do go in, stick on the puck, not a hit, and never push or hit anyone from behind. Blocking a shot, head up and out of the lane. After a goal against, a tap on your goalie's pads and back to work. And after a bad call, skate away. Never put a hand on an official.

Now the people around you, and the long season.

Thirteenth idea. Coaches, ice time and criticism.

Treat feedback as two things, what was said and how it was said. Whether it is true and useful is the message. Whether it was fair, kind and well-timed is the delivery.

They are separate. A coach who shouts you're standing still across the bench said it badly, and may be completely right. If you throw out the message because you resent the messenger, you lose the information.

And the reverse matters too. Nice delivery doesn't make advice correct. A lot of coaching advice is a team's preference dressed up as a rule of hockey. Wingers never go below the goal line is a system choice, not a law. Ask which system, and ask why.

And there's a line. Being demanding isn't the same as being abusive. Criticism of your play is coaching. Putting you down personally, humiliating you in front of the group again and again, or anything that leaves you dreading the rink, is not. Don't put up with it quietly. Take it to your club's safeguarding lead if it has one, another club official, the league, or the governing body.

When you aren't getting the ice time you want, start with an honest point. Most players think they deserve more ice time than they get. Nobody has measured that, but it can't be true of everyone. You remember your good shifts, and you miss the plays where you were beaten, because you were behind them. That doesn't mean your coach is right. It means you aren't a reliable judge of your own case.

So ask one specific question, in private, a day or two later. Not on the bench, not straight after the game, and not in front of the group. What do I need to do to get on the second power play? A vague question like, why am I not playing more, gets a vague answer and sounds like a complaint.

Then work on the answer where the coach can see it, in practice, not just in the few shifts you get.

Be excellent in the minutes you do get. Playing really well in four minutes tends to get you six. Sulking through four tends to get you two. That is coaching know-how, but close to universal.

And keep your legs moving on the bench. A cold player who goes over the boards and gets beaten has just proved the coach right.

Some benchings are about things you can't control. If you've asked the specific question, done the work, and nothing has changed over a real stretch of time, moving teams is a fair answer, not a failure.

Fourteenth idea. Your mood is not private.

When you slump on the bench, throw your head back or bang your stick, you aren't quietly dealing with a bad shift. You are broadcasting it. Your linemates read it in about half a second, as one of three things. The coach is wrong. My linemate let me down. Or, this game is gone. None of those helps them on the next shift. And a stick slammed on the boards or the glass can be a penalty of its own.

Here is what the research says. Moods spread between players. Coaches' moods are linked to players' moods and to results. And closeness and status matter, so a senior player's body language carries further than a fourth-liner's. How strong the effect is hasn't been measured.

That doesn't mean faking enthusiasm. Nobody believes that anyway. It means a neutral floor. Whatever you feel, what people see is upright, eyes on the ice, and in the game. Feel what you feel. Don't broadcast it.

On a bad night, say something useful, and it is usually about the next shift. Get above me next time and I'll hit you with the pass, is useful. You've got to be there, is not.

When a teammate makes a mistake, say nothing, or one word of support. The head shake costs more than their mistake did.

If it was your turnover, say that's on me, once, and then stop. That ends the conversation. Saying it again and again makes everyone manage your feelings instead of playing.

And remember, your bad night isn't the main event. Seventeen other people came to play. On a night when you have nothing, your job is not to make it their problem.

Fifteenth idea. Getting through a season.

Improvement isn't a straight line. You usually climb fast early on, then hit long flat stretches, broken by sudden jumps. The flat stretches are normal. But they tend to arrive just as your early excitement wears off, which is what makes them hard.

Here is what a flat stretch usually is. Often a skill is becoming automatic. That doesn't feel like progress, but it is what makes the skill work under pressure. And sometimes it really is a limit of your technique, and you need a new way of doing it, not more of the same.

So measure something that moves. Wins, points and ice time can sit still for months. Your process goals move week to week.

Practice matters. It is a big factor, and it is the one you control. But hours on their own don't decide how good you get, so two players with the same ice time can improve at very different speeds.

Mid-season, with early mornings and cold rinks, motivation becomes habit. Turn up because it's Tuesday, not because you feel like it. Take a real off-season, because hockey twelve months a year is a good way to stop wanting to play. Breaks are part of recovering, not slacking off. And keep one technical thing you're working on, so a bad night still has something in it for you.

And watch for wanting to play turning into feeling you have to. That's an early sign of burnout. If it doesn't go away, take it seriously, and talk to someone qualified.

Let me pull that part together. Separate the message from the delivery. If you're short of ice time, ask one specific question in private, then do the work where the coach can see it. Criticism of your play is coaching. Humiliation is not, so take that to someone at your club or league. On the bench, hold a neutral floor, and talk about the next shift. Expect flat stretches, measure something that moves, and take a real off-season.

Last idea. When it isn't a hockey problem.

This episode is about playing better. It is not a replacement for care, and some of what looks like a mental-game problem isn't one.

If it has spread beyond the rink, it isn't a hockey problem. It needs a qualified professional, and looking for one isn't a lack of toughness.

Here are the signs it's time to talk to someone qualified, rather than learning more mental skills. Anxiety that starts well before game day, or doesn't settle once play starts. Low mood, losing interest, or hopelessness that lasts for weeks. Sleep that is disrupted all the time, or a big change in appetite. Dreading the rink, when you used to enjoy it. Problems that have spread into work, school, friendships or family. That is the clearest sign, because a real hockey problem stays at the rink. Using alcohol or anything else to manage how you feel about playing. And any thought of harming yourself or of suicide. That one is not something to weigh up.

The main international statement on athletes' mental health says depression and anxiety are about as common in top athletes as in everyone else. It also says eating disorders and disordered eating are more common in athletes. Hockey isn't usually a sport where you make a weight, but roster weights and body targets do happen, so that matters here too. And it says mental health and physical health can't be separated.

For anything less urgent, talk to your GP or family doctor, or to a qualified clinical or sport psychologist. They aren't the same job. If the problem has left the rink, you probably want a clinical psychologist. A lot of national federations and leagues now have mental health support you can use.

And if you are thinking about harming yourself or about suicide, stop listening and get help now.

If you're in immediate danger, call your local emergency number. Nine nine nine in the UK and Ireland. One one two across the EU. Nine one one in the US and Canada. Or go to your nearest emergency department.

In the UK, call Samaritans, free, any hour of any day, on one one six, one two three. Or text the word shout to eight five two five eight.

In Ireland, call Samaritans on one one six, one two three, free, any hour. Or text the word hello to five zero eight zero eight.

In the US, call, text or chat with nine eight eight, any hour.

In Canada, call or text nine eight eight, any hour, in English or French. In Quebec you can also text five three five three five three, or call one eight six six, two seven seven, three five five three.

In many other European countries, one one six, one two three is the number for emotional support. And anywhere, find a helpline dot com lists a crisis line for each country.

Numbers change, so check your own country's line. But call someone tonight, not after the season.

None of this means you're failing at hockey. It means you have a problem that hockey advice isn't the right tool for.

Let me pull it all together.

After every mistake, run the reset. A physical trigger, one long breath out, a cue word, and name your next job before your skates hit the ice. On the bench, recover, watch, and plan one thing. Leave the mistake for after the game.

Decide early and commit. Simple in your own end, creative in theirs. A simple play on time beats a careful one made late.

Judge your night on three process goals for your role, and keep an outcome goal on the list. Never put finish every check on it. In a slump, shoot more, and rebuild with reps of the exact thing you lost.

Switch your focus on triggers, with a shoulder check. Call your nerves your body getting ready, and use the warm-up to set your level. Keep a routine that runs in any rink.

Beat physical fear with technique and graded steps. On the boards, the short version. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the checker, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly. Already facing the boards, stick and gloves up on the glass, head off it. And when you do go in, stick on the puck, not a hit, and never push or hit anyone from behind. Blocking a shot, head up and out of the lane.

Keep your frustration off your face and off the officials. And never put a hand on one.

Much of this depends on your team and your league. So here are three questions to ask your coach, and one for your league. Which system do we play, so I can write the right process goals? How much do you want me blocking shots, and how? And when I'm not playing much, what do I need to do to get more ice? And for the league. Which category do we play in, and is body checking allowed?

Go and ask. Their answers tell you where to aim all of this, and now you know why each part works.

And if it has spread beyond the rink, talk to someone qualified. And if you are thinking of harming yourself or of suicide, call your local emergency number or your crisis line, tonight.
