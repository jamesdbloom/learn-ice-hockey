This is pressure, so here is the rule for every bit of pressure in this episode. Stick on the puck, not a hit. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call. Building up speed into contact, even with two hard strides, can be charging. And if you see their numbers, pull up. Think about the retriever. They have turned to get the puck, so their back is to you and their head is over it. Arriving through them is checking from behind. If you hit a player from behind, plan on being thrown out of the game. If you drive them into the boards when they can't protect themselves, or the hit is reckless, in some leagues it is a match penalty. The whole point of the angle is that you take their escape away, so you never need to go through them.

Stay on the inside, between the puck and the middle of the ice. Then if they get away, it is along the boards, not into the slot. And lead with your stick, on the ice, in the passing lane you are taking away, before your body arrives.

The measure of a good chase isn't whether you got the puck. It is whether the retriever's only way out was one you had already covered.

Now who does what. The first forward to arrive is called F one, the next F two, and the third F three. Those are roles, not positions. Whoever gets there first is F one, centre or winger.

Under a common forecheck called the two-one-two, F one goes to the puck and takes away the easy escape. F one's job is to force a decision, not to win the puck. F two reads F one's angle and covers the side the puck was forced toward. F two is the one who usually collects it. F one creates, F two collects. Both jobs are pressure, so the same rule holds. Stick on the puck, not a hit, your league's call on hitting, and pull up on the numbers.

F three stays high, around the tops of the faceoff circles, guarding against a pass up the middle. If the puck gets out, F three is the first one back.

Other teams do it differently. In a one-two-two, F one pressures, but the other two hold higher and steer the puck to one side. Here is why a team plays it. You win fewer pucks deep, but you give up far fewer odd-man rushes, where they outnumber your defence coming back.

So find out which forecheck your team plays, because the same dump-in needs a different second player in each.

Let me pull the dump-ins together. Dump only to a place a teammate is already skating to, and pick the dump to what you see. Land a flip short of their goal line. Where there is a trapezoid, aim for the corners. Where there isn't, plan for the goalie to come out. Chase on an angle, stick first, and arrive on the puck. Never on a player's back, and if the goalie comes out for it, peel off.

Fourteenth idea. Carry, dump or delay. You have about half a second to decide, so read four things, in this order. First, the gap. If the defender is well off and backing in, carry it. They have given you the line, so take it and get to the faceoff dots. If it is a normal gap and closing, you need a move or an angle. Attack the outside shoulder, or chip it past them if they are committing. If it is tight, with their stick on you and them standing up at the line, a carry is a low-percentage play. Chip it, dump it, or delay.

Second, support. Two or more teammates at the line with you at speed, carry it or pass it in. One teammate arriving late, delay. A second of curl usually turns that into two. Nobody, dump it to a place someone can get to, or delay if anyone at all is coming. Only if truly nobody is coming do you put it in deep and change, and then you have already lost the shift.

Third, the score and the clock. Tied, or early on, play the odds and be aggressive. Protecting a one-goal lead late, a goal against costs you more than a goal for gains you. So the common choice is to dump it in, forecheck and burn the clock. Chasing a goal late, carry it almost whatever happens. A dump with ninety seconds left gives away a puck you can't afford to lose. On a line change, the entry matters less. Get it deep or out of danger, and change cleanly. How far the score moves this is your coach's call.

Fourth, who you are. A skilled line against a weaker pair should try a carry at almost any excuse. A fourth line against their top pair has a higher bar. But even that bar is well below only carrying when you are certain. Those bars come from one team's games, so use the shape, not an exact figure.

Here is the whole decision in a few lines. Carry if you have the line or the support. Chip it if they are committing. Delay if support is a second away, in open ice. Dump only to a place a teammate is already skating to. And never dump and change if you can help it.

Fifteenth idea. When the carry fails, the next few seconds decide what it cost. A failed carry is cheap only if the next few seconds go right. All of its cost is the rush coming back the other way. So know in advance what each of you does when the puck is lost at the line.

The carrier and the nearest forward pressure straight away. The defender who has just won it is usually facing their own net or the boards. Pressure with your stick on their puck, never a chop and never at the hands, and never into their back. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call, and if you see their numbers, pull up. A stick on the puck in the first second often wins it straight back, or forces a rushed clear.

The other forward gets above the puck, between it and your own net, in the middle of the ice. Then a quick pass up the middle has somebody to go through.

And your defencemen hold their gap rather than charge it. On the turnover they stop and skate backwards, instead of jumping at a puck they can't reach first.

Some teams send only one forward to pressure and have the other two get back straight away, especially late in a game or protecting a lead. Which your team plays is a coaching choice, so ask.

Sixteenth idea. Read what the defence puts in front of you. If they stand up at their blue line, they are holding the line, taking away the middle, and daring you to go round. So chip it past them and chase. Here is why. A defenceman standing still has no momentum to recover, so this is the perfect chip. Or attack the seam between the two of them, because two defenders holding a line always have a gap between them.

Or use a late player. A defence standing up has to watch the puck, so a fourth attacker arriving behind the play is often unmarked. Or change where you go in. Swing the puck to the far side, and go in where they aren't.

Against a one-three-one in the neutral zone, never skate into the three across the middle. That is the trap working. Flip it over them. Move the puck side to side quickly. The wall of three is strong facing forward but slow turning sideways. Attack the outside of the wall, because the trap gives you the boards to protect the middle. And hold a forward high, behind their three. That makes their last player stay back, which thins out the wall.

Against a defence that closes hard and early, chip it past them. A defender closing hard has committed their momentum, so this is the best chance there is for the chip. Get the puck off your stick a stride before they arrive. Use the space behind them, with a teammate skating into it. And change your speed. A hard slow-down and then a burst ruins a defender who is closing fast, because their speed is now working against them. That is coaching craft, not something anyone has measured.

Seventeenth idea. Power play entries. The power play is when your team has an extra player because of a penalty. Coaches say a bad power play usually fails not in its setup, but because it can't get into the zone. That is coaching emphasis, not a count.

The common approaches are coaching choices, not laws. Four players across the line, two wide and two inside the faceoff dots. Here is why. The carrier always has an option, and the penalty kill has to defend four threats.

Attack the middle first. Pushing the puck wide too early invites pressure and forces a chip.

Against a penalty kill that sits back, use the drop pass. Skate straight at the first penalty killer to freeze them, then drop it underneath into the space that makes.

Put the puck below their goal line on purpose, with your support already skating there. And only rim it if a teammate is on the far side to get it. Without that player, a rim is just a slow turnover.

In women's professional hockey tracking, a carry on the power play still produced about two and a half times the shots of a dump-in. But the gap was smaller than at even strength, because a kill with one player missing is worse at getting a dumped puck back. So control still matters most. But if your unit can't get in clean, a planned dump costs less there.

Let me pull the decisions together. Read the gap, then the support, then the score, then who you are. When the carry fails, the nearest two pressure with sticks, and one forward gets above the puck. Against a standing or hard-closing defence, chip it past. Against a trap, go over it or round it, never into it.

Eighteenth idea. Defending the entry. It is the same game from the other side. As a defender, your gap is the space between you and the puck carrier. Too much gap, and they come in with speed, get their head up, and make a play. Too little, and one move or one chip beats you, and now they are behind you. So shrink the gap the whole way to your line, and never let it grow.

The bigger idea is this. Your gap should be whatever lets you match their next change of speed. And skate backwards fast enough that you never have to turn and run. The moment you turn, the attacker chooses everything.

You don't have to stop the entry. You only have to make it uncontrolled. A stick in the passing lane that forces a dump has done the same job as a hit. In a league with no body checking, it is the only one of the two you have.

How you meet the entry is a system, and there are four you may hear about. The first is standing up at the line. You meet the entry at your own blue line rather than backing in. Here is why a team does it. It kills carries outright. If they can't cross with the puck, they can't get the shots a carry is worth. But if you are beaten, you are beaten badly. You are flat-footed at your own line, with a forward behind you and only your goalie left. It needs good reads, your forwards back, and a partner who slides across with you. Stepping up is pressure, so stick on the puck, not a hit. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call. And if you see their numbers, pull up.

The second has the first forward back pressure the carrier, while the defencemen hold their depth at the dots. The defencemen have to talk, and step on the pass across the ice. That forward's pressure is stick on the puck, not a hit, your league's call on hitting, and pull up on the numbers.

The third is the traditional one, often called receiving the entry. The defencemen stay roughly between the faceoff dots, mark the carrier and the slot, and steer the play to the outside. Here is why. It is very hard to beat cleanly, and it keeps the middle protected. The cost is that it lets them in. The attacker gets in with the puck, gets time on the wall, and can shoot looking for rebounds.

The fourth is called squash and slide. The defenceman, not the forward, forces the carrier to the outside, while the first forward back protects the slot. The two defencemen hold the same depth and slide across together, like a bar on a table football game. Here is why a team plays it. It wins back time and space. The cost is that it asks a lot of the forwards. One lazy backcheck, and the weak side is wide open.

Whichever one you play, you steer with your position and your stick. Stick on the puck, not a hit, and whether you may hit is your league's call. No shove, no lean, never your stick across anyone, never into a back, and never lift anyone off their feet. If you see their numbers, pull up. Even going slowly, a crash into the boards can hurt.

These are systems, not laws. Which one your team plays changes your whole job. Whether you step up, whether you hold the dots, and whether the first forward back takes the puck or the slot. So ask, and then talk to your partner about it before the puck drops.

Two more things for a defender. First, the offside rule works for you too. If you step up at exactly the right instant, you can make an onside attacker cross before the puck, which makes them offside. That step is still stick on the puck, not a hit. Whether you may hit is your league's call, and if you see their numbers, pull up.

Second, when you are getting the puck back on their delayed offside, keep your head up. The raised arm is not a shield. Where a whistle protects you at all, it protects the player with the puck, so that ends the moment you pass it. In some leagues there is no such whistle. And no whistle stops a body already coming at speed. Know where the forechecker is before you put your head down over the puck.

Let me pull it all together. Carry it in when you can. A failed carry costs less than it feels, and dump and change is the worst play there is. If you think the carry works better than about one time in three, take it.

Attack the space between two defenders, head up, so somebody has to choose. If you get there alone, turn away in open ice and come again with support. On the wall, here is the position. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the other player, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly.

As the carrier, be last to the line, slower thirty feet out and fastest as the puck crosses. As a winger, glide the trailing blade on the line, with your head up.

When the arm goes up, tag up together, and never forecheck a defenceman who has turned for the puck. Without the puck, drive the middle hard, to the edge of the paint and no further, head up, chin off your chest, and never into the goalie.

Dump only to where a teammate is going, and chase it on an angle, stick on the puck, never on a player's back. If their goalie comes out for it, peel off.

And defending the entry, keep a gap that shrinks all the way, and make the entry uncontrolled rather than stopping it outright.

Much of this depends on your team and your league. So here are four questions for your coach, and three for your league. Which entry patterns do we run? How do you want us to play the score late in a game? Which forecheck do we use, so I know who goes in second? And how do our defencemen meet the entry? And for your league. Can my trailing skate leave the ice? Does my age group have a delayed offside? And is there a trapezoid behind the net, and is it enforced?

Go and ask. Their answers tell you how your team wants you to play, and now you know why each answer makes sense.
