===== SCRIPT START =====

This episode is about playing defence. It takes its time. We will build the job up one piece at a time, and for each piece I will tell you what to do, and also why it works. If you know why something works, you can still make the right play when the game looks a little different from the picture in your head.

Before any instructions, a few words you will hear all the way through. I will explain them now, so they don't get in the way later.

A defenceman. Your team plays forwards up front and two defencemen behind them. You and the other defenceman are a pair. You are the last two players between the puck and your goalie.

Goal side. This means being between the player you are covering and your own net.

The crease and the mouth of the goal. The crease is your goalie's area, the blue paint in front of the net. The mouth of the goal is the ice right in front of the net, at the edge of the crease.

The slot. This is the ice in front of the net, between the two faceoff circles. It runs from the tops of the circles down to the mouth of the goal. Its top part, between the faceoff dots and the tops of the circles, is called the high slot.

High and low. In whichever end you are in, high means toward the blue line, and low means toward the goal line, the line the net sits on.

Strong side and weak side. The strong side is the side of the ice the puck is on. The weak side is the other side. For a pair of defencemen, these are job titles. The strong-side defenceman plays the puck. The weak-side defenceman holds the mouth of the goal.

The point. This is the area just inside the blue line, toward the boards, where defencemen stand in the other team's end. It is an area, not the line itself.

The gap. This is the space between you and a player skating at you with the puck.

The forecheck. This is the other team chasing the puck in your end, to win it back. A player doing it is a forechecker.

That is the vocabulary. Now the job.

I will start with four big ideas, because they matter most. If you only listen to the first part of this episode, take those four with you. After that we will go back through the rink, one end at a time, and add the detail.

First idea. Stay on the defensive side of the puck.

That means being between the puck and your own net. And it means staying goal side of the attacker you are covering.

Here is why it matters so much. You and your partner are the last players before your goalie. A forward who gets between you and your net has beaten you without touching the puck. Now your goalie is facing a player you should have been covering.

It usually goes wrong in a simple way. You watch the puck, and you lose the player. So keep checking where your player is while you follow the puck.

Sometimes you leave that spot on purpose. You might pinch, which means stepping down into their end at their blue line, to keep the puck in. You might step up, which means skating forward to close on an attacker early, to take the puck, not to hit them. Or you might join the attack. Those are good plays, but only once a teammate is covering behind you. So before you go, know who that teammate is.

How often you do this is your coach's choice. Some coaches want their defencemen jumping into the play, and others want you home. Ask yours.

Second idea. Work as a pair. One of you on the puck, one at the mouth of the goal.

In your own end, the strong-side defenceman goes to the puck. The weak-side defenceman guards the mouth of the goal. Every time the puck crosses the middle of the ice, the sides flip, and so do your jobs. And you say the swap out loud, every time.

Here is why. The pair goes wrong most often when both of you go to the same puck. Then nobody is in front of your net. Nobody is on the screen, the player standing in your goalie's line of sight. Nobody is on the tip, where a stick changes the direction of a shot. And nobody is on the easy tap-in. Coaches name this as one of the commonest ways to give up goals. That is coaching agreement, not a counted figure.

Saying the swap out loud is what keeps one of you at the net.

When you are the weak-side defenceman, stay at the mouth of the goal. Don't get drawn to the puck. Your partner and your centre are handling it.

This split, one on the puck and one at the net, belongs to one system, called the low zone collapse. Other systems split the work differently, and teams pick them for different reasons. For now, ask your coach which one your team plays.

Third idea. At the front of your net, get inside first, then lift their stick.

Forwards from the other team will park in front of your net. Every defenceman is told to clear the front of the net. Almost nobody is told how. There are two steps, and they work wherever you play.

Step one. Take the inside position. That means the spot between the forward and your goalie. Get there before they do, and stand beside them.

Step two. As the shot or pass comes, lift their stick. Lift it up, not sideways. Take it low on the shaft, well below their bottom hand. And let go once it is up.

Here is why it works. A forward stands there to tip a shot or tap in a rebound. With their stick up in the air, they can do neither.

And here is why you race to the spot. Once a forward already holds it, you can't shove them off it. So getting there first is the real battle.

You also don't have to be bigger than them. Plan the shift around lifting their stick, not around pushing someone bigger. Coaches say position beats strength, but that is a saying, not a law, and against a much bigger player it fails.

Now the limits. They are part of the job, not an extra.

Never shove anyone off their spot in front of your net. Not with your body, and not with your stick. That can be a penalty in any league, whether checking is allowed or not. Hold your spot without leaning on anyone, and make them go round you.

In some leagues even a box-out, holding your spot so a forward can't get where they are going, can be called a penalty. There, take the inside spot and lift the stick, but don't use your body to stop them.

Never put your stick across anyone. Using the shaft of your stick, held in both hands, to hit or shove a player is a cross-check. Having your blade on the ice doesn't change that.

Lift the stick, don't hold it. Holding on to a stick is a penalty. Never lift at their hands, because that can be called hooking. And never chop at the stick or the hands, because that is slashing.

Any of these can get you thrown out of the game, especially if it is reckless or someone gets hurt. Under some rules a reckless one is a match penalty, which goes past being thrown out.

And don't stop defending the front of your net just because you have been told you can't check there. An unmarked forward at your goal is exactly what this job is for. Take the inside, hold it, and lift the stick.

Sometimes a body does move in front of your net. That means a scramble for a loose puck, or a legal check on a forward who has the puck, if your league allows checking at all.

Whenever a body moves there, move it toward the corner. Never across the front of your own net.

Here is why. The path across your net has a steel goal post at one end and your goalie at the other. The player being moved along it is going sideways or backwards, and can see neither. Even going slowly, a crash there can hurt. Moving bodies toward the corner is a coaching safety habit, not a rule, and it holds in every league.

Keep their skates on the ice. Never lift anyone off their feet. A player whose skates have left the ice can't brace, and can't choose what hits the post first.

And never push anyone in the back. A push from behind sends a player into the post or the boards without seeing it coming. If you can see the numbers on their back, pull up. A hit from behind is checking from behind, and in most leagues it gets you thrown out of the game. If you drive them into the post or the boards when they can't protect themselves, you will almost certainly be thrown out. In some leagues that is automatically a match penalty.

Fourth idea. Never carry the puck, or pass it, across the front of your own goal. Go behind the net instead.

Say you have the puck in your own end, and you want to get it to your partner on the other side. Go behind the net. Pass it behind the net, or skate it there. Never through the slot.

Here is why. Lose the puck behind your net, and you have a battle for it behind the goal line. Lose it in the slot, and they get a shot from right in front of your goalie.

Some teams pass between their defencemen higher up instead, above the tops of the faceoff circles. There, a pass that gets picked off is a loose puck at the top of your slot, not a shot from close in. That is a different route, not a riskier version of the same one. So ask which your team uses. But your own slot is never the route, for a pass or for a carry.

With no pass on, skate it behind the net to the other side.

Let me pull those four ideas together, because they are the core of the job.

One. Stay on the defensive side of the puck, goal side of your player. Leave it only on purpose, with a teammate covering behind you.

Two. Play as a pair. One on the puck, one at the mouth of the goal. Swap out loud every time the puck crosses the middle.

Three. At the front of your net, get inside first, then lift their stick low and let go. Never shove, never lean, never your stick across anyone, and never into a back. If a body moves there, take it toward the corner, with their skates on the ice.

Four. Never take the puck across the front of your own goal. Go behind the net.

Everything else in this episode adds detail to one of those four. So now let's go back through the rink, starting in your own end.

Fifth idea. The system your team plays in its own end.

You have heard the name low zone collapse. Here is how it works, why a team plays it, and then the main alternative.

In a low zone collapse, each player covers an area of the ice, not a person. Down low, the strong-side defenceman and the centre fight for the puck. The weak-side defenceman holds the mouth of the goal. The centre usually covers the high slot, one layer further out. And the wingers stay high, covering the other team's defencemen at the points. So don't expect your wingers to come into the corner.

Why do teams play it? It protects the middle of the ice just by everyone standing in their spot. You don't need to make perfect reads to keep the dangerous ice full. It is the easiest system to learn, because the areas stay the same even when the play gets messy. It doesn't ask a tired player to win a race. And when you win the puck, both wingers are already high, ready for a pass.

What does it give up? Shots from the point, on purpose. The idea is that a long shot with a stick in its way is one your goalie should save.

The main alternative is man-on-man. Here each player follows one opponent, everywhere in your end. The wingers follow the other team's defencemen, even down into the corner. So you will get help low. And the front of the net belongs to whoever's player is standing there.

Why do teams play that? Nobody is ever left unmarked. It is very good against a team that likes to work the puck round the boards, because every pass goes to a player who is already covered. And all that pressure forces bad passes, so it wins the puck back more. The cost is that one beaten player leaves an attacker free, with nobody near to help.

Other systems mix the two, and some send both defencemen low. So ask which system your team plays, before your first shift.

Here is why that question matters so much. Coaches say a breakdown in your own end is usually two players working on different ideas of who has the corner. Both are working hard, just to different plans. That is coaching emphasis, not a counted figure.

For the rest of this episode, I will assume the low zone collapse.

Sixth idea. When they have the puck in your corner.

If you are the strong-side defenceman, the puck is yours to go after. Pressure the player with the puck, or angle them away from the net and the slot. Angling means skating a route that leaves them only one way to go, toward the boards.

Pin the puck against the boards, not the player. Use your skate or your stick blade, for a second or two, until help arrives. Then move the puck, or play it off the boards.

Here is why you pin the puck and not the player. Your job is to win the puck, and your centre is coming to help. Two of you against one is a battle you should win. Pinning the player to the boards can be called holding, and it doesn't win you the puck.

No push, no lean, no ride, and never your stick across them. Pressure never means a hit into a back, and where checking isn't allowed it never means a hit at all. If the player turns their back to you at the boards and you can see their numbers, pull up and play the puck. Never lift anyone off their feet. Even going slowly, a crash into the boards can hurt.

Two things never to do with the puck there. Never hold it against the boards to get a whistle. That is a penalty everywhere. And in some leagues, keeping it tied up there for three seconds is a penalty even under pressure. And never gather it in against your body. That is a penalty too.

Now the weak-side job, at the front of the net. In the collapse there are two layers there. Your partner, the weak-side defenceman, has the mouth of the goal. The centre has the high slot.

But what if your centre goes into the corner to help? Then there is a hole in the high slot. In the collapse, the winger on the far side drops down to fill it. Anyone with a clear view can call for that switch, including you.

If nobody comes, the defenceman at the net holds the mouth of the goal anyway, and calls louder. Here is why. An unmarked player right at your goal is more dangerous than a shot from the high slot through traffic.

There is one exception. Say the other team's far-side defenceman has skated down off the point, and your far-side winger is following them. Then nobody is free to fill the high slot. You and your partner have to watch the slot harder, and call out anyone arriving.

Under man-on-man there is no such switch. That winger stays with their player. So know your system before you wait for help that isn't coming.

Now say their player takes the puck behind your net. Don't chase them all the way round. Here is why. Follow them out the other side, and they have the slot to themselves.

The usual answer is a handover. The defenceman on the side they are heading for meets them at that post, stick on the puck, not a hit. The one who followed them in peels off to the front of the net. And your goalie seals the post, in case the carrier comes round and tries to stuff the puck in there.

Some teams keep one defenceman on that player all the way round, and send the partner to the front of the net instead. Ask which your team plays. And call the handover out loud either way.

Seventh idea. Going back for the puck, with a forechecker behind you.

Nobody on your team goes back for pucks with pressure behind them more than you do. So decide what you will do on the way back, not once the puck is on your stick. Glance over your shoulder as you skate back. See which side the forecheck is coming from.

Here is why. A decision made while you are still skating is one you make without a forechecker on top of you.

Now the safety part, and it matters more than anything else in this idea. I will call whoever comes at you on the boards the other player, whether they are after the puck or just coming at you.

Never turn your back to the other player. And never duck.

Both protect your head and neck. Turn your back, and you can't see the hit coming or brace for it. Duck, and your head meets the boards first. That is true even at slow speed, so a slow forechecker doesn't make it safe.

So turn your feet early, before they get there. Skates parallel to the boards, so you are side-on. Forearm and hip to the boards. Never the point of your shoulder, and not the whole shoulder if you can help it. Head up. Chin off your chest.

If you are already facing the boards and can't turn in time, that is the last resort, not a choice. Get your stick and gloves up and spread wide on the glass, with your head off it. And still never turn your back to the other player.

That position matters more than anything else about the boards. From here on I will use one short version of it, the same words every time, so you can learn it like a line. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the other player, never duck.

Now the puck. Under pressure, don't panic. Shield the puck with your body. Sometimes the best play is to hold it tight behind your net until a teammate gets open.

And you have two simple plays to beat the forechecker.

A rim is a hard pass around the boards, following the curve of the rink to a teammate further round. It beats a forechecker who is sealing you against the boards, because it goes past them.

A reverse sends the puck back the way it came, to a teammate arriving behind you. It beats a forechecker who has come in too hard and skated past you, because it goes into the space they just left.

Your glance over your shoulder is what tells you which one you face. Reading that is a core defenceman skill.

And never carry the puck through your own slot under pressure. A turnover there is a straight chance against you.

Eighth idea. Starting the breakout.

The breakout is your team's exit from its own end with the puck under control. When you get the puck, look up the ice before you look down.

Here is why. In the two or three seconds after your team wins the puck, the other team is the least organised it will be. A quick pass up the ice to an open teammate beats them before they are set. Most defencemen never look for it, because their eyes go down instead of up.

Here are your options. Hear them as a list for when you are under pressure, not a ranking. Run down it and take the first one that is really on.

Quick-up. A fast pass up the ice, before their forecheck is set.

Up. A pass to your winger on the boards. In the usual breakout your winger waits low on the boards, but teams set this differently, so learn where yours stands.

Wheel. You skate it yourself, around behind the net and up the other side. That is good against a slow forecheck, or one with a single player chasing.

D to D. A pass across to your partner, behind the net. It makes their forecheck shift across, which is often all you need.

Reverse. Rim. A rim is safe, but it gives the puck away more often than the others.

Off the glass and out. You give up the puck, but you don't give up a goal, and it is always there when you are in trouble.

Choose by reading their forecheck, not by picking your favourite.

Now, when your partner has the puck. You are the second option, and usually the voice. Stand where a pass behind the net reaches you cleanly. That is usually the other side of the net, level with the goal line or just above it.

Here is why you are the voice. You can see the forecheck coming in behind your partner, and your partner can't. So the calls are mostly yours. Up. Over. Wheel. Reverse. Rim. Man on, which means someone is right on them. And time, which means they have time.

Only say time when you are sure. A wrong time sends a teammate into a hit they weren't ready for.

Where you stand and who makes the calls are coaching choices, so learn your team's way.

When nothing is on, off the glass and along the boards is never the wrong play. Over the glass is different. Put it over the glass from your own end, and in many leagues that is two minutes, even by accident. Do it on purpose and it is two minutes anywhere. So off the glass, never over it.

And never aim at the players' bench. A puck over the glass behind the bench is still a penalty.

Ninth idea. Whose puck is it when a rim dies in your corner?

Sometimes the other team rims the puck round, and it stops dead in your corner. Is it yours, or your goalie's? That depends on your league's rulebook, so ask which one you play under.

Some rulebooks paint a trapezoid behind the net. That is a shape on the ice, narrow at the goal line and wider at the end boards. Your goalie may only play the puck behind the goal line inside it. NHL rules and international rules both use it. In Britain, play as though it applies, and ask your league.

Where there is a trapezoid, the puck in the corner is yours. So plan your route before it gets there. A forechecker will be coming, so go in the same way as any time you go back for a puck. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the other player, never duck. Here is why that matters. That plan is the difference between a breakout and a battle lost facing your own end boards.

And aim your own rims where a teammate can meet them. A rim aimed straight into the corner usually ends up with their forechecker.

Your goalie can still help, just not out in the corner. They can stop a puck right behind the net and leave it there for you. The call for that is leave it, and it has to come while you are still skating back.

USA Hockey and Hockey Canada rules, and some adult leagues, paint no trapezoid. There, the dead rim can be your goalie's. A defenceman who sprints into that corner out of habit is racing their own goalie for the puck, with a forechecker coming.

So one of you calls it, while you are still skating back. If your goalie calls I've got it, turn and go to your breakout spot. If your goalie calls leave it, they stop it and leave it for you. Agree these words with your goalie before the game, because the words vary by team.

And in those leagues your goalie can play that corner puck, but can't fall on it to stop play. So expect it back to you moving, and be ready for the pass.

Let me pull your own end together before we move up the ice. Find out which system your team plays. In the collapse, the strong side takes the puck and the weak side holds the net. Pin the puck, not the player. Hand over a carrier behind your net, rather than chase them round. On the way back for a puck, glance over your shoulder. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the other player, never duck. Look up the ice first. Off the glass, never over it. And know whose puck the corner is in your league.

Tenth idea. Gap control, the skill that defines the position.

Your gap is the space between you and a puck carrier skating at you. Too big, and they get a free run at you with speed and options. Too small, and one move puts them past you. You want to be close enough to reach the puck with a poke check, a quick jab of your stick blade at the puck. But far enough away that one fake doesn't beat you.

Gap control is a skating problem before it is a decision. You can't close a gap from standing still. The most common mistake defencemen make is standing still and letting an attacker build up speed into them.

So start skating backwards early, before they reach you. Then you are already at their speed when they arrive.

A rough coaching guide is a stick and a half of space as they cross the centre red line. Close that to about a stick length by your own blue line. Those are a guide, not a law.

Stay low. Stand tall going backwards, and they beat you wide.

Watch their chest and hips. Not the puck, their hands or their head. Here is why. A good attacker fakes with the puck, the stick, the hands and the head. But their chest and hips have to point where their body is really going.

Take away the middle, so the only way left is toward the side boards. Here is why. The boards then act like a second defender.

How much the boards help depends on the rink. On a wide rink, an attacker forced outside still has room, so your gap and your stick matter more than steering.

Keep your stick on the ice, out in front of you, in the passing lane. That takes the pass away without committing your body.

And turn as late as you can. Turning from skating backwards to skating forwards is called pivoting. Once you pivot, you can't react to what they do next. So hold your backward skating as long as you can.

All of this needs fast backward skating. Build that speed with the moves coaches call C-cuts and backward crossovers, not by pushing straight back. If you have just moved back from forward, this is the hard part. Moving to defence is a rebuild of your skating, not just a set of new decisions, so expect it to take time.

Now, which way to pivot. This one has a real choice in it.

The usual coaching default is to pivot toward the boards side. When you have to turn, you open up toward the boards, not toward the middle. Here is why. If you are beaten, you are beaten toward the wall, not into your own slot.

Some coaches want a middle pivot in certain situations. One is when turning away would take your eyes off the puck. Another is when you have help on the inside, and the bigger danger is the attacker cutting in behind you.

So ask which your coach wants, and practise both. The right pivot depends on where the attacker is, not on which way you happen to be good at.

Eleventh idea. Forcing a carrier outside, and where it stops.

Forcing a carrier outside means steering them toward the boards with the route you skate. It doesn't mean hitting them into the boards. The boards take away their ice, not them.

So no bump, no push, no lean, and no ride along the boards. Never your stick across them. Play the puck.

Here is why that line matters. Steering with your route is allowed everywhere. Riding a player along the boards with your body is a different act.

Where checking isn't allowed, that ride is an illegal check. And checking isn't allowed in a lot of age groups and leagues. So check the game, not the age group. A team old enough to check can be in a no-checking game for the night, just because of who it is playing.

Where checking is allowed, the ride still has a limit. Put a player dangerously into the boards and it is boarding. That includes a player racing you for a loose puck on an icing, who can see you perfectly well. Boarding can be far more than two minutes, and it can get you thrown out of the game.

In women's hockey the rule says it outright. Steer with your skating only. Stepping or gliding into a player at the end of it is a penalty. And using the boards to pin a player isn't allowed either.

Now the trap hidden in this idea. Forcing a carrier outside turns them. They shield the puck on the boards, so by the time you arrive their back is half turned to you. Finish a check on their back, on the numbers on their jersey, and that is checking from behind.

So when you can see their numbers, pull up. Take the puck, not the player. Your steering has already done its work, and the boards are holding them there for your stick.

Don't finish the hit because they turned into you, either. In some leagues the hit can still be a penalty, so it has to be fine on its own.

A hit from behind gets you thrown out of the game in most leagues. If you drive them into the boards when they can't protect themselves, you will almost certainly be thrown out. In some leagues that is automatically a match penalty.

Never lift anyone off their feet against the boards. And even going slowly, a crash into the boards can hurt.

Twelfth idea. Stepping up and pinching are two different plays.

You step up in the neutral zone, the middle of the ice between the two blue lines, or at your own blue line. You skate forward to meet an attacker early, instead of backing up. You close the space, and you never finish it as a hit. Miss, and there is nothing behind you but open ice. Your partner is left alone against two attackers, which is called a two-on-one.

You pinch at their blue line. You step down into their end to stop the puck coming out. Miss, and the puck is behind you, going the other way. But your forwards are still in their end to help.

So a pinch is usually the safer of the two. But safer here only means less chance of a rush back at your net. It says nothing about anyone's body.

Step up only when you are confident you can win the puck or force a turnover. Confident, not hoping. Otherwise, back up under control, so they can't get into your end cleanly.

How confident is enough is your coach's call, and it depends on your team's system. A team that presses in the neutral zone wants you challenging at the line. A team playing a trap, which sits back and clogs the middle of the ice, wants you holding and steering the carrier wide. Ask which risk your coach wants to take.

A step-up closes the space. It is not a hit. Your feet keep moving, you don't speed up into them, and the attacker stays in front of you. Close the space, and play the puck.

A carrier with their head down can't change direction, so you can get to the spot in front of them first. That is all it tells you. It is not a chance to hit them. Speed up into them instead, and it is charging, which means building up speed to drive into a player. Charging counts whether your league allows checking or not. No number of strides makes it safe, and it can get you thrown out of the game.

Thirteenth idea. Defending the rush, when they attack up the ice at you.

A rush where the attackers outnumber the defenders coming back is called an odd-man rush. There are three situations you need to have automatic.

One attacker against you is a one-on-one. That is gap control. Force them outside, keep your stick in the passing lane, and don't lunge. Steer them to a bad angle for a shot, rather than going for a hit. Take the angle, not the player.

Two attackers against you alone is a two-on-one. Here is the usual way to play it, and why.

Take away the pass, and leave the shot to your goalie. Sit in the passing lane between the two attackers, with your stick flat on the ice on the passing side. Stay on your feet until the pass has gone.

Here is why that works. Your goalie can see the shot, and is set for it. A pass across to an open forward is a chance your goalie can't stop. So you take away the thing your goalie can't handle, and leave the thing they can.

The other real option is a timed slide. You slide flat across the passing lane, to take the pass away and force the shot. Go too early, and you are out of the play and can't get to the rebound.

Even when your coach asks for the slide, it can cost you. A slide that takes the attacker down is tripping, and in most leagues getting to the puck first doesn't excuse it. A slide that catches them low, in the legs, can be called clipping, and clipping can get you thrown out of the game. And never slide into anyone from behind. That is checking from behind, and it can get you thrown out.

If you do go down, keep your head up and out of the shooting lane. A slide across a passing lane is not the same move as dropping to block a shot. This episode doesn't teach how to go down for a slide, so ask the coach who wants it to show you.

So ask your coach which way your team plays the two-on-one. And make sure your goalie gives the same answer.

One exception. Say a teammate racing back, called a backchecker, has picked up the second attacker. The shooter is walking in with nobody on them and no real pass to make. Then it isn't a two-on-one any more. Close on the puck.

Three attackers against the two of you is a three-on-two. This one really has no single answer. There are two named ways to play it.

The common one is called two wide, middle to the backchecker. Each defenceman takes an outside attacker. Nobody chases the carrier. The middle attacker belongs to whoever is backchecking down the middle, usually your centre.

The other is called strong side takes the puck, weak side splits. The defenceman on the puck side plays the carrier one-on-one. The other drops back between the middle attacker and the far one, sitting in the passing lane, the way you would on a two-on-one.

Why would a team pick one over the other? Each one gives something up. The first gives up the middle if the backchecker doesn't arrive. The second never gives up the middle, but it gives the far attacker more room.

So ask which your team plays. Two defencemen playing different versions on the same rush is worse than either one. Whichever it is, back up together, and keep the middle closed as long as you can.

Under all three of those sits the first idea. Stay on the defensive side of the puck.

Fourteenth idea. The neutral zone, with your own team going forward.

When you have the puck with space, carry it with confidence and look to pass to a forward. Or chip it off the boards ahead of a forward skating onto it.

Under pressure, move it quickly. A bank off the boards, a pass to a forward, or a dump into their end are all good options. Don't try to carry it through heavy pressure, and don't stickhandle through the middle.

Here is why. The middle of the ice is an expensive place to lose the puck. Coaches say that is where odd-man rushes against you get made. That is coaching agreement, not a count, and not every one starts there. So simple and safe beats clever and risky there. When in doubt, go simple.

If every forward is covered, pass it across to your partner. That is called a regroup. Here is why it works. It resets the play. It gives your forwards time to swing back and come again with speed. And it makes their forecheckers turn and skate. Forcing a pass into a lane that is already shut is how a turnover becomes a rush against you, with you as the last player back. Where your forwards swing on a regroup is a coaching choice, so ask what your team runs.

When a forward has the puck, you and your partner hold your own blue line. One of you on each side of the middle, roughly between the two faceoff dots in the neutral zone. You are the safety valve, the pass back if a forward needs one. Keep your stick ready to cut out passes in your lane.

And don't drift too far forward. If your team loses the puck, you need to be in place to stop the rush.

Let me pull the middle of the ice together. Win the gap with your feet, and pivot late. Steer the carrier wide with your skating. Don't ride them into the boards, and if you see their numbers, pull up. Step up only when you are sure, and close the space, never charge. On a two-on-one, stay on your feet in the passing lane until the pass has gone, unless your coach wants the slide. On a three-on-two, know which version your team plays. And with the puck, keep it simple through the middle.

Fifteenth idea. Their end. Keep the puck in, and stay able to get back.

In their end you have two jobs at once. Keep the puck in, and stay able to get back.

Keeping it in comes first. Hold the blue line, and don't panic and back off it. If they try to clear it up the boards, step in and stop it. And keep your own skates inside the blue line too. If you drift out while the puck stays in, you are no longer an option for your forwards.

Here is why the line matters so much. Lose the blue line, or get caught deep in their end, and they get out cleanly. So if you really can't hold the line, get out and reset rather than gamble. Under heavy pressure, a pass back to your partner, or a chip out of the zone, resets the play.

Now the shot from the point. The first decision isn't the shot. It is the lane, a clear path to the net.

Walk the line, which means skating sideways along the blue line, to open up a better angle. Once you have a lane, get the puck through rather than shooting it hard. Keep it on net at knee height or below, with enough pace that a small touch still carries it.

Here is why. A shot from the point is there to make a tip, a rebound or a scramble, not to beat the goalie clean. A low shot lets your teammate at the net tip it, and the rebound comes out to them, not into the goalie's glove. That is coaching know-how, not a rule.

If there is no lane, move the puck until there is one. Pass across to your partner, or down to the half-wall and back. The half-wall is the stretch of boards roughly level with the faceoff dot, between the corner and the point.

You can also pass to a winger in the faceoff circle, or set up your partner for a one-timer. A one-timer is a shot straight off a pass, without stopping the puck. If you play adult rec hockey, ask whether your league bans slap shots. At least one adult rulebook counts a one-timer with a big backswing as a slap shot.

Now picture three depths in their end.

Just inside the blue line is the normal spot.

The top of the faceoff circle, the edge nearest the blue line, is more active. The strong-side defenceman can step down there to support a cycle, where your forwards keep the puck by passing it round the boards and behind the net. Only do it when there is no immediate threat of a turnover. From there, you can still get back.

Below the faceoff circles is always a commitment. Down there you are a fourth forward. If the puck turns over, you can't get back. That is fine if somebody has already taken your spot at the blue line. If nobody has, it is an odd-man rush against you.

So only one of you goes below the puck at a time. Below the puck means deeper into their end than the puck is. The other one holds the blue line. Here is why. If you both go in and the puck turns over, there is nobody in position.

Nearly every coach teaches that, but it is not a rule, and coaches do call exceptions. A full-ice press is one. Pulling your goalie for an extra attacker is another. Unless you have been told one is on, assume it.

Sixteenth idea. When they win the puck back in their end.

When their team gets the puck back in their own end, the strong-side defenceman can pinch, to challenge the player with the puck. Challenge the puck with your stick. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call, and if you see their numbers, pull up. The weak-side defenceman holds back, against an odd-man rush. If the puck is deeper in their end, both of you hold at the top and cut out passes, rather than chase.

If you had stepped down when it turned over, get back to the line first. Read it, then decide. Pressure from the top, not from below.

Here is the timing test most teams use for a pinch. Go only if you will arrive as their player gets the puck. And go only when a forward is high enough to cover the point you are leaving. Ask what your coach wants.

The commonest way a pinch goes wrong is their winger chipping the puck past you. Then turn and skate after the puck. Never cut across into the winger, and never hit them once the puck has gone. Here is why. The hit doesn't get the puck back, and the window for a legal hit then is almost nothing, even where checking is allowed. Where it isn't, and in women's hockey, there is none at all.

And if you do meet their winger on the boards, it is the same position as in your own end. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the other player, never duck.

Seventeenth idea. Joining the attack.

Joining the attack is called activating. Staying back is the default, not a ban. Modern defencemen create a lot of offence by jumping into the play. The difference between a good jump and a bad one is the read.

Only go when a forward can fill in above the puck for you. Somebody has to be the safety valve. If all three forwards are below the circles and you jump, there is nobody home.

Go when your team clearly has the puck, low in their end. Not while the puck is still up for grabs in a corner.

Call it. Shout, so your partner slides across to cover the middle, and your forwards know to rotate high.

And arrive late, as the second wave. Arriving late into the slot is what beats their coverage. Arrive early, and you are just one more player for them to mark.

Which side do you come down? The common way is the weak side, into the back door, the far side of their net. Here is why. The player arriving unmarked is the dangerous one, so coming down the weak side usually beats driving into a crowded battle on the strong side.

Some teams activate on the puck side on purpose. Teams that rotate all five players do it, and so do teams that use the defenceman as support on the boards where the puck is. Ask which your team does.

The rule of thumb is that somebody is above the puck. On a pair, that is normally one of you two. But think of it as a person, not a position. Some teams rotate all five players and cover the line with whoever is highest. Some keep only one player high, and make that the far-side winger. So before you go below the puck, know exactly which teammate is above it.

Let me pull their end together. Keep the puck in, and keep your skates inside the line. Find a lane, then get the shot through, low, for a tip or a rebound. Below the circles is a commitment, so go only once somebody has your spot. Only one of you below the puck. If the winger chips it past you, chase the puck, not the winger. And before you join the attack, know who is above the puck.

Eighteenth idea. Blocking shots.

Blocking shots is part of your job. It is a technique, not an act of courage. Done badly, it hurts you and doesn't stop the puck. So here is the whole of it, and it goes together.

First, your gear. Blocking puts your shins, feet and hands in the puck's way on purpose. So wear shin guards that cover your shin and your ankle, and gloves that aren't worn through in the palm.

Get close. Ten feet beats thirty. Here is why. The further away you are, the more the shooter can adjust around you, and the more time the puck has to rise or change direction. Close in, you take away more of the net. That it is also safer for you is coaching know-how, not measured.

Be big and square to the shooter. Shin pads face the shot, and your stick is down on the ice beside you, to seal the gap low.

Stay on your feet if you can. A standing block keeps you in the play.

And stay up until the puck is actually shot. Here is why. Going down early is exactly what a fake shot is trying to get you to do. Close the distance with your feet. Don't lunge.

Keep your head up and out of the shooting lane, standing or down. Hands behind your body while you are standing.

If you do go down, go down toward the shooter, not sideways. Head still up and out of the lane. Hands behind your shin pads once you are down, with the backs of your gloves out. Feet on the ice. That direction is coaching know-how, not measured.

Four things you never do. Never turn your back to the shot. Never turn sideways. Never lift a leg. And never lead with your stick.

Here is why. Turning your back or your side opens up the parts your pads don't cover, and you lose sight of the puck. A lifted leg puts the gap between your shin pad and your pants in the puck's path. And a stick pushed out in front can send the puck up, at your own face and at your goalie. That last part is coaching know-how, not measured.

Never block from a screening spot, in your goalie's line of sight. Half blocking a shot your goalie can't see is worse than not blocking it at all, because you have changed the path of a puck they never saw. So if you are in that line, either step out and block square, or get out of your goalie's eyes.

And know when not to block. On a two-on-one, blocking the shot means leaving the pass open, and your goalie wants the shot.

How much your team blocks at all is a coaching choice. Some teams want defencemen dropping and blocking a lot in front of the goalie. Others want the lane taken away on your feet, with the goalie seeing everything. Neither is right on its own. So ask your coach which, and ask your goalie what they want, before you decide a teammate who stayed up was being soft.

Nineteenth idea. Faceoffs.

Your centre takes the faceoff. Where you line up depends on the zone and on what your team runs. These are common setups, not rules, so ask what yours are.

In your own end, one defenceman lines up near the hash marks on the boards side, backing up the winger there. The hash marks are the short red lines on the outside of the faceoff circle, roughly level with the dot. That defenceman is ready to chase the puck into the corner, or get behind the net. The other defenceman is in the slot, ready to block a point shot or cover the front of the net.

In their end, one of you is at the point on the side of the draw, ready to shoot if your centre wins it cleanly. Here is why that spot is worth having. A set play off a clean draw is one of the few times you get to shoot with your feet already set and traffic already in front. The other defenceman holds the far point.

At centre ice, you both stay back at your own blue line, in case they win it forward and come at you.

Whatever the draw, be ready to react straight away to a tie-up or a loose puck.

Twentieth idea. The power play, when your team has an extra player.

On the power play, your team's setup decides whether you are even on the ice, and whether you have a partner up there.

In a one-three-one, there is only one defenceman, up at the top. That setup is widely described as the modern default. If that is you, you are the passer who runs the play. Move the puck quickly. Walk the line to open shooting lanes. And use the threat of your shot to pull their four players out of shape. If you are the second defenceman, you are on the second unit, not on the ice.

In an umbrella, there are two defencemen up top, so you have a partner to pass back and forth with.

In a two-three, both defencemen are at the points. It is the older setup, and it is still common in rec and youth hockey, though that is an impression, not a count.

In an overload, you are usually the lone outlet on the far side. Your job is to be the release when the near side gets sealed off.

Whichever it is, your first job is keeping the puck in their end. Here is why. A power play that gets cleared has to come back in against four players already set up, and that is much harder than holding the line was. Your second job is shooting for tips and rebounds, not trying to beat the goalie clean through four bodies.

Twenty-first idea. The penalty kill, when your team is a player short.

A penalty kill is four skaters, usually two forwards and two defencemen. Where you stand depends on your team's shape.

In a box, you and your partner are low, owning the front of the net and the two low corners. It is the shape almost everyone is taught first.

In a diamond, one of you usually takes the bottom point, at the front of the net, and the other takes a middle spot. Which spots go to the defence is your coach's call. Here is why a team uses the diamond. Against a one-three-one power play, a box leaves their middle player, called the bumper, unmarked. The diamond covers that player.

In a wedge plus one, three players sit in a tight triangle around the net, and the fourth, the plus one, pressures whoever has the puck. You are almost always in the triangle. But when the puck moves, the nearest triangle player steps out as the new plus one, and that can be you. The old plus one drops back in. Here is why it works that way. Following the puck around the outside is how this shape gets beaten. The route the plus one takes out is a coaching choice, so ask which yours runs.

In all of them, stay tight, hold the inside at the front of the net, block shots, and clear the puck every chance you get.

Don't chase the puck around the outside. Let it come to you. Here is why. The power play wants you to chase, because that is what opens the gap they are waiting for.

In many leagues, while your team has fewer players on the ice, icing isn't called against you. Where that is true, use it, rather than trying to make a play you don't need. But at some younger levels a team that is a player short is still called for icing, so check your league. And on the last shift of a kill, get the clear away before the penalty ends.

Twenty-second idea, and it is short. Line changes and tired clears.

Your shift can be a little longer than a forward's. Here is why. More of it is gliding, reading and skating backwards, rather than sprinting. But longer doesn't mean less work. One study tracked professional players through a game. The defencemen covered more ice in total, and the forwards did more hard skating per minute. And the difference in shift length is small, only a second or two.

Your pair changes on its own timing, not with a forward line. Here is why. If all five players step over the boards at the same moment, nobody is there to stop a rush. So let the forwards go, and change at the next safe moment.

And if you are the last player back, you don't change until somebody takes your place. Say it out loud. Last man.

When you are tired, don't fire a flat clear up the middle. Here is why. That is the one that gets picked off at the blue line, and it is the one that ices. Aim wide and high off the glass instead. A glass clear rarely ices, it is hard to pick off, and it gets the puck out even when it doesn't get it out cleanly. Off the glass, never over it.

If you do ice it, the faceoff comes back to your end. And in many leagues, after your own icing, your team can't change players. So a tired clear can keep you out there, tired, for a faceoff in your own end. When you are gassed, the glass is the answer.

The exception that matters is an injured player. An injured player can be replaced, so a hurt teammate never has to stay out for that faceoff.

Let me pull it all together.

Stay on the defensive side of the puck. Leave it only on purpose, after calling it, with a teammate you know is covering.

Play as a pair. One on the puck and one at the mouth of the goal, swapping out loud every time the puck crosses the middle. And know which system your team plays.

At your own net, get inside first, lift the stick low, and let go. Never shove, never lean, never your stick across anyone, and never into a back. If a body moves there, take it toward the corner, with their skates on the ice. And never take the puck across the front of your own goal.

On the way back for a puck, glance over your shoulder. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the other player, never duck. Look up the ice first. Off the glass, never over it.

Win the gap with your feet, not with a hit. Steer the carrier wide, play the puck, and if you see their numbers, pull up. On a two-on-one, take away the pass and leave the shot to your goalie.

In their end, keep the puck in, get your shot through low, and know who is above the puck before you go below it.

Much of this depends on what your team plays. So here are three questions to ask your coach, and two for your league. What system do we play in our own end? How aggressive do you want me at the blue lines? On a two-on-one, do you want me in the passing lane, or sliding? And for the league. Which rulebook do we play under? And is body checking allowed in our games?

Go and ask. Their answers tell you how your team wants you to play, and now you know why each answer makes sense.

### 7. Consequences checked against the source wording, never cheaper

- Shove off a spot: "can be a penalty in any league" (:278, CM :904 "can be called interference wherever you play").
- Stick fouls and walk-out: "can get you thrown out if reckless or someone gets hurt", "under some rules a reckless one is a match" (:279, :340, :350-355). The source's worst case is a USA Hockey match plus a likely suspension; "a match penalty, which goes past being thrown out" carries the tier without the book name.
- Checking from behind: "in most leagues thrown out", "almost certainly thrown out" into the boards or post unable to protect themselves, "automatically a match in some" (:322-327, :488-494). USA Hockey's minor-plus-misconduct floor away from the boards is why it says "most".
- Boarding: "can be far more than two minutes, can get you thrown out" (:461, :486).
- Charging: "counts whether your league allows checking or not … can get you thrown out" (:526, :540, :542).
- Tripping on a slide: "in most leagues getting to the puck first doesn't excuse it" (KF :19). Clipping: "can get you thrown out" (:886 "every book can eject you for it"). "Low, in the legs" is wider than the NHL/IIHF knees line, matching Hockey Canada's hips line (:866), so it is not cheaper anywhere.
- Over the glass: "in many leagues two minutes even by accident; on purpose two minutes anywhere" (:50, :111).
- Freezing: "a penalty everywhere" for a whistle; "three seconds … even under pressure" in some leagues (:215, :244).
