===== SCRIPT START =====

This episode is about playing hockey in Britain. It is not a tour of the rulebook. It is about the handful of places where a British game asks you to play differently from the hockey you see on television.

Some of what changes is your kit. Some of it is how you behave at the faceoff, at the net, and when a game turns rough. And some of it is the shape of the game itself, depending on your age group and your league. For each one, I will tell you what to do and also why it makes sense. If you know why a rule is there, you can still do the right thing when a game looks a little different from the example.

Before any instructions, a few words you will hear all the way through. I will explain them now, so they don't get in the way later.

The IIHF. That is the International Ice Hockey Federation. It publishes the rulebook that British hockey is played under.

The NHL. That is the big North American professional league. Most of the hockey you watch, and most hockey writing in English, follows its rules. Those are not your rules.

Ice Hockey UK, or IHUK. That is the governing body for hockey in the United Kingdom.

The In-House Rules. These are Ice Hockey UK's list of changes to the IIHF rulebook. They cover most British hockey. That means the National Ice Hockey League, called the NIHL. The Scottish National League, called the SNL. Women's hockey. Recreational and junior hockey. And university hockey.

EIH and SIHA. These are England Ice Hockey and the Scottish Ice Hockey Association. A few of the In-House Rules are written only for EIH or SIHA competition, meaning the games those two bodies run.

The Elite League, or EIHL. That is Britain's top professional league. It doesn't use the In-House Rules. It has its own list of changes, called the EIHL Casebook.

Now the penalties. A minor is two minutes in the penalty box, and your team plays a player short. A major is five minutes, for a more serious foul. A misconduct is ten minutes in the box for you, but your team doesn't play a player short. And a game misconduct means you are thrown out for the rest of the game.

Checking, or body checking, means using your body to knock an opponent off the puck.

The crease is the light-blue painted area in front of each net. It belongs to the goalie.

And a faceoff, or a draw, is the restart. An official drops the puck between two players, usually the two centres, at one of the dots painted on the ice.

That is the vocabulary. Now the job.

I will start with four big ideas, because they matter most. If you only hear the first part of this episode, take those four with you. After that we will go through a game in order. First your kit, before you step on the ice. Then the faceoff, the net and your stick. Then keeping yourself on the ice. Then how your age group and your competition change the game. And last, what to do if someone is badly hurt.

First idea. Play to the IIHF rulebook, and find out which British layer sits on top of it.

Every level of hockey in Britain plays under the IIHF rulebook. The NIHL. Recreational and junior hockey across England, Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland. Women's hockey. University hockey. The Great Britain national teams. And the Elite League too.

Here is why that matters on the ice. Most coaching videos, books and websites in English are written to NHL rules. When one of them points out a difference for the IIHF, that difference is yours. When it points one out for USA Hockey or Hockey Canada, the two big North American rulebooks, it isn't. So before you copy something you learned from a video, ask which rules it was made for.

On top of the IIHF book sits a short British layer. In most competitions that layer is the In-House Rules. In the Elite League it is the Casebook. The Great Britain national teams play the IIHF book with no changes at all. Where the IIHF book and the British layer differ, the British layer wins at home. So in a British game, play the British version.

Neither British document works on its own. Each one is a list of changes. For anything they don't mention, the IIHF book decides.

One more question for your league. There are two recent editions of the IIHF book. They differ in ways that change what some fouls cost you, most of all fighting and high sticks. The Elite League plays the newer one. Anywhere else, ask which edition your officials use. Even the In-House Rules aren't clear about which edition they mean, which is why the question is worth asking. And where the two differ, play to the stricter one.

Second idea. Wear a neck guard every time you go on the ice. And if it comes off during play, leave the ice without picking it up.

A neck guard is a collar that protects your neck from cuts. In Britain it is mandatory for every player, adults included. You may have read that adults don't need one. That exemption is North American, and it isn't yours. USA Hockey exempts adults, and Hockey Canada's rule covers only youth and women's hockey. And university hockey is not exempt either.

Wear it properly. It must be unaltered, and it must cover as much of your neck as it can. A guard cut down to fit, or hanging loose below your chin, doesn't count.

Here is why this one is so sharp. Under the In-House Rules there is no warning. If you go on the ice without a neck guard, it is straight away a ten-minute misconduct. If it happens a second time, it automatically becomes a game misconduct, and your game is over. So the very first time you forget it is already a penalty. The British documents don't fully agree on whether a warning comes first. So assume there isn't one. Assuming that costs you nothing.

In the Elite League the referee warns you first, and then it is a minor. But that isn't the ceiling there. If you refuse to fix it, or come back out without fixing it, it keeps climbing, all the way to being thrown out. So put it right the moment you are told.

If your neck guard comes off during play, leave the ice. Don't stop to pick it up. You are not allowed to. Go to the bench, and let somebody else collect it. Why go straight off? Because under the In-House Rules, playing on without it is the same ten-minute misconduct as starting without it.

Wear it at practice too. In England and Wales the rule covers every session on the ice, practices and stick-and-puck included. Stick-and-puck is open ice that players book to practise on their own. In the Elite League the rule covers warm-ups and practice sessions. Elsewhere, nothing we found puts the neck guard on you at training. Wear it anyway.

Third idea. Before your first shift, ask your league whether your age group is checking. Until you have an answer, don't hit anyone.

Neither the IIHF book nor the In-House Rules sets an age where checking starts. Each competition sets it in its own regulations, called its Rules of Competition. And the North American ages don't carry over. So an answer you heard from a North American friend, or read online, may simply be wrong for your league.

Four of Ice Hockey UK's Rules of Competition settle it for the leagues they cover. In IHUK's junior leagues, under-tens and under-twelves are non-checking. Under-fourteens and older are checking. NIHL Divisions One and Two are checking. The women's league, the WNIHL, is non-checking, at under-sixteen and at senior level.

The line goes with the game you are dressed for, not your birthday. A junior can play up one age group. So an under-twelve player dressed for an under-fourteen game is in a checking game. And from sixteen, or fifteen with approval, a junior can play up into the NIHL, where Divisions One and Two check. So before you play up, find out whether the game you are joining checks.

That is all that is settled. It doesn't cover the Elite League, university hockey, NIHL National, the PNIHL, the SNL, or other Scottish hockey outside the junior leagues. And knowing that Ice Hockey UK runs your league doesn't answer it either. The In-House Rules reach leagues those four documents don't cover. So ask the league you actually play in. Until it answers, don't hit anyone.

Fourth idea. Play as though your first major ends your game, adult or not. And after one misconduct, play as though the next one ends it.

Under your rulebook, a major for any of twenty listed fouls also throws you out, automatically. The newer edition lists twenty-two. No injury is needed. Once the major is called, the ejection isn't the referee's to hold back.

So here is how to play. Never hit a player who can't see you coming, and never from behind. Even a light hit from behind can cause a serious head or neck injury. Never hit a player in the head. Never shove with both hands on your stick. Keeping your blade on the ice doesn't make it legal. Never chop with your stick, at anything. Keep your stick below every shoulder. And never go low at a player's legs.

Here are some of the fouls on that list, so you know what each one is. Checking from behind, which is hitting a player in the back. Cross-checking, a hit or a shove with the shaft of your stick, with both hands on it. Slashing, a chop with your stick, at a player or at their stick. Hooking, using your stick to hold a player back. High-sticking, your stick hitting a player above the shoulders. Slew-footing, taking a player's feet out from under them with your leg or foot. Clipping, a hit low, on a player's legs. And a check to the head.

There is also a catch-all, for any dangerous act the referee judges puts another player in danger. So not knowing the exact list is no defence.

Five more fouls can throw you out with the major, at the referee's judgement. They are boarding, charging, elbowing, interference and tripping. Play as though the ejection follows the major there too. Being wrong that way costs you nothing. And in the Elite League, a major for boarding, charging or elbowing that injures an opponent's face or head must come with a game misconduct.

Then there is the count. A second major in one game throws you out, whatever the two were for. So does a second misconduct. In the NHL a player is only thrown out on a third major, and misconducts aren't counted that way. So if your habits come from the NHL, you think you have one more major and one more misconduct to spare than you really do.

Here is why the misconduct count matters so much in Britain. Under the In-House Rules, misconducts come from your kit as well as from fouls. A missing neck guard. A loose chin strap, once your team has been warned for one. Playing on after your helmet comes off. And a dive. So a forgotten neck guard and a dive, in the same game, end it. Once you have one misconduct, play the rest of the game as though the next one ends it.

Let me pull those four ideas together, because they are the core of playing in Britain.

One. Your rulebook is the IIHF's, with the In-House Rules or the Elite League's Casebook on top. Ask which edition your officials use, and play to the stricter one.

Two. Wear your neck guard every time you go on the ice, practice included. Assume there is no warning. If it comes off during play, leave the ice without picking it up.

Three. Ask your league whether your age group checks, and don't hit anyone until you know.

Four. Play as though your first major ends your game. Never hit a player who can't see you coming, and never from behind. Never hit a player in the head. Never shove with both hands on your stick. And once you have one misconduct, play as though the next one ends your game.

Everything else in this episode adds detail to one of those four. So now let's go through a game in order, starting in the dressing room, with your kit.

Fifth idea. Get your helmet, your face protection and the rest of your kit right before the warm-up.

Your kit can cost you misconducts in Britain, and two misconducts end your game. So the time to fix your kit is before you step on, not at the first whistle.

Start with your chin strap. Tighten it until no more than one finger fits between the strap and your chin. Under the In-House Rules the limit is two and a half centimetres, which is about one finger.

Here is why it matters to the whole team. The first loose strap on your team sends that player off to fix it, and it counts as a warning for everyone. After that, a loose strap is a ten-minute misconduct to whoever has it. So a teammate's mistake can use up your warning. And a loose strap is how a helmet comes off.

If your helmet comes off during play, leave the ice straight away. Don't wait for a whistle. Under the In-House Rules, playing on bare-headed is a misconduct. Keep your helmet on in the handshake line at the end of the game too. A bare head there is a misconduct as well, with a warning to your coach.

In the Elite League, the warm-up is refereed too. A standby referee oversees it. Referees there can call any penalty they see from the start of the warm-up until half an hour after the game. So treat the warm-up as part of the game.

Somebody now checks your kit before the warm-up. Under the In-House Rules, your coach or manager has to check every player's helmet fit, chin strap and neck guard. Make their job easy by getting it right before they look.

If you wear a full cage, the chin cup has to sit on your chin, inside the helmet's clips. Loose or dangling cages and straps aren't allowed. The referee will send you off to fix it. If you come back with the same problem, it costs a minor. After that it climbs to misconducts, and the second of those throws you out.

Only put a cage on a helmet it was sized for. A helmet and cage that don't match the maker's sizing count as dangerous equipment. You are taken off until it is fixed. This is the rule that catches second-hand kit. So check the maker's sizing before you fit a cage to a helmet you already own.

As a junior skater, wear a cage. That includes juniors who are eighteen or over. A cage meets every British rule. A full visor, the clear shield over your whole face, doesn't meet England Ice Hockey's rule that juniors wear cages. So a cage settles it.

In university hockey, every player wears at least a half visor, which is a shield over the top half of your face. Scottish recreational and university hockey say the same. That is a minimum, so a cage or a full visor is fine too. And any player under eighteen wears full face protection, a cage or a full visor, in every game. That includes a university game against a team that fields an under-eighteen.

In women's hockey, a player may wear a half visor only from the start of the season after their eighteenth birthday. Before that, wear full face protection. And at an IIHF event, full face protection applies whatever your age.

Now the general rule for kit, and it applies everywhere in Britain, the Elite League included. It is the IIHF's own rule, and the Elite League's Casebook copies it word for word. That is why it reaches every British player.

If the referee tells you a piece of your kit doesn't meet the rules, go and fix it. Argue afterwards if you still want to. Here is why. After a warning, refusing is a minor. Coming back on the ice without changing it is a misconduct. And if that happens a third time, it is a game misconduct. What moves you up that ladder is refusing, and coming back out unchanged.

Under the In-House Rules your jersey has to cover your pads, elbow pads included. After a first warning, an uncovered pad is a ten-minute misconduct. So check your elbow pads are under your sleeves.

And jewellery. The Elite League bans it outright, adults included. Elsewhere, no British rule we found mentions it. Don't read that silence as permission, because the referee can still order any piece of equipment changed. Tape it or take it off.

Last thing about kit. Keep your neck guard on while you are on the bench. In junior hockey and in the WNIHL it is required there too. A junior who isn't playing that night can only sit on the bench in a helmet with full face protection, a neck guard and upper-body protection. The WNIHL says the same of its under-sixteens who aren't playing. Otherwise they have to leave the bench.

Let me pull your kit together. Chin strap to one finger, before the warm-up. Leave the ice if your helmet or your neck guard comes off. Cage on a helmet it was sized for, and a cage as a junior. Pads under your jersey. And when the referee tells you to fix something, fix it.

Now onto the ice. First the faceoff, then the net, then your stick.

Sixth idea. Keep your wingers out of the faceoff circle until the puck drops, and have a second player ready to take the draw.

Here is why. Under the In-House Rules, the linesperson replaces the centre for almost any faceoff violation. That includes a winger stepping into the circle early, because the rule removes the centre, not the player who moved. So a winger's early step costs your team the player you chose to take that draw.

The one exception is the draw after an icing. Icing is shooting the puck from your own half all the way across their goal line, untouched. Don't read that exception as a free draw. A second violation by your team on that same draw is a bench minor, a two-minute penalty against your team. So treat one attempt as all you have.

In the Elite League the same thing happens on most draws, because the newer edition of the IIHF book replaces the centre too. A winger in the circle still costs your centre the dot there. So keep the wingers out wherever you play.

That is also why you need a second player ready. When the centre is replaced, somebody has to step in at a moment's notice. Know who that is before the draw, not after the linesperson waves your centre out.

Centres, two things at the dot change by competition. Under the In-House Rules, either centre may put their stick down first. So you don't have to wait for the other centre to go first. And the whole of your blade has to be in the white part of the circle, not just the toe or the heel.

In the Elite League, the order is fixed instead. The defending centre's stick goes down first, except at centre ice, where the visiting centre's does. And there, the toe of the blade in the white is enough.

Seventh idea. Keep out of the blue paint, and stay off the goalie, inside the crease and out of it.

The crease includes the line painted around it, and it goes up as high as the top of the crossbar. So keep your skates off that line, not just out of the blue.

A goalie outside the crease is not fair game. Any contact with a goalie can be a penalty, and it can get you thrown out of the game. That is true wherever the goalie is, and whether or not a goal goes in. Here is why it is treated so seriously. A goalie who is down or turned can't protect themselves, so contact can hurt them. And it can cost your team the goal. If you start contact with a goalie outside the crease, beyond the accidental kind, the goal comes off.

You don't even have to touch a goalie to be penalised. Say the goalie has come out to play the puck. If you stop them getting back to their crease on purpose, that can be goaltender interference on its own. So leave the goalie a path home.

And if the goalie is trying to get set, give ground.

At the net, keep your head up and your chin off your chest. A goal post can hurt you the same way the boards can.

In the Elite League, the League's own guidance says contact in the blue paint is enough to disallow a goal. Read that for what it says. It is what is enough to wipe a goal out. It is not a line beyond which contact is free. The referee weighs what the contact was more than where it happened.

And in EIH and SIHA competition, there is no video to look at a net-front call again. It is decided once, on the ice. So stay clear of the goalie, and leave the referee nothing to decide.

Eighth idea. The trapezoid. Goalies, play as though it binds. Attackers, plan as though their goalie may come out.

The trapezoid is the shape painted behind each net, narrow at the goal line and wider at the end boards. It marks where a goalie may play the puck behind their net. A goalie who plays the puck outside it can take two minutes.

England Ice Hockey suspended that rule, to give rinks time to repaint. That is why so many British rinks have no trapezoid painted. But the suspension sits in an older edition of its rules. Whether it still stands, and how far it reaches, is unsettled. And the Elite League has not suspended it. So it may be live where you play. Don't go by the paint. The rule turns on where the puck is, not on the markings. It matters because it decides where your goalie may go to stop a puck rimmed around the boards, several times a game.

Here is how to play it. Goalies, play as though it binds, wherever you are. For you that costs nothing, and the alternative is two minutes. Attackers, plan the other way round. Assume their goalie may come out for a dumped puck.

If you race anyone to that puck, play the puck, not the player. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call. And if you can see a player's numbers, whether they have the puck or are just going for it, pull up. If it is the goalie you are racing, play the puck, never the goalie. A goalie out of the crease is still not fair game, and any contact with them can be a penalty and can get you thrown out. And ask your league whether the trapezoid applies.

One separate rule stands, whatever happens to the trapezoid. A goalie who freezes the puck, or plays it so as to stop play on purpose, outside the crease, gets a minor for delay of game. The suspension never touched that. Coming out to cut down the angle, and covering the puck after making the save, is fine.

Ninth idea. Keep your stick below every opponent's shoulders, and lift sticks the safe way.

Stick contact above an opponent's shoulders is at least a minor. If it injures their neck, face or head, it is a double minor, which is four minutes. It doesn't have to draw blood to count. And a reckless one can be a major, which throws you out.

Here is why the newer edition of the rulebook matters here. Under it, a stick that was never carried high can still cost four minutes if it injures someone. And the height that counts is measured on the player you hit, not on you. A stick barely above waist height is high-sticking if it catches an opponent bent over the puck, in the face. So near someone's head, there is no safe height. The IIHF's own summary of what changed mentions only the narrower half, so don't rely on a summary. Play to the stricter reading.

When you lift an opponent's stick, lift it up, not sideways. Go low on the shaft, well below their bottom hand, and never at their hands. Then let go. Never a chop. And never lift at a player who is bent over the puck.

Tenth idea. If your stick breaks, drop it straight away.

Playing on with a broken stick is a minor. Under the In-House Rules, even skating with it, anywhere on the ice, is a minor. So don't carry it back to the bench. Drop it where it broke.

Playing without a stick is fine. Get a replacement handed to you from your bench, or by a teammate on the ice. Or pick up your own or a teammate's unbroken stick. Never have one thrown to you, because that is a penalty in itself. And never play with an opponent's stick or a goalie's.

Eleventh idea, and it is short. Look at your own rink before you plan around its size.

The IIHF book's rink is sixty metres long, and twenty-six to thirty metres wide. The In-House Rules allow rinks smaller than fifty-six metres by twenty-six, at every level. So a British rink can be shorter, narrower, or both.

Here is why that changes your game. On a smaller sheet the distances shrink, but the angles don't. So everything arrives sooner. Check your own rink before you assume the distances in any coaching video apply to it.

Let me pull the faceoff, the net and your stick together. Wingers out of the circle until the puck drops, and a second player ready for the draw. Out of the blue paint and off its line, and off the goalie, in the crease and out of it. Leave the goalie a path home. Head up at the net. Goalies, play as though the trapezoid binds. Attackers, expect their goalie to come out, and play the puck, never the goalie. Keep your stick below every shoulder, and lift sticks up, low on the shaft, then let go. Drop a broken stick where it broke. And know how big your rink really is.

Now, keeping yourself on the ice.

Twelfth idea. Stay on your feet. Under the In-House Rules, a dive costs twelve minutes, not two.

A dive, or embellishment, means faking a foul, or making a fall or an injury look worse, to win a penalty. Under the In-House Rules it is a minor plus a ten-minute misconduct, called two plus ten. You sit for twelve minutes. Your team is only short for two, because a substitute serves the minor for you. But the misconduct counts against you. So a dive plus one other misconduct, a missing neck guard, say, ends your game.

Goalies, this counts for you too. A goalie never goes to the box. Teammates serve both halves. But the misconduct is still recorded against the goalie, and a second one in the game puts them out. A teammate or the backup then takes the net. So keep your own count, because there are no ten minutes in the box to remind you.

In the Elite League a dive is a plain minor. But the League can also fine a player for diving afterwards, whatever the call on the ice was.

Thirteenth idea. A new name for a foul is not a cheaper call.

Referees under the In-House Rules have been told to call each foul by what it is. A player tripped without the puck is called tripping. And a cross-check to the head or neck is now called cross-checking, not checking to the head.

Don't hear that as the lesser call. Cross-checking and a check to the head are both on the list that throws you out with the major. So a cross-check to the head carries exactly the same risk, whichever name the referee uses. The new names don't change what is legal, either. They only change what the call is called. In particular, this is not a rule about whether you may check. That is still your league's question.

So don't cross-check at all. Never shove with both hands on your stick. Keeping your blade on the ice doesn't make it legal. A cross-check into the back of a player who doesn't see it coming is checking from behind, and there is no two-minute version of that. Plan on a major, and on being thrown out.

Fourteenth idea. Take the referee's call as the call.

In EIH and SIHA competition there is no on-ice video review, and no coach's challenge. The call on the ice is the call.

Here is what that means for how you play. A goal that goes in off a skate is decided by what the officials saw, once. Play to the whistle, and keep playing until you hear it. Don't stop to appeal for a goal or a penalty, because there is nothing to appeal to. If you think a call was wrong, let it go, and get on with the next shift.

The Elite League is different. It keeps both video review and the coach's challenge. So know which kind of game you are in. And in both kinds, video is still used after the game, to look at incidents again. It can reach an incident nobody penalised at the time.

And a major is not the end of it. The referee reports every major after the game. Every game misconduct goes to a body that can add more. And the authorities can act on an incident whether or not the referee penalised it at the time.

That further punishment is called supplementary discipline, and it can mean a ban for games after this one, too. In the Elite League it is the League's own Department of Player Safety that acts. Under the In-House Rules it is the home association's disciplinary process. What it actually costs isn't published under the In-House Rules. So treat a major as the start of a process, not the whole bill.

Fifteenth idea. When a fight or a scuffle starts, stay on the bench, or stay in the box, until an official releases you.

Not to help a teammate. Not to change. Not because your penalty has just run out. This applies at every age and in every competition. It is the IIHF's own rule, and the British documents don't soften it. Playing in the NIHL changes what a fight costs you, not what leaving the bench costs you.

Here is why the bar is so low. In the rulebook, an altercation needs only one penalty, not one punch. So a scrum after the whistle, where somebody takes a roughing minor, counts. From that moment the bench door and the penalty box are shut.

Leaving costs a lot. The first or second player off the bench during an altercation gets a game misconduct. Being third out isn't a free pass, because nobody is allowed to go. A penalised player who leaves the box during an altercation gets a minor and a game misconduct. That includes a player who does nothing but skate around it. Skating around it is not staying out of it.

Your time running out isn't a release either. You may only leave the box when your penalty is over and your team is entitled to an extra player on the ice. When both teams have penalties running, those two moments often don't line up. So stay in the box until an official waves you out.

In the Elite League, leaving the bench to join an altercation also brings an automatic suspension of at least three games.

Sixteenth idea. Don't fight, even when someone else starts it. Expect a fight to end your game.

Under the In-House Rules, a fight between two willing players throws you out, with one exception. The NIHL, the SNL and university Varsity games treat that plain fight more lightly, as a five-minute major. But not in the last five minutes of an NIHL game, or in its overtime. Not in women's hockey, where you should plan on any major ending your game. And in junior hockey any major ends your game anyway.

Don't hear that as permission. Under the In-House Rules, everything around a fight still keeps its full price, and that price is being thrown out. Starting the fight. Being the aggressor. A sucker punch. Fighting before or after the periods. And joining a fight that is already going, even to break it up, can get you thrown out too, at the referee's call.

Even if you didn't want the fight, punching back can still cost you. In the NIHL, the SNL and Varsity games, a player dragged into a fight they didn't want can still get a minor or a five-minute major, at the referee's call. And late in an NIHL game, in the last five minutes or overtime, that major throws them out too. So don't count on a few punches back being free.

When a linesperson steps in, stop. Carrying on after you have been told to stop, or resisting a linesperson, can get you thrown out.

Under the newer edition of the rulebook, a grudge from an earlier game, even one from months ago, can be what makes you the instigator. So don't bring an old score into a new game.

The Elite League writes its own fighting rule, and it is lighter on the night but heavier afterwards. A first plain fight during play there need not end your game. Don't hear that as permission. It is at least a major, and at least means the referee can give you more. A fight in the warm-up or after the buzzer still throws you out. A second fight on the same whistle ends your game. So do two majors of any kind. And the League doesn't say whether it counts fighting majors across the whole season, so play as though it does.

Starting a fight in the Elite League is a minor, a major and a ten-minute misconduct, and it goes to the League for review. Starting a second one, or being the aggressor as well, throws you out. In the last five minutes or overtime, starting one throws you out, with a one-game suspension pending that review. And keep your jersey on and tied down there. Taking it off before a fight, or losing it untied, can get you thrown out.

Seventeenth idea. In women's hockey and junior hockey, plan on a major ending your game.

In women's hockey a major throws you out at every age, adults included. Read junior hockey the same way, from under-nineteen down to under-ten. That junior reading is the safe reading of the rule rather than its exact words. Confirm both with your league.

Women's hockey also has its own contact line in the IIHF book. Any step or glide into an opponent is at least a minor for an illegal hit. So is using the boards to take an opponent out of the play, to push them into the boards, or to pin them there.

Here is how to play it. If an opponent is standing still, skate around them. That ice is theirs. And if you are carrying the puck straight at an opponent, avoiding the contact is your job. WNIHL games are non-checking. In any other game, ask before you hit anyone.

Let me pull keeping yourself on the ice together. Stay on your feet, because under the In-House Rules a dive is twelve minutes and a misconduct. A cross-check to the head called cross-checking is no cheaper. Never shove with both hands on your stick. Play to the whistle, because in EIH and SIHA games the call on the ice is the call. If an altercation starts, stay on the bench or in the box until an official releases you. Don't fight, and expect a fight to end your game. And in women's and junior hockey, plan on any major ending it.

Now, how your age group and your competition change the game.

Eighteenth idea. Take your age group from this season's rules, and nowhere else.

Junior age groups in Britain are set by birth year, and they are the same for boys and girls. The bands move by a full year every season. So a player born in two thousand and ten was under-sixteen last season and is under-nineteen this one.

Here is why that matters so much. A table copied from last season puts a child in the wrong age group. In a collision sport, the gap between a fourteen-year-old and an eighteen-year-old is the whole point of having age groups. So take the bands from the current season's In-House Rules, and nowhere else.

Nineteenth idea. At under-twelve in IHUK's junior leagues, play on without waiting for an icing or offside whistle, and clear the zone at once when the official calls your team out.

Under-twelve is a different game, and neither British rulebook describes it. It is set out in IHUK's junior competition rules.

There are no icing calls at under-twelve. Nothing is whistled, and nothing comes back. But giving the puck away down the ice is still giving it away.

An offside doesn't stop play either. Offside means an attacker going into the attacking zone ahead of the puck. Instead of a whistle, the official calls your team out of the zone, for example, Reds out. The whole team has to get back out over the line before going in again. Do it at once. Not clearing quickly is a penalty for delay of game. So when you hear the call, everyone turns and skates out together.

The clock keeps running. A game is three periods of eighteen minutes on three lines, or twenty minutes on four. And a buzzer changes the lines every sixty seconds. When the buzzer goes, leave the puck where it is. Moving it on purpose gets a warning, and then a minor.

You play on the line you were put on. Your coach hands in the lines before the game, and each line is marked, with numbered jerseys, armbands or helmet tape. So wear whatever marking your team uses. Moving up a line is only allowed for an injury or an ejection. Jumping on with the wrong line is a minor the first time.

At under-twelve a minor is three minutes, or four in a four-line game, and you serve it on your own line's shifts. The rules don't say whether that time runs on the game clock or only while your line is on the ice. Nor do they say whether your team plays a player short while you sit. So ask your coach or the officials before the game.

Two penalties don't cancel each other out at under-twelve, the way they can in an older game. A goal in the same shift ends a minor early, but not a major. And a major with a game misconduct sends you off straight away, with the five minutes left unserved.

Under-ten is different again. It is played across the ice or on half the ice, the lines change on a buzzer, and there is no body checking. After a goal, a line change or a frozen puck, the faceoff goes back to centre. And penalties are explained to the player rather than written down. Nothing written sets an icing or offside rule for under-ten. So ask the coach running the day how they are restarting play.

From under-fourteen up, it is the ordinary full-ice, stop-clock game.

Twentieth idea. Find out before the game how a tie is settled, because overtime isn't played everywhere.

Under the In-House Rules, overtime is only played in NIHL games, and in SNL games whose competition rules provide for it. University games marked Varsity OT on the schedule follow the NIHL rules for overtime too. Plenty of other games simply end level.

Where an NIHL game does go to overtime, it is five minutes of three-on-three. Then comes a shootout with three shooters a side. If it is still level, it goes to one shot each until somebody wins. Whether one player may shoot twice differs between the NIHL leagues and the SNL. So name your three shooters before the game, and ask which version is yours before you plan on your best shooter taking two of the shots.

At the NIHL National playoff finals weekend, a level semi-final gets twenty minutes of five-on-five, then a shootout. The final never goes to a shootout. It plays twenty-minute periods of five-on-five until somebody scores. So plan your bench and your legs for as many extra periods as it takes.

In the Elite League, plan for five shooters, not three. A level regular-season game plays five minutes of three-on-three, then a five-shooter shootout. Its knockout games have their own formats, so check yours.

Let me pull the age groups and formats together. Take your age group from this season's rules. At under-twelve, play on without waiting for an icing or offside whistle. Clear the zone the moment your team is called out. Leave the puck at the buzzer, and stay on your own line. Under-ten plays on a smaller sheet with no checking. And find out before the game how a tie is settled, and how many shooters you need.

Now the last part, and it is the one to learn before you need it. What to do at the rink when someone is badly hurt. You may never need this, but it is worth knowing calmly in advance.

Twenty-first idea. Before you need it, know who the first aider is, where the phone is, and what the plan is.

At an England Ice Hockey game, five things are required. A first aider, though the rule sets no qualification for that person. A first aid kit, checked before each game. A risk assessment and an emergency action plan, both shared with the visiting team. And a telephone to call the emergency services.

A doctor or physio is only recommended, so check the team sheet to see whether one is there. And an ambulance waiting at the rink is not required. So a game with a first aider, a phone, no ambulance and no doctor is exactly what the rule allows. That is why the phone is on the list.

Whether that rule reaches the Elite League, Scotland or Northern Ireland, the documents don't say. It sets nothing for practice either. Every registered coach must hold a basic first aid certificate, but that tells you nothing about the building. So wherever you play, ask your club what cover your rink actually has. Before the game, find out who the first aider is, where the phone is, and whether your team has been given the rink's emergency action plan. Ask the same at an away rink.

Twenty-second idea. A knock to the head.

Treat every knock to the head as a possible neck injury too. Don't help the player up, and don't move them, except as far as you need to keep their airway clear. Leave the helmet on. If anything looks serious, like being knocked out, a fit, neck pain, or numbness or tingling, phone for an ambulance first. In the United Kingdom that is nine nine nine.

Even if nothing looks serious, a player with a suspected concussion comes off, and doesn't come back that day. Not that shift, not that period, not that game. A medical professional decides when they play again, not the player. A concussed player will often tell you they are fine.

Afterwards, someone stays with them. They don't drive, and they don't go home on their own. If they get worse, or their neck hurts, get medical help straight away, even though they got up.

Here is when to call which number. The UK concussion guidance for grassroots sport, which England Ice Hockey has adopted, gives a list of red flags. Call nine nine nine for any red flag, or any other serious injury. For a knock to the head with no red flag, call one one one, the NHS line, for an assessment within twenty-four hours. And don't leave the player alone for those twenty-four hours.

The red flags apply to a player who has taken a blow to the head, or a blow to the body that moved the head. There are fourteen. Learn all of them, because a partial list teaches you to stop looking. Any one of them, reported or seen, means call nine nine nine.

One. Any loss of consciousness because of the injury.

Two. Consciousness getting worse, the player getting more drowsy.

Three. No memory of what happened before or after the injury.

Four. Confusion or irritability that keeps increasing.

Five. An unusual change in behaviour.

Six. Any new problem with how the brain or nerves are working. Trouble understanding, speaking, reading or writing. Less feeling. Loss of balance. Weakness. Or double vision.

Seven. A seizure or convulsion, twitching arms or legs, or lying rigid or motionless because of muscle spasm.

Eight. A severe headache, or one that keeps getting worse.

Nine. Being sick more than once.

Ten. Severe neck pain.

Eleven. Any suspicion of a broken skull. A cut, a bruise, swelling or severe pain where they were hit is enough to suspect one.

Twelve. A history of brain surgery, or a bleeding disorder.

Thirteen. Being on blood-thinning medication.

Fourteen. Being drunk or on drugs right now.

Any one of those fourteen is enough on its own.

Three of them are facts about the player, not about the injury. Brain surgery or a bleeding disorder, blood-thinning medication, and being drunk or on drugs. They matter because they fire when nothing else has. A teammate on blood thinners who takes a knock to the head is a nine nine nine call on that alone. That holds even if they get up and tell you they're fine. So find out in advance who on your team is on one.

There is also an international list, from the Concussion Recognition Tool, and in places it differs. Where the two differ, act on whichever fires first. Neither list says that. It is what reading them side by side leaves you with, and it can only make you call sooner.

So call nine nine nine for any neck pain, without waiting for it to be severe. Don't wait for a skull to look out of shape, because any suspicion of a break is enough. And a player who goes stiff and still after a hit to the head needs an ambulance, even without a fit. It can look like being knocked out.

If a player is lying still, check whether they respond. If you can't get a response, call nine nine nine. You don't have to tell a rigid player from an unconscious one, because both are on the list.

A player lying still but awake and answering you still needs watching. Call nine nine nine for neck pain, stiffness or any other red flag. If there is none, they may not need an ambulance. But don't move them or get them up. Let the rink's first aider or medical cover assess them where they lie.

If a neck injury is suspected, phone for help first. Only trained healthcare professionals should move the player. Don't move them yourself, except as far as keeping their airway clear needs. And leave the helmet on.

Now coming back. The British guidance says no return to competition before twenty-one days from the injury. The day of the injury counts as day zero. That is a floor, not a date, and not a clearance. The medical assessment still has to happen. And a clearance that arrives sooner doesn't put you in a game sooner.

Training with a risk of a head injury waits until you have had no symptoms at rest for fourteen days, and not before day fifteen. The only players who can be cleared to compete sooner are elite athletes, managed by sports concussion specialists. Grassroots hockey is not that.

Let me pull it all together.

Your rulebook is the IIHF's, with the In-House Rules or the Elite League's Casebook on top. Ask which edition your officials carry, and play to the stricter one.

Wear your neck guard every time you go on the ice, and assume there is no warning. If it comes off during play, leave the ice without picking it up. Tighten your chin strap to one finger, and leave the ice if your helmet comes off.

Don't hit anyone until your league tells you your age group checks.

Play as though your first major ends your game, adult or not, and once you have one misconduct, as though the next one does. Never hit a player who can't see you coming, and never from behind. Never hit a player in the head. Never shove with both hands on your stick. Keep your stick below every shoulder. Stay on your feet, because under the In-House Rules a dive is a misconduct.

Keep your wingers out of the circle, and have a second player ready for the draw. Stay out of the blue paint and off the goalie, in the crease and out of it. Goalies, play as though the trapezoid binds.

Play to the whistle. If an altercation starts, stay on the bench or in the box until an official releases you. And don't fight.

After a knock to the head, treat it as a possible neck injury. Don't move the player, leave the helmet on, and phone for an ambulance if anything looks serious. A suspected concussion means off for the day, and a medical professional decides when they play again. Someone stays with them, and they don't drive or go home alone. Learn all fourteen red flags. Any one red flag means nine nine nine. If they get worse, or their neck hurts, get help straight away, even though they got up.

Much of this depends on where you play. So here are three questions for your coach. Who takes the draw if our centre is replaced? Who is our first aider, where is the phone, and what cover does our rink have? And how are ties settled in our games, and who are our three shooters?

And three for your league. Which British rules do we play under, and which edition do the referees carry? Is my age group checking? And has a rule bulletin been issued this season? A bulletin can change a rule without changing the published documents.

Go and ask. Their answers tell you which rules are yours, and now you know why each one changes how you play.

### 7. Departures from the source and the template

- **Template shape.** The template runs the rink one end at a time. A rules page has no ends, so after the spine this script runs a game in order: kit before you step on, then the faceoff, the net and your stick, then keeping yourself on the ice, then age-group and competition formats, then injury. The vocabulary block, four-idea spine, recap per part, close and "three questions" are kept. The close also has three questions for the league, because the source's own repeated instruction is "ask your league".
- **Sibling-traced sentences.** The goalie injury reason (brief item 24), the from-behind injury reason, the goal-post head-up limb, the dumped-puck chase gate and the foul glosses come from `body_contact_and_battles.md` or the brief, not from `uk_rules.md`. The aftercare lines come from `conditioning_and_recovery.md`. The minor, major, misconduct, checking and faceoff definitions are generic vocabulary carried from the reviewed remake.
- **Derived, not quoted:** "keep your skates off that line" (from the line belonging to the crease, :205); "so you don't have to wait for the other centre to go first" (from either centre may go first, :270); "a goal that goes in off a skate is decided by what the officials saw, once" (from Q3 :544 plus :41); "In the United Kingdom that is nine nine nine".
- **The ban paragraph comes before the foul definitions** in idea four. It was moved there so no seam can separate the definitions from the bans, and so the listener hears what to do before what the fouls are called.
- **Gender.** The source's women's-hockey contact line says "her" and "hers" (:35). The script says "them" and "theirs", and "an opponent".
- **Corrections against the remake, from the current source:** the women's half visor is now stated as the rule (:223), where the remake said the rules only "appear" to allow it. The awake-and-answering player is now "don't move them or get them up, let the first aider assess them where they lie" (:476), where the remake said that player "comes off the ice". The remake's "true in both editions" grudge claim is now scoped to the newer edition (:136). The remake's clipping gloss, "across or below a player's knees", is now "low, on a player's legs" (brief item 17).
- **The fighting section is compressed.** The plain willing fight is now stated as "throws you out under the In-House Rules, with one exception", following the source's "a willing combatant's fighting major carries an automatic game misconduct" (:304). The remake said "a fighting major throws you out", which reached the unwilling combatant too.

### 8. What I found unclear in the source

- **KT13 (:638) states flatly that "In women's hockey a major ejects at every age, adult included."** The body (:373) says no official British interpretation has been obtained and tells the reader to "Plan on it". The script follows the body's hedge ("Confirm both with your league").
- **:300 against :304.** :300 tells a women's player to plan on any major ending the game because Section 6 never says whether its NIHL scope reaches a women's fixture. :304 says the In-House softening "reaches those three competitions and no others". Both lead to the same instruction, and the script voices it once.
- **Overview :41 says "no on-ice video review in EIH or SIHA competition"; :490 says Northern Ireland's scope under that wording is open.** The source does not say whether a Northern Irish In-House game has video review. The script keeps the rule's own scope and does not guess.
- The injury part has no recap of its own. Its recap is the close's head-knock paragraph, and that paragraph lands at the start of request 6, intact.

### 9. Where this script is weakest

- **Length is +28.0%.** It was grown from reasons and source detail. A cut of more than about 1,100 characters would take it under the 25% floor.
- **The rules share is 28.2%** on the broad regex. That is lower than the remake's 31.9%, but it is still high, because this is a rules page. Most of what remains is consequence sentences ("is a minor", "throws you out"), each attached to an instruction.
- **The fighting section is still tariff-shaped,** especially the two Elite League paragraphs. They are the first cut on the list.
- **The fourteen red flags take about 2.5 minutes.** The owner asked for brief, calm warnings. The source says a partial list "teaches you to stop looking" (:449), so the whole list stays, in plain words and without graphic detail.
- **The seam window is narrow** (9,100–9,300). Any edit moves it.
- **The review fixes in §0 have not been re-reviewed.** Every tariff was checked against `uk_rules.md` wording, not against the primary rulebooks.
