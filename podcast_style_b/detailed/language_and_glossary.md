Here is the thing to hold on to from the start. Hockey's working vocabulary is small, and almost all of it is relative. High, low, strong side, F one. Stay in your lane, because three wide forwards stretch a defence, unless your team stacks a lane on purpose. A shape name can mean either end of the ice, so ask. The wall is usually the boards, but behind the wall means behind a line of players. Regroup rather than force a bad entry. Keep your blade on the ice at the blue line, and tag up as a five. Find out whether your icing is a race. And every chase, whether it's a dump-in, the trap or an icing race, is the puck, not the player, and pull up if you can see their numbers.

Fourteenth idea. Their end, and the forecheck words.

You know F one, F two and F three. Here are the words for what they do.

Angling means forcing an opponent to go where you've chosen, usually toward the boards or the outside. It's the base of forechecking. Where body checking isn't allowed, angling is the whole toolkit, not a beginner's version of hitting.

Steering means shadowing the puck carrier to push them into an area where you can take away their time and space. Sealing means arriving second and closing the escape route the first forechecker has forced them toward.

All three are pressure, so they all carry the gate. Stick on the puck, not a hit. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call. If you can see their numbers, pull up. Steer them toward the boards with your skating. And never put an arm, shoulder or hip into them at the end of the angle. Never push or hit anyone into the boards, and never from behind, because they can't protect themselves. Many games have no body checking at all. In women's hockey, contact while you're both playing the puck can happen. But any step or glide into a player is a penalty, and so is pinning or pushing them into the boards.

A pinch is a defenceman stepping down from the blue line in the other team's end, into their zone, to keep the puck in. It's fairly safe, because your forwards are still behind you to recover. But it's still a challenge, so the same gate rides with it. Stick on the puck, not a hit. Your league decides whether you may hit. If you can see their numbers, pull up.

A pinch and a step-up are different plays on different ice. A pinch has your forwards behind you. A step-up has nothing behind you. Know which one you're being asked for.

On most teams only one defenceman pinches at a time, while the other holds the middle as the safety, so somebody stays back. It's a near-universal coaching habit, not a rule. Some teams give both defencemen a standing green light. And every team sends both on a delayed penalty, a full-ice press, or six-on-five with the goalie pulled.

Activation means a defenceman joining the attack as an extra forward. The usual condition is that a forward fills in behind you, so somebody stays above the puck. Being behind late in a game makes a team accept more risk. But on its own it isn't a reason for you to go. What lifts that cover requirement is a call from the bench, not your own read of the scoreboard.

Fifteenth idea. Keeping the puck in their end.

For a winger, the half-wall is home base when your team has the puck in their zone. From there you can see the point, the slot, the corner and the net front. For a centre at even strength, it's not your ice. It's where the wingers live, and drifting onto it squashes your own line's spacing.

A cycle is keeping the puck in their zone by moving it round the low part of the zone with short passes, while protecting it. The corner, the half-wall, behind the net. Here's why it works. It wears defenders out and pulls their coverage out of shape. It's a set-up, not a finish.

The attack triangle is the shape three forwards make when your team has the puck in their end. One is below the puck, one level with it, and one above it. That gives the attack width and depth at once. It's a coaching choice, not a law. An overload that loads one side, a five-player rotation and a one-three-one spread are real alternatives. Find out which yours plays.

The top of the circle is the edge of a faceoff circle furthest from the goal line, about thirty-five feet out. It's a common landmark for where a shot starts to become dangerous. It's also where a defenceman usually stands to support a cycle.

Walking the line means a player at the point skating sideways along the blue line. It changes the shooting angle and pulls the defence out of shape.

Sixteenth idea. Words for making plays.

A tape-to-tape pass arrives flat on the taped part of your teammate's blade, ready to play straight away.

A saucer pass is flipped so the puck flies flat, off the ice, and lands flat. Use it to get over a defender's stick, or a body in the passing lane.

A give-and-go means passing to a teammate and skating straight into open space to get it back. It's the simplest way to beat a defender without stickhandling.

A one-timer is a shot taken straight off a pass, without stopping the puck. It works because the goalie can't reset between the pass and the shot. A one-touch is a pass played the same way. It beats coverage, because every extra touch gives the defence time to reset. For both, pick your target before the puck arrives, and don't play a bouncing puck first time. A missed one usually ends up going the other way.

The royal road is an imaginary line down the middle of the ice, from the middle of one net to the middle of the other. Nothing is painted for it. A pass that crosses it just before a shot forces the goalie into a hard sideways push, so the shot arrives before they're set. Shot data tracked by volunteers rates it the most valuable kind of pass they measured, about twice the next one.

A deke is a single fake with the puck, your body or your head, to make a defender or the goalie commit the wrong way. A dangle is the same idea, usually a longer run of moves. And a wrap-around is carrying the puck from behind the net round the post, and trying to stuff it in at the far side before the goalie can seal. In close to a goalie, whichever you try, go round, never through. Pull up short of the goalie. Head up. Contact with a goalie can get you thrown out of the game.

Seventeenth idea. The net front in their end.

Stay out of the paint is the instruction. Live just outside the paint is the skill.

Treat the red line around the crease as part of it, and keep your skates off that line too. Off the line is the only ice that's safe in every league. And keep your stick out of the crease as well as your feet. In some leagues, a goal comes off if your stick is in the crease while the goalie is in there, even with both your feet outside it. Nothing on the ice tells you which kind of league you're in, so keep all of you out.

A screen means standing in the goalie's line of sight so they can't see the shot. Screening from outside the crease is normally legal. Stay outside it, off the red line, with your stick out, and don't touch the goalie. Keep your head up and your chin off your chest, because the post can hurt you the same way the boards can.

Don't mix up a screen with traffic. Traffic is any bodies between the goalie and the puck, from either team. A screen is an opponent standing in the goalie's line of sight. Your own defenceman can be traffic without being a screen.

A screen is something you do to a goalie. Doing it to a skater is called a pick, and a pick is a penalty, not a play. Hockey is not basketball. There's no legal pick on a skater. A pick means checking a player without the puck who doesn't see it coming, or moving into their path when you didn't have the spot first. That's interference, and it can cost more than two minutes.

What you may do is hold the ice you're already standing on, without stepping into anyone's path. Never plant yourself in a defender's path to free your puck carrier, and never stop in their path to take the contact. Calling it a screen doesn't make it legal.

A net drive means skating hard at the net without the puck. Here's why it's worth doing. It drags a defender with you. It makes a rebound or a deflection possible, which changes how far out the goalie plays. And it pulls the defence toward the net, which opens the high slot and the point behind you. A half-hearted glide does none of that.

But the drive stops at the blue paint. Drive under control. Keep both feet and your stick outside the crease. Pull up short of the goalie, and keep your head up, chin off your chest. Crashing the net for a rebound fits with all of that. Shoot it at once, pull up, and play the puck, not the goalie. Never start contact with the goalie, inside the crease or outside it. Contact with a goalie can be a penalty and can get you thrown out of the game.

A screen is not a licence to stand in a goalie's way home, either. Say the goalie has come out of the crease and played the puck. If you deliberately stop them getting back, that can be a penalty with no contact at all. In some leagues a goal your team scores off it comes off the board as well.

A tip-in means redirecting a shot, usually one from the point, with your stick blade as it goes past. It changes direction late, so the goalie can't adjust.

There's a ceiling on how high you can tip it, and what counts is where the puck touches your stick. In many leagues the ceiling is the height of the crossbar. If the puck touches your stick above that and goes in, it's no goal. In some leagues the ceiling is lower, at your shoulder. So the simple habit works everywhere. Keep your blade below your shoulders, and aim to tip at knee, waist or chest height.

Let me pull their end together.

F one is whoever gets there first. Angle, steer and seal with your skating. Stick on the puck, not a hit, and never push or hit anyone from behind. Pull up if you can see their numbers. A pinch has your forwards behind you, and only one defenceman goes at a time on most teams. Activate only when a forward fills in behind you. The half-wall is a winger's home and, at even strength, not a centre's ice. Cycle to wear them down. Stay out of the paint and off its red line, and keep your stick out too. A screen is for a goalie, never for a skater. Drive the net under control, pull up short of the goalie, head up. Tip below your shoulders. And never into the goalie, because that can get you thrown out.

Eighteenth idea. Power play and penalty kill words.

The power play is playing with an extra skater while the other team serves a penalty. There are three common shapes.

The one-three-one has one player at the point, two on the half-walls, one in the slot and one in front of the net. The player in the slot is called the bumper. Don't confuse the bumper with the net-front player, who is right at the goalmouth.

The umbrella has three players high across the top and two low. A true umbrella has no bumper.

And the overload puts four players on one side of the ice, to swamp that side and open up a weak-side option.

On the power play, the half-wall spots usually go to forwards picked for which way they shoot, rather than for their position. Ideally that's a player on their off wing, a left-handed shot on the right side or the other way round, so the puck sits on the inside. A centre can be on one, or down low instead. Ask where your unit puts you.

The quarterback is the player running the power play from the top. In a one-three-one, that's the single point player. The job is moving the puck and walking the line, not just shooting.

The penalty kill is playing a player short. There are three common shapes there too.

The box is two forwards high and two defencemen low.

The diamond has one player at the top. Here's why a team uses it. Against a one-three-one, a box leaves the bumper unmarked, and the diamond covers it.

The wedge plus one is three players in a tight triangle round the net, plus one more, the plus one, who pressures whoever has the puck. When the puck moves, the nearest wedge player steps out as the new plus one, and the old one drops back in. So the plus one pressures the puck rather than following it round the outside. How the plus one moves is a coaching choice, and published descriptions differ, so ask which one your team runs.

When you're shorthanded, meaning playing with fewer skaters because of a penalty, you're allowed to ice the puck. It's the single most useful rule fact on the penalty kill. Count skaters, not the penalty clock. You may ice it while you have fewer skaters on the ice than the other team. So on a five-on-three, you still may after the first penalty runs out, because you're still one short. When you're killing a single minor, the moment it runs out, you can't. But a clear you got away a stride before that moment still isn't icing, however late it crosses the line.

One catch. In some leagues the younger age groups don't get that exemption, and a shorthanded team is called for icing like anyone else. Check yours.

Nineteenth idea. The goalie's words. These matter to skaters too, because several of them name the thing you're trying to beat.

Depth is how far out of the net the goalie is, measured along the line from the puck to the middle of the goal. Square means chest, hips and shoulders facing straight at the puck, with the middle of the body on the line from the puck to the centre of the net.

Challenging means coming out toward the shooter, to cut down how much net they can see. The opposite is staying deep, or playing back.

Challenging has a cost. When a goalie challenges, the shooter is the front door, and the space opened up behind the goalie, usually toward the far side of the net, is the back door. Every positioning choice a goalie makes trades one door against the other.

That's a goalie's back door. A skater's back door is the weak-side area at the net front, usually the far post. A backdoor pass goes across to a player arriving there unmarked. They're usually the same ice, but not always. And the shouted back door is narrower than either. It means an unmarked opponent at the far post, to pick up right now. Read the word from who is saying it.

So as a shooter, read how far out the goalie is, and whether they're square, before you choose your shot. As a goalie, pick your depth and your save on purpose.

Save selection means choosing which save to make. The word selection matters. It's a decision, not a reflex. The butterfly is a good example. It means dropping to your knees with both pads out to the sides, which closes the five-hole, the gap between a goalie's legs. It covers most of the lower part of the net, and that's the usual reason it's the base save of modern goaltending. But it's a choice, not a style. Go down when going down is the answer.

The six-hole is a different gap, between the goalie's stick arm and body. It opens when the stick's paddle is too long, or the shoulder rolls. Two different targets, with two different causes.

The blocker is the rectangular pad on the goalie's stick hand. The catching glove on the other hand is the glove, or the trapper. A shot blocker side goes to the goalie's stick hand.

The posts are the upright pipes at the sides of the net. Far post and near post are read from the puck, so they swap as it moves. When a goalie seals against a post is a coaching choice. The cautious North American default is late on and early off, sealing only for pucks right below the goal line or right on top of you. Other coaches, especially in the Swedish tradition, teach an earlier seal with a set trigger line. Both are defensible, so ask what your trigger is. What nobody defends is dropping to a post out of habit.

Put it all together and a goalie doesn't play a series of shots. A goalie plays situations, and many of them never end in a shot at all.

Let me pull it all together.

Most hockey words are relative. Before you act on one, work out what it's measured from. High is toward the blue line you're facing. Low is toward the goal line. Strong side is where the puck is, and it flips when the puck crosses the middle.

F one is whoever gets to the puck first, not the centre by default, and the numbers belong to whichever team is forechecking. Forecheck and backcheck are opposite jobs. Every chase is the puck, not the player, and if you can see their numbers, pull up. Never hit a player who can't see you coming, and never from behind. Even a light hit from behind can hurt someone's head or neck.

In your own end, find out which system you play. A winger's stay high ends the moment your team wins the puck. Keep the goalmouth and the high slot apart. Rim past a forechecker who's pinning you, reverse behind one who's gone past you. On the boards, use the same words. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the checker, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly. Stick on the puck, not a hit, and never push or hit anyone from behind.

A shape name can mean either end of the ice, so ask. Keep your blade down at the blue line, and tag up as a five.

At the net front, stay out of the paint and off its red line, and keep your stick out too. When your goalie shouts screen, hold the ice you already have, no lean and no push. Never shove with both hands on your stick, and never into anyone's back. Head up, chin off your chest. And play the puck, not the goalie, wherever they are standing. Contact with a goalie can get you thrown out of the game.

Much of this depends on what your team plays. So here are three questions to ask your coach. What system do we play in our own end, and if it's a hybrid, where is the line? Do you hand out F one by order of arrival, or by position? And on the power play, where do you want me?

And three for your league. Is body checking allowed in our games? Is icing a race, or automatic? And does the goalie have a trapezoid?

Go and ask. Then you'll know what each word means for you, and now you know why each answer makes sense.

Departure: the special-teams and goalie parts have no recap of their own (§1).
- **Grouped by situation, not alphabetically.** The source's own layout is themes then an alphabet. The script regroups by where on the ice a player meets each word (own end, middle, their end, special teams, goalie), as the caller asked.
- **"The blue lines … three zones" and "the goal line … the net sits on"** are plain glosses not defined on this page. They carry no rule.
- **"Never lift anyone off their feet"** comes from brief item 6, not from the three sources. It adds a limb, never a permission.

### 8. What I found unclear in the source

- **:39 carries a "so" trap** — "do not walk the screener out of the lane — they have no puck, so that is interference". Voiced alone it implies a puck-carrying screener may be walked out. KF :25 says it without the condition. A content row may want :39 aligned with KF :25.
- **The goalie-contact paragraphs never say "can hurt them" as the reason** for any goalie limb. The injury reason the brief requires (item 24) comes from SH :925 by analogy, not from this page.
- **"Checking" in the shoulder-check entry** (:447-449) prices the bump-shove-push minor only for Hockey Canada and the step-or-glide minor only for women's IIHF/PWHL; "even an on-purpose bump, shove or push can be a penalty where checking is banned" generalises the Hockey Canada wording. It says "can", which is true wherever the book has a no-check rule, but no other book's wording was checked here.
- **Hash marks** are "four per circle" (:335) and also "the short red lines … on the outer edge of both sides" — the script says "on the outside of each faceoff circle", as the remake did.

### 9. Where this script is weakest

- **Length: 50.5 minutes.** It is the longest Detailed script I know of in this series, because a glossary has many words. The middle third (ideas ten to thirteen) is a dense run of definitions with less "why" than the systems pages offer; the source gives few reasons for terms.

It is conservative, but a stick play near a goalie still risks contact. The next sentence's "pull up and play the puck" is what carries the safety.
