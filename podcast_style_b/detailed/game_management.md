===== SCRIPT START =====

This episode is about game management. That means how the score and the clock change the way you play. It takes its time. For each idea I will tell you what to do, and also why it works. If you know why something works, you can still make the right choice when the game looks a little different from the picture in your head.

A one-goal lead with four minutes left is a different game from a one-goal lead in the first period. So the whole episode comes back to one question. How much risk do the score and the clock allow right now?

Most of what follows is coaching choice, not rulebook. Your team may want something different, and I will say so where it matters. Rules also differ from league to league. Where a rule changes what you do, I will say it once, and tell you to check yours.

Before any instructions, a few words you will hear all the way through. I will explain them now, so they don't get in the way later.

The neutral zone. This is the middle third of the ice, between the two blue lines. Your own end is the zone with your net in it.

The slot. This is the ice right in front of the net. Chances from there are the most dangerous ones.

The point. This is the area just inside the blue line where defencemen usually stand. It is an area, not the line itself.

An odd-man rush. This is a rush where more of them are attacking than you have coming back. A two-on-one, or a three-on-two. Coaches treat these as the most dangerous chances in open play.

The forechecker. This is the player chasing you for the puck in your own end.

A change. Players swap with teammates from the bench. A change during play, without a whistle, is called a change on the fly.

And icing. Icing is shooting the puck from your own side of the centre red line all the way past their goal line, with nobody touching it. Play stops, and the faceoff comes back to your end.

That is the vocabulary. Now the job.

I will start with four big ideas, because they matter most. If you only listen to the first part of this episode, take those four with you. After that we will go back through the game, one situation at a time, and add the detail.

First idea. With a lead, manage the puck, not the clock.

With a lead, the question isn't whether you can make a chance. It is whether they can get the puck back somewhere dangerous. Here is why that is the right question. A goal against costs a leading team more than a goal for gains them. And time is on your side. If the clock runs out with the score unchanged, you win. So the trailing team has to force things, and you don't.

That gives you three passes not to make. No passes across the middle of your own end or the neutral zone. A turnover there becomes an odd-man rush against you. No blind pass from one defenceman to the other across the front of your own goal. And no long pass you aren't sure about. A puck picked off at your own blue line is worse than a chip out.

A chip is a short, deliberate flip, or a play off the boards, that gets the puck past the forechecker and out of your zone. Under pressure, a chip beats forcing a breakout pass. Why? You do lose the puck. But you lose it far from your net, with your team facing the right way, and you get your change.

Keep the chip in play. Put it along the boards or off the glass. In many leagues, including British hockey, shooting it straight out of the rink from your own end is a penalty, even under pressure. A clear over the glass that drops into a bench can still be called. And flipping the puck out of the rink on purpose is a penalty from anywhere, in every league.

Then take away the middle. Let them have the outside. Keep your stick in the passing lane, defend with your feet rather than lunging, and never chase a carrier to the boards and leave the middle open behind you.

Two more habits finish the first idea. Shorten your shifts, and win the faceoffs in your own end. There is more on both later.

Second idea. When you are behind, add risk one step at a time, and keep somebody back.

Behind, you have to make more chances. The trap is that the easiest way to do that, sending everybody, is also the fastest way to give up another goal and end the game. So add one thing at a time, in roughly this order of safety.

First, speed and support. Move the puck north faster, and arrive in numbers through the neutral zone. That costs you nothing at the back.

Second, a harder forecheck, sending a second forward deep after the puck. The cost is that a clean exit by them is now more dangerous. So your highest forward stays high, above the puck, as insurance.

Third, the pinch. A pinch is a defenceman stepping down from the other team's blue line to keep the puck in their zone. It keeps your attack alive. But a pinch that misses is a two-on-one the other way. So behind, you pinch on anything close. Ahead, you pinch only when you are certain.

Fourth, sending a defenceman into the rush. Now you have four attacking and one back. That is a real odd-man risk, so do it on purpose, never by accident.

Fifth, pulling the goalie. That is the biggest risk of all, and it gets its own part later.

And the one thing that never changes. Somebody is always the last player back. If a defenceman goes, a forward takes that place up high. Say that swap out loud.

A pinch, and a harder forecheck, take you to the boards, so here is how to arrive. Go for the puck with your stick, not for the player with a hit. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call. If you can see their numbers, pull up and play the puck.

Then the position at the boards. I will call the player coming at you the checker, whether they are after the puck or just coming at you. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the checker, never duck. Never turning your back and never ducking both protect your head and neck. Keep it up even going slowly. If you end up facing the boards, get your stick and gloves up on the glass, spread wide, head off it. That is only for when things have gone wrong. From here on, I will use that same short form, word for word.

Third idea. Change on the way out, and never ice it to get a change.

A change at the wrong moment gives away three things at once. You are a player short. There is a hole where that player's job was. And the teammate coming on joins the play blind. Coaches say few single mistakes give away as much for free. That is coaching judgement, not a measured ranking.

So go when your team has the puck heading north, when it has just put the puck deep in their end, or after a clean clear out of your zone. Those are the safe windows, because nobody needs your spot for the next few seconds.

Don't go when the puck is in your own end and your team doesn't have it. A player leaves, another arrives late, and for two seconds you are one short in the most dangerous part of the rink. Don't go as the last player back, until somebody relieves you. And don't go while they are on a rush. Finish the play first.

And never ice the puck to get a change. Under the professional rulebooks, and in British hockey, the team that ices it can't change before the next faceoff. It can't take a time-out either. So you get a faceoff in your own end, with your five most tired players, and no way to rest them. A hurt teammate can still come off. A tired one can't. Chip it out instead. You lose the puck far from your net, and you still get your change. Other leagues treat icing differently, so ask about yours.

Fourth idea. Give up the last word.

This habit matters at any score. When your feet stop, your stick starts reaching. A stick used instead of skating is what hooking and tripping calls are made of. So keep your feet moving.

After a whistle, be the one who skates away. If someone pushes you and you push back, plan on a minor for you as well. Officials are meant to give an extra penalty to whoever started it, but often they just give one each. So answering can wipe out the power play you had just earned.

Why does this sit beside the change? Because a bad change and a retaliation penalty do the same thing. Both hand the other team something for nothing.

Let me pull those four ideas together, because they are the core of game management.

One. With a lead, manage the puck. Nothing across the middle, chip it out and keep it in play, and take away the middle.

Two. Behind, add risk in order. Speed first, then the forecheck, the pinch, a defenceman up, and the goalie last. Somebody is always the last player back.

Three. Change when your team has the puck going north. Never when they have it in your end. And never ice it to get a rest.

Four. Keep your feet moving, and give up the last word.

Everything else in this episode adds detail to one of those four. We will go through the middle of a game first, then the end of a game, then contact and discipline, and last the extra situations, like overtime.

Fifth idea. Protecting a lead, in more detail.

Why do leading teams sit back? There are honest reasons. Time is on your side. And when the trailing team pushes extra players forward, a counter-attack opens up. Defending deeper and countering is a fair way to score, not just to survive. So giving up more shots is not automatically bad. A leading team is often trading more shots against for worse shots against. More from the point, fewer from the slot.

But that trade only works if it really happens. Teams at every level slide into pure retreat, and then it goes wrong in four ways. You never get the puck back, so you never change, so your legs go. Your defencemen spend the whole shift deep in your end, with no way out. Crowding all five players in front of your own net gives up screens, rebounds and scrambles, the most dangerous chances there are. And defending for minutes without the puck produces stick fouls. A power play against you is exactly what the trailing team wants.

So keep forechecking. Just choose the right forecheck. Many teams switch shapes with a lead. One common shape sends one forward in, with two forwards holding a line in the neutral zone. Another has one chaser, one player in the middle, and three across the neutral zone. That one is often called a trap.

In both, the first forward in isn't there to win the puck. That player takes away one side of the ice, so teammates can pick off the pass out. Do it with the line you skate. Stick on the puck, not a hit. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call. And if you can see their numbers, pull up.

Which shape your team switches to, and when, is your coach's choice. Turning up as an all-out chaser while the other four sit back is how leads get thrown away. So ask which yours plays.

Now shifts. Protecting a lead, shorten your shifts to thirty or thirty-five seconds, instead of fifty. That figure is a coaching prescription, not a measurement. Why? Fresh legs defend. Tired legs reach, and that is how sticks end up around waists and hands.

Faceoffs in your own end. Coaches treat losing one late, with a one-goal lead, as the most dangerous single moment in a game. That is coaching emphasis, not a count. The reason is simple. A lost draw hands them the puck, set up, in your end. So have your best faceoff player out, and a plan for losing the draw as well as winning it.

Two last traps with a lead. Don't play the clock. Watching the scoreboard makes you late to loose pucks. Play the puck, and the clock takes care of itself. And don't chase the extra hit. A hit that takes you out of position is one you can't afford. If they have already passed or shot, let them go, and get back into position.

Sixth idea. Behind, and when to take the risks.

Scale risk to how much time is left against how many goals you need. Not to how frustrated you feel. Here is a rough ladder.

Down one in the first or second period, play your normal game harder, with no extra risk. There is lots of time left, and a second goal against is what really hurts you.

Down one early in the third, forecheck harder and pinch more. Still keep the last player back honest.

Down two in the middle of the third, send your defencemen up and accept some chances against. You need two goals, so trading one goal for one goal is no longer a loss.

Down one or two in the last few minutes, the goalie comes out, and you go all in.

Down three or more in the third, go very hard early, rather than a bit harder late. The research is clear on this one. A team down three with ten minutes left that plays sensibly is choosing to lose slowly.

Every pressure step on that ladder works the same way. Your stick on the puck, not a hit. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call. If you can see their numbers, pull up. And if a pinch or a forecheck takes you to the boards, it is the same short form. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the checker, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly. If you end up facing the boards, stick and gloves up on the glass, spread wide, head off it.

Who covers the point behind a pinching defenceman is your coach's choice. Some teams send the highest forward back. Some slide the other defenceman across. Find out which yours plays.

How do you know it's time to change something, rather than keep doing the same thing harder? Watch for three signs.

If you've had lots of time in their end and no chances from the slot, your entries are dying at the blue line. Change how you enter, not how hard you skate.

If their goalie is seeing everything, you need bodies in front of the goalie, never on them, and pucks to the net. Contact with a goalie can hurt them, and it can be a penalty.

If you keep losing races out of your own end, you are slow starting your breakouts. Keep it simple and go north earlier.

And agree your time points before the third period starts. Something like, if we're still down one at six minutes, we change the forecheck and pinch on everything. Then nobody has to decide in the moment.

Seventh idea. Shifts, tired legs, and the change.

Tiredness doesn't just cost you a step. It costs you things in a set order. First your decisions slow down. You see fewer options, and later. Then your feet go. You stop skating and start reaching. Then your support goes, so a teammate loses a battle they should have won. Backchecking goes last, and it costs the most.

That is why a long shift is a bad deal. A ninety-second shift is worth less than a thirty-second one, and it costs your next shift too. That is the shape of the argument, not a measured number. A sensible target is thirty-five to forty-five seconds. Make it thirty to thirty-five when you're protecting a lead or stuck in your own end. Those are coaching figures too.

The signal to change is your legs, not a stopwatch. The moment you find yourself gliding to a spot you'd have skated to ten seconds earlier, you're already too tired to be useful. And you don't feel your decisions getting worse until you've made the bad one.

The fix for bad changes is discipline and talking, not fitness. So call it out loud. I'm changing. Stay on. Last one back.

Two more times not to change. In the middle of a six-on-five attack, change with the puck or not at all. And if you take the faceoffs, don't change away from an attacking faceoff you're needed for.

The last minute of a period has its own rules. A goal against then is the last thing everyone carries into the dressing room, and there's no time to answer it. So don't get caught changing. Don't force your way into their zone either. A turnover at their blue line with twenty seconds left is a two-on-one you won't recover from. Get the puck deep and finish the period there.

If you're tired with fifteen seconds left, stay on and defend. Nobody's line is more important than the clock. The one exception is a real chance. If you have the puck and the numbers, go. Just know the difference between a chance and a hope.

Then the first shift after a long stoppage, like an injury or an intermission. Everyone is cold, and both teams play that shift badly. That makes it a chance as well as a risk. Keep your feet moving during the stoppage, instead of leaning on your stick. Keep the first twenty seconds simple, because nobody's timing is right yet. Win the faceoff, because the other team is disorganised too. And be first to the first loose puck. That race usually sets the tone for the next couple of minutes.

Eighth idea. The change rules that are also tactics.

Icing again, because there is more to it. Where icing freezes your bench, the ways out are narrow. A pulled goalie going back in, an injured player, or a penalty that changes the numbers on the ice. That is all. So after you ice it, don't drift toward the bench.

Not every league does this. Many rec and youth leagues use a different icing, and plenty don't enforce the no-change rule at all. Ask before the game. It changes how hard you should work to get the puck over the red line before you dump it.

Two other things can freeze your bench the same way, under the professional rulebooks and in British hockey. Knocking your own net off, even by accident. And your own goalie freezing a puck that the other team shot in from their side of centre. So when your line is stuck out there, get to a routine dump-in before your goalie does, and move it.

Now changing on the fly. The player coming off has to be at the bench and out of the play before the new one joins. If either of you plays the puck or touches anyone too early, it's a bench penalty for too many players. And a goal your team scores before the whistle won't count. In some leagues the player coming off can cause it alone, before the new one even steps on. So be off the ice before you touch anything, and leave only through your own bench door.

Let me pull the middle of the game together. With a lead, forecheck the way your team switches to, and take away a side rather than chase. Short shifts, your best faceoff player out, and play the puck, not the clock. Behind, climb the ladder by the clock, and change how you enter when the chances dry up. Change on the way out, call it out loud, and don't change in the last minute of a period. And after an icing, a knocked-off net or your goalie freezing a dump-in, expect your bench to be frozen.

Ninth idea. Pulling the goalie, and why earlier than feels natural.

Pulling the goalie means taking your goalie off for an extra skater. You attack six against five, with nobody in your net.

The research agrees on one thing. Coaches pull too late. Here is why the numbers point earlier. Six against five scores about as often as a power play. The empty net is expensive too, because they score into it often. But the trade is still worth it, and worth it earlier. A pulled goalie is the biggest lever there is at the end of a game.

So expect it earlier than feels natural. Down two with four or five minutes left is normal, not desperate. The goalie usually comes out at a stoppage with the faceoff in their end, or on the fly once you have the puck in their zone. Not at just any whistle. Ask your bench which way your team does it.

Be honest about the odds, too. Even done well, most pulls still fail. Down one, recent NHL teams tied or won roughly fifteen times in a hundred. You need the goal, and this is still your best chance of getting it.

And there is a catch coaches point out. The earlier you pull, the more of your lines play six against five. If your second group can't handle it, pulling early isn't free.

Here is a common shape. Two players at the points. Two on the half-walls, the stretch of boards level with the faceoff dots. One in front of the net. One below the goal line or in the middle. Some teams put three players high instead, or four on one side, or just add a net-front player to their normal power play. The exact shape is your team's choice, so learn it in practice. There's no time to work it out in the game.

The principles matter more than the shape.

Get set up. A six-on-five that has to enter the zone again and again is wasting its advantage.

Move the puck with one touch. The defenders can only cover so much, and your extra player only counts if the puck moves faster than they can shift.

Get the puck to the net with a body in front. The extra player is worth most as a screen and a rebound collector, not as a sixth passer.

The screen goes in the goalie's line of sight, outside the crease, the painted area in front of the net. Never on the goalie, and never in their way. Staying off the goalie is your job, in every league. Here is why. A goalie who is down or turned can't protect themselves, so contact can hurt them. It can also be a penalty, and it can cost you the goal. Even standing where the goalie can't move freely in the crease can cost the goal. And keep your head up and your chin off your chest there, because a defender can move you into the post.

Never let both point players pinch. Somebody has to be able to stop a clear, because there is no goalie behind you. The one who does pinch goes for the puck with the stick, not a hit. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call, and if you can see their numbers, pull up.

And change with the puck, and change fast. If you change while chasing it, you'll give up an empty-net goal.

Now the six-on-four. If you're behind and on a power play, pulling the goalie makes it six against four. That's a much bigger edge than most coaches use. In one season's numbers it scored nearly as often as it gave up, and you need the goal while they don't. So the research says pull earlier on a power play than at even strength. And late in a game, down one, being a player short yourself is not on its own a reason to keep your goalie in. Earlier in a game, it still is.

Tenth idea. The delayed penalty, a free extra attacker.

When the other team commits a penalty while your team has the puck, the referee raises an arm and lets play go on. That is called a delayed penalty. The whistle only comes when they get control of the puck. Until then, they can't score.

So this is the one time you get an extra attacker for free. At the first sign of the arm, your goalie heads to the bench. The extra skater jumps on as the goalie arrives. Every second counts, and teams waste this all the time.

There is one way you can still be scored on. Putting it in your own net. A deflection off one of them doesn't count, but your own defenceman ringing it in off your own goalie does. So no blind passes across the front of your own goal while the arm is up.

And keep the puck away from them, because the whistle comes the moment they control it. Don't force a weak shot that could be smothered. A shot that misses and rims round to them ends the play.

Go on the raised arm, not on hearing the words delayed penalty. Those words can also mean a penalty clock waiting to start, and that gives you nothing. Not every league allows or practises pulling the goalie on a delayed call, and some officials blow early. So agree it with your goalie beforehand.

Eleventh idea. Once your own goalie is pulled, no foul in your own end buys you anything.

With a goalie behind you, a few fouls can be worth taking, and we will come to those. With your net empty, there's no goalie to make a save, so the rulebook skips the penalty shot and gives them the goal. If you haul down a player on a breakaway with your goalie pulled, expect the referee to award the goal.

The one that catches players out is a loose puck in your own crease. If a puck drops into your crease, knock it straight down where you stand, and play it. No league charges you for that.

You may catch a puck out of the air, but only if you put it straight down where you are. Not somewhere better. A player who caught one with their own net empty and dropped it outside the crease still had the goal given against them.

What gets you penalised is what you do after the catch. Holding it, taking a stride with it, turning away with it, or throwing it. And don't lay your hand flat over a loose puck. In your own crease, several leagues treat that the same as holding it.

With your goalie in the net, holding a puck that is in your crease gives them a penalty shot. With your net empty, treat any cover in your crease as a goal against. And catching it higher doesn't get you off. The crease runs up to the top of the net. So knock it down.

Twelfth idea. Defending against a pulled goalie.

Now you are the team with the lead, and they have six attackers. Don't chase. With six of them, someone is always open if you leave your area. Hold a tight shape in the slot. Block the lanes, and let them have the outside.

Coaches call the shape a box or a diamond. Those are penalty-kill words for four players, and here you have five. Where the fifth stands is your coach's call. One option is a collapsing box, all five of you tight in front of the net with sticks in the lanes. Ask before their goalie comes out. And don't reach for a shape whose fifth player chases the puck, because chasing is the first thing to avoid.

At your net front, hold the inside, next to the player screening your goalie. Don't try to move them. No shove, no lean, and never your stick across them. A two-handed shove with your stick is a cross-check, whatever your blade does. Never into their back, and never lift anyone off their feet. If they turn and you can see their numbers, pull up. Stay square, so you keep your balance. Keep your head up and your chin off your chest. The goal post can hurt you the same way the boards can. Even going slowly, a crash there can hurt.

Then, as the puck arrives, lift their stick. Get your blade under their shaft, low, well below their bottom hand. Lift it up, not sideways, then let go. Never at the hands, and never a chop.

Why the net front? Coaches say most six-on-five goals come from tips, screens and rebounds. A count of recent NHL games found tips and quick rebounds more common than usual, though well short of most. Either way, the front of your net is where it gets decided.

Every clear is a shot. Don't just chip it out. When the lane is clean, get your head up and put the puck at the empty net. If it goes in, it's a goal and there's no icing. But if it misses and crosses their goal line, it's icing. So shoot when you can actually hit the net, not as a blind heave. A blind clear straight to their point player is the worst outcome of the three.

And keep the backswing short. In at least one adult recreational league, a wind-up past about fifteen inches counts as a slap shot. That is a penalty there, and if it hurts someone it gets you thrown out of this game and the next. If you play adult rec hockey, ask whether your league bans the slap shot.

Yes, you can be iced against a pulled goalie. The goalie counts, so six against six is equal strength, and icing is called. Under the professional rulebooks and in British hockey, that means a faceoff in your own end, at the dot they pick, with no change and no time-out. Five tired players, against six. So get it out with a purpose, or put it on net.

Faceoffs in your own end at five against six decide games, so agree the plan before you line up. And take no penalties. Six against four is close to a goal.

So, the end of the game. Pull your goalie earlier than feels natural. With the extra attacker, get set up, move it with one touch, screen without touching the goalie, never let both point players pinch, and change only with the puck. On a delayed penalty the extra attacker is free, as long as you don't put it in your own net. With your own net empty, no foul helps you, and a loose puck in your crease gets knocked straight down. Defending an empty net, don't chase, hold the inside with no shove, no lean and never your stick across them, and remember you can still be iced.

Thirteenth idea. When a penalty is worth taking, and when it isn't.

Rarely, a penalty is worth it. The test is simple. Is the thing you're stopping more likely to become a goal than the power play you're giving them? Which trades are worth it is your coach's call.

Stopping an odd-man rush in the last minutes of a close game can pass that test. A clean breakaway does not. If you haul down a player on a breakaway from behind, they get a penalty shot, not a power play. Across recent NHL seasons, penalty shots went in a little more often than power plays did, though not every season. So that foul saves you little or nothing. The time to stop the breakaway was four seconds earlier, with your gap, the space between you and the carrier.

If it's a goal or a penalty, with your goalie in the net, take the penalty. But keep it a minor, because the trade only works while it is one. A reckless trip or hook can be a major and a game misconduct, which throws you out, with nobody hurt. In at least one league it always is, and there you should plan on a match penalty too. A hook that injures ends your game in most leagues. And with your own goalie pulled, take none at all.

Now when it isn't worth it. Never with a lead in the last five minutes. If you're beaten, be beaten, and let them have the outside shot. Never while you're already killing a penalty. Coaches rank five against three as the most dangerous spot in hockey. Never while you're on a power play. Nothing wastes an advantage faster. Never at six against five, either way. Never in the first period. And never after the whistle, to send a message, or to get even.

After a whistle, skate away and leave the puck where it is. Firing it away isn't always just two minutes. In some leagues it can get you thrown out of the game. In at least one, doing it in protest of a call does exactly that, and it costs you your team's next game too. That is exactly the mood you're in when you do it. And knocking the puck away from an official who is picking it up is a misconduct in every league.

Fourteenth idea. Contact, and finishing your check.

Before anything else, find out whether you may body check in this game at all. It depends on your age group, your league, and sometimes the game itself. In many youth, girls' and women's, and adult recreational leagues, you can't. In American hockey, when a checking team plays a non-checking team, nobody checks for the whole game. So check the game, not just the age group.

Where checking isn't allowed, take the ice the player needs, and play the puck. The contact isn't yours. A body check there is a penalty. A violent one can get you thrown out of the game, and in some leagues one that hurts the player always does.

Where checking is allowed, play the body of the player who has the puck, or take the ice they need. No run-up. Building up speed into contact, even with two hard strides, can be charging. Never your stick across them, never into their back, and never lift anyone off their feet. If you can see their numbers, pull up and play the puck. Keep every hit off the head. Never drive anyone head first into the boards. And even going slowly, a hit into the boards can hurt.

Finishing your check means hitting a player who has already passed or shot. That's a late hit. Where you may check, the check isn't late the instant the puck leaves. But the window is tiny, about the length of your arm and your stick at the moment they release it. The real test is simple. If you could still pull out of the hit, pull out. Skating one or two more strides after the puck has gone, and then deciding to hit, is not acceptable. Going looking for that window is how players get thrown out.

And the window never covers a player who isn't ready for the hit. If they no longer have the puck and aren't ready for a hit, don't hit them. In some leagues that hit counts as reckless, window or not.

What it costs. Two minutes is the floor for a late hit, not the ceiling. A violent or reckless one can be a major and a game misconduct, which throws you out, even with nobody hurt. In some leagues, plan on a match penalty for a reckless one. And a hit meant to hurt is a match penalty in most leagues.

If they have turned away from you, leave them. A hit on their back is checking from behind. In almost every league, that throws you out of the game, even with nobody hurt. And never drive anyone into the boards or the goal frame from behind. If they can't protect themselves, some leagues must make that a match penalty.

Fifteenth idea. After a goal against, and after you score.

After a goal against, play one deliberately simple shift. Win a puck, get it deep, pick up the player you're covering, and change. Nothing clever. No passes across the middle, no pinches, no one-on-one moves. Whoever is first in on the forecheck goes for the puck with the stick, not a hit, and if you can see their numbers, pull up. Reset your risk to where it was. Why? The instinct is to chase the goal back straight away, and that's how a one-goal deficit becomes a three-goal deficit.

A simple shift is not a lack of ambition. It is the right play for that moment. Its job is to stop the bleeding and hand the game back to the bench in one piece. And being the player who can deliver one makes you very hard to leave out of the lineup.

Say one short sentence, from anyone. Reset, next shift. Silence after a goal against does damage. Then play the game in front of you. Replaying the goal in your head costs you decisions you can't afford. Coaches treat the minute after a goal against as the most dangerous stretch in hockey. That's coaching lore, not a measured fact, but the habits are worth having either way.

And don't go looking for a hit to make yourself feel better. That's how a late hit happens, and it can end your game.

After you score, play the next shift like any other. The bench is loud, and somebody starts cheating up the ice for the next goal. So take the centre-ice draw like one in your own end. Win it back, get the puck deep, pick up your player. Whether your coach sends the scoring line straight back out, or a checking line to settle things, is a coaching choice. Ask which yours does.

Sixteenth idea. Frustration, and the reset.

Coaches say most penalties in amateur hockey come from frustration. Part of that is measured, and part isn't. Studies of youth hockey do find that most penalties are the aggressive kind, like roughing and slashing, rather than hooking and holding. But nobody has measured why a penalty was taken. So most being frustration is coaching experience, not data. The advice holds either way.

Three habits. Keep your feet moving, so your stick doesn't reach. Give up the last word, so a push back doesn't cost you the power play. And have a reset you actually use. A breath at the faceoff dot, a tap of your pads, a look at the clock. Something small and physical.

Let me pull discipline and contact together. A penalty is worth it only to stop something more likely to score than their power play, only while it stays a minor, and never with your goalie pulled. Never with a lead late, never on the kill or the power play, never after the whistle. Find out whether you may check at all. Where you may, if you could pull out of a hit, pull out, and if they've turned away, leave them. After a goal against, play one simple shift. And have a reset.

Seventeenth idea. Line matching and last change.

After a whistle, the visiting team puts its players out first, then the home team gets to change. That's called last change. The home coach can wait, see who you've sent out, and pick who goes against them. The classic use is a checking line against the other team's best scorers. A checking line is a line picked for defence, forechecking and faceoffs, rather than scoring.

Why does it matter? Last change is the main mechanical part of home advantage, though only part. Travel, rest and knowing the rink are in there too. The road team's answer is to change on the fly, during play, when the other bench can't react cleanly. That's why change discipline matters even more on the road.

If you're on a scoring line being checked, change on the fly, not at whistles, so you don't show your hand first. Don't take the bait and try to beat your player one-on-one into traffic. Move the puck early. If three of them are focused on you, two of your teammates are open. A shift with no goals either way and no penalties against a strong checking line is a win, if your other lines are winning theirs.

If you're on the checking line, your first job is not to get scored on. Get the puck deep and change. Long shifts against a top line are how the goal happens. Be first on the faceoff and first back. You were sent out to be reliable, not creative.

After a whistle, go straight to the faceoff dot, or straight to the bench. Under the professional rulebooks and in British hockey, dawdling on the way is a bench penalty, with no warning first. And plenty of rec leagues don't run a formal change at stoppages at all. The referee just drops the puck. Ask yours. If so, your matching tool is the change on the fly.

Eighteenth idea. Four against four.

When a player from each team goes to the penalty box at the same stoppage, and nothing else is on the clock, many leagues play four against four. In British hockey that usually isn't how it works, so ask.

There's more ice, so there's more time. Hold the puck a beat longer, and make the second option available. The middle is open, which cuts both ways. More clean entries for you, and more odd-man rushes against you. So the defensive worry is the rush up the far side. If you lose the puck at their blue line, it's two against one with nobody to help.

Nineteenth idea. Overtime and shootouts.

First, find out whether your league plays overtime at all. Formats run from four against four, to three against three, to a straight shootout, to none at all. In British hockey, many games simply end level, so a tied third period ends with the horn. Ask before the game.

Three against three is a possession game, not a shooting gallery. If you don't have a really good look, don't shoot. If you give up the puck, you face three against two the other way straight away. And a shot that misses the net often turns into a goal against.

With no good option in their zone, carry or pass the puck back out through the neutral zone, reset, and come again. Retreating without forcing anything is normal and correct. A pass back to your own goalie is a fair reset too. Change only when you have the puck. Never give it up just to get fresh legs.

Where teams switch ends for overtime, your bench is the far one. The winner often comes from catching a tired defender who can't get off. Where they don't switch ends, a tired player can reach the bench, so that plan is much less reliable.

Where overtime is full periods of five against five, it's normal hockey played tired, where one mistake ends it. Play simple, get pucks in behind their defence, don't be the one who makes the play that loses it, and keep your shifts short.

In a shootout, decide your move before you start skating. Deciding at the hash marks is how you end up doing neither thing well. Have two moves, and know which one you're using. Head up, puck out in front, so both a shot and a deke are possible. Get the goalie moving first, then go where they've just left. And hit the net. A miss is the worst outcome, because a shot on target at least gives the goalie a chance to make a mistake.

In goal, stay square and patient, track the puck, not the hands, and let the shooter decide first. Why? A goalie who moves first hands the shooter a side. How many shooters each team gets, and whether the same player can shoot again, depends on your league. Find out before it happens.

Last idea. The time-out.

Each team usually gets one. Use it for rest and instruction at a moment you choose, like before a big faceoff in your own end, or before going six against five. It isn't a way to change momentum. The study behind this found no real effect on winning. But it is free, and most benches never use it at all. Ask for it early, while the players are still changing after the whistle. In some leagues that is when the window closes, not when the puck drops. And some games with a hard finishing time have no time-outs at all, so find out at the start.

Let me pull it all together.

Keep asking how much risk the score and the clock allow.

With a lead, manage the puck. Nothing across the middle, chip it out and keep it in play, take away the middle, short shifts, and win your own-end draws.

Behind, add risk in order, scaled to the clock. Somebody is always the last player back, with the swap called out loud.

Change when your team has the puck going north. Never when they have it in your end, never as the last player back, and not in the last minute of a period. And never ice it to get a rest.

Pull the goalie earlier than feels natural, and screen without touching the goalie. On a delayed penalty, get the extra attacker on at once. With your own net empty, no foul helps you, and a loose puck in your crease gets knocked straight down. Defending an empty net, don't chase, and remember you can still be iced.

After a goal against, play one simple shift. Find out whether you may check at all, and if you could pull out of a hit, pull out. Keep your feet moving, and give up the last word.

Much of this is your team's choice. So here are three questions for your coach. What forecheck do we switch to with a lead? Who covers the point when a defenceman pinches? And what shape do we play at six against five, at both ends? And two for your league. Can we body check in this game? And what happens after an icing, and is there overtime?

Go and ask. Their answers tell you how your team wants you to manage a game, and now you know why each answer makes sense.
