===== SCRIPT START =====

This episode is about the offensive zone. That is the other team's end of the ice, once your team has the puck down there. Not how you get in. What you do once you're in. We will build it up one piece at a time, and for each piece I will tell you what to do and also why it works. If you know why something works, you can still do the right thing when the play looks different from the picture in your head.

First, a few words you will hear all the way through. I will explain them now, so they don't get in the way later.

High and low. In their end, high means toward the blue line, and low means toward the goal line, the line the net sits on.

Strong side and weak side. The strong side is the side of the ice the puck is on. The weak side is the other side.

The point. This is the area just inside their blue line, where your defencemen usually stand. It is an area, not the line itself.

The half-wall. This is the stretch of boards roughly level with the faceoff dot, between the corner and the point.

The slot. This is the ice straight out in front of the net, between the two faceoff circles. Its top part, from the faceoff dots up to the tops of the circles, is called the high slot.

The crease. This is the goalie's area, the blue paint in front of the net. Most rulebooks count the red line around its edge as part of the crease. So when I say stay out of the crease, I mean off the red line too.

The far post. That is the goalpost on the far side from the puck, behind the defence. Coaches also call it the back door.

That is the vocabulary. Now the job.

Almost every amateur team can get the puck into the other team's end. Far fewer turn that into a shot from somewhere dangerous. Everything in this episode is about making that happen.

I will start with four big ideas, because they matter most. If you only listen to the first part of this episode, take those four with you. After that we will go through the zone one job at a time, and add the detail.

First idea. Take your shot from a dangerous place, or with the goalie out of position.

The dangerous place has a name. It is called home plate, because it is shaped like the plate in baseball. It runs from the tops of the two faceoff circles, down to the faceoff dots, and then angles in to the two goalposts. The slot sits inside it.

Here is why it matters. How far you are from the net matters more than anything else about a shot. Shots from the middle beat shots from sharp angles. And the danger climbs fast the closer you get. So a clean look from the slot is worth several times a shot from the blue line that nobody screens. Not a bit more. Several times.

That is why "just get pucks to the net" isn't a plan. Lots of shots from the outside is what a team does when it has no plan.

There are only two ways to make a shot dangerous. The first is to move the puck. Get it into home plate before you shoot, by carrying it, passing it, or driving in on a rebound. The second is to move the goalie, so a shot from anywhere becomes dangerous. Take their eyes away with a screen. Make them slide sideways with a pass across the middle. Or make them commit with a rebound. Always know which one you are using.

The best chances do both at once. A pass across the slot, to a teammate already standing in home plate.

Why does a pass across the middle hurt a goalie so much? Because a goalie's save is mostly about position, not reflexes. A set goalie has already squared up to the puck. They are covering most of the net before you shoot. Make them move sideways, and four things have to happen at once. They push across the crease. They find their angle again. They find the puck again, because their eyes were set on the other side. And they reset their stick and pads. That last part is where mistakes creep in and gaps open. Shoot before they finish, and they are making a desperate save instead of an easy one.

This one has been tracked. In volunteer-tracked data from NHL games at five-on-five, shots after a pass across the middle went in about twice as often as shots after the next best kind of pass. That is a five-on-five figure, not a power-play one.

So here is the habit. When the puck comes to you on the half-wall, look across the middle before you look up the wall. The far side of the slot first. Then the far post. Then the far faceoff dot. Only then the safe pass up the wall.

Why that order? The pass across is open for a moment and then it is gone. The pass up the wall will still be there. If you look up the wall first, the gap has opened and closed by the time you look back.

Pass to where your shooter is going, not to their feet. A pass into their feet needs an extra touch. That extra touch is exactly the time the goalie needed.

You can carry it across yourself, too. Drive from the half-wall into the high slot, or curl out from behind the net into the slot. Either way you drag the goalie across, just like a pass does. Keep your head up when you do. You can't brace for a hit you haven't seen, and coming out past the post you can be driven into the goal frame.

Expect the other team to guard the middle. Taking it away is what most defensive systems are built to do. So the pass across opens only for a moment, and you have to be looking before it opens.

Second idea. Put a body in front of the net every time a shot is coming.

That player is the screen. A screen means standing in the goalie's line of sight, so they can't see the puck. From outside the crease, with no contact, a screen is normally legal. Do it on every shot.

Here is why. A screen takes the goalie's eyes. A goalie who can't see the puck can't square up to it in time. And with your blade on the ice, the screen becomes a tip as well.

Where do you stand? Inside the faceoff dots, six to eight feet out from the goal line. The crease reaches six feet out at its deepest. So live at the eight-foot end, just beyond the red line. Keep both skates out of the blue paint and off that line. A skate in there can cost your team the goal. In some leagues it can cost you the zone as well.

Stand between the goalie's eyes and the puck, not between the goalie and the net. Those are different lines, and they change as the puck moves. When your defenceman walks the line, which means skating sideways along the blue line, slide with them. A screen that was right three seconds ago is just a player standing near the net.

Now your body. Chest up and square to the shooter, so you block as much as you can. Hips low, feet wide, knees bent, so a defenceman can't move you. Think of a squat, not a bow. Standing tall and narrow is why players get shoved off the spot. Folding at the waist is why goalies see the shot.

Be still when the shot comes, because shuffling gives the goalie flashes of the puck. Don't turn to watch the shot. Turning opens the goalie's view and takes you out of the rebound. Keep your chest to the shooter, your eyes on the puck at their stick, and your blade on the ice for the tip.

Now the part that keeps you safe there.

You are standing still with your eyes on the puck. So a defender pushing you sideways is someone you feel, not someone you see. And that path has a steel goalpost at one end and the goalie at the other.

So know where the near post is before you plant. Keep your head up, chin off your chest, and never duck, not even away from the shot. If you meet a post, meet it with an arm or a leg, never your head first. That protects your head and neck. It holds even going slowly, and in every league, because a defender holding you off the net can put you on that path.

Hold your spot with your feet, and let the defender try to move you. Don't shove back or lean on them. Never put your stick across them, and never put your stick or your body into anyone's back. If you see a player's numbers, pull up. And never lift anyone off their feet. Even going slowly, a crash there can hurt.

Never brace against the goalie the way you brace against a defender. Never start contact with the goalie at all. If a shove is carrying you into them, give way, and make it obvious you are trying to avoid them. Here is why. A goalie watching the puck can't protect themselves from a body they don't see coming. Contact can hurt them, it can be a penalty, and it can cost your team the goal.

Third idea. Crash the net for the rebound, and decide to go before the shot.

Crashing the net means skating hard to the front of the net when a shot goes. Go hard, but under control, and play the puck, never the goalie. Here is why it matters. The seconds right after a shot are the most dangerous of the whole possession. The goalie has committed and is often down. The puck is loose in front. And every defender has just been pulled toward the shot. In the NHL's own game data, a team that has just put a shot on goal scores in the next three seconds about five times as often as usual.

And that is the moment most amateur teams stop and watch.

Here is why you decide before the shot. The defending team gets to most rebounds first. You only win one by going before you see where the puck went, not after.

Your timing depends on your job. If the net front is your job, you are planted there before the release. A screen you are still walking into isn't a screen yet. If you are driving in from the wall or your faceoff circle, arrive as the puck is released, not well before it. If you get there early, the defender has time to get position on you and turn you away from the puck. That split is coaching know-how, not a measured finding.

When you get there, stick on the ice. Shoot the rebound straight back. Don't settle it, and don't look for a pass. You have a second or two while the goalie is down, and that is all. If you are tied up on the near side, slide to the far post. Nobody is covering it. And in the scramble, play the puck, not a body, and never the goalie. A goalie who is down or turned can't protect themselves. Contact can hurt them, it can be a penalty, and it can cost your team the goal.

Fourth idea. Somebody is always above the puck.

Above the puck means higher in the zone than the puck, nearer the blue line. Usually that is your high forward, sitting at or near the top of the faceoff circle as the safety.

Why? It pays three ways. A rebound that comes straight out is theirs. A rebound that squirts to the high slot is theirs to jump on. And if the other team gets the puck, their counter-attack has to get past that player first.

Your two defencemen normally hold the blue line. If one steps down, a forward fills in at the line. That is the usual default, not a law. Some teams rotate all five players, so whoever is highest covers. Ask which your team plays.

Let me pull those four ideas together, because they are the core of the job.

One. Shoot from home plate, or move the goalie first. Look across the middle before you look up the wall.

Two. Put a body in front for every shot. Outside the paint and off the red line, blade on the ice. Know where the post is, head up, never duck. Hold your spot with your feet, never your stick across anyone. And never start contact with the goalie.

Three. Decide before the shot that you are going to the net. Stick on the ice, shoot it straight back, and play the puck, not a body, and never the goalie.

Four. Keep somebody above the puck.

Everything else in this episode adds detail to one of those four. So now let's go through the zone, starting with your shape.

Fifth idea. The triangle, and why it keeps moving.

A new term first. Your forwards are called F one, F two and F three. They are the first, second and third to the puck. Those are roles, not positions. A winger who gets to the puck first is F one. A centre who arrives last and ends up high is F three. And the labels change whenever the order changes.

Here is the shape most teams start from. One forward is below the puck, deeper toward the goal line than the carrier. That is the outlet back to safety, and the player who can attack the net from behind. Without them, every lost puck is a clean escape for the defence.

One forward is level with the puck, a short pass away. They sit in the inside lane, not flat on the boards. That is the close support who keeps the puck alive.

And one forward is above the puck, toward the top of the circles. That is the shooting option on a pass back up, and the first defender if you lose it.

That shape is the attack triangle. Why play it? It gives you width and depth at once. So the carrier always has at least two passing options, at different angles. And the players without the puck keep moving to give them those options.

The players rotate, and the shape stays the same. Say the puck goes down from the half-wall to the corner. The corner player becomes the deep one, the old carrier moves up, and the high player stays high. Say the puck goes up to the point. Then the low forwards attack the net and the slot. Say it goes behind the net. Then the net-front player slides to the far post as support, and the far-side forward moves into the slot.

One part never changes. At least one forward is always in home plate, or attacking it. Why? A triangle that stays on the outside never threatens the net. It is just three players passing the puck around a defence that is happy to let them.

This shape, with both defencemen on the blue line, is the common default, not a law. There are three real alternatives.

The first is an overload. Four players load onto the puck side, with only one outlet on the far side. It wins battles on the boards and grinds teams down. The price is the far side, which is left open.

The second is a five-man rotation. Your defencemen rotate into forward spots, and your forwards cover the blue line, as a matter of routine. It is how modern NHL teams with skating defencemen play. It only works if everyone is comfortable at the blue line.

The third is a one-three-one. One player at the point, one on each half-wall, one in the middle, and one at the net front. The player in the middle is called the bumper, and the bumper is not the net-front player. Those are two different jobs at two different depths. You will see this one most on the power play.

Nobody has counted what teams actually play. So learn your team's shape before you decide a teammate is out of position.

Sixth idea. The cycle, and why it wears a defence down.

A cycle is your forwards rotating around the low part of the zone. That means the corner, the half-wall and behind the net. The puck moves a few feet at a time, and you protect it from the defence. Think of a wheel.

Why does it work? Four reasons.

First, every rotation asks the defence the same question. Collapse low, or hold the slot? Over thirty or forty seconds, somebody gets it wrong.

Second, it is a cheap way to hold the puck. A pass along the boards that fails stays near the wall, with bodies around it. A pass picked off through the middle is the one that goes the other way, with your forwards caught below it. That ranking is coaching agreement, not a count.

Third, it tires the defenders. They are skating without the puck, which is harder work, and they can't change lines. A tired defender drifts toward the puck, and that opens the slot.

And fourth, it buys time for your defencemen to get set at the line.

Here is how it works. The carrier on the half-wall or in the corner passes backwards and down, to a teammate arriving behind them. Then the carrier rotates up into the space that teammate just left.

There are two ways to make that pass, and they are opposites. First, a new term. The checker is the player trying to take the puck off you. A rim sends the puck on around the boards, past a checker who is sealing you against the wall. A reverse sends it back the way it came, into the space a checker has just skated out of.

So read the checker first. If the checker is still in front of you, rim it. If the checker has skated past you, reverse it. Picking the wrong one is one of the commonest ways a cycle turns the puck over. That is coaching know-how, not a count. But the two reads point opposite ways, so one of them is always wrong.

You have three more tools. A bump pass fires the puck hard off the boards to a spot, not straight at a teammate. A stick in the passing lane can't stop it. Aim at the spot and call it, because a bump nobody expects is a turnover.

A cutback is skating down the boards, running out of room, and turning back up the way you came. Why it works. The defender heading for the corner has committed their speed that way, and can't turn as fast as you. It buys two or three seconds, and it often opens the pass back up to the point. Make the turn while they're still too far away to reach you. Once they can reach you, it's too late. Go side-on to the boards instead. Never turn so you're facing the wall with them behind you.

And in a scissor, the carrier and a teammate skate toward each other and cross. The carrier can drop the puck or keep it. Why it works. Defenders swapping who they are covering are, for a moment, covering nobody.

Now the boards, because the cycle lives there. How you take contact on the wall matters more than winning the puck.

Never turn your back to the other player. And never duck. By the other player I mean whoever comes at you on the boards, whether they are after the puck or just coming at you.

Both protect your head and neck. If you turn your back, you can't see the hit coming or brace for it. If you duck, your head meets the boards first. That holds even going slowly, and whether or not your league allows checking.

So here is the position you aim for. Skates parallel to the boards. Forearm and hip to the boards. Never the point of your shoulder, and not the whole shoulder if you can help it. Head up, chin off your chest.

If you are already facing the wall and can't turn, there is a fallback, only for when things have gone wrong. Get your stick and gloves up on the glass, spread wide, and keep your head off it.

From here on I will use one short version of that position, the same words every time. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the other player, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly.

Seventh idea. End every cycle in something.

The cycle only sets up a chance. It has to end in one. Finish it with a shot from home plate, a pass across the middle, a point shot with a screen in front, or a drive to the net. Otherwise it did nothing.

This is the biggest amateur mistake in the zone. A team cycles three or four times, looks organised and busy, and never threatens the net. The defence is perfectly happy. You have used thirty seconds of your shift in their end, at no risk to them.

Two signs you are doing it. Nobody is in front of the net, so even a good shot has no screen, no tip and nobody for the rebound. And the puck never goes up above the goal line or into the middle. You are passing around the outside of a box that has no reason to move. So fill the front of the net before the cycle starts.

When should you keep cycling? When the defence is set between you and the net, and there is no good shot. When your defencemen aren't set at the line yet. Or when you are protecting a lead and want to use up the clock with the puck.

When should you stop and attack? The moment a defender turns their back to the puck. When a defenceman steps up and leaves space behind them. When nobody is covering the slot. When the goalie is moving, or not set. And when your net-front player has beaten their check to the inside.

Let me pull the shape and the cycle together. One forward below the puck, one level, one above, and somebody always in home plate. Learn your team's shape before you judge a teammate. Cycle to make the defence decide, and read the checker. Rim it or reverse it. Fill the front of the net first, and end every cycle in something. And on the wall, the same position. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the other player, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly.

Eighth idea. The half-wall, and your four options.

The half-wall is the winger's home in this zone. Why is it such a good place to hold the puck? The boards protect one side. You can see the whole zone. And you are the same distance from four different plays.

Know all four before the puck arrives, and know which one is on.

Low, to the corner or behind the net. That is the safe one. It keeps the cycle going and makes the defence solve the problem again.

High, to the point. That is best when the defence has collapsed low and left the point free.

Middle, the pass into the slot or to the far post. Highest value, highest risk, shortest window.

Or yourself, driving the net under control or cutting to the middle, playing the puck and not the goalie. That is best when your check is flat-footed, has turned their hips, or has jumped at a pass.

Why know all four first? Because a player who arrives meaning to pass to the point will pass to the point, whether the slot was open or not.

You will often need two more seconds than you have. Here is how to make them.

Cut back. Skate a few feet toward the corner, then cut back up the wall. The defender's speed has to turn round, and yours doesn't. Make the turn while they're still too far away to reach you. Once they can reach you, it's too late. Go side-on to the boards instead. Never turn so you're facing the wall with them behind you.

Delay and curl. Before the checker can reach you, turn away from the pressure, protect the puck, and curl toward the middle, into open ice. Never curl against the wall, where turning would put your back to the other player. You come out of the curl facing the ice, with your options fresh.

Don't turn into pressure the instant the puck arrives. Take it with your body between the checker and the puck, then spin off. Only spin off someone who's already leaning on you — never off someone still coming at you, however slowly. Turning straight into pressure is how you get pinned.

Bump it and get it back. A short pass to your level teammate and a quick return resets the defender's read. Often you get the puck back in more space than you gave it away in.

And keep your feet moving. A player standing still on the wall gets pinned. A player who is moving can go three ways.

And the safety point outranks all of that. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the other player, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly.

Ninth idea. Behind the net is a place to attack from, not a dead end.

Most amateur teams treat behind the net as somewhere the puck ends up. Treat it as somewhere you go on purpose. Here is why.

From behind the goal line, you see the whole zone. All five defenders and the goalie are in front of you, and the defence has to play with their backs to their own net.

The goalie has to keep turning. Every time the puck crosses behind the net, they have to pick a post and turn their head to track you. For that moment, they aren't square to a shot.

And nobody can come at you from straight behind, because the net and the end boards are there. That is just the shape of the rink. It is not protection. A defender can still come from either side, and you can be driven into the goal frame back there. So keep your head up, and know who is arriving.

The main play is the pass out to the slot, to the high-slot forward or the player at the far post. It is the first thing you look for, not the last resort. In that same five-on-five tracking, passes from below the goal line were the second most dangerous kind, behind only the pass across the middle. Why? It does both things at once. It moves the goalie, and it puts the puck in home plate.

You have other options. The wraparound is carrying the puck round the post and stuffing it in the far side, before the goalie can push across. It works best as a threat. A goalie who fears it seals the post hard, and that opens the pass to the slot.

The walkout is coming out from behind the net into the slot yourself, on your forehand. You cross the middle with the puck, and you arrive in the most dangerous ice there is.

From a tight angle behind the goal line, you can even bank it in off the back of the goalie's pads. And if nothing is on, reverse it or rim it, and start the cycle again. Keeping the puck is a perfectly good outcome.

Tenth idea. Low to high, and only with somebody in front.

Low to high means passing from low in the zone, the corner, the half-wall or behind the net, up to a defenceman at the point, who shoots. It is the usual answer when the defence has collapsed low and taken away everything close to the net.

Everyone has a job. The passer skates toward the hash marks, the short lines at the sides of the faceoff circle. That is an outlet if the defenceman needs one, and a spot for the rebound. The defenceman walks the puck toward the middle before shooting. That changes the angle, makes the goalie move, and often opens a lane. One forward goes to the net front to screen and tip. One supports in the high slot, so the puck can move again instead of going into a shin pad. And the defenceman on the far side watches for a rebound squirting their way.

A common add-on is a pass across the top first, from one defenceman to the other. Coaches call it D to D. Why? The goalie has to slide across, and the shot comes from the other side.

Why go low to high at all? Because a point shot with a screen in front makes things a cycle never does. A rebound. A tip. A scramble. And a whistle in your favour.

Here is the catch. At even strength, a point shot is one of the shots least likely to go in. It is blocked more often than shots from anywhere else, and it makes fewer rebounds. It scores rarely, and less often still with nobody in front. On the power play a point shot is worth more, so this is an even-strength caution.

So only go low to high with the net front filled. A point shot with a screen, a tip threat and two players hunting the rebound is a good play. The same shot with nobody in front just gives the goalie the puck.

Eleventh idea. Shooting from the point. Get it through, don't shoot harder.

This one is mostly for defencemen. Point shots get blocked more than any others. So your job isn't to shoot harder. It is to shoot through.

Walk the line first. Skate sideways along the blue line toward the middle, until the shot-blocker has to move their feet. That is usually a step or two. A defenceman who shoots from the spot where they got the puck is shooting at a wall that is standing still.

Change your release spot. A quick snap shot from somewhere the blocker didn't set up for beats a blocker who has already dropped.

Take something off it. A slower, lower shot that gets through and gets tipped beats a big slap shot into a shin pad, every time. Aim low, at a tipper's stick or the goalie's far pad. In at least one adult league, a backswing past about fifteen inches counts as a slap shot, and that is a penalty there. So keep the backswing short, and check your league.

Fake the shot. A fake that puts a blocker down on the ice opens the lane for the real one, or opens the pass across. Sell it with your shoulders and eyes, not a big backswing.

Or go D to D first. That drags the blocker and the goalie across, and you shoot into a lane that has just been emptied.

Why does all this matter? A blocked point shot usually goes the other way, with your team spread out. And if nobody is in front, hold it.

Let me pull the rest of the middle together. On the half-wall, know all four options before the puck comes, look across first, and keep your feet moving. Behind the net, look for the pass out to the slot first. Only go low to high with somebody in front. And from the point, walk the line, and shoot low to get it through.

Twelfth idea. Know when to shoot, when not to, and what you are shooting for.

Shoot when you are inside home plate with any kind of look. Almost any shot from there beats almost any pass from there. Shoot when the goalie is moving, not set, or screened. Shoot when you have a real lane, not the memory of one. Shoot when somebody is at the net front, because a tip threat and a rebound hunter make it a different play. And when there is a rebound and the goalie is down, shoot at once.

Don't shoot when the lane is blocked and you would be firing into a shin pad. Don't shoot when a better shot is one pass away, especially a pass across the middle. The half-second it takes is usually worth it. A shot after the goalie has had to move across is worth a lot more. Don't shoot when nobody is in front, because you are just giving the goalie the puck and starting their breakout. And don't shoot from a sharp angle with no traffic. The goalie seals the post, there is nothing to tip, and there is no rebound to chase.

Now what to shoot for. This is the part most players never think about. From out on the edges, you aren't really trying to score. You are trying to make a second chance.

Shoot for a rebound. Aim low, at the far pad. Shots at the goalie's chest and glove get caught, and the whistle goes.

Shoot for a tip. Low and hard along the ice, through the screen, at your tipper's stick. Say who you are shooting at. The tipper shows you a blade, and your job is to hit it.

You can even shoot to miss on purpose. A hard shot off the end boards to a spot for a teammate is a real pass. Say it first.

Aim for a corner only when the goalie can't see the puck or can't get there. Picking corners on a set goalie who can see the shot is about the worst bet you can make.

Thirteenth idea. Tipping the puck.

A tip is changing the direction of a shot with your stick blade. Why is it so hard to stop? The goalie has tracked a puck going one way and set for it. Now it is going another way, from a few feet out.

Keep your blade on the ice, always. Almost every puck you can tip comes in low. A stick held off the ice at the net front is doing nothing.

You are redirecting, not shooting. A small change of angle is enough to beat a set goalie. A big swing usually misses the puck. So angle the blade, give a little, and don't follow through. Soft hands change its direction. Hard hands send it into the glass.

And give your shooter a target. Hold your blade out in the lane where you want the puck, and shout for it.

Tip low for a second reason. A tip that is too high can cost you the goal. Depending on your league, the limit is the height of the crossbar or the height of your shoulders. Keep it low and you are fine everywhere.

And watch where your blade goes in a crowd. The moment it catches a player above the shoulders, it is high-sticking, and no league caps that at two minutes. If you hurt someone with your stick on purpose, or try to, plan on a match penalty in most leagues. That goes past being thrown out. So keep your blade down after the tip as well as before it.

Fourteenth idea. Hunting the rebound, and the high forward's three jobs.

You already know to decide before the shot. Now, where will the rebound go? Guess before it happens, rather than react after. Roughly, a puck off a pad tends to squirt back toward the corner it came from. One off the blocker tends to go to the shooter's off side. One off the chest tends to drop in front. Those are tendencies, not rules. A good goalie angles their pads to break all three on purpose. So know where the shot is coming from, lean your feet that way, and be ready to be wrong.

When the shot goes, two forwards go to the net. One goes to the front for the screen and tip. One hunts the rebound on the puck side.

The third forward is F three, the high forward. If that is you, you have three jobs, in order. First, be the safety at the top of the circle. A rebound straight out is yours, and a counter-attack has to go through you. Second, if a defenceman has stepped down or cut to the middle, take their spot on the blue line. Third, if the rebound squirts out to the high slot, jump on it. You are the closest player moving toward the net, and that is a great chance handed to you.

Why does F three cover the line? In the common setup, your team wants two players on the blue line at all times. Not every team does it this way. In a five-man rotation, nobody has a fixed line job, and whoever is highest covers. Some teams leave one player on the line, and slide the far-side defenceman across to the middle instead. Find out which your team plays.

Fifteenth idea. The goalie and the crease, in more detail.

What you do here is the same in every league. Screen from outside the crease. Never start contact with the goalie. Give ground when they set their position. What changes from league to league is how much a mistake costs. So play it the strict way, and you are as safe as any league allows.

Why stay out of the paint? Because inside it, your position alone can take the goal off, in every league, even with no contact.

If a goalie leans into you to set their position, give ground at once. Then set the screen again outside, with both skates off the line. Why? Holding your spot pays nothing. If your team scores, you can lose the goal, and you can take a penalty too.

Once a goalie has come out and played the puck outside the crease, get out of their road back. Blocking it on purpose can be a penalty, even with no contact at all. And a goalie who has come out of the crease is still not someone you run into. Unnecessary contact with them can be a penalty in every league.

In a scramble, play the puck. The most any league forgives is contact you couldn't avoid while you are both going for the same loose puck. Some leagues don't forgive even that. And if you push the goalie into the net with the puck after they have stopped it, the goal comes off.

If a defender shoves you toward the goalie, still try to avoid them, early and where everyone can see it. Being shoved isn't a full defence in any league. What decides it is your effort. In at least one league it has to be every effort you can make, not just a try. Speeding up through the contact, putting your arms out or dropping your shoulder is the opposite of an effort. If you do any of those, plan on being thrown out of the game, and in some leagues on a match penalty.

And never arrive into the goalie. That is charging the goalie, and no league caps it at two minutes. It can get you thrown out in every league, and in some it is automatic. In some leagues a reckless one is a match penalty, which goes past being thrown out. Why so firm? A goalie watching the puck can't protect themselves from a hit they don't see coming, and it can hurt them.

Let me pull the shooting and the net together. Shoot from home plate, or when the goalie is moving or screened, with somebody in front. From the edges, shoot low for a rebound or a tip. Tip with your blade on the ice, and keep it low. Guess where the rebound goes, and shoot it straight back. If you are the high forward, be the safety, fill the line, and jump on the high rebound. At the net, stay out of the paint and off the line. Give ground when the goalie leans in, get out of their road back, and in a scramble, play the puck. Shoved toward the goalie, try to avoid them anyway. And never arrive into the goalie.

Sixteenth idea. Off the rush or off a set play. Have both.

There are two ways to attack in their end, and most amateur teams only have one.

Off the rush, you come in with speed before the defence is set. The chance comes from numbers and momentum, like two of you against one defender. It shows up in the first three or four seconds, then it disappears. The good plays are the late player arriving into the high slot, and the drive to the net, under control, that pulls a defender with you. And there is the delay. That is curling back toward the blue line, so a second wave of your players can arrive. Why shoot off the rush? A shot there is worth more than the same shot later, because the goalie is still moving too.

Off a set play, the defence is back and nothing is open. Now the chance comes from patience. That is the cycle, the low to high with a screen, the pass across the middle, the net front, and the pass from behind the net. You hold the puck until a tired defence makes a mistake. A defence that has chased for forty seconds is about to.

Why does having only one hurt? A team that can only rush has nothing when the rush is stopped at the blue line. They come in, lose it, and go back the other way. A team that can only cycle never catches the defence before it is set. So every chance it gets comes from the outside.

So have a plan for the second wave when the rush is stopped. Chip it past the defenceman, put a soft dump into open ice, or pull back and come again. And when a cycle turns the puck over, somebody should already be reading it and going the other way with speed.

Seventeenth idea. For defencemen, where to stand, and keeping the puck in.

You are always at one of three depths, and the difference matters a lot.

Just inside the blue line, at the point, is the default. You are the outlet for low to high, the shooting threat that stops the defence collapsing, and the safety against a counter-attack. Stay a stride inside the line, not on it. Why? If the puck goes all the way out, the zone is gone, and your whole team has to leave and come back in.

Down to the top of the circle is the active spot. You step down to shoot from a better angle, walk the line, or take a pass in the high slot. And you can still turn and get back before most counter-attacks get going.

Below the circles, past the hash marks, is always a commitment. You are a fourth forward now. So somebody must take your job at the blue line before you go, not after.

Now keeping the puck in. Their way out is usually a rim along the boards or a clear up the middle. The point is where it stays in or leaves.

Read it before it comes. Watch the defender about to move the puck, not the puck already on the wall. Be a stride inside the line before it gets there.

Then stop it first, and play it second. On a rim, get your stick blade, a skate turned along the boards, or your body against the wall in its path. If you use your body, the wall position goes with it. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the other player, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly. On a clear up the middle, square up in front of it. Head up and out of its line, the padded parts of you toward it, and your stick out of the lane.

Why stop it first? A puck you settle at the line is a new possession. A puck you swipe at and miss can be an odd-man rush the other way. That means more of them coming at your net than you have back.

If it is hard or bouncing and you can't settle it, chip it back down the wall to the corner, and keep the shift alive. And if it is already past you, turn and skate back. Don't chase it. The forward covering for you should already be on the way.

How hard your team wants you to fight for the line is the coach's call. The common way is to hold anything you can reach, and get back on anything you can't. Some teams would rather you let a hard rim go than risk it. Ask which yours wants.

Then the rules for covering each other, in the usual setup. If one defenceman leaves the line, the other stays. If the one who went gets the puck, they play as a forward until the play ends, and F three fills the line. If they don't get it, their first job is to get back into position. Chasing after a failed attempt is how odd-man rushes start. And say it out loud. I'm going. I've got you. Hold. Silent rotations are the ones that leave the blue line empty.

Why so careful? Two defencemen gone at once, when nobody called for it, is a quiet way to give up an odd-man rush. Nothing looks wrong until the puck turns over. Some teams do send both on purpose, in a five-man rotation or with a green light from the coach. So ask what yours does.

Let me pull the rush and the blue line together. Have a plan for the rush and a plan for the long possession. On the blue line, stay a stride inside it. Read the clear before it comes, stop it first, and chip it deep if you can't settle it. In the usual setup, one defenceman goes at a time, somebody fills the line, and you say it out loud.

Eighteenth idea. The pinch.

A pinch is a defenceman stepping down from the blue line to keep the puck in, or to win it on the wall. It isn't a step-up. A step-up is challenging an attacker in the neutral zone or at your own line.

Before anything else, the pinch is about the puck. Stick on the puck, not a hit. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call. And if you see the winger's numbers, pull up.

The test is short. If you can't arrive on contact, don't go. Arriving on contact means you and the puck get there at the same time. On a loose puck nobody else is racing for, the bar is higher. You need to be clearly first, because second place just leaves your back to the play.

Go when their winger is flat-footed or facing the glass. That tells you they can't get away with the puck. It does not mean you may hit them. Go when a forward can rotate up to cover your spot. And go on a loose puck, a bobble or a tired line. Those are the best windows.

Don't go when your forwards are already below you and nobody can cover the line. Don't go when their player has already turned up the ice with speed. Don't go when you are protecting a narrow lead late. And don't go when your partner hasn't seen you go.

Why so strict? A late pinch leaves the puck behind you, with your forwards below it. That is how three-on-twos against you start, three of them coming at two of yours.

Here is how. Get your stick into the passing lane before your feet commit. Angle the winger toward the boards, not the middle. Close with short, quick steps, and get your feet under you for the last two or three strides. Don't speed up into the arrival. Building up speed into contact, even with two hard strides, can be charging. And jumping into a check is charging everywhere. Then take the puck.

Or seal the wall. Sealing means getting your body between the winger and the puck along the boards. You do it with position alone. No shove, no lean, no arm round them, never your stick across them, and never into their back. Keep your skates out of it, never pin them on the boards, and never lift anyone off their feet. Even going slowly, a crash on the boards can hurt.

A pinch takes you to the wall, so the wall position is yours too, in every league. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the other player, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly.

If the pinch fails, get back toward the slot, not down the boards. Following the puck down the wall leaves the middle open. Cutting to the slot gets your gap back, and slows them down until help arrives.

How often to pinch is your coach's call. Some let both defencemen go, some only the one on the puck side, and some say never below the tops of the circles. Coaches see a pinch the system doesn't expect as one of the commonest causes of odd-man rushes against amateur teams. That is coaching experience, not a count. Ask.

Nineteenth idea. The hits you must never make on a pinch.

First, find out whether you may hit at all. Check the game, not just the age group. Body checking isn't allowed in younger age groups, in most girls' and women's hockey, or in most adult leagues. And a local league can ban it at any level. Where checking isn't allowed, the pinch is angling, body position and playing the puck. Some leagues that allow a little contact for the puck still make any step or glide into a player a penalty, and a hard one can get you thrown out.

Where checking is allowed, the gate is the same. Stick on the puck first. Arrive while the winger still has the puck. If you decide on a hit after the puck has gone, that is a late hit. No league caps a late hit at two minutes. In some leagues it can throw you out even if nobody is hurt. And in at least one league, any check you could have avoided on a player who has just passed or shot is a penalty.

Most often, the winger chips the puck past you as you arrive. Then go for the puck, not the winger. Turn and skate to it. Never cut across into the winger, and never hit them. Moving sideways into a player without the puck can be a penalty in every league, and the same hit can cost more than two minutes.

A winger facing the glass can't see you or protect themselves. It is on you to avoid the contact. If you can see their numbers, pull up. Take the puck, seal the wall, or angle them off it.

A hit in the back is checking from behind. No league lets that cost just two minutes. Plan on being thrown out of the game. If you drive a player from behind into the boards or the net when they can't protect themselves, plan on a match penalty. In some leagues the referee has no choice. A match penalty goes past being thrown out.

Never pinch in as a check onto a battle your teammate is already in. Arrive beside your teammate. Seal the wall, not the other player's body, and keep your arms, your stick and your skates out of it. If you end up behind them, play the puck. In at least one league, a player already battling for the puck can't be body checked at all, anywhere on the ice. A reckless one throws you out. And in at least one league, any reckless hit is a match penalty, so plan on that.

Seal the wall with your position and pin the puck, not the player — never hold with an arm or push off with a skate. If you wrap an arm round a player on the boards, that is holding. If you use your skate to push them off, in at least one league that is a major, you are thrown out, and you miss the next game too.

Here is the pinch, once more. Go only when you will arrive as the puck does and a forward can cover your line. Stick on the puck, not a hit. Find out first whether you may hit at all. Feet under you, and no speeding up into it. Seal with position alone. Never pin them, and never an arm, a skate or a stick across anyone. Never hit the winger who chipped it past you, never hit a winger facing the glass, and never check into a battle your teammate is already in. If you see their numbers, pull up. And if it fails, get back to the slot.

Twentieth idea. Faceoffs in their end.

A faceoff in their end is the one time you can put all five players exactly where you want them, with the puck about to arrive at a known spot. Most amateur teams waste it. That is a coaching view, not a count.

Why does a set play work? Four players start moving on the drop, before anyone knows who won it. So the shooter and the screen are already on their way.

Here are the common ones. Win it back to the point and screen at once. The inside winger goes straight to the net front, and the outside winger goes to the rebound spot. It is the simplest and the most reliable, because everyone was already moving.

Win it back to a winger set up for a one-timer, a shot straight off the pass. That winger has to line up as a shooter before the drop, not as a defender. Keep that backswing short if your league limits slap shots.

Win it forward and attack straight away. It is high reward if their centre is slow to react. But it puts your centre below the puck, and it goes badly if you lose the draw.

Tie up their centre's stick, and let a winger take the puck. Your centre lifts or presses the other centre's stick with their own, for an instant, over or under the blade, then plays the puck. Stick on stick only. Never their hands, never a hook, never holding it down, and never a hand on it. Why do it? It is the usual answer to a centre who keeps beating yours clean. Nobody wins it cleanly, and your wingers are already leaning in.

Or delay for the screen. The defenceman takes the win but skates sideways for a beat before shooting, so the net-front player can get set. A shot half a second late with a screen beats one on time without.

Whatever your team runs, everyone must know it before the whistle. The two commonest failures are a net-front player who arrives after the shot, and a defenceman who fires it into the first shin pad. And keep your wingers out of the circle until the drop. A winger creeping in gets your centre thrown out of the draw.

Plan for losing it too, because you will lose roughly half. One forward, usually the winger nearest the puck, pressures straight away. Stick on the puck, not a hit. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call, and if you see their numbers, pull up. The point is to stop a clean pass out and force a rim or a chip. The other two forwards turn and skate, rather than watch. A forward standing still when the puck is lost is beaten. The defencemen hold the line and read it. Is it a rim to stop, or a clean exit to back off from?

Why plan for the loss? Having a job for the loss is what lets you be aggressive on the win. Teams play the draw timidly because they have no plan for losing it.

Let me pull it all together.

Take your shot from home plate, or with the goalie out of position. Look across the middle before you look up the wall. Put a body in front for every shot, outside the paint and off the line. Know where the post is, head up, never duck, and hold your spot with your feet. Never start contact with the goalie. Decide before the shot that you are crashing the net, stick on the ice, and play the puck, not a body, and never the goalie. Keep somebody above the puck.

Keep the triangle, with somebody always in home plate, and end every cycle in something. On the wall, keep your feet moving. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the other player, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly. Behind the net, look to pass out to the slot. Only shoot from the point with somebody in front, and shoot it low to get it through. Have a plan for the rush and a plan for the long possession.

On defence in their end, keep the puck in, and pinch only when you will arrive as the puck does and someone can cover your line. Stick on the puck, not a hit. Find out whether you may hit at all. Never hit a player who no longer has the puck, and never hit one facing the glass.

Much of this depends on what your team plays. So here are three questions for your coach. What shape do we play in their end, and who takes the net front? When do you want our defencemen pinching, and how deep can they go? And what is our faceoff play in their end, and what does everyone do if we lose it? Then one for your league. Is body checking allowed in our games?

Go and ask. Their answers tell you how your team wants you to play in their end, and now you know why each answer makes sense.

### 9. Where this script is weakest
