===== SCRIPT START =====

This episode is about getting your body ready to play hockey, and looking after it. It takes its time. For each piece, I will tell you what to do, and also why it works. If you know why something works, you can still make a good choice when your week, or your game, doesn't go to plan.

Before any instructions, a few words you will hear all the way through. I will explain them now, so they don't get in the way later.

A shift. That is your turn on the ice. You skate hard, then you come off and sit on the bench while another group of players goes on.

Aerobic fitness. Your body has a steady engine that burns fuel using oxygen. It is slow to get going, but it can keep running for a very long time. Aerobic fitness is how good that steady engine is.

Conversational pace. This is a pace easy enough that you could hold a chat while you do it. If you can't talk, you are going too hard for easy work.

Intervals. These are hard efforts with easy rests in between, done again and again. A hockey game is really one long set of intervals.

Your groin. That is the inside of the top of your thighs. The muscles there pull your legs back in toward each other. Coaches call them the adductors.

Carbohydrate. That is food like rice, pasta, potatoes and bread. Your muscles store it, and it is the main fuel for a hockey game.

A concussion. This is a brain injury caused by force reaching the head. It can come from a hit to the head, from a hit to the body that snaps the head, or from a fall.

A red flag. In this episode, a red flag is a sign after a knock to the head that means you phone for an ambulance straight away.

CRT six. That is short for the Concussion Recognition Tool, version six. It is a free sheet made for people who aren't medical, standing on a bench. It lists the signs to look for, and what to do.

The checker. In this episode, the checker means any player from the other team coming at you, whether they are after the puck or just coming at you.

That is the vocabulary. Now the job.

Start with what a game asks of your body. A shift lasts about thirty to eighty seconds. Then you sit on the bench for two to five minutes. You do that about twenty times a game. All together, that is around fifteen to twenty-five minutes on the ice.

So the question isn't how hard you can go once. It is how quickly you recover between shifts, and how well you still think when you are tired. That decides whether your third period looks like your first.

I will start with four big ideas, because they matter most. If you only listen to the first part of this episode, take those four with you. After that, we will go through your training week, then game day, then looking after yourself after the game, and add the detail.

First idea. Build the easy fitness that rebuilds you between shifts.

To see why, it helps to know how your body powers a shift.

Your first three or four hard strides run on a small store of ready-made energy in your muscles. That store also powers a shot, or a battle on the boards. It runs down in seconds.

After that, your muscles burn carbohydrate fast, without waiting for oxygen to arrive. That is the burning feeling in your legs late in a long shift.

Then you sit down. And while you sit on the bench, your steady aerobic engine rebuilds that ready-made store. The rebuilding needs oxygen. So the steady engine is the one that refills the fast ones.

That is why hockey is short, all-out effort inside a shift, but aerobic across a whole game.

A good aerobic engine gives you more than that, too. It clears away the waste from hard efforts faster. And your heart rate sits lower at any given effort. Put together, that means a third period that looks like your first.

Most amateur players are short on this steady fitness, not on the all-out kind.

How do you build it? With work that is genuinely easy, at conversational pace. Cycling, rowing, running, or a brisk walk uphill, for thirty to sixty minutes. It should feel too easy. That is the point. Most amateurs do their easy sessions too hard and their hard sessions too easy, and get the benefit of neither.

Do it off the ice. Ice time is expensive, and it is better spent on skills and on efforts that look like shifts. This is the session most amateurs skip, because it feels pointless. It isn't.

And one more reason it matters to you. If your team only rolls two or three lines, you get far less rest between shifts than the professionals do. So the rebuilding matters more to you, not less.

So that is the first idea. Easy, steady work off the ice, at conversational pace, because it is what refills you on the bench.

Second idea. Change before you need to, not when you need to.

Here is why. That ready-made energy is mostly gone in the first fifteen to twenty seconds of hard skating. Your heart rate climbs toward its maximum. And the first thing to go usually isn't your legs. It is your decisions.

A tired player doesn't usually fall over. Instead, they stop looking around, because looking around takes attention, and attention is hard to find when you are gassed. They take the easy pass instead of the right one.

They stop skating back and start gliding. That is how the other team ends up with more attackers coming at your net than you have defenders back. Coaches call that an odd-man rush.

They reach with the stick instead of moving their feet, which is how coaches explain a lot of tripping and hooking calls. And they stay out for another twenty seconds, because deciding to change is a decision too.

That chain, your judgement going before your legs, is coaching reasoning from how the body works. Nobody has measured it as a sequence. But what it leads to costs you nothing.

A forty-second shift played well beats a seventy-second shift where you were a passenger for the last thirty. So aim for shifts nearer thirty to forty-five seconds. The long shift you get stuck in is where things fall apart.

You won't always choose. Sometimes you are stuck out there, in a long spell in your own end, or on a team with only two and a half lines. That is exactly why shift length is part of how you play, not just part of how fit you are.

And here is a test you can use in any game. If you catch yourself gliding, you needed to change fifteen seconds ago.

The first two ideas fit together. The fitter you are, the longer your judgement lasts. And the shorter your shift, the less of it you use up.

That is why conditioning is a hockey sense issue, not just a fitness issue. What you are really training is good decisions late in a shift.

So that is the second idea. Short shifts, and change early, because your thinking goes before your legs do.

Third idea. Warm up off the ice for ten to fifteen minutes before every game, every time.

Of all the things you control yourself, a warm-up is the best-supported way to lower your chance of getting hurt.

Here is why it works. A warm muscle tightens and relaxes faster, and it is less stiff. So you can produce force faster, and you move more freely. And you are less likely to ask a cold muscle for an all-out effort it wasn't ready for. A warm-up also helps get your head ready for the game.

And here is the finding that matters most. What the research rewards isn't a clever routine. It is doing it every time. A plain routine you never skip beats a clever one you do now and then.

In coaches' experience, the off-ice warm-up is the thing most often skipped in adult rec hockey. Nobody has counted it. Players arrive fifteen minutes before the puck drops, get dressed, and go straight out cold for a hard lap. The fix is ten minutes of moving before you put your kit on.

So that is the third idea. Warm up off the ice, by moving, before every game. Doing it every time is what counts.

Fourth idea. Concussion. This is the one part of this episode that is not a judgement call.

Most of what you will hear today is advice you can weigh up for yourself. This part isn't. It is simple, and it is worth knowing before you ever need it.

After a knock to the head, if a concussion is suspected, the player comes off straight away and does not play again that day. Not that shift, not that period, not that game.

Suspected, not confirmed. You don't need to be sure. Nobody at a rec rink is qualified to rule a concussion out. If in doubt, sit them out.

And it is never the player's own decision. Symptoms can show up late. Judgement is one of the things a concussion affects. And a concussed player will often tell you they are fine.

You don't have to be knocked out to have a concussion. Being knocked out happens in fewer than one in ten concussions. Most happen without it. So don't wait to see whether someone was knocked out before you take a knock seriously.

Now what to do if a player is hurt.

Treat every knock to the head as a possible neck injury too, even if they have no neck pain. If a player may have hurt their head or neck, somebody phones for help first. Don't help them up, and don't move them. Nobody moves them except to keep their airway clear so they can breathe, unless they are trained to do more. Leave their helmet on, and the rest of their kit.

Make sure the scene is safe, and check they are breathing. Reassure them, keep them warm, and tell them to keep still. Then wait for whoever is trained.

Phone for an ambulance straight away if anything looks serious. That includes neck pain or tenderness, however mild. A fit or a seizure. Lost vision, or double vision. Being knocked out. Confusion that is getting worse, or getting drowsy and less responsive. Weakness, numbness or tingling in an arm or a leg. Being sick more than once. A severe headache, or one that is getting worse. Getting more and more restless, agitated or aggressive. And a skull that looks out of shape. Any one of those is enough.

Some of those can look like ordinary signs, so here is the difference. A headache is a sign of concussion. A severe headache, or one that is getting worse, is a reason to phone. Being a bit confused about the score is a sign. Confusion that is growing, or a player getting more and more drowsy, is a reason to phone. A sign that is getting worse can mean bleeding inside the head, so treat it as a red flag.

But don't wait for the others to get worse. Neck pain, being sick more than once, or weakness or numbness is an ambulance call the first time you see it, even if they got up by themselves. Getting worse can add a red flag. It never takes one away.

CRT six asks for one more thing. A player with a known physical or developmental disability should be taken off sooner.

Short of a red flag, the ordinary signs that should make you suspect a concussion include these. Headache. Pressure in the head. Dizziness. Feeling dazed, stunned or not right. Blurred vision. Being bothered by light or noise. Feeling sick. Balance problems. Slowed responses. Confusion about the score or the period. A change in mood or behaviour. And gaps in memory around the knock. Any one of those still means off for the day.

CRT six is free. Have it on your phone, and know where the red flag list is on it before you need it.

Someone stays with a player who might be concussed, from the moment they come off. This is the part most often skipped, because it carries on after everyone has left the rink. None of it depends on how the player says they feel.

CRT six says someone stays for at least the first three hours, and the British guidance says the first twenty-four hours. They don't go home alone. They don't drive until a healthcare professional says they can, and that is not the same as feeling well enough. No alcohol, no recreational drugs, and no medicine their healthcare professional hasn't prescribed. And if anything gets worse, get medical help straight away.

The simplest way to make sure this happens is that whoever drives them home also stays with them.

And feeling better is not the all-clear. A medical professional decides when they play again. Until they have been assessed, they stay out of anything with a risk of a knock to the head, a fall or a collision, even if their symptoms have gone. That is wider than hockey. A mountain bike, a skateboard and a five-a-side game are out too.

If you play in Britain, learn one more thing now. England Ice Hockey has adopted the UK concussion guidance for all its clubs, and its list of red flags is longer.

Five things on it aren't on CRT six's list at all. Losing memory for what happened before or after the knock. An unusual change in behaviour. Being on blood-thinning medicine. Being drunk or on drugs at the time. And a history of brain surgery or a bleeding disorder.

The blood-thinner one is easy to miss, because it is a fact about the player, not about the injury. A teammate on blood thinners who takes a knock to the head is an ambulance call on that alone, even if they get up and say they are fine. So find out in advance who on your team takes them.

The British list also fires earlier in a few places. Twitching in an arm or a leg is a red flag. So is a player lying stiff and still. Any sign that the skull could be broken is one, like a cut, a bruise, swelling, or bad pain where they were hit. So is any new trouble speaking, understanding, reading or writing, or with balance.

And if you can't get a response from a player, phone for an ambulance. Where the two lists differ, act on whichever tells you to phone first.

Let me pull those four ideas together, because they are the core of this episode.

One. Build the easy aerobic fitness that rebuilds you on the bench. Conversational pace, off the ice, thirty to sixty minutes. It should feel too easy.

Two. Change before you need to. Your judgement goes before your legs. If you catch yourself gliding, you needed to change fifteen seconds ago.

Three. Warm up off the ice before every game, and do it every time. Doing it beats doing it cleverly.

Four. If a concussion is suspected, the player comes off and doesn't play again that day. Treat every knock to the head as a possible neck injury. Phone for help first, don't move them, and leave the helmet on. Any red flag, or any sign getting worse, means an ambulance. Someone stays with them, and they don't drive or go home alone. And a medical professional decides when they play again.

Now your training week. This part is about what you do off the ice between games, and why each piece is there.

Fifth idea. Train the shift itself.

The easy work rebuilds you. But you also need to train the hard effort, and the best way is to copy the game. About thirty to forty-five seconds hard, then two to four minutes easy. Repeat that six to twelve times, on a bike, a rower, a hill, or the ice.

Why train both kinds of fitness? Picture two players.

The first is a good distance runner who is exhausted after two shifts. Their steady engine is strong. They can keep going for a long time. What they can't do is produce a big effort very quickly, put up with the burn that comes with it, and then repeat it.

And the skating stride loads the hips, the groin and the backside in a way running never does. Running fitness is useful in hockey. But it is fitness for running.

The second player is a sprinter, or a strong gym player, who is great on the first shift and fades by the third period. Their steady engine is weak. So they come back for each shift with a less full tank than the time before. By the third period, there isn't much left.

So here is the practical point. Your all-out fitness decides how good your best shift is. Your aerobic fitness decides how much your twentieth shift looks like your first. You need both, and most amateur players are short on the second.

If you get the balance wrong either way, it shows. If you train only with long, steady runs, the first hard shift blows you out. If you train only with sprints and weights, you disappear in the third period. Among keen amateurs, that second mistake is the more common one.

It helps to know how hard a shift really is. Your heart rate climbs toward its maximum during each one. In one small study of six elite junior players, they spent about eleven minutes of a single game above ninety percent of their maximum heart rate.

Here is why the rest matters so much. The long rest is what makes it hockey. If you shorten it, you have built a different session. That is still useful, but it is no longer the thing a game asks of you. Those numbers are a sensible starting shape worked out from the game, not a tested programme.

If you train only with easy work, you will cope with the bench, but not with the shift. Hard efforts with long rests give you a bigger effort, and you can repeat it. They also teach you to put up with the burn of a long shift.

Short sprints help too. Six to ten seconds flat out, then thirty to sixty seconds of rest. Those train the ready-made energy that powers your first few strides.

And don't judge your fitness by a lab number. One small study found that the usual lab test of aerobic fitness didn't predict who coped best with repeated hard efforts. The steady engine still does the rebuilding. So train the quality, not the test score.

So that is training the shift. Hard efforts the length of a shift, with long easy rests, because that is the shape of a game.

Sixth idea. Get strong in your legs, and be honest about what it does.

Leg strength gives you a harder push, a faster start, more balance on your edges, and more weight behind you in a battle for the puck. But strength explains only part of your skating speed. It won't fix a fault in your stride. And as your skating improves, technique is what holds you back.

Train squats, deadlifts, split squats, lunges, hip thrusts, calf work and jumps. Skating is done one leg at a time, so train one leg at a time as well. And give sideways and twisting movement real attention, because your stride pushes out to the side, not straight back.

Get coached on the lifts before you add weight. A badly loaded barbell is one of the few ways an off-ice programme can injure you outright. And a young player should get qualified coaching before lifting at all.

Your middle matters too. Shot power comes up from the ground through a turning body, not from your arms. And in a battle along the boards, your middle has to pass the force from your legs on without folding.

So train it to resist twisting and bending, with things like planks. Carrying something heavy is good too, because that is close to what a battle feels like. Then add some twisting work, like medicine-ball throws.

And your hips. Your stride is a deep, wide push and pull, thousands of times a game. Nothing else in daily life does that. So build strength at the end of your range, not only stretching into it. That means hip work through the full range, hip rotations sitting on the floor, and deep squat holds. Being able to reach a wide position is no use if you can't control it when you push hard from there.

Seventh idea. Strengthen your groin on purpose, before anything hurts.

Your stride pushes your leg out and brings it back, thousands of times a game. That trains the muscles that push the leg out far harder than the ones that pull it back in. And a groin strain is the classic hockey injury. In one study of professional goalies, groin strains were the most common injury, and they cost the most days out.

One study measured professional hockey players before the season. A player whose pull-in strength was under eighty percent of their push-out strength was seventeen times more likely to strain their groin. So your groin is probably weak compared with the muscles that work against it. Training it is fixing a gap, not an optional extra.

Does strengthening it help? The same researchers then gave professional players a groin-strengthening programme before the season. Groin strains fell from eleven in the two seasons before to three in the two seasons after. That was a before-and-after comparison, not a proper trial, so other changes could explain some of it. But it points the right way.

But don't believe anyone who says one exercise will keep you safe. No single exercise has been shown to prevent a groin strain.

The best-known one is the Copenhagen exercise. You lie on your side and press your top leg against a bench or a partner. The main review behind its strength numbers rated its own evidence as very low certainty, and it has since been withdrawn. So train your groin because of that weak-groin finding in hockey players, not because one exercise is proven.

In short, strengthen the pull-in muscles before they get hurt, but don't treat any exercise as a guarantee.

If you try the Copenhagen exercise, start very gently, with an easier knee-supported version if you need it. Diving straight in makes people extremely sore. That is coaching caution, not a tested prescription.

And if you have groin or hip pain that keeps going, see a physiotherapist rather than stretching more. Pain that has lasted more than a couple of weeks, or a deep pinch in your hip on a wide stride, goes to a physiotherapist or a sports doctor. Hip and groin pain is common in hockey players, and it is not just a tight groin.

Studies of hockey hips often find changes in the shape of the hip bones. That is not a reason to panic. Plenty of players have those shapes and no pain at all. What it does mean is that ongoing pain needs a proper look, not more stretching.

Eighth idea. Put it together in a realistic week.

If you are an adult rec player, you have a job, and one or two skates a week. Here is the realistic version, and the reasons behind it.

Doing it regularly beats doing it hard. Three sessions a week for a year beats six a week for six weeks followed by nothing.

Your games are already your interval training. If you skate twice a week, you are already getting hockey-shaped hard work, and you don't need to add much more. So your time off the ice should mostly go on what games don't give you. Strength, an aerobic base, and strong hips and groin.

And if you are new to hockey, working on your skating technique is a better investment than fitness. A good stride makes everything cost less energy.

Here is a sensible default of about ninety minutes a week. It is a reasoned plan, not a tested programme, and you should get coaching on the lifts.

Session one is strength, forty-five minutes. Five minutes of warm-up first. Then a squat or a hinge, like a goblet squat, a trap-bar deadlift or a barbell squat, three sets of five to eight. Then a one-leg exercise, like a split squat, a step-up or a lunge, three sets of eight on each leg. A push and a pull, like a press and a row, three sets of eight each.

Then groin work. That is the Copenhagen exercise or its easier knee-supported version, plus a squeeze you hold, starting very gently. And finish with core work that resists twisting, or a heavy carry, three sets.

Session two is conditioning, thirty minutes, and it changes from week to week. One week, hockey intervals. That is eight efforts of forty seconds hard on a bike or a rower, with two to three minutes easy between them. That is the game, off the ice. The next week, thirty minutes genuinely easy, at conversational pace. That is the one that rebuilds you between shifts, and the one people skip.

The last fifteen minutes is hip and groin mobility, waking up your backside muscles, and a few minutes for your shoulders and upper back. Three five-minute doses across the week work better than one fifteen-minute session. You can do them in front of the television.

If you only have forty-five minutes a week, keep the strength session, and get your conditioning from your games. Strength and groin work are what your hockey doesn't give you. Aerobic fitness is the thing you will miss in the third period. So if you can add one thing, add one easy thirty-minute aerobic session. It costs little and gives you a lot back.

Don't go from nothing to a full programme in one week. Build up. Coaches see enthusiasm as how adult amateurs get hurt in training, but that is an observation, not a measured ranking.

The measured evidence points at games. In one review of women's college hockey, games had more than three times the injury rate of practices, and concussion was the most common injury. So don't treat the collision risk as the smaller one. And don't drop the warm-up because you are short of time.

Ninth idea. Build in the off-season, keep it in-season, and never go hard the day before a game.

Two patterns waste the year. One is doing nothing all summer, panicking in September, training hard for six weeks, getting hurt or bored, and coasting through the season. The other is hammering the gym in-season until you are too sore to skate, and then taking four months off.

So split the year in three.

The off-season is for the big changes. Your aerobic base, your top strength, power, and fixing stiffness and imbalances. You can do more and get more tired then, because there is no game on Thursday to be fresh for.

It is also the time to get a nagging problem properly sorted with a physiotherapist, rather than working round it between games. Don't take the whole off-season off, because the strength and fitness you built don't keep. And skate now and then if you can, because fitness from a bike doesn't carry over perfectly to skating.

Pre-season is for sharpening. Shift toward hockey-shaped intervals, power, and getting on the ice. That means hard efforts the length of a shift, thirty to forty-five seconds, with two to four minutes easy. And cut your total training back, so you arrive fresh rather than worn out.

In-season, the games are your hard conditioning. Your gym work is there to keep what you built, not to add to it. Keep the weights challenging, and cut the number of sets.

Two good strength sessions a week is a reasonable amount to hold on to what you have. The evidence for that exact number is thin. It supports twice a week being enough, not twice a week being best.

And lifting in-season won't wreck you if it is sensible. In one study, young footballers did twelve weeks of moderate strength training during their season. They got stronger without raising the signs of muscle damage.

Never do heavy leg lifting, or add a hard conditioning session, the day before a game. You will play worse and gain nothing. Put your hardest session as far from game day as your week allows. And keep some easy aerobic work going. It is the cheapest thing to keep, and the first thing people drop.

So across the year, build when there are no games, keep it ticking over when there are, and arrive at every game fresh.

Tenth idea. Young players.

If you are a young player, or you coach one, this part is for you. Youth training is not adult training made smaller. Young players are still growing, and the growing parts of their bones can be hurt in ways adult bone can't.

So the focus should be broad athletic skill. Coordination, agility, balance, movement and speed, rather than squeezing out as much hockey fitness as possible.

The usual guidance from youth sports medicine has three parts. Don't specialise in one sport before about age twelve. Don't train more hours a week than the player's age in years, with a general limit of around sixteen hours a week. And play another sport in the off-season.

Those are agreed recommendations, not tested limits. But the evidence points the same way. Specialising early has been linked with more long-lasting injuries. Among professional American football players, playing several sports at school was linked with fewer injuries and longer careers. And burnout is a real risk, as well as injury.

The hip studies point the same way. The players with changes in their hips had played a great deal of hockey from a young age. That is another reason for variety. And take a young player's hip or groin pain seriously if it keeps going. Don't put it down to growing pains.

And strength training. Expert statements support it for young players, on one condition. It has to be properly planned and supervised. So get qualified coaching before a young player lifts at all. If you coach a young player, work with qualified coaches and, where it is needed, medical professionals.

Let me pull the training week together.

Train the shift, with hard efforts and long easy rests. Get stronger in your legs, one leg at a time, and get coached on the lifts. Strengthen your groin before it hurts, and take pain that keeps going to a physiotherapist. Do it regularly rather than hard. Build in the off-season, keep it in-season, and never go hard the day before a game. And a young player needs broad skills, more than one sport, and qualified coaching before lifting.

Now game day. This part follows you from your last meal to your last shift.

Eleventh idea. Fuel before you play.

Hockey burns through the carbohydrate stored in your muscles far more than its short playing time suggests. Single muscle fibres can run empty, even while the muscle as a whole still has fuel. And an empty fibre can't contract hard.

That is why running low on fuel and fluid is one of the likeliest reasons for third-period tiredness. In elite players, how well someone plays in a game has been linked to how much fuel was stored in their muscles before it.

So eat your main meal three to four hours before the game. Build it round carbohydrate, like rice, pasta, potatoes or bread, with some protein. Keep fat and fibre low, because they slow digestion and leave your stomach heavy.

If you need it, have a small top-up one to two hours before, like a banana, toast or a cereal bar. Keep it small and familiar.

Those times are standard sports-nutrition advice, and they are approximate. What you have tested and can digest matters more than the clock. If you have a dietitian, use their numbers instead.

Never try a new food or drink on game day. Test it at practice first.

And arrive with enough to drink already in you. In one small study of para hockey players, four in ten turned up a little short of fluid before they had even started. A glass of water an hour before is worth more than a litre at the rink.

Twelfth idea. The warm-up itself.

Why bother, when the evidence is mostly from other sports? Here is what it shows.

There is wider evidence too. In one review of teenage team athletes, warm-up programmes with several parts cut total injuries by about a third. Most of those athletes played other sports, not hockey, and the review calls its own evidence low quality. But it is the strongest reason to bother.

In studies of hamstring injuries, players who did their prevention routine in at least three sessions out of four had roughly two-thirds less risk of injury. Players who rarely did it got almost no benefit.

Be honest about that evidence. Most of it is from football, meaning soccer, and other team sports, not hockey. And the review rates its own evidence as low certainty. So take the pattern, not the exact numbers. A plain routine you never skip beats a clever one you do now and then.

So here is an off-ice routine. Ten to fifteen minutes, before you get dressed, in four parts.

First, raise your temperature. Three or four minutes of light jogging on the spot, skipping, or a fast walk around the building. Get warm enough to notice it.

Second, loosen up by moving. Leg swings front to back and side to side, and the side-to-side ones are for your groin. Then walking lunges with a twist, hip openers, deep squat holds, arm circles and shoulder rolls.

Third, wake up the muscles your stride depends on. Glute bridges, side steps with a band round your legs, and a few squats with no weight.

Fourth, a few short sprints, jumps or hops, building up to nearly full effort. That gets your nervous system ready to produce force fast. It is the part people leave out.

That four-part shape is a standard coaching framework applied to hockey. The research backs structured warm-ups in general, not this exact order.

Warm up by moving, not by holding stretches. Long, still stretches just before you play can leave you a little weaker and slower for a while. And stretching before you play doesn't prevent injury. The loss comes mainly from long holds. So a short hold of ten or fifteen seconds inside a fuller warm-up is unlikely to ruin your game. Stretch for range of movement at a separate time.

Then the few minutes you get on the ice. Most rec games give you three to five. Skate first, shoot later.

Two or three laps, building from easy to brisk. Include skating backwards, crossovers both ways, and a few hard starts and stops. Your edges need waking up more than your wrist shot does.

Then handle the puck a little. A few touches and some short passes get your hands and your feel for the puck going. Then shoot, starting easy. Don't open with a full slap shot from the blue line. That asks a cold body for an all-out twist.

Give your goalie proper warm-up shots. Not the top corner from the slot on your first shot. The top corner is head height, and a cold goalie is least ready to track that first one. Start with easy shots from distance and work in. Your goalie can't ease into the game the way you can. That is a coaching caution, not a law of hockey.

Finish with one or two full-speed bursts of about five seconds, so your first shift isn't your warm-up.

Thirteenth idea. Drink while you play, and keep your shifts short.

You sweat more than a cold rink suggests. Elite players have been measured losing around two litres an hour. So drink on the bench every shift or two, in small amounts, rather than one big drink between periods. That little-and-often habit is the usual advice from practitioners, not something those studies tested.

Water is fine for most rec games. For a long, hot or very hard game, a standard sports drink with carbohydrate and salt makes sense, because it replaces fluid, adds fuel and replaces salt.

Between periods is your best chance for a slightly bigger drink. On a long tournament day, add a small carbohydrate snack.

And keep drinking after the game. In one study of college players, some of the fluid they lost was still missing the next morning.

And keep your shifts short. Change before you need to. A forty-second shift played well beats a long one where you were a passenger.

Fourteenth idea. Meeting the boards. No amount of gym work protects your shoulder the way meeting the boards correctly does. Shoulder pads that fit, and a strong upper back and shoulder blades, help too. But most of the protection is skill and awareness. And this is your job in every league, checking or not, because a wall doesn't need an opponent to hurt you.

The shoulder is the most common place for an upper-body injury in young hockey players. The classic hockey shoulder injury comes from landing on the point of your shoulder, or being driven into the boards.

Go into the wall on an angle, not straight at it. If contact is coming and you still have time to turn, here is what you do. Skates parallel to the boards, feet apart, knees bent. Forearm and hip to the boards. Never the point of your shoulder, on any approach, and no part of your shoulder if you can help it. Head up, chin off your chest. Never turn your back to the checker. And never duck.

Here is why those last parts matter. They protect your head and neck. If you turn your back, the contact becomes a hit from behind, and you can't see it coming. If you duck, or tuck your chin to your chest, your neck straightens and your head meets the boards first. That can injure your neck even at walking speed. So keep it up even going slowly.

Taking a hit in open ice has its own technique, and what it says about your shoulder is the opposite of what you do at the wall. So don't bring it to the wall.

If you are already facing the wall and can't turn, things have gone wrong, and this is damage limitation. Spread the hit over as much of you as you can, with your stick and gloves up on the glass. Head up, chin off your chest, and keep your head off the glass. It is not the position you were aiming for.

From here on, I will use one short version, the same words every time. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the checker, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly.

Two more injuries you can make less likely.

Your wrist. About three in ten upper-body hockey injuries land on a wrist or a finger, and the wrist is one of the most often broken places in the game. So wear gloves that cover the gap between the glove and your elbow pad, because that is where slashes land. Wear elbow pads that fit. And don't put a bare hand out to break a fall.

Your knee. The ligament on the inside of the knee is the classic hockey knee injury. It usually comes from a blow to the outside of the knee, or a leg trapped in a collision or against the boards. General leg strength, one-leg strength, balance, and landing and slowing down well all help. Some knee injuries are just collisions, and no training prevents those.

So that is meeting the boards. On an angle, forearm and hip, head up, and never your back to the checker. It is your job in every league.

Fifteenth idea. Know whether body checking is allowed in your game.

A body check is using your body to knock an opponent off the puck. One review of youth hockey reports that body checking is the biggest injury risk that can be changed. It links checking with about three times as many concussions. And it reports that banning checking has cut injury rates by fifty to seventy percent.

Those are figures the review gathered from other studies, not ones it measured. But the direction is clear. No amount of fitness does what a ban does.

So find out which kind of game you are in before you play. Your age doesn't settle it.

In North America, checking is banned in the younger age groups. That is twelve and under in the United States, and under-thirteen and below in Canada. But that is a floor, not an answer, because local bodies can ban it at older ages too. Adult hockey is split into checking and non-checking leagues.

And in girls' and women's hockey in the United States and Canada, checking is banned at every age. Playing the puck doesn't make a check legal there.

In Britain, your competition's own rules set the age. In the twenty twenty-six to twenty-seven season, in the junior leagues IHUK runs, under-tens and under-twelves are non-checking, and under-fourteens and up are checking. NIHL Divisions One and Two are checking. The women's national league, the WNIHL, is non-checking, so don't body check there at all. For any other league, ask.

British women's hockey has extra limits, even where a league allows any checking at all. If your league says non-checking, that wins. Otherwise a check is only allowed when you are clearly playing the puck. Even then, stepping or gliding into someone is a penalty. So is pushing or pinning them on the boards, or hitting a player who is standing still. That holds however clearly you were playing the puck.

Where checking is banned, a check is a penalty, and a deliberate bump, shove or push can be one too. If it is reckless or violent, or it injures someone, plan on being thrown out of the game. And it can mean a ban for games after this one, too.

Let me pull game day together.

Eat a carbohydrate meal hours before, and nothing new on game day. Arrive with enough to drink in you. Warm up off the ice, by moving, every time. On the ice, skate first and shoot later, and give your goalie easy shots to start. Drink a little every shift or two, and change before you need to.

At the boards, use the short version. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the checker, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly. And find out whether checking is allowed in your game before you play.

Now after the game. This part is about recovering, so your next game, or your next morning, goes better.

Sixteenth idea. Protect your sleep before you buy any recovery product.

Sleep, fluid, carbohydrate and protein are where the recovery evidence is. Of the recovery tools, sleep is the best supported, the cheapest and the most ignored. It is also the first thing most players give up when life gets busy. That is why it sits at the top. Nobody has measured it as the biggest effect in hockey.

There is also an early link between short sleep and a higher injury risk. That comes from a study that hadn't finished being checked by other scientists, so treat it as a hint, not a fact.

Aim for a regular seven to nine hours, which is the general advice for adults. A regular time to sleep and wake beats a big lie-in now and then. If you have to choose, protect the night before a game more than the night after.

Before a heavy stretch, like a tournament or a run of late games, get extra sleep beforehand rather than trying to catch up after. Extra sleep banked in advance helps you stay alert when your sleep is cut short later. And a twenty to thirty minute nap is fine on a day with an evening game.

Seventeenth idea. The late game.

A puck drop at half past ten at night is what most adult players face. It causes three problems at once.

First, you haven't eaten for hours. Dinner at half past six and a game at half past ten means playing on an empty tank. So eat a proper meal with carbohydrate in it three to four hours before. Then have a small, easy top-up an hour or so before, if you feel empty.

Second, you are already tired from a day's work. Accept that your ceiling is lower, and keep your shifts shorter to suit.

Third, and this is the real cost, you can't sleep afterwards. You finish near midnight, wound up, thirsty and hungry. So drink and eat something during the game and straight after it, not at one in the morning. Keep screens and bright light down afterwards.

Keep the post-game beer modest, because alcohol dries you out further and makes your sleep measurably worse. And give yourself a wind-down routine, rather than going straight from the rink to bed.

If you play at half past ten on a work night, lost sleep, not training, is your main recovery problem. No product fixes it. Plan the next morning around it.

So for a late game, eat early, keep your shifts short, and refuel at the rink, so you can get to sleep.

Eighteenth idea. Refuel, move easy the next day, and be honest about the products.

After the game, refuel. Carbohydrate comes first, with some protein for repair. Replace your fluid, using how you feel and the colour of your urine as a rough guide.

For most amateur players, how much you eat matters more than exactly when. The idea of a thirty-minute window after exercise is far weaker than it was once made out to be. If your next game or practice is more than a day away, a good meal within a couple of hours is plenty. Fast refuelling matters when you play again the same day or the next morning, like on a tournament weekend.

The day after, easy movement helps. A walk, an easy bike ride, or easy skating. Just don't make it hard enough to count as training.

Now the products. Ice baths help you feel less sore. But straight after a strength session, the cold may blunt what the session was for. That is because cooling cuts the blood flow to the muscle, and slows how it takes in the protein it uses to rebuild. So use one after a game or a tournament, when you want to feel better and play again soon, not after you have been lifting.

Compression clothing is genuinely unresolved. One broad review was favourable, and a recent trial found nothing. It is harmless and comfortable, but don't count on it.

Foam rolling and massage can make you feel less sore and move more freely for a while. But the evidence that they help you play better, or prevent injury, is weak. Feeling less sore can help you get back to training, but it is not repair. It doesn't break anything up inside the muscle.

And stretching after a game does little for soreness. Stretch because you want range of movement.

Most of this research is small and short. A lot of it measures how sore people feel, not whether they played better the next week. Where two reviews of the same method disagree, which happens a lot, the honest reading is that the effect is probably small. So spend your effort on sleep, fluid and food first.

Nineteenth idea. Going back after a concussion.

This part is about the days after a knock to the head, not the moment itself.

Going back to play is a medical decision, and it happens in stages. Everyday activity first, then light aerobic exercise, then exercise for your sport, then drills with no contact. Only after a medical professional clears the player does full-contact practice come, and then games. Each stage depends on coping with the one before. If symptoms come back, you step back.

Here is why it is so careful. A brain that is still recovering is more easily hurt again. And a second injury during recovery is linked to worse problems that last longer. That is the reason for the same-day rule, and the reason going back happens in stages.

A qualified medical professional who has assessed the actual player manages those stages. Children and teenagers are managed more carefully than adults. So be suspicious of any hockey resource that gives you a schedule.

So come off. Tell someone. Don't drive yourself home. See a doctor. And don't go back on the ice until a medical professional says you can, even if you feel fine by then. If you are a coach or a captain, taking a player off is yours to enforce, not the player's to overrule.

If you play in Britain, the UK concussion guidance adds some minimums underneath that medical decision. There is no return to competition before day twenty-one. The day of the injury counts as day zero.

That is a minimum, not a date. You aren't ready on day twenty-one because it is day twenty-one, and it doesn't replace the medical assessment or the stages. So a clearance on day ten doesn't put you in a British game on day ten.

Training with a risk of a knock to the head, including contact practice, waits until you have had no symptoms at rest for fourteen days. And it can't start before day fifteen.

The British guidance no longer expects a doctor to sign you through each stage. But it still needs the first step. No more physical sport or work activity, even if the symptoms go, until you have been assessed by a healthcare professional, or by calling one-one-one for the NHS. Get that done within twenty-four hours.

After that, call one-one-one if your symptoms get worse, or haven't improved fourteen days after the injury. And see your GP if you still have symptoms after twenty-eight days.

So at grassroots level, those minimums may be the only checks anyone makes after the first assessment. They apply even if nobody has cleared you, and you should still get cleared. The only exception is a player in elite or professional sport whose concussion is managed by specialist sports-concussion professionals. Grassroots hockey is not that group.

Let me pull it all together.

The big four first. Build the easy aerobic base that rebuilds you between shifts, and do it off the ice. Change before you need to. If you catch yourself gliding, you needed to change fifteen seconds ago. And warm up off the ice before every game, every time.

If a concussion is suspected, the player comes off and doesn't play again that day. Treat every knock to the head as a possible neck injury. Phone for help first, don't move them, and leave the helmet on. Phone for an ambulance if anything looks serious, like neck pain, a fit, being knocked out, or numbness or tingling, or if any sign gets worse. Someone stays with them, and they don't drive or go home alone. And a medical professional decides when they play again.

In your week, train the shift, with hard efforts and long rests. Get strong one leg at a time, and get coached on the lifts. Strengthen your groin before it hurts. Build in the off-season, keep it in-season, and never go hard the day before a game.

On game day, eat hours before and drink on the bench. At the boards, use the short version. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the checker, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly. And know whether checking is allowed in your game.

After the game, protect your sleep and refuel. Don't spend money on recovery before you have sorted sleep, fluid and food.

Some of this depends on your team and your league. So here are three questions to ask your coach. Is body checking allowed in our category? Who at our games is trained to look after a hurt player, and who phones for help? And how long do you want our shifts to be? If you play in Britain, add one more. Who on our team takes blood thinners?

And one thing to do before your next game. Put CRT six on your phone, and know where the red flag list is on it.

Then warm up, and keep your shifts short.

- **Concussion rule** → "suspected, not confirmed", "if in doubt, sit them out", "never the player's own decision" (same chunk, chunk 1).
- **Any knock to the head** → treat as a possible neck injury, even with no neck pain → somebody phones for help first → don't help them up or move them, except for the airway, unless trained → helmet and kit stay on → scene safe, breathing, reassure, warm, keep still, wait for whoever is trained (one chunk, chunk 2).
- **Ambulance list** → open ("That includes … Any one of those is enough") → a worsening sign becomes a red flag → neck pain, repeated vomiting and weakness are calls the first time → escalation never removes a flag → a disabled player comes off sooner (chunk 2).
- **Aftercare** → someone stays with them from the moment they come off → not conditional on how they feel → three hours CRT6, twenty-four hours UK → not home alone → no driving until a professional says so → no alcohol, recreational drugs or unprescribed medicine → worse means medical help → whoever drives them home stays → symptoms gone is not the all-clear → a medical professional decides → no risky activity, wider than hockey (chunk 2).
- **British red flags** → the blood-thinner flag fires alone → find out in advance → act on whichever list fires first (chunk 2).
- **Every recap that mentions concussion** (spine and close) carries: off for the day; every head knock a possible neck injury; phone first, don't move, helmet on; ambulance if anything looks serious or any sign worsens; someone stays, no driving, not home alone; a medical professional decides.
- **Boards method** → "your job in every league, checking or not" → never the point of the shoulder → head up, chin off chest → never your back, never duck → the reason (head and neck; a turned back is a hit from behind you can't see; a tucked chin straightens the neck; injury even at walking speed) → keep it up even going slowly → don't carry the open-ice shoulder cue to the wall → the facing-the-wall fallback labelled as damage limitation, head up and off the glass (one chunk, chunk 5).
- **Short form**, word for word, with "Keep it up even going slowly", three times: the boards idea, the game-day recap and the close.
- **Leg lifts** → "Get coached on the lifts before you add weight" → a young player gets qualified coaching before lifting at all (same chunk, chunk 3).
- **Copenhagen exercise** → no single exercise is proven → evidence very low and the review withdrawn → start very gently, knee-supported if needed → persistent pain to a physiotherapist (chunk 3). The weekly plan repeats "starting very gently".
- **Youth strength training** → only if properly planned and supervised → qualified coaching before lifting at all.
- **Checking** → find out your game; age doesn't settle it → WNIHL don't check at all → "if your league says non-checking, that wins" before the women's-hockey limits → where banned, a penalty; reckless, violent or injuring, plan on being thrown out (chunk 5).
- **Return to play** → a medical decision, staged → why → a qualified professional manages, children more carefully → no schedules → come off, tell someone, don't drive, see a doctor → British floors with "a minimum, not a date" (chunk 6).

### 7. Departures from the source and the template

**From the source**
- "Weakness, numbness or tingling in an arm or a leg" is unscoped. That is stricter (§4.2).
- "Somebody phones for help first" and "reassure, keep warm, keep still" come from BC, the additional source.
- "Keep your head off the glass" is from brief item 5; the source says head up and chin off in every case.
- "Keep it up even going slowly" is the brief's wording of the source's walking-speed finding.
- "Plan on being thrown out" covers violent and injuring checks. It is stricter than Hockey Canada's discretionary rung for a violent check.
- "If your league says non-checking, that wins" before the women's-hockey limits is new framing. It carries brief item 13's gate, from the remake's own line, and the source's WNIHL instruction.
- The UK guidance's "first 24 hours" is voiced. The remake did not have it.
- Parents are removed (owner rule).
- "Coaches call that an odd-man rush" defines a term the source uses without a gloss.
- The three questions are new framing. The "who is trained, who phones" question is the closest to an invention. It rests on "wait for whoever is trained" (:285) and "somebody phones" (BC :37).

**From the template**
- There is no rink "one end at a time". The parts are the spine, the training week, game day, and after the game.
- **No four-beat systems.** This page has no team system with a realistic alternative and an "ask which yours plays". The only coach-dependent item is shift length, and it is in the questions. Where the source gives a "why" it is voiced; where it gives an alternative (the forty-five-minute week, water against a sports drink, intervals against sprints) it is voiced.
- The spine ends at 29%, not 25% (§1).
- There are four questions, three for the coach plus one for Britain, then "one thing to do before your next game" (CRT six on your phone), which was carried over from the remake.
- Nineteen ideas, against the template's eighteen.
- Synthesis cap **8,635**, not 9,320.

### 8. What I found unclear in the source

- **"In Britain" against England Ice Hockey's adoption.** The source scopes the British red-flag list and the return floors to "Britain" (KF :29, :49, KT14, KT18), but the adoption it cites is England Ice Hockey's, "for all its clubs" (:289, :330). It is not clear that a Scottish or Welsh club is under the same guidance. The script follows the source's "If you play in Britain" and names England Ice Hockey as the adopter. A reviewer should decide whether "Britain" overstates the adoption.
- **"Phone for help first" against "an ambulance immediately".** CR says a red flag means an ambulance immediately (:268). BC says call for help first after any possible head or neck injury (:980). The script voices both, in that order. They do not conflict, but CR never says to phone when there is no red flag.
- **CRT6 "three hours" against UK "24 hours".** Both are in the source (:338). The script voices both and does not say which binds a non-British reader.

### 9. Where this script is weakest

- **The spine is at 14.8 minutes (29%).** A listener who stops at ten minutes has the concussion rule and the neck limb, but not the aftercare or the British list.
- **It is 51.1 minutes long.** The owner's "shorter is better" (19 Oct) and "aware, not freaked out" (20 Oct) both push against a long concussion block. It is shorter and calmer than the remake's, but it is still the densest four minutes in the episode.
- **The fifteenth idea is still rules.** It is short, but it is the one place a listener hears age groups and league names.
- The one-line idea summaries ("So that is the first idea …") repeat the idea headings closely. That is pacing, and it is the first cut.
- **Backward and forward pointers were audited by hand for shape.** "Here is an off-ice routine" no longer says "I promised"; "the same shape as before" was rewritten. The remaining signposts are the part openers and "From here on, I will use one short version".
