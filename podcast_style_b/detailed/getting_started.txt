===== SCRIPT START =====

This episode is for anyone starting ice hockey, or coming back to it after a long time away. There is a part on how children start, too. It takes its time. We will build things up one piece at a time, and for each piece I will tell you what to do and also why it makes sense. If you know why, you can still do the right thing when your rink does things a little differently.

Most of starting hockey is not about skill. It is about asking the right person the right question, before you need the answer.

Before any instructions, a few words you will hear all the way through. I will explain them now, so they don't get in the way later.

Body checking. This means using your body, on purpose, to knock an opponent off the puck. Some leagues allow it and some don't.

Body contact. This is the bumping that happens when two players go for the same puck. It is a separate thing from body checking.

The boards. This is the wall around the rink, with the glass on top of it.

The checker. In this episode, that means any player from the other team who comes in on you, whether they are after the puck or just coming at you.

A shift. This is one turn on the ice, from the moment you go on until you come off and a teammate replaces you.

Changing on the fly. Hockey swaps players while the game keeps going. You skate to your bench, and a teammate jumps on in your place.

The crease. This is the painted blue area in front of each net. Goalies treat it as theirs.

Your governing body. This is the national organisation that runs hockey in your country.

That is the vocabulary. Now the start.

I will begin with four big ideas, because they matter most. If you only listen to the first part of this episode, take those four with you. After that we will go through the rest in the order you meet it. Getting in, your kit and your paperwork, hockey in Britain, your first session, and your first few weeks.

First idea. Find out whether your league allows body checking. Do it before your first game, not after your first hit.

Here is why this one comes first. Almost everything else about starting is local, and you can sort it out as you go. This one is not local. Whether your league allows checking is set by your age group, your league and your country. It is written down, in a rulebook or in a competition's own rules, and nobody at the rink can change it.

So ask the right person. Ask your coach, or your league secretary, the person who runs the league's admin. Don't ask the rink desk. The desk sells the ice. It can't answer this question.

Now a different question, and people mix the two up. What does one casual session allow? A drop-in session might call itself non-contact. That is a house rule, a rule the rink sets for that session. The desk can tell you what it means that night. But it tells you nothing about your league.

And body contact and body checking are separate questions too, so ask about both.

This is not only for children. Adult hockey is not all non-contact. Some adult leagues allow checking, in North America and in Britain. Being a beginner does not put you in a non-checking group. So when you ring a league about joining, ask in the same call whether it is a checking league.

For a child, ask before you choose where they play. Whether checking is allowed depends on their age and their division.

Second idea. Before you turn up to any session, ask three things. What gear do I need? What standard is it? And is there contact?

There are three kinds of casual session, and you will hear their names a lot. Stick-and-puck is usually open ice for practising on your own. There are lots of pucks, and no game. Drop-in is usually a pick-up game. You pay at the door, you get a jersey colour, and you play. Shinny is informal pick-up hockey, and its house rules vary enormously.

Notice the word usually. Those names are loose labels, and rinks use them differently. So a session's name tells you almost nothing. The rink's own description of the session is what counts. If it doesn't say, ring and ask.

Here is why each of the three questions matters.

Gear first. Some stick-and-puck sessions need only a helmet and gloves. Most drop-ins need full equipment, and they will turn you away without it.

Then the standard. Rinks often run separate beginner, intermediate and advanced drop-ins. Turning up to the wrong one is the most common beginner mistake. It is also the easiest to avoid, because rinks run separate sessions so you can choose. An advanced drop-in is no place to learn to stop.

Then contact. Turning up to a full-contact session expecting a skills session is how people get hurt.

And while you are asking, ask whether lifting the puck off the ice is allowed. Some sessions ban it. That one is a house rule too.

Third idea. Learn to skate first, or alongside. You don't need to be good.

Here is why skating comes before everything else. Skating is what sorts players into levels. Not shooting, and not how well you read the game. That comes from experience, not from a study. And skating decides how fast you get into real games.

So work hardest on two things. Stopping on both sides, and skating backwards. Until you can do both, you belong in a beginner group, however well you handle a puck.

Now, choosing your level. Start one level below where your pride puts you. That is judgement, not a rule. But there is a real reason behind it. Moving up part way through a season is easy, and people welcome it. Moving down is awkward, and it usually comes after a bad injury or a bad month.

If you put yourself a level too high, you spend the season hanging on, with a real chance of getting hurt. If you put yourself a level too low, you spend it improving. Those two mistakes don't cost the same. That is why you lean low.

Fourth idea. On the ice, two habits carry a new player further than any skill. Short shifts, and eyes up.

Short shifts first. Go hard, and come off well inside a minute. A well-managed shift lasts thirty to forty-five seconds. Your first shifts will run long, because you don't yet know when to leave. That is the biggest reason your first sessions feel so exhausting.

Here is why it matters. Hockey is short bursts of flat-out effort. A tired player is slower and sloppier. And at a drop-in, a long shift isn't saving your energy. Nobody is managing the ice time, so a long shift is you taking someone else's turn.

Now eyes up. You will barely touch the puck. That is true at every level, from beginner to professional. It is not a sign you played badly. Most of a shift happens away from the puck.

So use the time between touches to look around. Where are your teammates? Where is the space? Who is coming? Here is why it pays. A glance while a pass is on its way to you costs nothing. A glance taken after it arrives does cost you.

Two safety points belong here, because they matter most.

The first is the goalie. Never shoot at a goalie who isn't ready. A goalie is ready when they are facing you, set, with their stick down. A goalie who is drinking, fixing a strap or talking to someone is not ready. Shoot at a net, not at a person. Of all the habits at a rink, this is the one that carries real injury risk. It is also the one that makes people angriest.

The second is the boards. Sooner or later you will race someone for a loose puck and end up there.

On the way to that puck, go for the puck, not the player. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call, or your session's. Never hit a player who can't see you coming, and never from behind. If you can see their numbers, pull up.

Then think about how you will meet the wall. Decide on the way in. There is no time to decide when you arrive, but you usually have several strides to choose.

Best of all, go in at an angle rather than straight at the wall. Then a side, an arm or a leg takes the knock, and your head stays clear.

If you can't avoid meeting it square on, turn your skates parallel to the boards, so you are side-on to them. Forearm and hip to the boards. Never the point of your shoulder, and not the whole shoulder if you can help it. Head up. Chin off your chest.

And two things you never do. Never turn your back to the checker. And never duck. Both protect your head and neck. If you turn your back, you can't see the hit coming or brace for it. If you duck, your head meets the boards first. A wall does not need an opponent to hurt you, and that is true even going slowly.

If you are already facing the boards and can't turn, that is when things have gone wrong. Get your stick and gloves up on the glass, spread wide, with your head off it.

This holds whether or not your session allows contact. Unexpected contact happens in non-checking hockey too.

From here on I will use one short version of that, the same words every time, so you can learn it like a line. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the checker, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly.

Let me pull those four ideas together, because they are the core of starting.

One. Before your first game, ask your coach or league secretary whether your league allows body checking. Not the rink desk.

Two. Before any session, ask about gear, the standard and contact.

Three. Learn to skate, and work on stopping both ways and skating backwards. Start a level lower than your pride wants.

Four. Short shifts and eyes up. Never shoot at a goalie who isn't ready. Racing for a loose puck, play the puck, not the player. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call, or your session's. Never hit a player who can't see you coming, and if you can see their numbers, pull up. At the wall, go in at an angle if you can. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the checker, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly.

Everything else in this episode adds detail around those four. So now let's go through it in order, starting with how you get in.

Fifth idea. The way in comes in stages, and they come in order.

The stages are these. Public skating, or a learn-to-skate class, first. Then a one-off taster session. Then a beginners' block of several weeks. Then a team or a league.

Here is why the order helps. Skating comes first, because skating decides how fast you get into real games. And the taster comes before you buy anything, so you have been on the ice before you spend money on kit.

Public skating is the cheapest ice you will ever buy. Going twice a week for a month, before you spend anything on gear, is a good plan. And when you go, bend your knees. Almost every adult skating fault is a straight-legs fault. The NHL's learn-to-play programme for children strongly encourages a learn-to-skate class before signing up for hockey. That advice works just as well for adults.

Sixth idea. What an adult can sign up for. There are four kinds of thing.

First, an adult learn-to-play programme, sometimes called hockey one-oh-one. It is a fixed block of sessions that mixes coaching with a supervised game.

Here is why it is the best way in for an adult beginner. Everyone else there is new too. One example ends every session with a guided game of half an hour.

Blocks vary a lot in length and price, even inside one league. They change every season, too. So don't budget from a price you find online. Ask the club for this season's figure.

Second, stick-and-puck, drop-in or shinny. There is no coach, and no registration beyond a waiver, a form you sign before you skate. You pay at the door. So before you go, ask about the gear, the standard and contact. These sessions are worth finding. Drop-in and stick-and-puck ice is usually the cheapest, most useful ice you can buy. That matters most once you are on a team that gets one slot a week.

Third, an adult recreational league. That is a season of scheduled games, usually split into divisions by standard. Some leagues take single players and put them on a team. Others only take whole teams. So ask which, before you assume you need a full team of friends. And ask in the same call whether it is a checking league.

Fourth, a club with an adult section. In England, most clubs run beginner sessions for adults and children. Most are happy to arrange a trial session before you commit. So asking to try it is normal, and it costs nothing.

When you are choosing a level for any of these, three things help. Watch a game at the level you are thinking about. Twenty minutes in the stands beats any description. Watch whether players can stop on both sides, take a hard pass, and change on the fly without crashing into each other.

Then ask the league or the club to place you. They do it all the time. They would rather place you right than lose you, and they usually judge better than you do. And ask for a trial or a skills session before you commit. That is standard practice at most clubs, and free at many.

Level names won't help you much. Local adult leagues use letters, numbers or words, like A to D, or division one to six, or beginner up to elite. They mean different things in different places. So ask what each level means at that league.

And think about time. For an adult, a recreational season is usually one game a week, plus any extra ice you buy.

Now a word on playing goal. If you are thinking about it, say so early and loudly. Goalies are scarce at every level of amateur hockey. So a goalie place is often free or cheap, though not always. Ask rather than assume.

Clubs often own goal equipment and lend it out. Borrow the pads, the blocker, the catching glove and the chest protector. But buy your own mask. A goalie mask counts as a helmet, and a shared one is where borrowing stops. You can't see a crushed liner or a hairline crack, and you can't know what a shared mask has been hit by.

Seventh idea. How children start. The stages are the same, and there are named programmes for each one.

For a first taste, USA Hockey runs Try Hockey For Free. It is a one-off session for ages four to nine, at hundreds of rinks. You don't need a membership. In Canada, Hockey Canada's Esso Fun Days give girls and women of all ages a first taste of hockey. There are no fees, and no obligation to join a league.

For skating, the rink usually runs a learn-to-skate class. In the United States there is Learn to Skate USA, and in Canada there is CanSkate. A learn-to-skate class isn't hockey, and a child doesn't need to finish it first. But here is why it helps. A child who can stand up, glide and stop on their own gets far more out of a first hockey session.

Then a learn-to-play block. In the United States that is Learn to Play, run by the NHL and its players' association. It is for first-time players aged four to nine. It runs about four to eight weeks, one lesson a week, with kit provided. Registration is online only, and popular sites have waiting lists, so look early.

In Canada it is The First Shift, a six-week programme for ages six to ten, with kit included. Find it through Hockey Canada's own website. Don't type an address you remember, because the obvious address has led to the wrong place.

After that comes a team, with a local association. Children are grouped by age, and the cut-off is set by the national body each year. So check this year's age chart, not what happened to a brother or sister two seasons ago.

Within an age group there are usually levels. In Canada, house league is the recreational level. Players almost always get equal ice time, whatever their skill, and the time is a few hours a week. Rep is the top team, picked by tryout, and it takes about twice the time. In the United States the level names are set locally, so one word can mean different things two states apart. Ask your association what its levels mean.

The youngest age groups play on part of the rink, across it or on half of it, rather than the whole sheet. The small surface is the point, not a compromise. Both the American and the Canadian bodies do this on purpose.

And expect far more practices than games. In USA Hockey's guidance, practices far outnumber games at every age. So a programme there selling you mostly games isn't following its own governing body's advice. Elsewhere, ask your association what it recommends.

If a child is starting in England, a few things are worth knowing before you ring a club. Junior hockey is mixed, boys and girls together. The youngest group is under tens. It is the one group you can join at any point in the season.

Every junior game needs a face cage, worn tight against the chin. Every club with players under eighteen must have a named safeguarding lead. That is the person responsible for keeping children safe. Ask who yours is. A club that can't answer has a problem. And junior games are filmed, so don't be surprised by a camera.

Eighth idea. Kit. Borrow before you buy, with one exception. The helmet.

Don't kit yourself out completely before your first session. People do it all the time. Go to a public skate, then a taster, then buy. Ask the club first. Many lend or hire gear, or run swaps of used gear.

Now the exception. Never use a second-hand helmet, whether it was bought, borrowed or handed down from a brother or sister. A goalie mask counts as a helmet too.

Here is why. A helmet can be damaged inside where you can't see it. You can't see a crushed liner or a hairline crack in the shell. And you can't know what it has been hit by. So borrow everything else, and buy the helmet new. The same goes for a mouthguard.

That includes the loaner kit at a taster session. A loaner helmet has been on other heads, with nobody keeping a record of what it stopped. The advice against a borrowed helmet has no exception for a taster. Try Hockey For Free says most sites have loaner equipment, but you are welcome to bring your own. So take your own helmet if you have one. If you don't, ask the host site before the session. And make a helmet the first thing you buy.

Learn to Play kit is different. A child keeps it at the end, so it is issued to one child rather than shared round. But nothing we found from the programme says the kit is new, and for a helmet that matters. So ask whether the helmet is new before the first session. Learn to Play also recommends buying a mouthguard, a neck guard and an athletic supporter yourself.

What you need to be allowed on the ice depends on the session, the league and the governing body. So check your own league's list before you buy. Don't rely on a shop assistant's summary of which safety standard applies.

A typical full list looks like this. A helmet, a mouthguard and a neck guard. Shoulder pads, elbow pads and gloves. Groin protection, hockey pants and shin pads. Socks, skates, a stick, a practice jersey, and a bag to carry it all.

Get two things fitted in person. Skates and helmet. Everything else can be bought online, used or borrowed.

Here is why skates matter so much. Skate fit is checked with your foot in the boot. Does your heel lift? Do your toes just brush the end? No size chart can tell you that. A boot that is too big holds your skating back for as long as you own it. You will think you have weak ankles, when what you have is loose boots.

And when you get home, take your gear out of the bag, every time, so it can dry.

Let me pull the way in together. Go in by stages. Public skate, taster, beginners' block, then a team. For an adult beginner, a learn-to-play block is the best first step, because everyone there is new. If you want to play goal, say so, and borrow everything except the mask. Borrow kit before you buy it, but never a second-hand helmet, including at a taster. And get skates and helmet fitted in person.

Ninth idea. Register before you play, and check the league is sanctioned before you pay.

Here is how hockey is run, because it tells you who to ask. Your governing body is the national level. It sets the playing rules and the insurance. It also runs safeguarding, the rules that protect children and others from harm. And it sets two things beginners expect to be local. Your age group, and the protective gear you must wear.

Everything else comes from much nearer. What a season costs, which night you skate, what your level is called. Your club, your rink or your local association sets those. They change every season. The governing body's website can tell you what registering is. It can't tell you which night you skate. So the answer is usually to ring your club or rink and ask.

Registering means taking out a membership with your governing body. You almost always do it through your club, not on your own. And it is not just paperwork. It is your insurance, it is the safety rules that protect children, and it is your right to play.

Sanctioned means the governing body recognises the league. Here is why that matters, and there are two reasons. In an unsanctioned league you may not be insured. And playing in one can cost you the hockey you already play.

In Canada, for example, Hockey Canada says it cannot insure players in unsanctioned leagues. A child put in a non-member league loses all their Hockey Canada membership privileges for the rest of the season. And a player in unsanctioned hockey after the thirtieth of September can't play sanctioned hockey for the rest of that season.

So before you join a midweek league somebody set up at your rink, ask one question. Is it sanctioned?

A registration year doesn't run from the day you pay. USA Hockey's runs from the first of September to the end of August. So joining in June may buy you three months, not twelve. Wherever you are, ask your club which year you are paying for, before you pay.

And youth registration happens months before the season, so ask your association when it opens. In Canada it mostly runs from spring through August, for a season starting in September. So deciding in October is late there. Ring anyway, but expect to be squeezed in.

Now Britain, where the governing body has just changed. On the thirtieth of June twenty twenty-six, England Ice Hockey, Scottish Ice Hockey and Ice Hockey UK merged. Ice Hockey UK is now the single governing body for England, Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland.

The old English and Scottish memberships ended that day and are being reissued. Insurance arrangements carried on unchanged. So don't assume a registration process you read about last season still applies. Ask your club which body you are registering with, which membership year, and what your insurance covers.

For England, the clearest guide is still England Ice Hockey's last published rules, written before the merger. So treat what comes next as the shape of things, and check it with your club.

England's adult game has a recreational section, with its own registration and its own competition rules. It is not a loose word for casual hockey. An adult beginner in England almost certainly comes in through it. Above it sit the senior national league and, at the top, the professional Elite League. You will start in the recreational or junior sections.

There are different kinds of registration, too. One is a short trial registration, for training sessions with a club. Another covers a learn-to-play scheme run by a club or a rink. The fact that a trial registration exists tells you that asking to try is normal.

You are insured only when the game or tournament is sanctioned. So an unsanctioned game is an uninsured one. And if you play in a competition that isn't recognised, you face a suspension from all recognised hockey, at home and abroad, and severe financial penalties. That falls on you, not just the club.

Register before the day of the game, not on it. If you play unregistered, your team is punished for fielding you.

There are no part-season discounts. And the registration window normally opens around the start of July and closes at the end of January, except for under tens. So February is still fine for a taster. It is too late to register for that season.

Two different registration years are in circulation in England. One runs from August to July, and the recreational section has been described as running from October to September. So ask your club which year your registration runs on before you pay. It changes what you get for your money.

In England, if you are hurt, an insurance claim for your injury needs a doctor's certificate, evidence and witnesses. So get seen, and get it written down on the night, not a week later.

As for money, costs are set locally, so ask your own association for its number. Ask what the fee includes, and what it doesn't. Ice time, officials, jerseys, tournaments, travel. Expect extras too, like team clothing. And help with costs exists, and not enough people use it. Ask. Nobody will offer.

Let me pull registration together. Ask your club or rink the local questions, because they set the costs and the nights. Register through your club before you play. Check the league is sanctioned before you join, because an unsanctioned game may leave you uninsured. In Britain, ask which body you are registering with. And ask which registration year you are paying for.

Tenth idea. If you play in Britain, your rules are not the NHL's.

The NHL is the North American professional league. Most hockey writing in English assumes its rules. But every level of hockey in Britain plays the international rulebook, the one written by the IIHF, the world governing body. Britain adds some changes of its own on top.

So when coaching material tells you what is allowed, check it before you trust it. And for how your own competition runs, things like team sizes and suspensions, ask your club for a copy of its competition rules.

The biggest difference on day one is the neck guard. Its proper name is a neck laceration protector, and it guards your neck against cuts. In Britain, wear one every time you go on the ice, training included.

Here is why this one catches people out. If you learned from North American material, you have probably picked up that adults don't need one. In Britain they do. It is required in every game, at every level, in all four home nations, adults included.

And don't count on a warning. An official who finds you without one penalises you on the spot, with an immediate ten-minute penalty, and a second one puts you out of the game. Only the professional Elite League warns first.

Face protection is next. Put a junior in a cage for every game. A cage meets every British junior rule, so it is the simple choice.

In women's hockey in Britain, a player counts as an adult from their fourteenth birthday. But they still can't wear a half visor, the kind that covers only the top of the face, until the start of the season after their eighteenth birthday. Whether a half visor is allowed even after that is less settled than it looks. Full face protection meets every rule, so it is the safe choice then too.

In university hockey in England and Scotland, and in Scottish recreational hockey, you need at least a half visor. And in England, wear your full kit, helmet included, in the warm-up before a game.

One rule worth knowing early, because North American material may tell you otherwise. Never hit anyone from behind. In the rulebook British hockey plays, there is no two-minute penalty for it at all. If the referee judges it reckless, it is a major penalty, and you are thrown out of the game. A check on a player without the puck who can't see it coming counts as reckless. So if you hit someone from behind in Britain, plan on being thrown out.

North American coaching material may mention two minutes for it. That is never the whole penalty. Under Hockey Canada rules, a penalty for hitting from behind always ends your game. And if you drive someone from behind into the boards so they can't protect themselves, that is a match penalty there, the most serious penalty there is. Under USA Hockey rules, even the lightest one adds at least a misconduct, a ten-minute penalty, on top. And a forceful hit on a player facing the boards ends your game there too.

And look at the rink you are actually standing on. Nearly every diagram and distance in hockey coaching comes from the NHL's rink, two hundred feet by eighty-five. The international rulebook allows rinks of different widths, and some British rinks are smaller still.

Here is why that matters. On a wide rink the corners are further from the net. So a distance you learned from a diagram may not be yours. Learn the names of the areas from coaching material, but not the distances.

Let me pull Britain together. Your rules are the international ones, with British changes, not the NHL's. Wear your neck guard every time you go on the ice, adults included, and expect no warning. Put a junior in a cage. Never hit anyone from behind. And learn the names of the areas, not the distances.

Eleventh idea. Your first session, before you get there.

Before you leave the house, confirm the session, the time and the gear. Do it by phone or from the rink's own page, not from a friend's memory.

Pack the night before. The two things new players forget are a mouthguard and tape. Bring water and a towel. Bring something to wear on the walk back to the car, because you will be soaked. And bring your paperwork if you haven't registered online.

Now the time. On time in hockey means dressed and ready before the stated time. That means arriving forty-five to sixty minutes before a game, and twenty to thirty before a practice.

For a genuine first session, arrive a full hour early. Here is why. Dressing takes twice as long the first three times as it will afterwards. Learn to Play asks children to arrive at least an hour before their first session, to check in and get dressed.

Twelfth idea. The dressing room, and getting on the ice.

In the dressing room, take a free spot near the door and ask if it's somebody's. Keep your bag under the bench, not spread across your neighbour. Say hello. Listen more than you talk. And leave the goalie alone in the last few minutes before you go on.

Here is the thing new players worry about. Nobody will comment on your gear or your skating. What people do notice is someone who takes up three spaces, or who is still lacing skates when the ice is ready.

When you get on the ice, step on and skate. A couple of easy laps, the same way as everyone else. Don't stand at the gate.

And don't skate through the crease. Goalies clear it of snow, and they treat it as theirs. Skate round it.

Thirteenth idea. How a drill line works.

If you have never been to a coached practice, the drill line is what confuses people most. This is the usual way. But it varies by coach and by rink, so watch what others do.

The coach explains the drill, and usually shows it once. Get close enough to hear, or you will go the wrong way.

Players form lines, usually in the corners. Join the shortest line, not the one with the good players.

You go on the whistle, or when the player in front is about halfway through. Watch the first two groups go before your turn. That is what the first two groups are for.

Go at full speed, and get out of the way at the end. The most common beginner mistake is finishing a drill and then standing exactly where the next player needs to be.

If you get it wrong, keep skating and rejoin the line. Nobody cares. Here is why that matters. Stopping in the middle of the ice to apologise is what causes the collision.

And when the whistle goes, stop what you are doing and look at the coach. Shooting pucks while someone is talking gets you noticed for the wrong reason.

Fourteenth idea. Drop-in habits.

No governing body runs your local drop-in. So these are habits, not rules, and they vary by rink. But they are close to universal.

Change quickly. At a drop-in, nobody manages the ice time. So head to the bench when the puck is going the other way, and call for your replacement. The player coming on waits until you are within a stick's length of the boards.

Let faster groups play through. Look before you cross the ice, and don't skate through someone's drill.

Collect your own pucks. Don't shoot into a group. And never shoot at the far net if someone is near it collecting pucks.

Match how hard you play to the session, and to the least willing player in the battle. Adult drop-in is usually non-contact. But that is a house rule, so check at the desk. Even where contact is allowed, nobody at a Tuesday drop-in is being scouted. The skills that matter there need no hit at all, and they annoy nobody.

Pass to the new player. The person who does that on your first night is the reason you come back. Next year, you can do the same for someone else.

Get off promptly at the buzzer. Ice time is rented by the minute, and the next group is waiting at the gate.

And thank the goalie, with two taps on the pads at the end. They paid to be there too, and they spent the session stopping your shots.

Let me pull your first session together. Confirm it, pack the night before, and arrive a full hour early. In the dressing room, take a spot near the door and listen more than you talk. Step on and skate, and stay out of the crease. In a drill, go full speed and get out of the way at the end. When the whistle goes, look at the coach. At a drop-in, change quickly, collect your own pucks, and play at the level of the least willing player in the battle.

Fifteenth idea. Your first few weeks, and what your body will tell you.

You will be more tired than you expect. Hockey is short bursts of flat-out effort, not steady exercise. Your legs will go before your lungs. The hockey stance, knees bent and low, works the front of your thighs and your inner thighs like nothing else. Groin and inner-thigh strains are the classic hockey overuse injury.

Expect cold hands and feet for the first ten minutes, then heat. Expect sore feet in new skates. Expect bruises you don't remember getting, even in non-contact hockey.

A day-two ache in your hip flexors and lower back is usually the stance, not an injury. But pain that is sharp, on one side, or still there after a few days is different. That is a question for a qualified medical professional.

Build up gradually. Coaches say the classic way amateurs get hurt is a sudden jump in how much they do. Nothing for four months, then five sessions in the first week. That is a coaching observation, not a measured ranking of injuries. So don't take it to mean collisions are the smaller risk.

Sixteenth idea. Knocks to the head. This matters in your first weeks, and in every week after.

A concussion is an injury to the brain. It can come from a knock to the head, from a hit to the body that snaps the head, or from a fall. You don't have to be knocked out to have one. Most concussions don't involve being knocked out at all.

If a concussion is suspected, yours or a teammate's, the player comes off the ice straight away. They don't go back on that day. Not that shift, not that period, not that game. And they don't play again until a medical professional says they can.

Suspected is enough. Here is why. Nobody at a recreational rink can rule a concussion out. And a concussed player will often tell you they are fine. So it is never their call.

After any knock to the head, treat it as a possible neck injury too. If a player is still down, phone for help first. Don't help them up and don't move them, unless you need to keep their airway clear so they can breathe. Leave their helmet on.

Phone for an ambulance straight away if anything looks serious. That includes being knocked out, a fit, neck pain, double or lost vision, weakness, numbness or tingling, or being sick more than once. It includes a severe headache, growing confusion, getting drowsy or less responsive, getting more and more agitated, or a skull that looks out of shape. Any one of those is enough. And any sign that is getting worse is a reason to call too.

If you play in Britain, the UK guidance adds more reasons to call. Things like memory loss, a change in behaviour, one weak arm or leg, twitching or going rigid, or trouble speaking, understanding or balancing. Any sign of a broken skull, even just a cut, a bruise, swelling or bad pain where they were hit. A player who has been drinking or taking drugs. And a player who has had brain surgery, has a bleeding disorder, or takes blood-thinning medicine. Any of these is a reason to call, even for a player who gets up and says they are fine.

Even without any of those signs, someone stays with them, for at least the first three hours. The UK guidance says the first twenty-four hours. They don't drive, and they don't go home on their own. No alcohol, and no drugs that weren't prescribed for them. They don't do anything with a risk of a knock to the head until a medical professional clears them. Symptoms clearing up is not the all-clear.

In British grassroots hockey there is also a minimum wait, on top of the clearance. No return to competition before day twenty-one, counting the day of the injury as day zero. And no training with a risk of a knock to the head until fourteen days free of symptoms, at rest.

Seventeenth idea. How long until you are really playing.

The timeframes you will see come from coaching businesses and shops, not from research. There is no measured evidence behind them, so treat them as experienced guesses. One of them says six to ten hours of ice to get from clinging to the boards to skating forwards with confidence. Then three to six months to playing in a beginner league. Then one to two seasons to feeling comfortable. People vary hugely.

The useful way to think about it is this. Months for the basics, years for the rest. And progress comes in steps, not a smooth line. You will have flat spells where you feel stuck, then a sudden jump, more than once. That too is what coaches see, not a measurement.

Here is why it helps to know that. A flat spell does not mean you have stopped improving.

Let me pull it all together.

Before your first game, ask your coach or league secretary whether your league allows body checking. Not the rink desk. Before any session, ask what gear you need, what the standard is, and whether there is contact. Learn to skate, and work on stopping both ways and skating backwards, because skating sorts you into a level. Start a level lower than your pride wants.

On the ice, short shifts and eyes up. Never shoot at a goalie who isn't ready. Racing for a loose puck, play the puck, not the player. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call, or your session's. Never hit a player who can't see you coming, and if you can see their numbers, pull up. At the boards, go in at an angle if you can. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the checker, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly. And never hit anyone from behind.

If a concussion is suspected, the player comes off and does not come back that day. They don't play again until a medical professional clears them. Someone stays with them, and they don't drive or go home alone. If a player is still down after a knock to the head, phone for help, don't move them, and leave the helmet on. If anything looks serious, phone for an ambulance.

Borrow kit, but never a second-hand helmet. Register through your club, and check the league is sanctioned. And in Britain, your rules are the international ones, not the NHL's, and you wear your neck guard every time you go on the ice.

You can ask almost everything else in one phone call to the club, the association or the rink. What do you run for someone at my level, and when does the next one start? What does it cost, and what isn't included? What does registration involve, and what dates does the year run? What equipment do I need on day one, and do you lend any? What level is this, and is there a trial? How much time is it a week, with travel? Is there contact, and what are the house rules? In Britain, add two more.

Is this sanctioned, and am I insured in it? And which body am I registering with?

Then three questions to ask your coach. Is our league a checking league? Which level would you put me in? And who should I talk to, now that I'm completely new?

Amateur hockey runs on volunteers. At most clubs, somebody has the job of answering exactly that last question. And any teammate could explain most things in thirty seconds. They want to hear from you.

### 8. What I found unclear in the source

- GS :575 says "Do not move them, other than to keep their airway clear, unless you are trained to", while KT14 :719 says "move the player only to keep their airway clear", without the trained exception. The two layers disagree in the permissive direction for a trained responder only; the script follows the stricter.
- GS :28 says the neck guard is penalised on the spot "in IHUK, England, Scottish and university competitions", and :538 says the In-House Rules cover "all IIHF categories". The script says "everywhere except the professional Elite League", which is how KF :28 phrases it.
- GS :18 tells a reader to ask the coach or league secretary, and CM :660 says the same, but the source never defines "league secretary". The script glosses it as "the person who runs the league's admin", which is the writer's gloss.
- The source's boards posture (:490) has no slow-speed limb and no "spread wide, head off the glass"; both had to come from BC. The source could carry them in its own words (a plan row, not a script fix).
