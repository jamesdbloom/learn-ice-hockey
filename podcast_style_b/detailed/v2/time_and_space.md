It's stick on the puck, not a hit, and whether you can hit at all is your league's call. No shove, no lean, and never your stick across anyone. Never lift anyone off their feet. Never hit a player who can't see you coming, and never from behind. If you can see their numbers, pull up and play the puck. Building up speed into contact, even with two hard strides, can be charging. And the boards are where the angle ends, not something to use. Never use them to push or pin the player. Even going slowly, a crash into the boards can hurt.

PLAYER: What about leagues with no hitting?

COACH: Body checking is barred or limited in younger age groups, in women's hockey, and in non-contact adult leagues. There, your route is part of the test. Veering off your line to make contact, stepping or gliding into them, or adding a hip, shoulder or arm when you arrive, is a penalty. So is pinning them. Contact there can be legal only if you held your line, were playing the puck, and put nothing into it.

COACH: And in a CARHA league, don't touch them at all. Not avoiding contact is itself a penalty there, and a double penalty if they go into the boards. That double penalty counts as two of the three penalties that put you out of a game. Contact you meant can get you thrown out even if nobody's hurt. If contact you meant, or contact into the boards, hurts them, expect to be thrown out, and to miss at least the next game too.

COACH: Fourteenth idea. Your stick in the lane. Putting your stick blade in a passing lane costs nothing, and it takes an option away. Point your blade at a lane or a threat. Don't rest it on the ice between your skates. And keep it flat on the ice. A blade flat on the ice blocks the pass. A blade waving at hip height doesn't.

PLAYER: But if they never try the pass, did it do anything?

COACH: That's the whole point. A blade already in the lane stops passes that never even get tried. The carrier looks, sees your blade, and looks somewhere else. That costs them time. Compare that with a stick jabbed at the puck after the pass. That's a gamble. If it misses, you've given the carrier an easy way to the net.

COACH: Coaching material calls stick position arguably the defensive habit that makes the biggest difference. It says arguably, and nobody has measured it. But it's also the first thing tired players stop doing. So when you feel tired, check your stick.

COACH: Fifteenth idea. Deny the catch, not the pass. You'll spend a lot of time trying to cut off passes, and mostly failing. Passes are fast, and you're guessing. What would you go after instead?

PLAYER: The player who's getting it?

COACH: Close. Don't lunge at the lane. Arrive as the puck does. Skate a route that puts you on the receiver just as the puck reaches them. Your stick goes in their receiving lane, or on their blade as the puck lands. Arriving on them is stick on the puck, not a hit, and whether you can hit at all is your league's call. But never hit a player who can't see you coming, and a receiver looking down at the puck can't. If you can see their numbers, pull up and play the puck. With your stick on their blade, it's a press, not a chop. Press the blade briefly, then let go. Don't pull it. Never at the hands. Never a chop at their stick, because a chop can be called a slash. And never a two-handed shove. That's a cross-check, even with your blade on the ice. The timing is what keeps it legal. If you pin the stick of a player still waiting for the pass, you're interfering with someone who doesn't have the puck. Knocking the stick out of their hands is interference too.

PLAYER: Why does arriving with the puck beat stopping the pass?

COACH: Two things. First, you're defending what the receiver does next, not the pass. Take away their forward option as the puck lands, and the pass got them nothing. They have to turn back, with you on them. Second, a receiver being closed on has to look down at the puck. That's a second of blindness you created without touching anybody. That's what taking away time really means. You're not stopping the pass. You're making sure the receiver has no room when it arrives.

COACH: Sixteenth idea. Take the middle away first. The middle of the ice is worth more than the outside, in every zone, because more options spread out from the middle.

PLAYER: Even in their zone?

COACH: Everywhere. In your own zone, the middle is the slot, and shots from the slot go in far more often than shots from the boards. In the neutral zone, the middle of the ice between the blue lines, a carrier in the middle can attack either side. A carrier on the boards can only attack one. Even in their zone, taking away the middle forces play to the outside. There, a shot has to beat a screen, a stick, and a goalie who can see it.

PLAYER: And when I'm beaten?

COACH: Give up the outside. Steer them to the wall and defend their inside shoulder. You're trading a wide shot for a dead pass into the slot. Steer with your route and your stick. It's stick on the puck, not a hit, and whether you can hit at all is your league's call. No shove, no lean, and never your stick across anyone. Never lift anyone off their feet, and never go into a player's back. If you can see their numbers, pull up and play the puck. Even going slowly, a crash into the boards can hurt.

COACH: For a defenceman at their blue line, it's the same choice. An attacker going round you on the outside is a mistake you can fix. One going through you on the inside is a scoring chance.

COACH: This is a strong general principle, not an absolute. Some coaches want the wall taken away in certain spots. Some penalty kills, when your team is a player short, deliberately load up one side. Ask what yours wants.

COACH: Seventeenth idea. Never pressure alone. Picture this. The puck's in their corner, you're first there, and your teammates are still a long way back. Do you go?

PLAYER: I'd go. Get on them before they can look up.

COACH: On your own, that doesn't take space away. It moves it. You leave the ice you came from. If you're beaten, that ice is now an open lane, and your team is a player short behind the puck. Pressure only works with a second layer. That's a teammate placed to take the puck if the first pressure forces a rushed play, and to cover the ice the first player left.

PLAYER: So who's the second layer?

COACH: That depends on your forecheck. The forecheck is how your forwards pressure the other team in its own end, to win the puck back. It's a coaching choice. This episode assumes one called the two-one-two. The first forward in pressures the puck. The second takes the carrier's first way out. The third holds the high middle, as the safety. Those roles go by order of arrival, not position. Whoever gets there first is the first forechecker, whatever their position. Whoever goes first, the pressure is stick on the puck, not a hit. Whether you can hit at all is your league's call. If you can see their numbers, pull up and play the puck.

PLAYER: Does every team play it that way?

COACH: No. In a one-two-two, one forward goes in to steer the carrier rather than hunt the puck, and two hold back. That gives up the puck behind the other team's net, but it shuts down the neutral zone. A one-three-one trap gives up even more, and denies harder still. So ask which forecheck yours plays.

COACH: But the point isn't which one is right. If you go and your teammate doesn't, you've made a hole rather than closed one. So find out what your team plays before your first shift, and go on the same trigger as everyone else.

COACH: Let me pull the defending together. Act before the puck arrives. Close your gap early, but know what you're leaving behind you. Angle, don't chase, and take the puck, not the player. Keep your blade in the lane. Arrive with the puck, not after it, with a press, not a chop. Give up the outside and keep the middle. And never pressure alone. Every time you close on someone, it's stick on the puck, not a hit, and if you can see their numbers, pull up.

COACH: Eighteenth idea. Learn where the space is, like a map. It tells you where to go with the puck, and where to go without it. Where do you think the space usually is?

PLAYER: Wherever the puck isn't?

COACH: That's a fair start. Here are the places. Behind their net. No shot is possible from there, so it's under-defended, and a defender who commits to one side gives you the other.

COACH: The weak side. The strong side is the side of the ice the puck is on, and the weak side is the other side, away from the puck. Defences slide toward the puck, so the far side is covered last. That's why a pass across the ice so often beats a pass straight up it. You get the puck there before the defence can slide.

COACH: The point. That's up by their blue line, the spot in the zone furthest from the net, and it's defended last for that reason. The space there is real, but it's the least dangerous on the ice. So use the time it gives you. Don't just stand there with it.

COACH: The half-wall. There's a wall on one side, you can see the whole zone, and a defender has to come out to get you. And the seams, between two defenders, anywhere on the ice. That space is there because neither defender is sure it's theirs.

PLAYER: And where is there no space?

COACH: The slot. Every defensive system is built to fill it. That's why it's so valuable, and also why you should plan to arrive there with no time. One touch, a tip or a rebound. Never plan to stickhandle there.

COACH: The strong-side corner, the corner where the puck is. Two, three, sometimes four players end up there. It's a place to battle, not a place to make plays. Win it, then move the puck out. In that battle, it's stick on the puck, not a hit, and whether you can hit at all is your league's call. If you can see their numbers, pull up. And on the boards there, the boards position. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to anyone coming at you, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly.

COACH: And the middle of the neutral zone. Both teams pass through it, and every trap is built to fill it. A trap is a defence that sits back and clogs the middle. A carrier who slows down there gets swarmed. Cross it with speed. If you can't, don't carry the puck through it.

COACH: So, with the puck, move it toward space. Without the puck, move yourself to where the space will be. That's usually the weak side, because that's where the puck will need you next.

COACH: Nineteenth idea. Three moments where reading the space decides the play. First moment. A breakout is how your team gets the puck out of its own end. A breakout pass is coming to you on your own half-wall, and a forechecker is arriving. What would you do?

PLAYER: Look before it lands.

COACH: Good. Then read them. If they're closing hard, chip it past them up the boards, or bump it, a short pass, to a teammate swinging under you. If they've skated past you, the space is back where they came from, so turn into it. If they get to you anyway, the boards position. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to anyone coming at you, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly. Which outlet your team wants first is a breakout choice, so ask.

COACH: Second moment. You're carrying the puck through the neutral zone, and their defence is backing off. Don't slow down in the middle. Attack a seam, or go wide with speed. If nothing's there and support is coming, delay at their blue line. If support isn't coming, curl back and regroup, or chip it past them and chase it.

PLAYER: Chase it hard?

COACH: Chase for the puck, not a hit. Whether you can hit at all is your league's call, and if you can see their numbers, pull up. Some teams want the puck in deep every time, and others want to carry it in. Find out which yours wants.

COACH: Third moment. You have the puck on their half-wall, with time. Where do you look first?

PLAYER: Down the wall, toward the net?

COACH: Look across before you look along. Check the weak-side seam, the back door, which is the far side of the net, and the far faceoff dot first, because that's where the space is. Only then look up the wall, because the near slot will be full. And the moment a defender commits to you, pass to the ice they just left.

COACH: Twentieth idea. Use the rules as a map of where space is allowed. The rulebook isn't just a list of things you can't do. It's a set of lines that make space, and stop teams from killing it. Read it that way, and it's easier to remember.

COACH: Offside first. Offside stops cherry-picking, which means an attacker waiting behind the defence for a long pass. You're offside if both your skates are fully over their blue line before the puck is. One skate on the line, or behind it, keeps you onside.

PLAYER: So how do I use that?

COACH: At their blue line, don't brake early. Braking early gives away free attacking space. Drag your trailing skate instead, keeping it on or behind the line, with its blade on the ice. Your body and stick can be ahead, at speed, and you're still onside. Keep the blade down, because in some leagues a skate held up in the air doesn't count. A blade on the ice is onside everywhere.

COACH: Sometimes the linesperson raises an arm instead of blowing the whistle. That's a delayed offside. To fix it, every attacker gets out of the zone together, each with a skate touching the blue line. That's called tagging up. Then you can all come back in. Keeping the puck is worth the few seconds.

PLAYER: Does every league do that?

COACH: Find out first. Some leagues don't give younger age groups a delayed offside, and the whistle goes straight away. A player taught to tag up there can come back in after play has stopped, into a defenceman who heard the whistle and isn't braced. And when the arm is up, get out and tag up, and leave their carrier alone. In some leagues nothing stops play just because you're about to hit them, and they're not braced for you.

COACH: Next, icing. Icing means sending the puck down the ice past their goal line from too far back, and play is stopped for it. Ice the puck only when you have to. After your own icing, the faceoff comes back to your end, and in some games your tired players can't change. Know which kind of icing your league plays.

COACH: Then the trapezoid. Some rinks have a shape painted behind each goal. Where the rule is enforced, a goalie can't play the puck behind the goal line outside that shape. What would you do with that?

PLAYER: Put the puck where the goalie can't get it?

COACH: Exactly. Dump the puck in wide of it. Then their defenceman, not their goalie, has to go and get it, with your forechecker arriving. That forechecker arrives stick on the puck, not a hit. Whether you can hit at all is your league's call. If you can see their numbers, pull up and play the puck. And if their goalie comes out for it anyway, stay off the goalie. Any contact with a goalie can be a penalty, and it can get you thrown out of the game. A goalie is watching the puck, not you, so contact can hurt them.

COACH: Some leagues' rules have no trapezoid. Where there isn't one, expect their goalie to come out and play the puck, and wide dumps are worth much less. A goalie who comes out can play the puck, but in many leagues can't cover it out there. In Britain the published rules don't settle whether it's enforced. So plan as though their goalie may come out, keep your own goalie inside it, and ask your league.

COACH: Twenty-first idea. Space changes with the score, the clock and the rink. The score first. A team protecting a lead, especially late, gives up space it doesn't need and takes away space that matters. It keeps fewer players below the puck, and funnels everything outside. So if you're the team behind, you'll find room at the point and on the wall, and almost none in the middle.

PLAYER: Why not just force the middle anyway?

COACH: That's how you give them a counter-attack. Take what they offer instead. Walk the line, skating sideways along their blue line with the puck. Shoot for tips. Work below the goal line, and on the boards down there, the boards position. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to anyone coming at you, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly. Pull them out of shape, rather than trying to go through them.

COACH: The clock changes it too. When a team pulls its goalie late in a game, six attackers make a spare player and leave a lot of room at the blue line. And for the defending team, every clear becomes worth a lot, because the net at the far end is empty.

COACH: Your legs change it as well. At the end of a long shift, your legs are gone, so the gap you can really close is bigger than it looks. That's the real reason for short shifts.

PLAYER: So being tired is a space thing too.

COACH: Tiredness and the rink both change the same thing, which is closing speed. Anything that slows how fast defenders arrive gives everyone more time. Take the size of the rink. The international sheet is about the same length as the North American one, but it can be wider. All the change is sideways. On wide ice, a forechecker who commits to the boards takes longer to recover, and moving the puck across the ice gains more. On narrow ice, space shrinks and the forecheck arrives sooner.

PLAYER: So what do I change when I move between rinks?

COACH: On wide ice, delay more, move the puck across more, and be more patient before entries. On narrow ice, decide earlier, use the walls, and value the puck behind the net over the puck in the corner. On those walls, the boards position. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to anyone coming at you, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly.

COACH: In Britain, games run under the local rules are allowed rinks smaller than the international minimum, both shorter and narrower. Those rules say nothing about Elite League rinks. So in those games, expect narrow, short ice until you've seen it. Whatever rink you play in, skate a warm-up lap and notice how far it is from the half-wall to the far dot. That distance tells you how much time you'll have there.

COACH: Let me pull the map together. With the puck, move it toward space. Behind their net, the weak side, the point, the half-wall. Without it, go where the space will be. Expect no time in the slot, the strong-side corner, or the middle of the neutral zone. Drag a trailing skate at their blue line, blade on the ice. Find out whether your games have a delayed offside before you tag up. Against a team protecting a lead, take the point and the wall. And notice how big your rink is.

COACH: Last, how to build all of this. Time and space is a habit you can train. First, play small-area games, across the ice or in half of it.

PLAYER: Why does a smaller space help?

COACH: Less space per player means more touches, more decisions, and less time for each one. Train your decisions where it's crowded, and a full rink starts to feel roomy. If your team doesn't run them, run them yourself. Three-on-three in one zone, two nets, no offside, first to five. It's also the most fun hockey there is.

COACH: Second, when you watch hockey, don't watch the puck. Pick one player and watch only them for a full shift, including the twenty seconds they don't touch it. You'll see how much of what looks like skill is really arriving at open ice before the puck does. Watch a defenceman's gap from the moment the attacker gets the puck. Most of it is decided before the attacker reaches the red line. And count how often a good centre looks over their shoulder before a breakout pass. It's more than you think.

COACH: Third, and this is the one that cures the panic pass. After every shift, ask yourself one question. Did I have more time than I thought?

PLAYER: And the answer's usually yes?

COACH: For almost every developing player, yes. Panic is the normal state for a player still learning, and when you panic, you spend time you actually had. Every rushed pass, and every puck flipped to nobody, was a moment with more time than it felt.

PLAYER: So how do I stop panicking? Just be braver?

COACH: Nobody can act on "be braver". Here's what you can act on. Get one more look before you receive, so you know your answer before the puck arrives.

COACH: Let's try it. You're in the neutral zone, outside your own end. The puck comes to you, a checker is closing, and you haven't seen a teammate. What would you do?

PLAYER: Get rid of it. Someone might be there.

COACH: That's the panic pass again. When you don't know, keep it, and turn side-on, away from the pressure, rather than passing into it. Your shoulder goes to the checker, with the puck on the far side of your body. Never turn your back to anyone coming at you, anywhere on the ice. Keeping it costs you a second. A pass into pressure is a turnover. And near the boards, keep it in the boards position.

PLAYER: Should I keep it in my own end too?

COACH: No. In your own end, holding the puck is how the turnover happens. One look, one decision, move it.

PLAYER: Is there anything that helps in the moment?

COACH: Trust "time" when you hear it. If a teammate or your goalie shouts it, it means nobody's on you. Believe them. They can see what you can't. And if you're the one calling it, be sure. If you're not sure, say nothing. A false "time" sends a teammate into a hit they weren't braced for.

COACH: Track that question for ten games, and the habit takes. For almost any player, it's the fastest way to get better.

COACH: Let me pull it all together.

COACH: Judge yourself by the room you had when the puck arrived. Read how fast they're closing, and their hips. Look before the puck arrives. When the play isn't there at or inside their blue line, turn away from pressure, side-on, and let help arrive. Never in your own end.

COACH: Don't panic pass. Get one more look before you receive. Outside your own end, when you don't know, keep it, side-on to the checker. In your own end, one look, one decision, move it, and never pass the puck, or skate it, across the front of your own net. And anywhere on the ice, never your back to anyone coming at you.

COACH: With the puck, change your speed and attack the seam. Fake only when you have time. On the boards, the boards position. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to anyone coming at you, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly.

COACH: Without the puck, change direction, and break when the carrier's head comes up. Never step into a defender's path. Screen the goalie from outside the crease, head up, touching nobody, and stay off the goalie.

COACH: Defending, act before the puck arrives. Close your gap early, angle instead of chasing, and take the puck, not the player. Blade in the lane, keep the middle, and never pressure alone. Stick on the puck, not a hit, and if you can see their numbers, pull up.

PLAYER: What should I ask my own coach?

COACH: Much of this depends on what your team plays. So three questions for your coach, and one for your league. Which forecheck do we play? On a breakout, which outlet do you want me to look for first? When I'm beaten, do you want the outside given up every time, or the wall taken away in some spots? And for the league. Which rules do we play under, and is body checking allowed?

COACH: Then, after every shift, ask yourself the one question. Did I have more time than I thought?
