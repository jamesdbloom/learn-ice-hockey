COACH: Picture this. Your team practises once a week. One hour, on the same night, every week. You want to get better, and you can feel that one hour isn't going to do it. So where does more ice come from?

PLAYER: I'd go online and search for sessions near me.

COACH: Most people would. But most of the extra ice out there isn't found by searching. It's found by asking the right people. This episode is the detailed one on finding more ice. I'm the coach, and with me is a player who already plays on a team and wants more time on the ice to get better. We'll go through who to ask, what to ask them, and the reason behind each question.

PLAYER: Is one session a week really that little?

COACH: For getting better, yes. Most players get far less time on the ice than they need to improve. That's the problem this whole episode is trying to fix. We'll start with four big ideas, because they matter most. If you only hear the first part, take those four with you. After that we go through each place to find ice, closest first.

COACH: First idea. Ask your own club for more ice before you look anywhere else. So, you want more ice. Who do you ask first?

PLAYER: The rink, I suppose?

COACH: Closer than that. Your own club. It's the easiest source of extra ice you have, and it's the one players forget to ask. A common mistake is waiting for your team to give you more ice. Don't wait. Ask your club, and add a session yourself.

PLAYER: And if the club has nothing?

COACH: Then you work outwards. Your club first. Then your rink. Then the national governing body, which is the organisation that runs hockey in your country. It can tell you which clubs exist. It can't tell you when they skate. For that, you go back to the rink.

COACH: Second idea. Add one more session a week if you can, and pick the right kind of ice for the job. Think of a toolbox. A hammer is a great tool, but not for a screw. Extra ice works the same way. Each kind is good for one thing.

PLAYER: What kinds are there?

COACH: Four you'll hear about all the time. Public skating is the sessions anyone can pay for and skate at, with no hockey game. It's the cheapest ice you can buy, and it's for your skating, not for hockey.

PLAYER: And for working with the puck?

COACH: Stick-and-puck. That's open ice for practising on your own with a stick and a puck. There's no game. For most players, it's the most useful extra ice there is. Then there's a drop-in, which is a pick-up game you pay for on the night. A drop-in, or a place on a second team, is for reading the game. That means learning what to do in a real game, with real players around you.

PLAYER: And the fourth?

COACH: A learn-to-play block. That's a set of beginner sessions that teaches the basics of playing hockey. It's for anyone still learning to stop. One warning about all these names. They're loose, and rinks use them in different ways. So read the rink's own description of a session. That tells you what it really is.

COACH: Third idea. Ring the rink, and ask your teammates. Lots of sessions are never listed anywhere central. If you only search online, you'll miss them.

PLAYER: So the rink just knows?

COACH: Usually, yes. Think of a school office. It doesn't run every club that meets in the building, but it knows who's using which room and when. A rink is like that with its ice. It usually knows every session and every club that skates there. And your teammates know about the sessions players run themselves. Between the rink and your teammates, they know almost every session there is.

COACH: Fourth idea. Before you join anything, ask four things. What level is it? What gear do I need? What does it cost? And is there contact? Turning up to the wrong session is an easy mistake. Turning up to a session with contact when you expected none is how people get hurt.

PLAYER: Is a league any different?

COACH: For a league, there's one more question, and it matters most of all. Does this league allow body checking? Body checking means using your body, on purpose, to knock an opponent off the puck. Some leagues allow it and some don't.

PLAYER: Who do I ask? The front desk?

COACH: No. Ask your coach, or your league secretary. The league secretary is the person who runs the league's admin. Whether checking is allowed is set by your age group, your league and your country, and the rink desk can't answer that. And being a beginner does not put you in a non-checking league. Some adult leagues allow checking. So find out before your first game, not after your first hit.

COACH: Those four are the core. Give them back to me.

PLAYER: Ask my own club first, and don't wait to be given more ice. Add one session a week, and match the ice to the job. Ring the rink and ask my teammates, because most extra ice is found by asking. And before I join anything, ask the level, the gear, the cost and whether there's contact. For a league, ask my coach or the league secretary whether it allows checking, not the rink desk.

COACH: That's them. Now we go through each place to find ice, starting with your own club.

COACH: Fifth idea. The four questions to ask your club. Take them to your coach, or to your club secretary, the person who runs the club's admin. Let's try it first. Your team practises once a week. What would you ask your club for?

PLAYER: More practices, I guess. Maybe whether I can train with another team?

COACH: That's the first question. Can I join another team's practice? A second team, or a development session, often has room for one more player. One thing to watch. If it's a child moving up an age group to get the extra ice, ask whether that age group allows body checking. Ice Hockey UK's junior leagues allow checking from the under-fourteen age group upwards. So ask before the child moves up.

PLAYER: What's the second?

COACH: Is there a train-only place? In England, England Ice Hockey describes a train-only option. It's for players who want to train with a club without the pressure of having to play in matches. It's aimed at older children who start late.

PLAYER: And the third?

COACH: Does the club book any extra ice? Some clubs run a skills hour, or book their own stick-and-puck.

PLAYER: And the last one?

COACH: Is there a spare list? That's a list of players a team rings when it's short on the night. Get on it, and a team can ring you when they're short.

PLAYER: So I could get a call at five o'clock and be playing that night?

COACH: Only if you're already registered. Register through your club before your first game, wherever you play, because that's your insurance and your eligibility. In Britain, get registered to that club, and to that team, before the day of the game. It can't be done on the night. And if a team plays someone who isn't registered, it can lose the result. So register early, and then you're ready when the call comes.

COACH: Sixth idea. If you might play goal, say so.

PLAYER: Does that really help?

COACH: A lot. Goalies are scarce at every level of amateur hockey. So a player who will go in goal gets offered more ice. Clubs often lend goal pads. But buy your own mask.

PLAYER: Why not borrow the mask too, if they lend the pads?

COACH: A goalie mask protects your head the way a helmet does. You can't see damage inside a used one, and you can't know what it's been hit by. Pads, borrow. The mask, buy your own.

COACH: Seventh idea. Public skating. It's the cheapest ice you can buy. Use it for your skating, not for hockey. Rinks list their public sessions on their websites, usually under public skating, or what's on. Now, say you're heading to a public session with your stick in your bag. What would you check first?

PLAYER: Whether I'm allowed to use it?

COACH: Right. Check the posted rules on sticks and pucks. Assume they're not allowed unless the rules say they are. If in doubt, leave the stick at home. Then ask the desk which sessions are the quietest. A quiet session gives you room to work, and it matters because of who else is out there. A public session is full of children and new skaters. So keep fast starts, hard stops and skating backwards to a clear patch of ice, and only if the posted rules allow them at all.

COACH: Eighth idea. Stick-and-puck and drop-in. This is the most useful extra ice for most players. Stick-and-puck gives you room to work on your puck handling on your own. A drop-in gives you a real game to read. Here's a scene. You look at your rink's website and there's no stick-and-puck anywhere. What would you do next?

PLAYER: Give up on that rink?

COACH: Not yet. There's an order to it. First, check you looked in the right place. Look at the rink's timetable under hockey, or under ice hire, not on the public skating page. Second, if nothing is listed, ring the desk and ask.

PLAYER: And if the desk says no?

COACH: Third, ask your teammates about sessions players run themselves. Some drop-ins are booked by a club, or by a group of players, and they fill up by word of mouth. You won't find those online. Fourth, try other rinks within reach. A rink a little further away might run sessions where yours runs none.

PLAYER: Anything to ask before I turn up?

COACH: The level, the gear and whether there's contact. Turning up to the wrong drop-in is an easy mistake, and one phone call avoids it. And once you're on, look before you cross the ice, don't shoot into a group, and never shoot at a net while someone near it is collecting pucks.

COACH: Let's pull that part together. Your turn.

PLAYER: Ask the club about another team's practice, a train-only place, extra ice and the spare list. In Britain, register with the club and the team before the day of any game. If I might play goal, say so, and buy my own mask. Use public skating for skating, check the rules on sticks, and keep the hard work to a clear patch. And find stick-and-puck and drop-in on the rink's hockey timetable, by ringing the desk, and through my teammates.

COACH: Spot on.

COACH: Ninth idea. Joining a rec or adult team when you don't have one. Rec is short for recreational. It means hockey played for fun, rather than in the top leagues. Say you've moved to a new town and you've got no team. What would you do?

PLAYER: Find a team that needs players and ask them?

COACH: Simpler than that. Ask to be placed as an individual. And ask about checking in the same call. To find clubs, ask the rink which clubs and leagues use its ice. Then ask five questions.

PLAYER: Go on.

COACH: First, what level is it? Judge yourself on your skating, not your shot. Skating is what sorts players into levels. And if you're unsure, take the lower level.

PLAYER: Isn't that selling myself short?

COACH: That's experience, not a rule. But think about it from the other end. Moving up part way through a season is easy, and people welcome it. Moving down is awkward. Too high a level is also where people get hurt. So lean low. Second question, is it a checking league? Ask your coach or the league secretary, not the rink desk. Being a beginner does not put you in a non-checking league.

PLAYER: Third?

COACH: Do you place individual players? Some leagues do. Others only take whole teams. In Canada, the Adult Safe Hockey League has a sign-up form for individual players. And one warning here. If you're still on a junior team in Britain, ask before you register with an adult team. Under Ice Hockey UK's junior rules, a player who registers for senior membership can't play junior hockey for the rest of that season. So check first, or you could lose the rest of your junior season.

PLAYER: What's the fourth?

COACH: Is it sanctioned? Sanctioned means the national governing body has approved it. This one matters more than it sounds. In Britain, hockey that isn't sanctioned isn't insured. If you get hurt, the national body's insurance doesn't cover you. And under England Ice Hockey's last published rules, a registered player who joins a league it doesn't recognise faces a suspension and fines. In Canada, Hockey Canada can't insure players in a league that isn't sanctioned. And a child registered in one loses all their Hockey Canada membership privileges for the rest of that season. So know which kind of hockey it is before you sign up.

PLAYER: And the fifth?

COACH: What does it cost, and do you need a goalie? If you might play goal, this is the moment to say so.

COACH: Tenth idea. Learning to skate and learning to play, for adults and for children. The order is simple. Learn to skate first, or alongside. Then learn to play. Then join a club team. And tell the club you're coming.

PLAYER: Why would I need to tell them? Wouldn't the programme just pass me on?

COACH: Let's test that. Say someone finishes a learn-to-play block at their rink in England. Why might no club know they exist?

PLAYER: Because the rink runs it, not the club?

COACH: Often, yes. A learn-to-play programme and the club may not be connected at all. England Ice Hockey says learn-to-play programmes are usually run by the rink, or sometimes by the club. It also says the rink's programmes are not directly linked to junior clubs. So a player can finish a block at the rink, and no club knows they exist. Contact the club as well, so the block leads somewhere.

PLAYER: And the skating first part?

COACH: Skate first if you can. England Ice Hockey advises working up to level five skating before starting learn to play. Rinks run their own skating levels, so ask the rink what its level five means.

PLAYER: Is anything different if it's a child starting?

COACH: Two more questions. Are the coaches background-checked? In Britain, that's a DBS or a PVG check. And who is the club's safeguarding lead? That's the person at the club whose job is keeping children safe.

COACH: Eleventh idea. Camps and clinics. These are extra coaching that you book on top of your team's practice. No camp finder turned up on the national websites. So ask your club, your rink and your coach.

PLAYER: How do I tell a good one from a bad one?

COACH: Four questions before you book. Who coaches it? What level does it suit? How many players are there for each coach? And is there contact? Don't book a camp just because of its name. Those four answers tell you more. In Britain, also ask whether the camp is sanctioned by Ice Hockey UK. Its insurance covers a camp only if the camp is sanctioned and you're registered. For a child, ask the same two safeguarding questions as before. Are the coaches background-checked, and who is the safeguarding lead?

PLAYER: Any kind of session worth looking out for?

COACH: Power skating. That means coaching on skating technique alone. One power-skating session a season is worth it, if you can.

COACH: Let's pull that part together. Give it back to me.

PLAYER: With no team, ask to be placed as an individual, and ask the level, whether it checks, and whether it's sanctioned. Judge my level on my skating, and lean low. Still on a junior team in Britain? Ask before registering with an adult team. Learn to skate, then learn to play, then join a club, and tell the club I'm coming. For a child, ask about background checks and the safeguarding lead. And for a camp in Britain, ask whether it's sanctioned, because you're only insured at a sanctioned camp, and only if you're registered.

COACH: That's it. Last part. Where to look, depending on where you live.

COACH: These lists tell you which clubs exist. They don't tell you when those clubs skate. For that, ring the rink. And if a page has moved, go to the organisation's home page and look for play, or clubs.

PLAYER: Start with Britain, then.

COACH: Across Britain, Ice Hockey UK now governs the sport in all four home nations, but its website has no club finder. To find a rink and its learn-to-skate classes, use British Ice Skating's rink finder.

COACH: In England, England Ice Hockey's Play page explains how to get into hockey in England. Its club finder isn't live. For adult rec hockey, it links to its Rec section. That has a team list naming each team and its home rink. Some older pages still carry the EIHA name. They're out of date, so use the current England Ice Hockey site.

COACH: In Scotland, Scottish Ice Hockey is now part of Ice Hockey UK. It lists junior clubs, recreational clubs and women's clubs. Its recreational page says some clubs run Hockey For All sessions for beginners.

PLAYER: Say I live in Wales and want to join a rec team. Where do I look?

COACH: What would you guess?

PLAYER: A Welsh hockey body?

COACH: No separate Welsh ice hockey body was found. Welsh clubs play in the English leagues. So use the England pages. The Rec team list includes the Cardiff and Deeside teams.

PLAYER: And Northern Ireland?

COACH: Scottish Ice Hockey's junior and recreational lists include Northern Ireland clubs. The Irish Ice Hockey Association describes itself as the governing body for ice hockey in Ireland. Its clubs page lists the Belfast clubs and their training times. Ask the club you join which body it registers with.

PLAYER: What about outside the UK?

COACH: In the USA, USA Hockey's Find Hockey Near You finds local youth programmes by zip code. Its Come Play Youth Hockey page links to Try Hockey For Free, to Learn to Skate USA, and to the NHL and NHLPA Learn to Play programme. That programme's locations are on each NHL team's own page. For adult hockey, ring the rink and ask which adult leagues and drop-ins use its ice.

COACH: In Canada, Hockey Canada's Pathway to Minor Hockey page links to three things. Its association finder. The First Shift, which is a learn-to-play programme. And Esso Fun Days, a free taster for girls and women. Hockey Canada calls its finder a starting point, so check with the local association that your child can play there. For learning to skate, use Skate Canada's Find a Club, or your local parks and recreation department. For adults, look at Hockey Canada's Adult Recreational Hockey page, and the Adult Safe Hockey League's page for individual players.

COACH: Anywhere else, the IIHF has a page listing every national body it recognises. Find yours there. Then ring the rink.

COACH: Let me pull it all together.

COACH: Most of the extra ice you need is found by asking, not searching. Ask your own club first. Ask for another team's practice, a train-only place, extra ice, or a place on the spare list. In Britain, get registered with the club and team before the day of any game. Add one session a week if you can. Use public skating for your skating, and stick-and-puck or drop-in for your hockey. Ring the rink, and ask your teammates.

Being a beginner doesn't put you in a non-checking league. In Britain, ask whether it's sanctioned, because unsanctioned hockey isn't insured.

COACH: Three questions, this week. Can I join another team's practice? Is there a spare list I can go on? And is our league, or the one I want to join, a checking league? Then ring the rink, and ask what hockey it runs that you could join.
