That's the case for a coach calling a timeout, changing lines, or having the goalie freeze the puck. And it's the case for you taking a full, deliberate reset, not just calming down a bit. That picture comes from a very small study of eight basketball players. So treat it as a useful way to think, not a proven fact.

COACH: Ninth idea. A pre-game routine that runs in any rink. A routine is there to make your state the same every week, so the only thing that changes is the game. Routines work. The research supports that. A large review of studies found that routines before a performance help, and they helped under pressure too. It didn't find that age or skill level made a difference, which is a reason not to think routines are only for experts. What nobody can tell you is which parts of a routine do the work. So build one, and don't believe anyone who says exactly which steps are essential.

PLAYER: I always wear the same socks. Is that a routine?

COACH: Routines and superstitions look the same from outside. Here's the test. If you couldn't do it, would it wreck you? A routine is what you do for its effect. A warm-up that gets your body warm. Breathing that settles you. If the room is different or you arrive late, you squeeze it down, and you're fine. A superstition is what you do because of what you fear will happen if you don't. Same socks. Same tape job. Tapping the goalie's pads a set number of times. It's harmless on its own. But it makes your calm depend on something you don't control, and one day the socks will be in the wash.

PLAYER: So cut the socks.

COACH: Keep the routine, and cut anything that would break you if you couldn't do it. In an away rink, a different room, or on an odd schedule, your routine still has to run. Here's a timeline that works. The exact times are coaching know-how, so adapt them to your league and your travel.

COACH: The night before, pack and check your kit, and get your skates sharpened if they need it. Sleep. Sleep has better evidence behind it than anything else in your routine. And don't cram tactics late at night. Going over systems at eleven at night doesn't make them stick. Your last full meal is about three hours before. Arrive an hour to an hour and a half before, at the same point every week if you can. Rushing is an easy way to arrive wound up. Get dressed in the same order. That isn't a superstition. It's one less decision.

COACH: Twenty to thirty minutes before, warm up off the ice. Get your body warm with moving exercises, not holding stretches. This is where you push your level up or down. In the on-ice warm-up, skate at full game speed at least twice. A warm-up done entirely at sixty percent teaches your body that tonight is a sixty percent night. Goalies, track the puck properly from the first shot.

PLAYER: And right before the puck drops?

COACH: In the last minute, two or three slow breaths out. And one thought about the game, about something you'll do. Not, we need this one. Something like, first pass up the wall, or, gap early. Between periods, the same thing, shorter. Recover, make one change, take one cue. And don't spend the break on the referee.

PLAYER: Let me pull the before-the-game part together. Nerves are my body getting ready, so call them that. Breathe out longer than in. My best level is my own, so track it over about eight games, and use the warm-up to push myself up or down. Keep a routine that runs in any rink, and cut anything that would wreck me if it were missing. And skate at game speed in the warm-up.

COACH: That's it. Now the harder parts.

COACH: Tenth idea. Fear, and how to work on it. Fear is common in hockey and rarely talked about. Nobody has measured how common, so don't trust any figure you hear. If you're scared of something on the ice, you aren't soft, and it isn't a flaw in your character. It's a normal reaction to a fast game with hard surfaces, and you can work on it.

PLAYER: Is it all one thing?

COACH: There are four kinds, and they're four different problems. Fear of contact. Being hit, or hitting. It's common in players who started late, players coming back after time away, and anyone who has just moved up a level. Body checking isn't allowed in non-checking hockey, so ask your league which kind it plays. But body contact, like battles, pins and collisions, is in every version of the game.

COACH: Fear of injury. This one is usually specific, and usually earned. Someone with a bad shoulder is afraid of that shoulder, not of hockey, most of all in the first few games after it heals. Fear of the puck. Flinching when you block a shot, turning away from a shot from the point, not wanting to be in the slot when the puck is coming. And fear of making a mistake. That's the careful, tidy player from earlier. It's the most damaging of the four, and the least visible.

PLAYER: So how do you work on the physical ones?

COACH: The approach that works is called graded steps. Do the thing you're afraid of, a little at a time, at a level where you stay in control. Only move up when that level feels boring. Think of getting into a cold sea. You don't dive off the rocks. You go in to your knees until it stops feeling cold, then a bit further.

PLAYER: Is that a hockey thing?

COACH: The evidence for it with fear and anxiety in general is strong. Using it for fear of contact in hockey is coaching know-how. But it does follow the order Canadian and American hockey use to teach checking. Positioning and angling first, then stick checks, then body contact, and body checking last. Those are ways to teach a skill, not tested treatments for fear.

COACH: And technique lowers fear more than courage does. A lot of the fear of contact is a fair fear of getting hurt, because you don't know how to take a hit. So learn it. This is the part that keeps you safe, so I'll go slowly. Picture yourself chasing a puck into the corner, and you can hear someone coming.

PLAYER: What do I do?

COACH: See the hit coming. Get your weight low, keep your feet moving, and keep your head up with your chin off your chest. Come into the boards on an angle, not straight on. If the contact is coming anyway, get your skates parallel to the boards, feet apart and knees bent, with your forearm and hip to the boards. Your shoulder can turn toward the checker. The boards are what it should not meet. Never the point of your shoulder into the boards or the glass, and not the whole shoulder if you can help it.

COACH: Head up and chin off your chest is the part you never give up. It's the half of getting low that players drop. Never turn your back to anyone coming at you. And never duck. Both protect your head and neck. If you duck, your head meets the boards first, and a head-first hit like that can injure your neck even at walking speed. If you turn your back, the contact becomes a hit from behind. You can't see it coming or brace for it, and even a light hit from behind could cause a serious head or neck injury. So a slow checker doesn't make it safe.

PLAYER: What if I'm already facing the boards and can't turn?

COACH: That's the last resort, not a choice. Get your stick and gloves up and spread wide on the glass, with your head off it. And still never turn your back to anyone coming at you. That boards position matters more than anything else in this part. From here on I'll use one short version, the same words every time. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to anyone coming at you, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly. Learn that, and the fear drops for a good reason.

PLAYER: Then the steps for contact?

COACH: They might go like this. Leaning on a teammate who's standing still and ready for it, in a drill your coach runs. Then angling along the boards at half speed. Then one-on-one battles on the wall. Then the same at game speed. Your coach sets these up. Stay at each step until it bores you. Once there's an opponent, in the angling and the battles, win with your feet, and your stick on the puck. No bump, no lean, no shove, and never your stick across anyone. Never into anyone's back, and if you can see their numbers, pull up. Never take a player's skates off the ice. Whether body checking is part of your game at all is your league's call. And on the boards, the boards position. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to anyone coming at you, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly. A crash into the boards can hurt even then.

PLAYER: And fear of the puck?

COACH: The same idea. Tennis balls first, then slow pucks, then real shots from a distance, then closer. And learn to block a shot properly, because it's close to the opposite of what instinct tells you. How much your team blocks shots at all is a coaching choice. Ask your coach, and ask your goalie.

PLAYER: What does blocking properly look like?

COACH: If you block, here's the whole of it, and it goes together. Head up and out of the shooting lane, the line the puck travels. Square to the shooter. On your feet, and close. Shin pads facing the shot. Hands behind your body, with the backs of your gloves facing out. Never turn your back. Never turn sideways. Never lift a leg. Each of those shows the puck a part of you with no protection, and turns you away from it. Head up and out of the lane is the one that never bends, whatever else your team asks of you.

PLAYER: What about diving to block?

COACH: Going down on the ice at a shot takes you out of the play, whether the block works or not. A timed slide to block a pass on a two-on-one is a different thing. If that's your team's plan, ask your coach how they want it done, rather than working it out at full speed. A player who was never taught to block a shot is right to be afraid of it. And check your kit. Pads that don't fit, or are too thin, make you flinch, and you're right to flinch. A player flinching at shots from the point in thin shin pads isn't being silly.

PLAYER: Should I always push through fear, then?

COACH: Not all fear should be pushed through. Fear that says, this is really dangerous, is doing its job. A player out of control. A hit from behind toward the boards. Going into a corner with your head down while someone is arriving. The goal isn't to switch fear off. It's to stop it running in situations that are actually safe.

PLAYER: What about coming back from an injury?

COACH: All of this assumes you've been medically cleared to play. If you haven't, that comes first, and the decision isn't yours to make. Your mind usually takes longer to come back than your body. It's normal to be cleared and still be protecting the injury three weeks later. Come back through the same graded steps, not straight into a game. And mental health symptoms raise the risk of injury and slow recovery. So a fear that hangs on isn't just uncomfortable. It's a physical risk too, which is one more reason to come back slowly, through the steps.

COACH: Eleventh idea. Goalies, and how the team treats them. Everything in this part is coaching know-how with a sensible reason behind it. It isn't research. The sources behind it are the weakest in the whole topic. Even for soccer goalkeepers, who have been studied more, researchers looked and found almost nothing tested.

PLAYER: I'm not a goalie, though.

COACH: Listen anyway, because most of it is about how you treat your goalie. A goalie's mistakes are the scoreboard, with nobody behind them to cover. After a goal, play stops and everyone looks at them, and they stand there with nothing to do but think about it. They can be idle for eleven minutes and then need a huge save. And they're often alone, training apart, with the only other goalie on the team competing for the net. Blame lands on them by default.

PLAYER: So what should a goalie do after a goal?

COACH: Build a short memory. It's a routine, not a personality. Run the same routine after every goal, including the ones that weren't your fault. If you only run it after bad goals, it becomes a sign that you failed. One way to do it. Take the puck out of the net yourself, or don't, but pick one and always do it. Skate a short loop out to the top of your crease, the painted area in front of your net, or to the corner. Move. Moving gives the reset a shape and breaks the frozen stance.

COACH: Water bottle, one long breath out, wipe your mask. Then say one plain sentence about the goal. Screen, didn't find it. And stop there. No judging, no blaming yourself. Go back to your crease, tap both posts, say your cue word, and set for the faceoff. That one plain sentence, and then setting for the faceoff, are the whole thing. Saying it once, plainly, satisfies the urge to go over it, so your mind stops replaying the goal all period. The rest is just a frame for it.

COACH: A goalie's confidence comes from saves made. That's the strongest source of confidence again, having done it. After a bad night, a goalie should face lots of saveable shots in the next practice, before the hard ones. Lots of shots matters more than hard shots.

PLAYER: And me, as the skater? What would you do when your goalie lets in a soft one?

COACH: Most teams get it wrong in one of two directions. Don't pile on. A head shake, a stick slam, skating away after a goal, all of that reads as blame to your goalie, whether you meant it or not. And it doesn't stay between the two of you. But don't over-console either. Skating over after every goal to say it wasn't their fault turns each goal into a big event. That's the opposite of what the short memory is for. The best default is normal. A tap on the pads on your way past, one word, and back to your job.

COACH: Include your goalie off the ice. In the dressing room, before the game, and socially. It costs nothing. And talk to them during play. The goalie is in the best spot on the ice to talk to the team, and giving them a job keeps them in the game through the quiet spells.

COACH: Twelfth idea. Keep your frustration to yourself, and off the officials. The referee has just called you for something you didn't do. What would you do?

PLAYER: Honestly, I'd tell them.

COACH: Skate away and run your reset. Your frustration, shown, hands the other team a power play, a spell where they have an extra player. And when they score on it, everyone blames the bad call, not you. The point isn't that you have to be nice. It's that your frustration costs your team.

PLAYER: What does arguing actually cost me?

COACH: In plain words. Arguing a call costs you at least a penalty, and that usually becomes their power play. If you keep at the official after that, the penalty gets longer. If you keep going after that, you're thrown out of the game. In some leagues the referee doesn't have to go step by step, and can throw you out much sooner.

COACH: Some things skip the climb. In most leagues an obscene gesture ends your game on the spot. Swearing at or abusing an official can get you thrown out too. A racist or other hateful slur at anyone can get you thrown out and suspended. Spitting at anyone, or threatening an official, can get you thrown out and suspended. You don't have to touch an official to be thrown out. And in some leagues, being thrown out also means missing your team's next game.

COACH: Banging the boards or the glass with your stick in protest at a call is a penalty, and in some leagues it ends your game. Throwing anything onto the ice from the bench is a penalty too, and in some leagues that ends your game as well. A water bottle, a towel, even a stick thrown to a teammate who has lost theirs. Swearing at or abusing anyone, an opponent included, is a penalty. And never hit back physically.

COACH: Never put a hand on an official, however wrong the call. Not to argue, and not to pull free of a linesperson breaking up a fight. In every league that can end your game, and it can bring a suspension as well. Treat any hand on an official as ending your game.

PLAYER: Let me pull the harder parts together. Beat physical fear with technique and graded steps, not courage. On the boards, the boards position. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of my shoulder, head up, chin off my chest, never my back to anyone coming at me, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly. Already facing the boards, stick and gloves up on the glass, head off it, as a last resort. And when I do go in, stick on the puck, not a hit, and never push or hit anyone from behind. Blocking a shot, head up and out of the lane. After a goal against, a tap on my goalie's pads and back to work. And after a bad call, skate away. Never a hand on an official.

COACH: Spot on. Now the people around you, and the long season.

COACH: Thirteenth idea. Coaches, ice time and criticism. Picture your coach shouting across the bench that you're standing still. How do you take it?

PLAYER: Badly. If they'd said it nicely I might listen.

COACH: Treat feedback as two things, what was said and how it was said. Whether it's true and useful is the message. Whether it was fair, kind and well-timed is the delivery. They're separate. A coach who shouts you're standing still across the bench said it badly, and may be completely right. If you throw out the message because you resent the messenger, you lose the information.

COACH: And the reverse matters too. Nice delivery doesn't make advice correct. A lot of coaching advice is a team's preference dressed up as a rule of hockey. Wingers never go below the goal line is a system choice, not a law. Ask which system, and ask why.

PLAYER: Is there a point where it's not just tough coaching?

COACH: There's a line. Being demanding isn't the same as being abusive. Criticism of your play is coaching. Putting you down personally, humiliating you in front of the group again and again, or anything that leaves you dreading the rink, is not. Don't put up with it quietly. Take it to your club's safeguarding lead if it has one, another club official, the league, or the governing body.

PLAYER: And ice time? I'm sure I deserve more than I get.

COACH: Start with an honest point. Most players think they deserve more ice time than they get. Nobody has measured that, but it can't be true of everyone. You remember your good shifts, and you miss the plays where you were beaten, because you were behind them. That doesn't mean your coach is right. It means you aren't a reliable judge of your own case.

PLAYER: So what do I do?

COACH: Ask one specific question, in private, a day or two later. Not on the bench, not straight after the game, and not in front of the group. What do I need to do to get on the second power play? A vague question like, why am I not playing more, gets a vague answer and sounds like a complaint. Then work on the answer where the coach can see it, in practice, not just in the few shifts you get.

COACH: Be excellent in the minutes you do get. Playing really well in four minutes tends to get you six. Sulking through four tends to get you two. That's coaching know-how, but close to universal. And keep your legs moving on the bench. A cold player who goes over the boards and gets beaten has just proved the coach right.

PLAYER: And if none of that works?

COACH: Some benchings are about things you can't control. If you've asked the specific question, done the work, and nothing has changed over a real stretch of time, moving teams is a fair answer, not a failure.

COACH: Fourteenth idea. Your mood is not private. When you slump on the bench, throw your head back or bang your stick, you aren't quietly dealing with a bad shift. You're broadcasting it. Your linemates read it in about half a second, as one of three things. The coach is wrong. My linemate let me down. Or, this game is gone. None of those helps them on the next shift. And a stick slammed on the boards or the glass can be a penalty of its own.

PLAYER: Is that really a thing, or just something coaches say?

COACH: Here's what the research says. Moods spread between players. Coaches' moods are linked to players' moods and to results. And closeness and status matter, so a senior player's body language carries further than a fourth-liner's. How strong the effect is hasn't been measured.

PLAYER: So I have to fake being happy?

COACH: No. Nobody believes that anyway. It means a neutral floor. Whatever you feel, what people see is upright, eyes on the ice, and in the game. Feel what you feel. Don't broadcast it. On a bad night, say something useful, and it's usually about the next shift. Get above me next time and I'll hit you with the pass, is useful. You've got to be there, is not.

COACH: When a teammate makes a mistake, say nothing, or one word of support. The head shake costs more than their mistake did. If it was your turnover, say that's on me, once, and then stop. That ends the conversation. Saying it again and again makes everyone manage your feelings instead of playing. And remember, your bad night isn't the main event. Seventeen other people came to play. On a night when you have nothing, your job is not to make it their problem.

COACH: Fifteenth idea. Getting through a season. Improvement isn't a straight line. You usually climb fast early on, then hit long flat stretches, broken by sudden jumps. The flat stretches are normal. But they tend to arrive just as your early excitement wears off, which is what makes them hard.

PLAYER: I've been stuck for about two months.

COACH: Here's what a flat stretch usually is. Often a skill is becoming automatic. That doesn't feel like progress, but it's what makes the skill work under pressure. And sometimes it really is a limit of your technique, and you need a new way of doing it, not more of the same. So measure something that moves. Wins, points and ice time can sit still for months. Your process goals move week to week.

COACH: Practice matters. It's a big factor, and it's the one you control. But hours on their own don't decide how good you get, so two players with the same ice time can improve at very different speeds.

PLAYER: What about when I just don't feel like going?

COACH: Mid-season, with early mornings and cold rinks, motivation becomes habit. Turn up because it's Tuesday, not because you feel like it. Take a real off-season, because hockey twelve months a year is a good way to stop wanting to play. Breaks are part of recovering, not slacking off. And keep one technical thing you're working on, so a bad night still has something in it for you. And watch for wanting to play turning into feeling you have to. That's an early sign of burnout. If it doesn't go away, take it seriously, and talk to someone qualified.

PLAYER: Let me pull that part together. Separate the message from the delivery. If I'm short of ice time, ask one specific question in private, then do the work where the coach can see it. Criticism of my play is coaching. Humiliation is not, so take that to someone at the club or league. On the bench, hold a neutral floor, and talk about the next shift. Expect flat stretches, measure something that moves, and take a real off-season.

COACH: Last idea. When it isn't a hockey problem. This episode is about playing better. It's not a replacement for care, and some of what looks like a mental-game problem isn't one. If it has spread beyond the rink, it isn't a hockey problem. It needs a qualified professional, and looking for one isn't a lack of toughness.

PLAYER: How would I know it's that, and not just a bad patch?

COACH: Here are the signs it's time to talk to someone qualified, rather than learning more mental skills. Anxiety that starts well before game day, or doesn't settle once play starts. Low mood, losing interest, or hopelessness that lasts for weeks. Sleep that is disrupted all the time, or a big change in appetite. Dreading the rink, when you used to enjoy it. Problems that have spread into work, school, friendships or family. That's the clearest sign, because a real hockey problem stays at the rink. Using alcohol or anything else to manage how you feel about playing. And any thought of harming yourself or of suicide. That one is not something to weigh up. Get help now, tonight.

COACH: The main international statement on athletes' mental health says depression and anxiety are about as common in top athletes as in everyone else. It also says eating disorders and disordered eating are more common in athletes. Hockey isn't usually a sport where you make a weight, but roster weights and body targets do happen, so that matters here too. And it says mental health and physical health can't be separated.

PLAYER: Who do I actually talk to?

COACH: For anything less urgent, talk to your GP or family doctor, or to a qualified clinical or sport psychologist. They aren't the same job. If the problem has left the rink, you probably want a clinical psychologist. A lot of national federations and leagues now have mental health support you can use.

COACH: And if you are thinking about harming yourself or about suicide, stop listening and get help now. If you're in immediate danger, call your local emergency number. Nine nine nine in the UK and Ireland. One one two across the EU. Nine one one in the US and Canada. Or go to your nearest emergency department. In the UK and Ireland, call Samaritans, free, any hour, on one one six, one two three. In the UK you can also text the word shout to eight five two five eight. In the US and Canada, call or text nine eight eight, any hour. Anywhere else, find a helpline dot com lists a crisis line for each country. The full list is at the start of this episode. Call someone tonight, not after the season.

COACH: None of this means you're failing at hockey. It means you have a problem that hockey advice isn't the right tool for.

COACH: Let me pull it all together. After every mistake, run the reset. A physical trigger, one long breath out, a cue word, and name your next job before your skates hit the ice. On the bench, recover, watch, and plan one thing. Leave the mistake for after the game.

COACH: Decide early and commit. Simple in your own end, creative in theirs. A simple play on time beats a careful one made late. Don't panic pass. Ask yourself after every shift whether you had more time than you thought. In your own end, one look, one decision, move it. And anywhere on the ice, never your back to anyone coming at you.

COACH: Judge your night on three process goals for your role, and keep an outcome goal on the list. Never put finish every check on it. In a slump, shoot more, and rebuild with reps of the exact thing you lost.

COACH: Switch your focus on triggers, with a shoulder check. Call your nerves your body getting ready, and use the warm-up to set your level. Keep a routine that runs in any rink.

COACH: Beat physical fear with technique and graded steps. On the boards, the boards position. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to anyone coming at you, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly. Already facing the boards, stick and gloves up on the glass, head off it. And when you do go in, stick on the puck, not a hit, and never push or hit anyone from behind. Blocking a shot, head up and out of the lane.

COACH: Keep your frustration off your face and off the officials. And never put a hand on one.

PLAYER: What should I ask my own coach?

COACH: Three things, and one for your league. Which system do we play, so I can write the right process goals? How much do you want me blocking shots, and how? And when I'm not playing much, what do I need to do to get more ice? And for the league. Which category do we play in, and is body checking allowed? Ask, because these are your team's and your league's choices, not rules of hockey. Their answers tell you where to aim all of this, and now you know why each part works.

COACH: And if it has spread beyond the rink, talk to someone qualified. And if you are thinking of harming yourself or of suicide, call your local emergency number or your crisis line, tonight.
