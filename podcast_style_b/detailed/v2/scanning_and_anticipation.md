COACH: That can be a trap, such as a one-three-one. There's almost no pressure in your corner, and that's bait. The boards and the middle are filled. So skate the puck up with speed and your head up, and make someone commit. Or chip it past their layer, instead of passing into it. Against an all-out press, everything is pressured at once. Take the safe out, off the glass or a rim, and live to play the next shift.

COACH: And off the glass means off it, never over it. From your own end, a puck that flies over the glass and out of the rink is a penalty in some leagues, even if you didn't mean it. If you put it out on purpose, it's a penalty in every league.

COACH: Here's a job almost nobody does. On your first shift, find out which shape they're running, and tell your bench.

COACH: One time to expect pressure straight away is right after your own team ices the puck. That's when your team sends it down the ice and the referee stops play. The faceoff comes back to your end. And in some leagues, the tired line that iced it can't change. An injured player can still be replaced. So decide your safe out before the puck is dropped.

PLAYER: Anything else to read in the forecheck?

COACH: Each forechecker's angle. The angle tells you where they want you to go. One cutting off the middle is steering you to the boards, to seal you there. One coming from outside is steering you into the middle, usually toward a teammate who's waiting. Go where they don't want you.

COACH: Thirteenth idea. Play where the puck will be next. The best read isn't about a player at all. It's about where the puck is about to be.

PLAYER: How can I read where a puck's going?

COACH: Start with the boards. A hard rim follows the curve of the boards, and arrives where you'd expect. A soft one dies in the corner. A puck flipped off the glass comes down somewhere else entirely. Rinks differ too, like a pinball table where every one plays a bit differently. Some have lively glass, and some have dead spots behind the net. So learn your home rink on purpose. And at an away rink, bounce a couple off the end boards in the warm-up.

COACH: Now rebounds. The rule of thumb is that a rebound comes off the way the goalie's pad or blocker was facing. So a shot from an angle tends to kick out to the far side, and the place to arrive is the far post. That's a coaching rule of thumb, not a measured finding. And a goalie who steers rebounds on purpose will break it.

PLAYER: So what can I rely on?

COACH: Arrive at the net moving, expecting the puck, not standing there watching for it. Arrive just outside the crease, the goalie's painted area, and off the goalie. Keep your stick on the ice, with the blade out of the paint. Moving doesn't mean flying in. Arrive under control. Building up speed into contact, even with two hard strides, can be charging.

COACH: Keep your head up and your chin off your chest at the net. A defender can push you while your eyes are on the puck. And the goal post can hurt you the same way the boards can.

PLAYER: And the goalie?

COACH: Never go into the goalie, in or out of the crease. Any contact with a goalie can be a penalty, and it can get you thrown out of the game. A goalie who is down or turned can't protect themselves, so contact can hurt them. And it can cost your team the goal.

COACH: Keep your skates and your stick out of the crease too. In some leagues, a goal doesn't count if even your stick or a skate was in the crease while the goalie was in it.

PLAYER: What about standing in front to screen?

COACH: Standing in the goalie's line of sight, so they can't see the shot, is called a screen. From outside the crease, with no contact, it's legal, and the goal stands. But leave the goalie room to move inside the crease, or the goal can still be taken away. And stay off the crease line too. Most leagues count the line as part of the crease.

COACH: Never get in the way of a goalie who's trying to get back to the crease. That's a penalty in every league. In some, it costs your team the goal as well. A reckless block, or one that hurts someone, can get you thrown out of the game. In some leagues, hurting someone that way always does.

PLAYER: And once the goalie's out of the crease, they're fair game?

COACH: No. A goalie outside the crease isn't fair game. Any unnecessary contact with one is a penalty. In some leagues, even accidental contact is. And in at least one league, any contact on purpose counts as charging. So never charge a goalie, in the crease or out of it. Charging a goalie can get you thrown out of the game in every league, and in some it always does. And trying to hurt a goalie is a match penalty in most leagues, the most serious penalty there is.

COACH: Fourteenth idea. Know which battles you're going to lose. Reading the next play also tells you which battles you'll lose, and this read saves goals. Picture it. You're arriving second at a puck in the corner, on your backhand, and the other player is bigger and already has position. What would you do?

PLAYER: Go in hard and try to win it anyway?

COACH: You'll probably lose that battle. Arriving second, off balance, on your backhand, or against a bigger player who already has position, all mean the same. So stop trying to win it. Play for what happens next instead. Get to the spot where the loose puck will squirt out.

PLAYER: Is there anything else I can do?

COACH: Pin the puck against the boards with your skate or your stick, just for a moment, for a teammate to dig it out. The player who gives up the battle and takes the loose puck ends up with it. Pin the puck, not the player. Your stick goes on the puck, never on the player and never across them. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call. If you can see their numbers, pull up. Never use your skate to push a player, even without a kick. Never use your hands on the puck.

PLAYER: What if I just keep it pinned till the whistle?

COACH: Never hold the puck there to get a whistle. Holding the puck to force a stoppage is a penalty in every league. In some, it's called after three seconds, even if someone is pressuring you. And you're on the boards, so the same every time. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to anyone coming at you, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly.

COACH: Let me pull the shape and the next play together. Read the forecheck's shape early. Against a two-one-two behind your net, reverse it or rim it hard, rather than skating it round. Off the glass, never over it. Find out what they're running on your first shift, and tell the bench. Learn how the puck comes off the boards in your rink. Arrive at the net moving but under control, outside the crease, head up, and never into the goalie. And in a battle you'll lose, go for the loose puck, and pin the puck, never the player.

COACH: Fifteenth idea. Anticipate, don't guess.

PLAYER: Aren't they the same thing? Getting there before the puck?

COACH: They look the same when they work, and the same when they fail. But they're not the same thing. Anticipating is acting on what you've seen. Guessing is acting on hope. And the test isn't whether you were right. Ask yourself this. Would my position still be useful if I turned out to be wrong?

COACH: Picture two defenders. The first one leans toward the likely pass, while staying between the puck and the net. That's useful either way. The second one commits to a spot that's worthless unless the guess comes off. That's a guess, even when it works.

PLAYER: So when can I jump a pass?

COACH: Jumping a passing lane means leaving your spot to step into the path of a pass and take it. Do it only when all of these are true. You have a real cue. The passer's shoulders, a closed blade pointed at that lane, or their eyes have already been there. Someone is behind you, covering the ice you leave. The puck is in the neutral zone, the middle of the ice between the blue lines, or in their end. Being wrong there costs a rush against, not a goal against.

PLAYER: Is that all?

COACH: Two more. You can get there with your feet, not by reaching with your stick. Reaching is the giveaway that you guessed. It leaves your body in the wrong place and your feet stopped. And the game situation allows it. Tied in the second period, fine. Protecting a one-goal lead with two minutes left, no. When you jump, you're there for the puck, not the player. Your stick goes on the puck, not a hit. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call. And if you can see their numbers, pull up.

PLAYER: And when shouldn't I?

COACH: Never jump a lane when you're the last player back. Never in your own end, especially the middle of it, where a miss gives them a shot from point-blank range. Not when you made up your mind before the passer even had the puck. That's a plan looking for a reason, not a read. And not again after you've already missed once this shift. Chasing one gamble with another is how one mistake becomes a goal.

PLAYER: That's a lot of conditions for one play.

COACH: Because the costs aren't equal. A jump that works usually makes a scoring chance. One that fails in your own end usually gives one up. So you need to be well over fifty-fifty sure, and more than that in your own end.

COACH: Sixteenth idea. Get your head up off the puck. None of this works if you have to look at the puck to control it.

PLAYER: Because I can't look around and look down at the same time.

COACH: Right. A player who has to watch their own stick can only look around when they don't have the puck. That takes away exactly the moments when looking is worth most. So head-up puck control is the way in to everything in this episode. It's why "keep your head up" is the most repeated instruction in hockey.

PLAYER: How do I get there?

COACH: Keep the puck where you can see it out of the corner of your eye. Be aware of it the way you're aware of your own hands. Keep your hands away from your body, so the puck is out in front of you. Tucked in at your feet, you can't see it without looking down. And hold the stick with a soft grip. Tense hands can't feel the puck, so you look down to check on it.

COACH: Train it with a drill that fails if you look down, not with willpower. Stickhandle while a partner holds up fingers or coloured cards for you to call out. If you look down, you fail the drill. Ten to fifteen minutes a day off the ice, with a ball or a puck, moves this along faster than almost any other off-ice work.

COACH: Seventeenth idea, and the last. How experience turns into instinct, and how to speed it up. Experienced players don't have faster eyes. They have fewer things to think about.

PLAYER: What does that mean?

COACH: A new player sees ten people in ten places, and has to work out what each one means. An experienced player sees one picture they recognise, with an answer already attached. Say the puck is about to go back up to the blue line, and nobody is marking the net front. They don't work that out. They know it, the way you know a friend's face in a crowd without checking each feature. That's why instinct feels instant. Nothing is worked out. Something is recognised.

PLAYER: So it just takes years.

COACH: It takes repetitions. But you can get far more out of the ones you already have. Name patterns out loud after they happen. On the bench, say it. That was an overload, three of them on the puck side, and their player on the far side of the blue line was wide open. If you don't name it, most of that experience just evaporates.

COACH: Predict out loud, then check. Watching a game, say what happens next. They'll reverse it. They're going to walk the line and shoot for a tip. A wrong prediction you notice is worth more than ten right ones you never made. And you can do this from the stands.

COACH: Work on one pattern a week. This week, I only watch what their far-side defenceman does on the breakout. Trying to notice everything means noticing nothing. And get more decisions per minute. A three-on-three game in one third of the ice gives you many more reads than a full-ice drill. Decisions build recognition, not ice time.

PLAYER: What about in practice?

COACH: On the ice, use drills you fail if you don't look. A shoulder-check drill, where the coach changes something behind you before the pass. Number or colour calls while you handle the puck. Small games with rules, like calling your target's name before you get the pass. And talk in every drill. A call is a look you didn't have to take yourself.

COACH: With video, watch your own shifts and really count your head turns. That turns "I should look more" into a number you can move. Pause a second before you get a pass, and name your three options. Then play it, and see what you did. The gap is what you're missing. And count the head turns of a teammate whose vision you rate.

COACH: At a live game, watch one player for a whole shift, away from the puck. Live is best, because the TV camera follows the puck and cuts everyone else out. Watch a defenceman without the puck too. Most of the interesting reading happens where the camera isn't pointing.

PLAYER: What about those reaction-light trainers?

COACH: Off-ice reaction tools like light trainers train your eyes, not your hockey. Use them as an extra. Never use them instead of playing, and being watched by someone who knows what to look for.

COACH: Let me pull it all together.

COACH: Take at least one look while the puck travels to you, and have your eyes back on it when it lands. Decide while it travels, with a first option and a bail-out, and let the last look overrule you. Don't panic pass. Ask yourself after each shift whether you had more time than you thought. Outside your own end, when you don't know, keep it, side-on to the checker. In your own end, one look, one decision, move it. And anywhere on the ice, never your back to anyone coming at you.

COACH: The puck is your anchor. Then pressure, outlets, danger and space. When you're defending, look for bodies, and keep asking who scores if the puck goes to the net right now.

COACH: Make every look a real head turn, both shoulders, without losing a stride. Use the far side, the moment after your pass, and every whistle. On the boards, the same every time. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to anyone coming at you, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly.

COACH: Read the carrier's chest and hips, the passer's shoulder and blade, and the shooter's hands and weight. Read the forecheck's shape. Play where the puck will be next. Arrive at the net moving but under control, head up, and never into the goalie. Anticipate, don't guess. And get your head up off the puck, because everything else depends on it.

PLAYER: What should I ask my own coach?

COACH: Three things. Do we defend our end with a zone collapse, man-to-man, or a hybrid? When do you want me jumping passing lanes, and when not? And who on the bench wants to hear what their forecheck is, after my first shift? Ask, because much of this depends on how your team plays. And next time a pass is coming to you, turn your head before it gets there.
