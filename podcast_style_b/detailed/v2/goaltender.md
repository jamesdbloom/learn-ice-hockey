In some games where checking is allowed, you can be hit for a puck you're holding. So arrive with your head up, expecting contact. Leagues with no trapezoid control the freeze instead. Covering the puck behind your goal line, with your body out of your crease, is a penalty in all of them.

COACH: Whether your goalie plays the puck at all is also a coaching choice. Some teams want the goalie handling everything they legally can. Others want the goalie to touch nothing and stay in the net. Both are reasonable. Ask, and then be consistent.

COACH: Twelfth idea. Moving the puck, in order of risk. You've stopped a rim, a forechecker is coming, and you have a few options. Which is safest?

PLAYER: Leaving it for my defenceman?

COACH: If they have time, yes. That's the lowest risk, and it starts a controlled breakout, your team carrying the puck out of its own end. Second, a short pass to a defenceman on the boards on the puck side. If it's picked off, it's picked off below the goal line, where it matters less. Third, a rim round the boards to your far winger. That gives up the puck but takes the pressure off. Keep the backswing short. In some leagues a big backswing turns that rim into a slap shot, which is a penalty there. If it hurts someone, it can end your game and sit you out the next one too. Fourth, off the glass and out. It's rarely wrong when you're in trouble, and goalies are allowed to be boring.

PLAYER: And up the middle?

COACH: Never. If you give it away there, it's a shot at an empty net, and you're the one standing in it. But an option that's plainly open still beats a safer one that's covered.

COACH: When you clear it, put it along the glass, never over it. In most leagues a clear that goes straight out of the rink is a penalty, whether you meant it or not. If the puck isn't settled on your blade, send it down the boards instead of lifting it.

PLAYER: What if I make a save and it flies over the glass?

COACH: That's different. It's not a clear, so don't start flinching at pucks you should stop. But once the whistle has gone, put the puck down. Firing it out of the rink after the whistle is a penalty, and in some leagues it climbs until you're thrown out of the game. Then your team needs another goalie. And move the puck with your stick. Don't pick it up and throw it with your glove. Throwing it can cost you a penalty, or stop play.

COACH: Thirteenth idea. The discipline of leaving it alone. The best puck-handling decision a goalie makes is usually to leave the puck alone. Picture a loose puck halfway to the corner, and a forechecker racing for it. You might get there first. Might.

PLAYER: That's a fifty-fifty.

COACH: Then don't leave your net for it. Lose the race and the net is empty, with you behind the play. Don't handle the puck just because you can, either. Every touch is a chance for a turnover, and playing the puck takes you out of your net. And if you do commit, commit. The worst outcome is starting to play it, changing your mind, and leaving you and your defenceman both out of position. Call I've got it, and mean it.

PLAYER: And if I do win the race to the boards?

COACH: You arrive facing the end boards with a forechecker coming from behind. So arrive with your head up and your chin off your chest. Never duck. If you do go into the boards or a post, make sure it's anything but your head first. That protects your head and neck. Do it even going slowly, because a head-first hit into the boards or a post can hurt your neck at walking speed.

PLAYER: Is there a proper position for a goalie taking a hit there?

COACH: What I just gave you is written for every player on the ice. This episode can't give you a full position for a goalie in pads taking a hit at the boards. The one taught to skaters may not carry over, so ask your goalie coach. Better still, don't race for a puck you might not win.

COACH: Fourteenth idea. Icing, and the puck dumped in at you. Icing is when a team shoots the puck from its own half all the way past your goal line, with nobody touching it. When the other team ices it at you, decide before it arrives whether you want the whistle.

PLAYER: Don't I always want it?

COACH: Usually you do. The faceoff goes back to their end. And in many leagues the team that iced it can't change players, so their tired five are stuck out there. In many leagues, you can wave an icing off without touching the puck. Just skating toward it, faking at it, or reaching for it with your stick is enough. Your defencemen can't do that. Only you can.

PLAYER: So if I want the whistle, I stay put.

COACH: Stand still. Do nothing, on purpose. Drifting toward the corner just in case throws the whistle away, and hands them a live puck in your end. In other leagues only touching the puck counts. There, drifting out gets you nothing, and you've left your net for no return.

COACH: Wave it off on purpose only when the counterattack is worth more than the whistle. That means your defenceman is arriving with time, and their forwards have already turned for the bench. Two strides toward the puck is the whole action, but decide before it arrives. Then talk either way. Leave it, or I've got it. Your defenceman is skating back with their head down and has no idea which you chose. If nobody says anything, you get two players and one puck.

PLAYER: Let me try behind the net. Stop the rim dead and call leave it early. Know how far back my league lets me play. Never pass up the middle, and clear along the glass, not over. Stay in my net for a fifty-fifty puck, and if I do race to the boards, expect contact, head up, chin off my chest, never duck, and never head first into the boards. On a puck iced at me, decide first, then say which.

COACH: Spot on. Now the rush, and the special situations.

COACH: Fifteenth idea. The breakaway. That's one attacker alone against you. You and a lot of time to think, which is the real difficulty. What would you do? Stand at the top of your crease, or charge out?

PLAYER: Stand there and wait for them?

COACH: Standing still at the top of the crease lets them pick a corner. Charging out lets them go round you. So meet them well out, then back up on C-cuts, matching their speed, so the angle stays closed the whole way. A C-cut is a curved push with one skate that moves you backwards while your chest keeps facing the play.

PLAYER: How fast do I back up?

COACH: Keep a little backward motion, called flow, so you can react either way. Flat-footed, you can react to nothing. Too much flow, though, and you back into your own net. Stay on your feet as long as you can. The shooter's whole plan is to make you move first. If you move first, they go where you aren't. The last one to move wins.

PLAYER: Do I watch the puck or the player?

COACH: That's a real coaching split. One school says watch the puck, not the body. Everything above the puck is acting, and a body fake only exists to move you. The other school says the puck is exactly what the shooter fakes with. So it reads the part of them that can't lie, their chest and hips. Ask your goalie coach which they teach, and stick to one. Switching in the middle of a play is how you end up reading neither.

COACH: Then take away what they show you. If they have the puck out in front and are winding up, expect the shot and stay big. If they pull it to their forehand with their head up, expect a deke and stay patient. The poke check, jabbing your stick out at the puck, works when they let it get too far in front of them. It's a commitment. Miss, and you have no reach and no balance left.

COACH: And never knock your own mask off to stop the play. That's a penalty in every league. Against a breakaway it's worse, because the other team can be given the goal or a penalty shot. If your mask comes off by itself, play stops for it anyway, though not always at once, so there's nothing to fish for.

COACH: Sixteenth idea. Penalty shots and shootouts. A penalty shot is one attacker against you, starting from centre ice, with no defenders. Stay in your crease until the shooter touches the puck. Leave early, and the shot is taken again. Keep doing it and the price climbs until you're thrown out, and in at least one league the goal is simply given.

PLAYER: Is there a time limit on the shooter?

COACH: No. There's no clock on a penalty shot. Any number of seconds you hear is a myth. So be patient, and let them run out of ideas. Play the shot once. A rebound can't score, except one that goes off the post or the crossbar, then off you, and in. A shootout is a run of these, one shooter after another. Patience is the whole thing. Each shooter's move is built to make you go first, so make them decide before you do.

COACH: Seventeenth idea. Two-on-ones and three-on-twos. A two-on-one is two attackers against one of your defencemen. Picture it coming at you now. Who takes what?

PLAYER: I take the shooter?

COACH: That's the usual arrangement. Your defenceman takes away the pass and you take the shooter. Your defenceman stays in the passing lane, with their stick flat on the ice. You play the puck carrier as if nobody else were there. Square up, come out to your normal depth, and make the save.

PLAYER: Why that way round?

COACH: Because the pass across to an open forward is the chance you can't stop. The shot is the one you can. But that's a convention, not a law. Some teams play a different system on purpose. The defenceman times a slide flat across the passing lane, to take the pass away and force a shot. Where that's your team's plan, the slide is right, and your job doesn't change. You still have the shooter, square and set.

COACH: What must not happen is the defenceman sliding early when your team plays the passing lane. That opens both options at once. Worse than either is the two of you arriving with different answers. So get it said out loud. Your defenceman says I've got the pass, or you say shot. And ask your coach and your defencemen what your team does, before the game.

PLAYER: And a three-on-two?

COACH: That's three attackers against two defenders. From your side it's the same read, with a wider back door. Don't over-challenge. Stay a little deeper than usual, so you can still get across. Moving sideways matters more than the last foot of angle. And call odd man. An odd-man rush is any attack where the attackers outnumber the defenders who got back. The call tells everyone to stop watching the puck and start counting. How your two defencemen split the three is your coach's choice. The common way is each defenceman taking an outside attacker, with the middle one left to whoever is skating back. The versions go wrong in different ways, so find out which your team plays.

COACH: Eighteenth idea. Faceoffs in your own end. A faceoff in your end is the one moment the play comes to you on a schedule, so use it. Get set before the puck drops, on your angle to the dot, square and at your normal depth. A draw lost straight back to a shooter can be a shot before you've moved.

PLAYER: Anything to say before the drop?

COACH: Talk before the drop, not after it. Check the front of your net is covered and every attacker has someone, and say so if not. If the draw is lost cleanly, expect a quick shot first and a tip second. If it's won to the boards, be ready to stop a rim behind your net.

COACH: Nineteenth idea. The power play and the penalty kill. When your team has the extra player, the power play, you see very few shots. Then now and again comes a breakaway or a two-on-one the other way, against tired defencemen.

PLAYER: So the danger is going cold.

COACH: Being cold and thinking about something else when it comes. So follow the puck at the far end, move your feet at every stoppage, and talk more, not less. Your players at the blue line have their backs to the play coming at you, and you have the clearest view of it.

COACH: On the penalty kill, it's the opposite. Expect traffic, shots you can't see, and passing side to side to make you move. So put seeing the puck first. Whether that means playing deeper is a coaching split, as it is for any screened shot, so follow your goalie coach.

PLAYER: Do I need to know how we kill?

COACH: Know which shape your team kills in, and which version of it. The shape tells you which lanes your team is giving away, and so where the shots will come from. The usual shapes are a box, a diamond, and a wedge plus one. A team may use a diamond against a power play with a player in the middle of the slot, because a box leaves that player unmarked. Ask which your team runs.

COACH: A freeze on the kill gets tired players a change and makes the power play set up again from a faceoff. It takes no time off the penalty, because the clock stops too. And it's still only a freeze you were entitled to make. Being on the kill is no excuse for making a stoppage you didn't need.

COACH: Twentieth idea. Empty nets, and the delayed penalty. Your coach pulls you for an extra attacker. What do you do?

PLAYER: Get off quick.

COACH: Go hard, and go the moment you're called. A slow change risks a penalty for too many players, or a goal with you halfway up the ice. Take your stick with you, and leave nothing near the net. A stick, a piece of equipment or piled snow left there can hand the other team an awarded goal or a penalty shot. In at least one league that counts even if you were knocked out of your stick.

PLAYER: And on a delayed penalty?

COACH: On a delayed penalty, the referee's arm goes up against the other team, and play stops as soon as they get the puck. So your bench may want you off for an extra skater. Whether you go is the bench's call, not yours, and plenty of recreational goalies stay in. The trip is longer than it looks, and an extra skater is only worth having if someone is ready to come on. Find out what your team does before it happens.

COACH: If you go, wait until your team has the puck, take the shortest route, and keep your eyes on the play. While you're off, the goal to fear is your own team's pass sliding back into your empty net. So agree beforehand that nothing goes back toward it. When play stops, get back in fast, and rebuild your picture of posts, paint and depth before the faceoff.

PLAYER: What about when they pull theirs?

COACH: When their net is empty and they have the extra attacker, play a little deeper, and expect passes to the back door rather than clean shots. Freeze every puck you're entitled to freeze, and only those. This is when you'll want a whistle most, and wanting one is exactly what the freeze rules are written against.

COACH: And if their net is empty and you get the chance, you may score. Shoot it with your stick, never throw it. And keep your skates and the puck on your own side of the centre red line. Playing the puck beyond it is a penalty in every league, and leagues measure it differently. Keeping both on your side is safe everywhere.

COACH: Let me pull the situations together. On a breakaway, meet them well out, back up at their speed, and be the last one to move. On a penalty shot, stay in your crease until they touch the puck. On a two-on-one, you usually have the shooter, and you say so out loud. Be set before a faceoff in your end. On the kill, see the puck, and freeze only what you're entitled to. And when you're pulled, go fast, and take your stick.

COACH: Now the last part. Your gear, your head, and what you need from your teammates.

COACH: Twenty-first idea. Your gear. Say your club offers to lend you a full set of goalie gear. Do you take it?

PLAYER: Gladly. It's expensive.

COACH: Borrowing is a fine way in. But borrow the pads, the blocker, the catching glove and the chest protector, and buy the mask.

PLAYER: Even if the mask has an in-date sticker?

COACH: Even then. A certification sticker is a clock, not a report. An in-date one tells you nothing about how many pucks that mask has stopped, or the state of the padding inside. A mask nobody can account for is not one to put your head in. Check the sticker on any mask you're handed. In some leagues an out-of-date one counts as no certification at all. And if there's no sticker, ask your league what it accepts.

COACH: Wear a neck guard, a neck laceration protector, at every age. A throat flap hanging from your mask is an addition, not a substitute. In Britain it's required at every age, adults included. Some North American rules let adults go without one. Wear one anyway.

COACH: Replace gear you've outgrown. Gear that hurts you teaches you to flinch, and a flinch can't be coached out. Fit and comfort beat brand and looks.

PLAYER: What if my stick breaks mid-play?

COACH: Drop it and call for a teammate's. That's safe in every league. Some leagues let a goalie play on with a broken one, and in others playing on is a penalty. Never go to the bench for a new one, during play or at a whistle. A teammate hands you one on the ice. Take it from their hand. In some leagues, picking up a stick that was slid to you is a penalty. And a skater's stick is legal in your hands until yours arrives.

COACH: At a whistle, stay in your net. If you really need the bench, for a repair or an injury, ask the referee first. Going without asking can cost your team a penalty, or get you replaced on the spot. And in many leagues, asking doesn't make it free. Your backup goes in, or your team takes a penalty or uses its timeout.

COACH: Twenty-second idea. A shot off your mask. Picture a hard shot catching you square on the cage. Your ears are ringing, but play goes on. What would you do?

PLAYER: Shake it off and keep going, probably.

COACH: Say something at the next stoppage. Tell the nearest official, your bench and your captain. It has to be you. Your face is behind a cage, and the rest of you looks fine. Nobody else can see that you're hurt. And don't wait for a whistle, because one may never come. In most leagues nothing stops play just for a shot off the mask. Play stops for a hurt goalie only once an official sees that you're hurt, which a cage hides.

PLAYER: But I feel fine.

COACH: A concussion is an injury to the brain. It doesn't need you to be knocked out, or a body check. And it affects your judgement, so this isn't a call to make on how you feel. If a concussion is suspected, come off, and don't play again that day. A qualified medical professional who has assessed you decides when you play again, not you. Afterwards, someone should stay with you. Don't drive, and don't go home alone.

COACH: Some signs after a knock to the head mean an ambulance straight away, and any one of them is enough on its own. Any neck pain is one. So are a seizure, a severe or worsening headache, and repeated vomiting, and there are others. And any symptom that's getting worse, like a headache or confusion, means an ambulance too. The Conditioning and Recovery episode lists them all.

COACH: After any knock to the head, treat it as a possible neck injury too. If a player is still down, phone for help first. Don't help them up and don't move them, unless you have to, to keep their airway clear so they can breathe. Leave the helmet on.

COACH: Think of it this way. A goalie who comes off and turns out fine has cost the team a goalie change. A goalie who stays on and isn't fine risks a second knock on an injured brain.

COACH: Twenty-third idea. Warm up, and look after your hips. Get a sweat on before you face live shots. Every butterfly drop, slide and post seal works the far end of your hip's range, over and over. One goalie coaching manual warns that going in cold invites groin and hip pulls. That's its judgement, not a measured rate. Warm up by moving, not by holding stretches.

PLAYER: My groin's always a bit sore. Isn't that just part of it?

COACH: Groin and hip pain gets treated as normal in this position. Don't treat it that way. Pain that keeps going or keeps coming back needs a physiotherapist or a sports physician, and when you play again is their call.

COACH: Twenty-fourth idea. After a goal goes in. Every goalie lets in goals, and some will be bad. The skill is not avoiding that. It's what you do in the next ninety seconds.

PLAYER: Usually I replay it in my head.

COACH: Save that for tomorrow. Have a reset routine and do it the same way every time. Skate a small circle, get a drink, scrape your crease, tap both posts. Doing the same thing every time tells your body that the last shift is over. The question of what you could have done takes attention you need for the next play. Forget the goal for the rest of the game, and remember the read that caused it in the week after.

COACH: And never point at a teammate. Not with your hands, your body or a look. It's the fastest way to lose a dressing room.

PLAYER: How do I know if my confidence is going?

COACH: Know the signs, because they look like other problems. Suddenly blaming your equipment, or your defence. Hiding deep or running at shooters. Going quiet and sorry for yourself, or acting cocky. Guessing, and dropping before the shot. Treat those as signs about your head, not as the real problem. They often show up plainly in warm-ups. And remember why you're there. Goalies need to enjoy making saves. If stopping the puck is the part you love, the goals you let in are just part of the price.

COACH: Twenty-fifth idea, and the last. What to tell your skaters. Many goals at the front of your net are not goalie mistakes at all. They come from skater habits. That's a coaching judgement, not a count. So here's what to ask of them.

PLAYER: Go on. I've got a list.

COACH: Shoot at the net, never at my head, in warm-ups and drills. And don't fire pucks at me from ten feet. A shot off the mask may never bring a whistle. Don't screen me. Standing in front of the net with your back to the shooter, in my line of sight, causes goals.

COACH: And don't back into my crease when you're being pushed. Hold your ground early instead, without pushing back. You land backwards on a goalie who is usually down, square to the shooter, and can't see you coming. A goalie in that spot can't protect themselves, so it's a collision as well as a screen. Don't block a shot from in front of me. A puck you half-block there is one I never saw. Get out of my line instead.

PLAYER: So how should they win the front of the net?

COACH: By position, never by moving the screener. Get your inside shoulder ahead of theirs and hold it. No push, no lean, and never a stick across them. Never walk them off their spot, which can be called interference in any game. As the puck arrives, you can lift their stick, then let go. Go low on the shaft, well below their hands, and lift up, not sideways. Never chop at the hands. And never a two-handed shove, which is a cross-check whatever your blade is doing.

COACH: On a rebound, box out. Take the inside position between that player and the puck, and hold it, beside them, not on them. No lean, and never use it to hit them.

COACH: Where your game allows checking, a battle or a legal check can move a body at the front of the net. Never let it cross the front of the net. The corner side is the safe one. That path across has a steel post at one edge and your own goalie at the other, and the player being moved can see neither. In all of this, at the front of the net, these limits hold. No shove, no lean, and never a stick across anyone. Keep that player's skates on the ice. Never lift anyone off their feet, because then they can't brace or choose what hits the post. Never into anyone's back. If you can see their numbers, pull up. If you hit a player from behind so they go head first into the post, plan on being thrown out of the game, whatever league you play in. A crash there can hurt even going slowly. And if you're the one being moved, head up, chin off your chest.

COACH: If my stick breaks, bring me yours and put it in my hand. Never throw it, shoot it or slide it. Let me play the puck when I call for it. I've got it means go to your breakout position, not to the puck. And answer my calls. One word back is enough.

COACH: Let me pull it all together.

COACH: Stand on the line from the puck to the middle of your goal, and be out, square and set when the shot leaves. Decide your depth before the shot. Watch the puck all the way in, and send rebounds to the corners, never the slot. Stay on your feet until going down is the answer.

COACH: In front of your net, talk early, short and every time, and only say time when you're sure. Go to the post on a read. Through a screen, pick one side. Freeze because an attacker is on you in your crease, never because you want a whistle. Keep your stick and your body to yourself, and in a fight, stay in your net.

COACH: Behind your net, stop the rim and call leave it early. Know how far back your league lets you play, never pass up the middle, and clear along the glass, not over it.

COACH: On the rush, be the last one to move, and agree with your defenceman who has the pass.

COACH: And look after yourself. Buy your own mask, wear a neck guard at every age, and warm up before live shots. After a hard shot off the mask, say something.

PLAYER: What should I ask my own coach?

COACH: Three things. Do you want me deeper or closer against a screened shot? When do you want me sealing the post? And on a two-on-one, does our defenceman take the pass, or slide? Then one for your league. Is there a trapezoid behind the net, and how far back may I play the puck? Ask, because much of this is your team's choice or your league's rule. Their answers tell you how your team wants you to play, and now you know the reason behind each one.
