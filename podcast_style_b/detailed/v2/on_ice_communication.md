PLAYER: Like your partner in that corner.

COACH: Exactly that. The defenceman going back has three plays to choose from, and has to choose before getting there. Without information, they guess. The defence partner across the net can see the side the player going back can't. Neither of them has to decide what to do with the puck. A voice joins the player who sees to the player who decides.

PLAYER: So is it the captain's job to do most of the talking?

COACH: No. The duty to speak belongs to whoever can see. Not the captain, not the oldest player, not the loudest. A rookie shouting at a veteran is doing exactly the right thing. A team where that feels wrong will give up goals for it.

COACH: Fewer blind turnovers. Its players arrive at loose pucks with a decision already made. And it covers its own end without anyone stopping to work out whose job it is. Slow down half a step and use them. "I'm high" means I'm the option up near the blue line. When the low options are covered, that's usually your safe reset.

PLAYER: And "far side"?

COACH: Your help is across the ice, not near you. That pass exists, but weigh it. Say you're in your own end and you hear far side. What would you do?

PLAYER: It's across the ice, so it goes past our net.

COACH: Right. In your own end, a pass across the ice goes across the front of your own net, and an interception there is a shot from right in front. Don't take it automatically. "Outlet" means I'm your way out of the zone, usually a winger on the boards or a centre swinging low. Move the puck and start the breakout.

PLAYER: And "stretch"?

COACH: A teammate is long, behind their forwards. Put it up to them. But check the blue line first. A teammate who's already across their blue line with both skates when the puck crosses is offside. One skate on the line keeps them onside. If you're the one waiting at the line, keep that skate touching the ice. In some leagues a skate lifted over the line doesn't count.

COACH: One common mess to finish the middle. Two players calling for the same puck, both arriving and neither taking it. The fix is a standing rule on who has priority. Usually the player facing up the ice, or whoever called first.

COACH: Tenth idea. The rush, when they come at you up the ice. This is where talking breaks down most. Everyone's skating flat out, and nobody wants to be the one who called it wrong. "Odd man" means they have more attackers than you have defenders. When you hear it, stop thinking about the puck and start thinking about numbers. Published call lists use just the number. "Two", or "one". Short wins at speed.

PLAYER: Say it's a two-on-one, and I'm the one defenceman back.

COACH: Two attackers against you and your goalie. What's your job?

PLAYER: Get to the puck carrier?

COACH: The usual way splits the job. You take the pass, and the goalie takes the shooter. Get in the passing lane, stick flat on the ice, with no slide until the pass is released. Each of you owns one half of the threat. But it only works if both of you say out loud which half is yours. Some defencemen say "I've got the pass", and the goalie answers "take the shooter". The goalie's own version is one word. "Shot".

PLAYER: Is that the only way to play it?

COACH: It's one of two real systems. Some teams coach a timed slide across the lane instead, to force the shot. So find out which your team plays before you need it.

COACH: Now the trap with that word shot. When a goalie shouts shot, they're not warning you a shot is coming. They're telling you they have the shooter, and you take the pass. A warning tells a defenceman nothing they can act on. "I have the shooter" tells them where to put their stick. So stay in the passing lane and don't drift toward the puck. If your team has been using shot as a warning, pick another word for the warning. You have half a second to decode it.

COACH: "Backcheck" means we've lost the puck, get back. A backcheck is racing back to defend. When you hear it, turn and sprint first, and look second.

PLAYER: Why not look first?

COACH: Watching the puck while you drift backwards is how the middle lane opens up. And on the way back, you're there for the puck and the open player, not a hit. Whether you can hit at all depends on your league, your age group and the game, so find out first. Never hit a player who can't see you coming, and never from behind. If you can see their numbers, pull up.

COACH: "Pick them up", or "take the middle", means there's an attacker nobody has, and you're the closest. Get on the defensive side of them, between them and your net, not behind their shoulder. Who takes which lane on the backcheck is a coaching choice. Most often the centre takes the middle and the wingers take the outside lanes. Some teams send the first player back to the middle, whatever their position. Others pick up man-on-man, starting from the puck and working outward. Find out which your team plays. Whichever it is, somebody owns the middle, and nobody assumes it's somebody else.

PLAYER: How do the defencemen know who's got who?

COACH: Backcheckers call their own pickups. "I've got the middle", or "I've got seventeen". The players skating back are the only ones who know who they have. Once they say it, your defencemen can commit to the puck instead of hedging.

COACH: Eleventh idea. Line changes. Some games, especially for younger players, change lines in a set way, and then your coach and the officials tell you how. In games where your line chooses when to change, changing while play goes on is run almost entirely by voice. For a moment your team is out of shape, so the timing matters.

PLAYER: So what are the words?

COACH: "Change" means the line is going off now. Go as a group. Never change during a scramble in your own end, or when you're the player caught deepest. "Stay" means don't change now, it's too dangerous. Finish the shift. You're exhausted and you hear stay. What do you do?

PLAYER: Stay, I suppose. Even with nothing left.

COACH: Stay. A tired player in position beats a fresh player arriving late. On the bench, "I've got first player off" claims the seat of the first teammate who comes off, so nobody else jumps for it. "Next shift" means you're up after this one. Get to the door, get your head into the game, and know who you're replacing.

PLAYER: Could we just say "last man" when the change is done?

COACH: Don't use last change or last man for any of this. Both already mean something else, and they'll get you the wrong reaction. Last change is the home team's right to put its players on the ice after the visitors. Last man means the deepest defender, the one player who must not leave. That's the opposite of "the change is done, go". Pick a word neither of them owns.

COACH: And the penalty for a bad change isn't about seats. It's about distance. The player coming off has to be right at the bench and out of the play before the new one joins in. The bench has one more job. It warns about what the players on the ice can't see. A player coming off their bench, a matchup, a shift that's gone on too long.

COACH: Give me the middle of the ice back.

PLAYER: Say here, and be there. Weigh middle and far side by where I am, and be certain in my own end. Check the blue line before a stretch pass. On the rush, odd man means think about numbers. On a two-on-one, the defenceman takes the pass and the goalie takes the shooter. A goalie's shot means exactly that.

COACH: And the backcheck?

PLAYER: Sprint first, look second. I'm there for the puck and the open player, not a hit. Never hit anyone who can't see me coming, and never from behind. Whether I can hit at all depends on my league, so I find out first, and if I see their numbers, I pull up. Somebody always owns the middle. And change as a group, never in a scramble.

COACH: Twelfth idea. Their end. All alone, point, and the forecheck. All alone first, because you hear it as you arrive. You're carrying the puck into their zone and someone shouts "all alone". What's it telling you?

PLAYER: That nobody's with me.

COACH: That's the usual meaning, no support. One published call list says it also means you're outnumbered. Some teams use it for the support half only. Agree which, because they lead to different decisions. No support says wait. Outnumbered says don't go in at all. Either way, when you hear it, don't attack their defence on your own. Slow down, protect the puck, or chip it in and wait for help.

COACH: "Point" means the defenceman at the point, just inside the blue line, has space for a shot. Get the puck up to them. Then get to the front of the net for the tip or the rebound.

PLAYER: Crash the net.

COACH: Get there under control, and set up beside the goalie, never into them. Keep your spot. Don't push back or lean on anyone, and never your stick across someone. Any contact with a goalie can be a penalty, and it can get you thrown out of the game. A goalie who is down or turned can't protect themselves, so contact can hurt them. It can also cost your team the goal. And keep your head up, chin off your chest. The goal post can hurt you the same way the boards can, even going slowly.

COACH: Now the forecheck. F one, F two and F three aren't positions. They're jobs, set by who gets to the puck first, and they change hands during a shift. So nobody knows which one they have until somebody says it.

PLAYER: So I just shout which one I am?

COACH: "I've got F one" claims the first-in job. "I'm high" claims the job of staying above the puck. It takes half a second, and it stops the most expensive forechecking mistake there is. Two players doing the same job, while nobody does the other one.

PLAYER: And if I'm the one going in first?

COACH: That's a chase. You're going for the puck, not the player. Whether you can hit at all depends on your league, your age group and the game, so find out first. Never hit from behind, and if you can see their numbers, pull up. And if the puck is on the boards, set your body for the boards. The boards position. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to anyone coming at you, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly.

COACH: Thirteenth idea. Warnings you can hear anywhere on the ice. "Feet", or "under", means the puck is in your skates and you've lost sight of it. When you hear it, look down, knock it to your stick with your skate, the skate at the puck and never at the player, and reset. It's one of the most useful calls there is, because a puck in your feet is invisible to you and obvious to everyone else.

PLAYER: Anything to watch with the skate?

COACH: Send the skate at the puck. Never at the player beside it, and never at the blade of your own stick. A puck in your feet usually has an opponent's feet next to it, so this is where a kick at a player happens. If you kick at an opponent, or even try to, you can be thrown out of the game in every league. In most, it's a match penalty, the kind that throws you out, whether or not anyone is hurt.

PLAYER: And just pushing with the skate?

COACH: Never use your skate to push a player, even without a kick. In some leagues that gets you thrown out too. Kicking your own stick blade to drive the puck is no shortcut either. Depending on the league, it stops play, it's a penalty, or the goal doesn't count.

PLAYER: Hang on. Feet tells me to look down. Man on tells me to keep my head up.

COACH: Exactly. They're opposite instructions, so be sure which one you heard.

COACH: "Heads up" means something is coming that you haven't seen. It's less precise than man on, but faster to shout. When you hear it, get your head up and brace. At the boards, take the boards position, never your back to anyone coming at you, never duck. Expect contact.

PLAYER: And just "heads"?

COACH: Heads, without the up, is a pure safety call. A puck or a stick is flying through the air toward someone. Turn away from it and cover up. Never use heads to mean anything tactical. And it's never an instruction to duck into contact near the boards. Ducking a flying puck in open ice makes sense. Ducking a checker at the boards is how spinal injuries happen.

COACH: "Behind you" means an opponent or the puck is behind you. That's its weakness, because you can't tell which. Some teams keep behind you for the puck, and use man on for opponents. If yours hasn't split it, check over your shoulder straight away and be ready for either.

COACH: Fourteenth idea. Faceoffs. Before a faceoff, nobody is moving and everyone can hear. So this is the easiest talking in hockey, and the most often skipped. Before you line up, the centre tells the other four skaters what the play is.

PLAYER: A code word?

COACH: An actual sentence, said while the linesperson is still skating in. Not a code word shouted at the last second. Coaches who run set plays off the draw agree that they only work when every player knows their job, and this is the moment that gets settled. Keep the menu tiny. Two or three plays that everyone knows cold beat ten that nobody remembers. For example, win it back to the point for a shot, with traffic already in front. Or win it to the winger on the strong side for a quick chance. Or, in your own end, win it to the defenceman on the boards side, who rims it out straight away.

PLAYER: And when we don't win it cleanly?

COACH: Most draws aren't won cleanly, so name a fallback. Agree what happens in a scramble, usually that everyone takes their player and you sort it out. Then a lost draw doesn't become a lost coverage.

COACH: Two things the wingers must be told. First, if a winger creeps inside the faceoff circle before the puck drops, in most leagues it's the centre who gets thrown out of the draw, not the winger. So stay out of the circle, and behind your hash marks, the short lines on the side of the circle, until the puck drops.

PLAYER: And the second?

COACH: Agree in advance which winger steps in when the centre is thrown out. A second violation by your team on the same draw can cost a two-minute penalty, so the replacement has to be clean. Nothing clever. If you step in and tie up the other centre's stick, it's stick on stick only, over or under the blade, for an instant. Never a hand on their stick, and never your stick on their hands. Get low from your knees and hips, not by pushing your head out over the dot.

COACH: Hand signals work well at faceoffs. You can be seen but not heard across a circle, and the other team can hear you. A tap on the boards, a glove pointed at a spot, a stick blade angled at the target. But only signals agreed in the dressing room. Never invent one in the middle of a game.

COACH: Let me pull their end and the warnings together. On all alone, don't attack their defence on your own. On point, move it up, then go to the net under control, beside the goalie, never into them, head up. Contact with a goalie can get you thrown out.

COACH: Forecheckers name their order, and the first one in plays the puck, not the player. Whether you can hit at all depends on your league, so find out first. Never from behind, and if you see their numbers, pull up. On feet, the skate goes at the puck, never at a player and never at your own blade, and never use it to push a player. Heads means turn away from a flying puck, never duck at the boards. And at a faceoff, the centre says the play, and the wingers stay out of the circle.

COACH: Fifteenth idea. Talking without your voice. Rinks are loud, and sometimes a voice won't carry. These signals back up your words. They don't replace them. And some things are better unspoken, because the other team can hear you. Start with your stick. Put the blade flat on the ice, out in front of you, as a target.

PLAYER: That says pass it here.

COACH: And it gives the passer something to aim at. A stick held up in the air says the opposite, that you're not ready. What about tapping your stick on the ice?

PLAYER: That's the classic I'm open.

COACH: It's the old signal, but every tap sounds the same. An opponent can tap and get a blind pass sent straight to them. So use a tap to back up a call, never on its own, and never pass blind on a tap. Make eye contact before the pass, too. A tenth of a second tells you two things at once. They've seen you, and they're ready.

COACH: Look a defender off. If you stare at the teammate you want, the defender covering them looks there too. So look at one option, then pass to another. It works because defenders read heads and eyes as a shortcut. That's why a defenceman facing a rush is taught to read the attacker's chest and hips instead.

PLAYER: Anything else that works without words?

COACH: Point your glove or stick at the space where you want a teammate to go, then put the puck there. It's common on breakouts and on the power play, when your team has an extra player because of their penalty. There the passer has a moment of calm and the receiver is looking up. And read hips. Where your feet and hips point tells a teammate what you're about to do. Hips open to the middle say you're cutting inside. Square to the boards says you're staying wide. Linemates who've played together a long time look like they can read minds. What they're really doing is reading hips.

COACH: Sixteenth idea. Building the habit. Be honest. In a game, how much do you actually talk?

PLAYER: Not much. Shouting feels a bit awkward.

COACH: That's normal. Almost nobody talks on the ice at first. The problem isn't tactics. Being loud in front of people feels uncomfortable. Newer players worry about being wrong, about being loud, about being the only one making noise. It fades with practice. Nobody has measured how common it is or how long it takes, so judge it against your own team.

PLAYER: So how do I get past it?

COACH: Talk in every drill, even the ones where it feels silly. A simple passing drill with nobody in the way is where the habit gets built, because you can talk without having to think about anything else. Coaches who accept silence in drills get silence in games. Treat a missing call the way you'd treat a missed backcheck. Start with man on and time. Get those two automatic before you add anything. And from the very first drill, only say time when you're sure. If you're not sure, say nothing. A false time sends a teammate into a hit they weren't ready for.

COACH: Coaches, make one player responsible for noise in each drill, and rotate the job. The quiet players get pushed through the awkward stage, instead of hiding behind the loud ones. Talk on the bench too. Players who talk on the bench talk on the ice. And afterwards, say out loud which calls worked. "That over saved us a turnover" does more for a team's talking than any general encouragement.

PLAYER: When do we actually agree the words?

COACH: In the dressing room at the start of the season, not on the ice in game three. Twenty minutes and a whiteboard is enough. One word for each meaning. The classic mix-up is one call carrying two meanings, like behind you. Ten to fifteen calls covers almost everything, and a longer list won't survive a real game. Bring the goalie into it, because theirs are the calls the defencemen have to act on instantly, and they're the ones most often left out. Look at the list again about a month in. You'll find two calls nobody uses and one situation nobody has a word for. And if you want a new call, bring it to practice before you shout it in a game.

PLAYER: What about talking to the referee?

COACH: Keep your voice for your teammates, not the referee. Arguing doesn't change the call, and it takes your attention off the play. Disputing a call can be a penalty in every league, and keeping on at the official after that can get you thrown out of the game.

COACH: One honest note. Nobody has counted what gets said on amateur ice, or ranked the mistakes. So when I've called something the most useful call, or the most common mistake, that's what coaches see, not a measurement.

COACH: Let me pull it all together. The puck carrier can't see what you can see. Whoever can see it makes the call, and where two of you can see it, both of you call it.

COACH: Man on and time are the two calls that matter most. Shout man on early. Only say time when you're sure. If you're not sure, say nothing, because a false time sends a teammate into a hit. And at the boards, called or not, the boards position. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to anyone coming at you, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly.

COACH: Call early, short and loud, with a name on it, and answer every call. In your own end, call back door and call the switch. Never send the puck, or skate it, across the front of your own net.

COACH: On a screen, hold your ground, no lean, no push, and lift the stick low as the puck arrives, then let go. Head up, chin off your chest. The post hurts like the boards. Never move them out.

COACH: Going back for the puck, on the backcheck and on the forecheck, play the puck, not the player. Whether you can hit at all depends on your league, so find out first. Never from behind, and if you see their numbers, pull up. On feet, kick the puck, never the player and never your own blade, and never push a player with your skate. And agree your words as a team, one word for one meaning, all season.

PLAYER: What should I ask my own coach?

COACH: Three things. What are our calls? Do we switch on a crossing, or follow our player? And on a two-on-one, do you want me in the lane, or sliding? Then one for your league. Which rules do we play under? Ask, because these are your team's choices, not laws of hockey. And now you know why each call works.
