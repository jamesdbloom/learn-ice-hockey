COACH: Picture this. You're at a game. A goal goes in, and the whole crowd jumps up. Ask anyone near you how it happened, and they'll tell you about the shot. Hardly anyone saw the defenceman who got caught standing still five seconds earlier.

PLAYER: That would be me. I'd tell you about the shot.

COACH: Most people would. This episode is about watching hockey to learn from it. Not just for fun, though you can still enjoy it. You watch so that you learn something, and then you take it onto the ice. I'm the coach, and with me is a player who already plays and wants to get more out of every game they watch. We'll take our time. For each idea you'll hear what to look at, and the reason it's worth looking at. If you know the reason, you'll keep spotting it when the game looks a little different.

PLAYER: So what's wrong with how I watch now?

COACH: Almost everything that decides a game happens away from the puck. But the puck is what everybody watches. So most of what a game could teach you goes straight past you.

COACH: We'll start with four big ideas, because they matter most. If you only hear the first part, take those four with you. After that we go back through and add the detail, one part of the game at a time.

COACH: First idea. Stop watching the puck, and give your eyes one job.

PLAYER: Where do I even start? I watch most games on TV.

COACH: Then start with how TV trains your eyes the wrong way. A TV broadcast is made to entertain you, not to teach you. The main camera keeps the puck near the middle of the screen. So you see the puck carrier, the nearest defender, and maybe two more players. The other six skaters are off the screen.

PLAYER: And the replays?

COACH: The director cuts to replays at the worst moments for learning. Right after a whistle, and right after a shot. Those are the seconds when the most is happening away from the puck. So the camera is built to show you the puck. But the puck is the least useful thing on the ice to watch. The puck shows you what happened. The players away from it show you why it happened.

PLAYER: So I just try harder not to follow it?

COACH: That won't work. Following the puck is a reflex. Your eye goes to the fastest, brightest thing it can see, and in hockey that's the puck. Concentrating harder won't beat a reflex, any more than you can stop yourself glancing at a phone that lights up on the table. What beats it is giving your eyes a different job.

COACH: There are four jobs. Watch one player for a whole shift. A shift is one turn on the ice, from the moment a player steps on to the moment they sit back down. Watch only the two defencemen. Watch the weak side. The strong side is the half of the ice the puck is on, and the weak side is the other half. The names swap the instant the puck crosses the middle. Or, when a goal goes in, watch the five seconds before it, not the goal.

PLAYER: All four at once?

COACH: One job per period, not per game. Each job is narrow on purpose. If you try to watch everything at once, you end up watching the puck again. The aim is to build a habit, one job at a time. Later, that habit runs in the background while you enjoy the game.

COACH: Second idea. Read a team's system from the players furthest from the puck. A system is the plan a team uses to attack or to defend, so that five players move as one. Every system is a coaching choice, not a rule of hockey.

PLAYER: Why the players furthest away? Surely the action's near the puck.

COACH: The players furthest from the puck aren't chasing anything. So where they stand is where their system puts them. They tell you the most about what a team is doing.

COACH: Here's the most reliable read in hockey, and it takes about ninety seconds. Picture the puck going into a corner in a team's own end. Everyone else is staring into the corner. You look at that team's winger instead. Does the winger follow their player down into the corner?

PLAYER: And the answer tells me the system?

COACH: There are three answers, and each one names a system. First answer, the winger stays high. In either end, high means out toward the blue line, and low means in toward the goal line, the line the net sits on. So the winger stays out on the point. The point is the area just inside the blue line, near the boards, where defencemen stand when their team attacks. It's an area, not the line itself. And the winger never follows anyone below the hash marks. Those are the short red lines on the outside of each faceoff circle in the end zones. Below them means between them and the goal line.

PLAYER: So they're guarding space, not a person.

COACH: Exactly. The team is marking areas of ice, not players. That's called a zone system, and the most common kind is the low zone collapse.

PLAYER: How does a collapse work?

COACH: The three players nearest the net sink toward it. That's usually both defencemen and the centre. They defend the front of the net. The wingers guard the points, and the weak-side winger drops into the high slot whenever the centre goes low. The slot is the ice right in front of the net, between the two faceoff circles. Its top part, from the faceoff dots up to the tops of the circles, is the high slot. Nobody is given an opponent to follow. You defend the area you're standing in.

PLAYER: Why would a team play it?

COACH: It's the easiest system to teach. So it's often called the most common system in amateur hockey. That last part is what coaches agree on, not a count. Nobody has found a survey of it.

COACH: One warning about this read. High means out on the point. It doesn't mean down at the faceoff dot. Those two spots are a long way apart, and mixing them up is how people misread the system.

PLAYER: And the second answer?

COACH: The winger follows their player everywhere, into the corner and even behind the net. Then the team plays man-to-man. Each player has a person to cover, not an area. Third answer. The winger follows their player only until that player leaves the winger's area. Then the winger hands them on. That's a mix of the two, called a hybrid. You may also hear it called collapsing man, or man-on, zone-off.

PLAYER: So after one trip into the corner, I know what they play.

COACH: Not after one. Every system you read is a coaching choice. Teams change it by the score, by the period, by the opponent and by who's on the ice. A team that sits back in the middle of the ice with a two-goal lead may press hard in your end when the score is tied. So watch five or six plays before you decide what a team runs. Better still, read a whole period. And expect the answer to change.

COACH: The realistic alternatives to a collapse are man-to-man and a hybrid. So ask your coach which one your own team plays.

PLAYER: What if a winger just looks like they're standing around?

COACH: Then don't grade them yet. Don't grade a player against a system you've guessed. Work out the system first. Then judge whether the player did their part. A winger holding high on the point can look lazy. In a collapse, that's exactly their job.

COACH: Third idea. After a shot, watch the front of the net, not the replay. Picture a shot from the point. The puck hits the goalie's pads and drops in front. On TV, the picture is already cutting to the replay.

PLAYER: And I've missed whatever happened next.

COACH: You've missed the most valuable half-second in the game. It's when the rebound is decided. And it's the moment the broadcast most often cuts away from. So when a shot goes toward the net, keep your eyes on the live play.

PLAYER: What am I looking for in that half-second?

COACH: Where does the attacker in front of the net go? Do they stop just outside the crease, with their stick on the ice? The crease is the goalie's area, the blue paint in front of the net. The red line around its edge counts as part of the crease too. Or do they drift past? Does anyone follow the shot in? A shot from the point with no forward arriving is a free clearance for the defending team. And which team reacts first?

PLAYER: Isn't that just whoever's fastest?

COACH: No. Rebound goals go to whoever moved before the puck landed. Not to the fastest player. To the one who decided first.

PLAYER: So in my own game, I want to be that player.

COACH: Yes. When your team shoots, get to the front of the net. Stop just outside the crease, off its red line as well as out of the blue paint. Keep your stick on the ice and clear of the goalie. Then you're first to the puck when it comes back out.

COACH: Now the part that keeps you safe there. Arrive under control, and pull up short of the goalie. Keep your head up and your chin off your chest. Know where the post is before the shot, so you never meet it head first. A post can hurt you the same way the boards can. That holds even going slowly, and in every league. And take a defender's shove braced, feet wide and knees bent, so it can't walk you into the post.

PLAYER: And the goalie? It gets crowded in there.

COACH: Never go into the goalie. Any contact with a goalie can be a penalty, and it can get you thrown out of the game. A goalie who is turned away, or down, can't protect themselves, so contact can hurt them. If the goalie leans into you, give ground straight away. If the goalie has come out to play the puck, leave them a clear path back to the net. If a defender shoves you into the crease, get out around the goalie, never through them.

PLAYER: What if a defender's trying to move me off my spot?

COACH: Hold your spot with where you stand. Don't lean on anyone or push to keep it. Never put your stick across anyone, and never put your stick or your body into anyone's back.

COACH: Fourth idea. Watch yourself, and take one thing to practice. Watching professionals shows you what good looks like. Watching yourself shows you what you look like. The gap between the two is almost always bigger than you think.

PLAYER: I don't exactly have a camera crew.

COACH: You don't need one. A phone on a tripod at the top of the stands is enough. Frame it wide. The whole ice if you can, or at least one zone plus the middle of the ice.

PLAYER: Wouldn't zooming in on me be better?

COACH: Zooming in ruins it, because then you can't see what you do away from the puck. And watch whole shifts, not your touches.

PLAYER: Honestly, I'd skip to the bits where I had the puck.

COACH: Everyone wants to. Those moments are a much smaller slice than they feel. NHL tracking, which the league itself calls unofficial, puts puck possession at roughly three to four percent of five-on-five ice time. On a forty-second shift, that's one or two seconds. The rest of the shift is where you can improve.

COACH: Ask three questions of every shift, and write the answers down. Where was I when we lost the puck, and where did I go in the next two seconds? Did I ever stop skating? And was I an option? That means a clean passing lane, an open line a pass could travel along to you, your stick on the ice, and moving somewhere a pass could reach.

PLAYER: And once I've answered them?

So here's how you meet the boards. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to anyone coming at you, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly. If you're already facing the boards and can't turn, that's a last resort, not a choice. Get your stick and gloves up and spread on the glass, and keep your head off it. And if someone else gets to the puck first, play the puck, not them. Whether you can hit at all depends on your league, your age group and the game you're in, so find out first. Never hit a player who can't see you coming. If you can see their numbers, pull up, and never push or hit anyone from behind. Even a light hit from behind can cause a serious neck injury.

COACH: That's the core of watching to learn. Everything else adds detail to one of those four. So now let's go back through, starting with the four jobs.

COACH: Fifth idea. The four jobs, one at a time. Job one. Watch one player for a whole shift. Pick a player. Watch only them, from the moment they step on to the moment they sit down. Don't look at the puck at all.

PLAYER: How long is that?

COACH: Measured shifts run roughly thirty to eighty seconds, and a well-managed one is nearer thirty to forty-five. So it's a short job, and you can repeat it many times in one game.

PLAYER: What am I looking for?

COACH: Four things. First, what they do in the two seconds after their team loses the puck. This one moment separates good players from everyone else. Do they stop and watch? Or do they turn and skate somewhere that matters?

COACH: Second, how often their head turns. Looking around is called scanning, or a shoulder check. How often do you think a good player looks?

PLAYER: Every second or two? That's what coaches keep telling me.

COACH: That's the usual coaching advice, a look every one to two seconds when you don't have the puck. The one study that has counted it found far fewer. It looked at professional players in Sweden, in the five seconds before they got the puck. On average they looked about once every three or four seconds. And before about four receptions in ten, they didn't look at all.

PLAYER: So the coaches are wrong?

COACH: To be fair to them, that study only counted the few seconds before a player got the puck. So it isn't measuring quite the same thing. But the part that matters for you is this. What separated players wasn't how much they looked. It was whether they looked first. The players who looked before the puck arrived did better with it, by the study's own measure.

COACH: Third, whether they ever stop moving. Amateurs are often said to glide for about a third of a shift. That's a coaching observation, not a measurement.

PLAYER: So gliding is bad.

COACH: Don't assume that, and don't assume professionals never glide. The one study that measured professional forwards found that gliding on two feet was the thing they did most. It's a balance position between strides and turns, not a rest. So the question isn't whether a player glides. It's whether the glide is taking them somewhere.

COACH: Fourth, how they get off the ice. Do they change on the correct side, at a safe moment?

COACH: Job two. Watch only the two defencemen of one team. Ignore the forwards and ignore the puck.

PLAYER: Why just the defencemen?

COACH: It's the fastest way to learn how a team defends. Defencemen are rarely near the puck. So nearly everything they do is about position, and position is what you're trying to learn.

COACH: Watch their gap. The gap is the distance between a defender and an attacker skating at them. Watch how it closes as the attacker crosses centre ice. And watch which of the two goes to the puck, and which goes to the net, every time a puck goes back into their end.

PLAYER: And when their team's attacking?

COACH: Watch how often they pinch. A pinch is a defenceman stepping down from the other team's blue line, to keep the puck in the attacking zone. That's a different play from stepping up, which is meeting an attacker in the middle of the ice. How often do they pinch, and what has to be true first? And watch where they stand on the power play, when their team has an extra skater, compared with five-on-five.

COACH: Job three. Watch the weak side, the half of the ice the puck isn't on. On TV you'll keep losing it off the screen.

PLAYER: That sounds frustrating.

COACH: It's part of the job. Every time the camera pans away, hold in your head what was happening there. In a team's own end, watch the weak-side winger. Are they high near the point, low near the net, or somewhere between? That one player tells you which coverage the team plays. Watch the weak-side defenceman too. How far off the post are they? And are they facing the puck, or the player they're covering?

COACH: And watch the backdoor, the far post. Many goals are scored there, and nobody watches it until the puck is already there. That's a common coaching view, not a counted share.

COACH: Job four. Watch the five seconds before a goal, not the goal. When a goal goes in, ignore the celebration and wait for the replay. Then ask one question. What happened five seconds earlier that made this possible?

PLAYER: What sort of thing do I usually find?

COACH: It's usually one of a few things. A battle lost on the boards. A clearance that failed. A defenceman caught flat-footed. A forward who didn't get back. A bad line change. Or a screen, a player standing in the goalie's line of sight.

PLAYER: And that teaches me more than the goal itself?

COACH: The finish is the part everyone sees. The cause is the part you can learn from. If you can name the cause instead of the finish, you're watching properly. It works the other way round too. When a shot is saved, ask what the defence did in the five seconds before. What made the shot come from where it did?

COACH: Three things make these jobs stick. Do one job per period, not per game. If you try to watch everything, you keep none of it. Say what you saw out loud, or write one line. Their weak-side winger stayed high all night. That's a finding. A vague impression is gone by the next morning.

PLAYER: And the third?

COACH: Expect it to be boring at first. Positional hockey is mostly quiet. Watching one player without the puck can feel like nothing is happening. But that quiet is exactly what you're there to learn.

PLAYER: Let me try the jobs back. One player for a whole shift, and what they do in the two seconds after a turnover. The two defencemen, and their gap. The weak side, and the far post. And the five seconds before a goal, and its real cause. One job per period, and one line written down.

COACH: That's all four.

COACH: Sixth idea. Count, don't guess, to read the forecheck. The forecheck is the pressure a team puts on the other team in that team's own end, while the other team has the puck.

PLAYER: How do I tell one forecheck from another? They all look like chasing to me.

COACH: Count. The names of the forechecks come from counting. They count how many forwards go below the tops of the faceoff circles. So sit and count, over five or six plays. Each count usually means something, and each has a reason a team would choose it.

COACH: One forward pressures, and two hang back near the blue line. That's usually a one-two-two. It's the careful choice. The team isn't trying to win the puck deep in the corner. It's hunting for a turnover in the middle of the ice instead. Two forwards go deep, the third stays high, and the defencemen hold the blue line. That's a two-one-two, the aggressive default.

COACH: One forward goes deep, and three line up across the middle of the ice. That's a one-three-one. It's often a trap, and we'll come to traps in a moment. Three forwards below the circles is full pressure. You usually see it late in a game, from a team chasing a goal.

PLAYER: Can't I just follow the centre and see what they do?

COACH: That's a trap for your eyes. The forwards in a forecheck are called F one, F two and F three. Those are roles, not positions. They're set by who arrives first. Whoever gets to the puck first is F one, even if that's the left winger. So don't try to read the forecheck by following the centre. Count bodies, and count how deep they go.

COACH: Then a second tell. Watch what F one does on arrival. Picture two teams. On the first, F one skates a straight line at the puck carrier. On the second, F one angles across, and steers the carrier toward the boards. What's the difference?

PLAYER: The first one wants the puck back right now?

COACH: Right. A straight line means the team is hunting turnovers. Angling across means the team is containing. It's waiting for F two to arrive and close the play off.

COACH: Seventh idea. Spot a trap, and check the coverage a second way. A trap is a system for the middle of the ice. The team gives up pressuring in the other team's end. Instead it builds a wall across the middle.

PLAYER: What does that get them?

COACH: The other team then has to dump the puck in and chase it, or lose it trying to carry it through the wall. Here are the signs. A line of three across the middle of the ice, usually near their own blue line or just past centre. That's a one-three-one, with one forward pressuring, three across, and one defenceman deepest. Nobody chases into the corners. A single forechecker gives a little pressure, then drops back. The defenders stop in the middle of the ice rather than skating at anyone. And the puck carrier keeps being steered toward the boards, and never reaches the middle.

PLAYER: It sounds a bit lazy.

COACH: Trapping teams look passive. They're meant to. But the pure one-three-one trap is far rarer now than its reputation. Most teams use parts of it, such as a one-two-two with a heavy middle. So watch what the players do, rather than trying to match a picture.

PLAYER: So a one-two-two could be either thing?

COACH: It can be two different things, and depth separates them. In one, the two middle forwards are up near the other team's blue line. In the other, they've dropped back to the red line. There, the carrier is allowed to skate, and gets steered to a wall. One thing decides which you're watching. Is the lone forechecker closing on the carrier, or gliding?

COACH: Now back to a team's own end, for a second check on its coverage. When the puck is behind their net, watch the defenceman on the puck side. In a collapse, they hold the front of the net. In man-to-man, they chase behind the goal line.

COACH: Eighth idea. Count the players at the point on the power play, and the shape of the penalty kill. The power play is when one team has an extra skater, because the other team has a player in the penalty box. The team a player short is on the penalty kill.

PLAYER: The power play always looks like five players just passing it round.

COACH: There's a shape, and you can count it. Two more words first. A half-wall is the stretch of boards roughly level with the faceoff dot, between the corner and the point. And the bumper is a power-play player in the middle, between the two half-walls, roughly in the high slot.

COACH: Now count the players standing near the blue line, once the power play is set up. One player at the point is usually a one-three-one. One at the point, two on the half-walls, a bumper in the middle, and one in front of the net. It's often called the most common shape today. That's what coaches agree on. Nobody has counted which shape teams actually run.

COACH: Three players up high is an umbrella. One in the middle at the top, and two on the sides a little lower, in an arc, like the top of an open umbrella. Two more are down low. A true umbrella has no bumper in the middle. Four players loaded onto one side of the ice is an overload. They work the puck along one wall.

PLAYER: And the team killing the penalty?

COACH: Count the shape of the four killers. Two high and two low, in a rectangle, is a box. The forwards are high, and the defencemen low. One at the top, two in the middle and one low is a diamond.

PLAYER: Why would a team switch from a box to a diamond?

COACH: Against a one-three-one, a box leaves the bumper unmarked. Then there's the wedge plus one. Three killers in a tight triangle near the net, plus one pressuring the puck. Watch the handover. When the puck moves, the one pressuring stops, and the nearest player from the triangle steps out instead. If one killer chases the puck all the way round the outside, the shape has broken. Teams use that fourth player in different ways, so expect some variation.

PLAYER: Can I use these on my own team?

COACH: You should. Use the same reads on your own team's games. Then check your answer with your coach. If your answer and your coach's don't match, that tells you something too. It tells you how clearly the system has been explained.

PLAYER: Let me pull the systems together. Count to read them. Forwards going deep give me the forecheck. A line of three across the middle, and nobody chasing, is a trap. Players at the point give me the power play, and the shape of four killers gives me the penalty kill. F one, F two and F three are set by who arrives first, not by position.

COACH: And every one of these is a coaching choice. So read five or six plays, expect it to change with the score, and check your own team's with your coach.

COACH: Ninth idea. On a breakout, look away from the puck. A breakout is a team's planned way of moving the puck out of its own end. Picture a defenceman going back for the puck behind their own net. Where do your eyes go?

PLAYER: To the defenceman, probably.

COACH: Look away from them. Find the other four skaters instead. Where's the winger on the puck side? The classic spot is on the boards, between the hash marks and the goal line. That's below the hash marks, not level with them.

PLAYER: What if the winger's up by the blue line?

COACH: They may be playing a different breakout. It could be a flat, spread or stretch one. Or they may never have come down from covering the point. Either way, a pass up there is long, and it arrives at their back. A winger behind the goal line, or in the corner, usually means the breakout has already broken down. The right spot for that winger moves with the team's breakout. So read where they stand as one of the places a winger can be, not as a target they're missing.

COACH: Where's the centre? Usually swinging low and curling up through the middle. A centre standing still means one fewer option. Is the winger on the far side heading up the ice early? That's called a stretch. The team is trading support for speed. Does the second defenceman offer a pass to their partner, usually behind the net and never through the slot? That's called a D-to-D. Or do they stay glued to the net?

COACH: Then watch what the defenceman with the puck chooses. A rim, sent hard round the boards. A reverse, sent back against the flow. A D-to-D. A pass to the wall. Or skating it out. Then ask which forechecker forced that choice.

PLAYER: And for my own game?

COACH: When your defenceman goes back for the puck, are you a pass they can make?

COACH: Tenth idea. Watch every entry at the blue line. The blue line is the most under-watched thirty feet in hockey. Games are decided there, and almost nobody watches it. Picture the puck coming up the ice toward the other team's blue line. Should the team carry it in, or dump it in and chase?

PLAYER: Carry it, if they can?

COACH: The numbers back you. Public tracking research looked at entries at five-on-five. Carrying the puck in produced roughly twice the unblocked shot attempts and goals that dumping it in did. An unblocked shot attempt is any shot that wasn't blocked, whether it hit the net or missed.

COACH: So when the puck comes up to the blue line, watch three things. First, was it carried in, dumped in, or turned over? Keep a tally for one period. Many entries fail completely. Second, where's the defender's gap as the carrier arrives? A defender standing on their own blue line as the carrier arrives has already lost. One who met the carrier at the red line has usually won.

PLAYER: Is there a right gap?

COACH: Coaching pages give rough distances in stick lengths. Two to three in open ice. About one and a half at the red line. About one at the blue line. Those pages disagree on the last two. So treat all three as a guide, not a law. A wider gap on a fast attacker isn't automatically a mistake.

COACH: Third, on a dump-in, how many attackers chase it? A dump-in with one chaser is just giving the puck away, dressed up as a play.

COACH: Watch offside too. A player is offside if both skates are completely over the attacking blue line before the puck completely crosses it. One skate still on the line keeps them onside. So attacking wingers drag their trailing skate along the line. In some leagues a skate lifted in the air still counts as onside. In others it has to touch the ice, so check yours.

PLAYER: I do that drag already.

COACH: Then keep your head up as you cross. Your body is in the zone before your skate is, and you can't brace for a hit you can't see.

COACH: Eleventh idea. Faceoffs. Read the setup before the puck drops. There are around fifty-six faceoffs in an average NHL game, so this comes up again and again. Before each drop, look at the four players on each team who aren't taking it. Their body angles tell you where the puck is meant to go.

PLAYER: How do I read an angle?

COACH: A winger squared up to the boards expects the puck there. A winger leaning in toward the middle expects the centres to tie each other up, and a scramble for the puck. At a faceoff in the attacking end, find the shooter. In the common setup, both defencemen stand at the point. The one on the draw side is the shooter, stick loaded. The far one holds the line, as the safety. Setups vary, so check where the puck actually goes on a clean win. In a team's own end, watch the winger on the boards side. They have to beat the other team's defenceman to a puck that goes back to the point.

PLAYER: Is there a rule I need to know there?

COACH: One, because it lands on a winger. A winger who creeps into the circle early usually gets their own centre thrown out of the draw. A second violation by the same team on the same draw can be a two-minute penalty. So when you play, stay out of the circle until the puck drops.

COACH: Twelfth idea. Line changes, where a team's shape falls apart. For one stretch of play, watch the benches, not the ice.

PLAYER: The benches? Nothing happens there.

COACH: Line changes are where a team's structure collapses, and nobody is watching. On the fly, while play goes on, watch for three things. First, a team changing while the puck is in its own end. That's the classic mistake. It's the one that gives the other team an odd-man rush, more attackers coming than defenders back. Second, does the player coming off reach the bench before the player going on leaves it? Too many players on the ice is almost always a failure to talk, not a failure to count.

COACH: Third, when only two forwards change, which one stays out? Usually the deepest one stays, and the two nearest the bench go.

PLAYER: And when play's stopped?

COACH: At a stoppage, watch the home bench. In the NHL the visiting team puts its players out first, and then the home team may change. So the home coach gets the last change. That lets the home coach pick the matchups. Watch them hold a line at the door until the away team commits.

COACH: Watch the benches after an icing too. Icing is usually when a team shoots the puck from its own half all the way across the other team's goal line, and nobody touches it. Play usually stops. In many leagues the team that iced it can't change players before the next faceoff. A hurt player can still come off. The other team changes freely. So a tired line gets trapped in its own end. Coaches name that as a reliable way to give up a goal. Not every league has that rule, and some use it only at certain levels. So expect benches to behave differently from league to league.

COACH: Thirteenth idea. Back to the moments after a shot, in more detail. Watch the far post. Somebody on the defending team has to cover it. Who do you think?

PLAYER: Depends on the system?

COACH: Right. In a collapse, it's the weak-side defenceman. In man-to-man, it's whoever's player went there. The player changes with the system, but the job doesn't. Staying there, instead of chasing the puck, is a skill a player has to learn.

COACH: On a save, watch what the goalie does with the puck. Do they freeze it, steer it into the corner, or leave it in the slot? Steering rebounds is a skill a goalie works on, not luck. A puck left in the slot is the rebound you've just been watching for. So from a seat, after every shot, ask two things. Where did the puck end up, and who moved first?

COACH: Let's pull the middle part together. On a breakout, find the four players without the puck, and the winger's spot on the wall. At the blue line, tally carried, dumped or turned over, and watch the defender's gap. Read the faceoff setup before the drop, and in your own game stay out of the circle. Watch the benches, because a bad change gives up rushes. And after a shot, watch the far post and where the goalie sends the rebound.

COACH: Fourteenth idea. Watch live for the shape of the game, and on TV for the detail. Live and TV teach different things. Use both, and know which one you're using.

PLAYER: What does live give me that TV can't?

COACH: Live, you see all ten skaters at once. That's the whole advantage, and it's a big one. A system is a pattern made by five players, and TV shows you two. So every system read in this episode is easy live and hard on TV. Live also gives you real speed and real distance. TV flattens both. Live, you see how fast a pass arrives and how little time anyone has.

COACH: It gives you sound. Hard passes, one-timers, which are shots taken straight off a pass, players talking to each other, and how physical a battle really was. And it gives you the benches and the warm-ups. Line combinations, who talks to whom, and power-play setups being practised before the game.

COACH: Now, if you could have any seat in the rink, where would you sit?

PLAYER: Front row, right on the glass.

COACH: That's the seat for fun. When you're there to learn, sit as high as you can. From high up, the ice looks like a diagram. You can see the spacing, the layers and the shape of a forecheck. From the front row you see speed and skill, and almost no structure. So take the top row when you're there to learn, and the tenth row when you're there for fun. Best of all is high up behind one of the nets, rather than at centre ice. From there, the gaps, the layers and the defensive depth are very easy to see.

PLAYER: So is TV any use at all?

COACH: TV is better for detail. Replays let you see the five seconds before a goal again, from another angle. Slow motion lets you study a player's hands on a shot, a goalie's push, or the moment a defender's hips turn. And the pause button is a great teacher. Pause as a pass is released, and ask what the passer could see. Overhead and end-zone camera angles sometimes come close to the live view. Commentary and the lines drawn on the screen can teach you something too. Treat them as one opinion.

COACH: TV's weakness never goes away. The main camera can't show five players and stay watchable. Some leagues and services offer a wide overhead or tactical camera that keeps everyone in the picture. What's available varies by league and by season. If you can get one, it's worth more than anything else.

COACH: So the practical answer is simple. Watch live for systems, and TV for technique. And if you go to games, spend at least one full period up high, watching nothing but the five players of one team.

COACH: Fifteenth idea. Learn a position by watching it. Spend a period on your own position. Then pick another one.

PLAYER: Why watch a position I don't play?

COACH: Knowing what the player next to you is asked to do makes you easy to play with. Start with a centre. The centre's job covers all three zones and the full width of the rink, and it connects every system. That's coaching craft, not a measurement. In a collapse, watch them work low in their own end, between the front of the net and the corners, never fully committing to either. On the breakout, watch the curl. They swing low and take the pass with speed. Where do they look first?

COACH: At a faceoff, watch the second after the draw. The follow-up is a bigger part of winning a faceoff than the draw itself. And on the backcheck, skating back to defend, the centre is usually the first forward back. Watch the lane they take, but don't grade it.

PLAYER: Isn't the centre always meant to take the middle?

COACH: The usual setup gives the centre the middle. But which backchecker takes which lane is a coaching choice. Other teams give the middle to whoever is back first, or go man-to-man. What every version needs is that somebody owns the middle. So watch whether anybody does.

COACH: And on a rush, watch for the most dangerous attacker. Who do you think that is?

PLAYER: The one with the puck.

COACH: Often it isn't. It's the one driving down the middle without it, who gets missed because everyone's looking at the puck. That's the clearest case of what the camera won't show you.

COACH: Watching a winger. The winger's job changes the most from system to system. That makes the winger the best player to watch if you want to read the system. Watch how high they stay in their own end. Watch their spot on the boards on the breakout, and whether they hold out their stick as a target or just wait. After a dump-in, do they become F one, or leave it to the centre? And watch whether they support the puck or drift wide. A winger out wide can look in position and still be no use for a pass.

COACH: Watching a defenceman. Almost everything a defenceman does is about position, and it happens before the puck arrives. Watch their gap on every rush, and the moment they stop skating backwards and start closing. Watch their stick. A stick held in the passing lane takes away options without any contact at all.

PLAYER: What about when they go back for a puck?

COACH: Watch whether they check over their shoulder before they reach it. When they decide whether to pinch or back off, watch what they check first. It's usually where their centre is, and whether someone is high enough to cover for them.

COACH: And watch how deep they go in the attacking end, because there are three depths. The point, just inside the blue line, is the normal spot. From there they're an easy pass back, a shooting threat, and the safety if the other team breaks out. The top of the faceoff circle is more active. It gives them a better shot, or a walk into the middle, and they can still get back.

PLAYER: And lower than that?

COACH: Below the circles is a commitment. Below the hash marks, a defenceman is really a fourth forward and can't get back. So watch whether a forward takes the blue line before the defenceman goes down, not after. How deep a team lets its defencemen go is a coaching choice. So read what this team allows. And in front of their own net, watch where they stand, and whether their eyes stay on the puck.

COACH: Watching a goalie. Goaltending is the hardest position to watch, because the interesting part happens before the shot.

PLAYER: So what do I watch before the shot?

COACH: Watch their depth. How far out of the crease do they start, and how does that change with how far away the shooter is? Watch their tracking. Their head should be still, with their eyes locked on the puck. You can see that clearly on replays. Watch how they move. Small shuffles for small moves, and bigger pushes for bigger ones. And are they set when the shot is released? A goalie still moving at the release can't push anywhere. So coaches rate being set above being perfectly deep. That's a coaching judgement, not a measurement.

COACH: Watch the rebounds. Do they go into the slot, out to the corners, or get frozen? And watch whether the goalie comes out behind the net to play the puck, and where. Some leagues limit where a goalie may play the puck back there. Even where they don't, a goalie who leaves the crease and falls on the puck can be given a penalty.

COACH: Sixteenth idea. Watch your own team, and yourself, properly. Start with getting good footage. If someone films your team, ask for the widest view, not the highlights.

PLAYER: The highlights are the fun bit, though.

COACH: And they're the least useful footage for getting better. They show the finish, and as we covered with the five seconds before a goal, the finish is the least useful part.

COACH: Then use a routine for your own shifts. Watch each shift twice. Once following yourself, and once following the play. The second time, ask whether you were where the play needed you. Ask the three questions, and write the answers down. Where was I when we lost the puck, and where did I go in the next two seconds? Did I ever stop skating? Was I an option?

COACH: Then count something. Touches, completed passes, or entries with the puck. Track the same count over three games. And pick exactly one thing to change for the next game.

PLAYER: Only one? I'll usually find about six.

COACH: A review that produces six fixes produces none. Pick one.

COACH: Now the hard part, watching yourself away from the puck. Watching your good passes is fun. Watching yourself stand still on the weak side while a goal goes in is not. So almost nobody does it. Do it anyway.

PLAYER: What should I look for?

COACH: Watch the three seconds after each of your passes. Did you move, or did you stand and admire it? Coaches name passing and then standing still as one of the most common habits at amateur level. That's an observation, not a count.

COACH: While you're on your passes, look for panic passes too. A panic pass is one made without looking, just to get rid of the puck. Pause the video on each one and ask yourself. Did I have more time than I thought? The honest answer is almost always yes. Panic is the normal state for a player who's still learning, and panic spends time you actually had. The fix isn't to be braver, because nobody can act on that. It's to get one more look before the puck reaches you, so you know the answer before it arrives. In your own end, that look is how you decide fast, not a reason to hold it. One look, one decision, move it.

COACH: Watch yourself on every goal against. Not to blame anyone, but to see whether you were where your system says. And watch your changes. Are you leaving at safe moments? Time three of your shifts.

PLAYER: I reckon I stay out about the right amount.

COACH: Coaches say most players stay out ten to twenty seconds longer than they think. That isn't measured, so time your own. And watch your head. Count your shoulder checks in a shift. Coaches say amateurs scan far less than they believe. The only published count is of professionals, so check it on your own video.

COACH: Then watch your own team as a team. Watch it in the last five minutes of a one-goal game, and compare that with the first period. If nothing changes, that's worth knowing. And scout the team you play next, if you can. Two things. Which forecheck do they run? And do their wingers follow into the corner in their own end?

PLAYER: Let me pull that part together. Watch live for systems and TV for technique, and sit high, ideally behind a net. Spend a period on my own position, then on the one beside it, and work out the system before I judge anyone. Watch each of my shifts twice, count something, and change one thing. And watch myself away from the puck, because that's where the improvement is.

COACH: Spot on.

COACH: Seventeenth idea. Numbers, and why most of them are misread. Numbers let you follow hockey when you can't be at the rink. They're also the fastest way to be confidently wrong. Most hockey statistics measure something a bit different from what their name suggests.

PLAYER: So which numbers can I trust?

COACH: First, a plain warning. Hockey statistics are a field people still argue about, not settled science. Different websites work out the same number differently. Models get rebuilt. Even the basic counts have errors, because scorekeepers at different rinks record shots and hits differently. So treat every number as evidence, not a verdict. And check how the site you're reading defines it.

COACH: Start with time on ice. It may be the most honest number in hockey, because it's the coach's own opinion of a player. Rising time on ice means rising trust. It's also what every other number needs to be divided by. A player who plays twenty minutes a night will pile up bigger totals than one who plays eleven. So turn every total into a rate per sixty minutes of ice time before you compare anyone.

PLAYER: What about points? That's what everyone looks at.

COACH: A goal goes to the player who put the puck in. Up to two assists go to the players who touched the puck before the scorer. Points are goals plus assists. That cap of two assists means the pass that broke the defence often goes uncredited. And points ignore ice time, linemates and power-play time. A player with five extra minutes and better linemates will out-point a better player.

COACH: Plus-minus is a player's goal difference while on the ice. On an even-strength or shorthanded goal, every skater on the ice for the team that scored gets a plus. Every skater for the other team gets a minus. Power-play goals count for neither side.

PLAYER: So a good plus-minus means a good defender?

COACH: Never judge a defender by plus-minus. It depends heavily on teammates, the goalie and the other team. It ignores power-play goals but counts shorthanded ones. And it can't tell the player who caused a goal from one who was just on the ice. A fourth-liner on a great team can beat a first-liner carrying a bad one. To judge a defender, watch them instead. Their gap, their stick, and where they were before each goal against.

COACH: Penalty minutes are the total minutes of penalties a player has taken. On their own they tell you almost nothing. A stick foul that gives the other team a power play is lumped together with a fighting major, which usually doesn't, because both players go off. So look at what the penalties were.

COACH: Faceoff percentage is draws won divided by draws taken. It's good for comparing centres on one clear task. But a win to an empty spot counts the same as one that starts a breakout. And a shorthanded draw in your own end is a different job from a power-play draw in theirs.

COACH: Shooting percentage is goals divided by shots on goal. In a recent NHL season, teams scored on about eleven percent of their shots on goal. Over short spells this number is very noisy. Say a player scores on a quarter of their shots over twenty games. What would you think?

PLAYER: That they're a great shooter?

COACH: More likely a normal shooter having a good month. Real shooting talent takes several hundred shots to show.

COACH: Now the shot-attempt numbers. Corsi counts every shot attempt, on goal, missed, or blocked. Fenwick counts only the attempts that weren't blocked. They're usually given as a percentage for the team while a player is on the ice. Fifty percent is break-even. Most players sit between about forty and sixty percent.

PLAYER: How did those catch on?

COACH: You can't shoot without the puck, so they stand in for having the puck. And attempts pile up roughly eighteen times faster than goals. So they start to mean something after a handful of games, where goals take most of a season. But they measure territory, not the quality of the chances. A harmless point shot counts the same as a chance from the slot. The score and where shifts start bend them. And they're really a team number, handed out to the players on the ice.

COACH: Expected goals, often written x G, gives each unblocked shot attempt a chance of becoming a goal. It's based on things like distance, angle, shot type and whether it was a rebound. Add those chances up and you get expected goals.

PLAYER: What does that fix?

COACH: A shot from the slot and a shot from the point are no longer worth the same. That's how it beat counting attempts. But there's no standard version. Different sites build it differently, so two sites will give different expected goals for the same game. Most public models don't know where the goalie or the defenders were, or whether the shooter was screened. And one game of expected goals is not a verdict on who deserved to win. Use several games, and know whose model it is.

COACH: One more thing to check. Expected goals usually counts only unblocked attempts. One well-known site, MoneyPuck, values a blocked attempt at nothing at all. Other sites may differ, so read the glossary.

PLAYER: And for goalies?

COACH: There are three measures, from weakest to best. Goals against average is goals let in per sixty minutes. It depends heavily on the team in front of the goalie. Save percentage is better, because it allows for how many shots the goalie faced. But it treats every shot the same. A point shot counts the same as a tap-in at the back door.

COACH: Goals saved above expected is better again. It compares the goals a goalie let in with what an average goalie would let in from the same shots. So it separates the goalie from the team. Picture two goalies. One faces thirty easy point shots a night. The other faces twenty chances from the slot. They can end up with the same save percentage. Goals saved above expected asks the fairer question. Given the shots you faced, were you better or worse than average?

COACH: But goals saved above expected carries every weakness of the expected-goals model underneath it. And it needs a big sample, because goaltending is the noisiest position in hockey. So use it first, then save percentage, then goals against average. And don't treat any of them as final.

COACH: PDO is shooting percentage plus save percentage while a team or player is on the ice. Across the whole league the two add up to almost exactly one hundred. Neither repeats well, so PDO tends to drift back toward one hundred. A team above about one hundred and two is probably not as good as its results. A team below about ninety-eight is probably better.

PLAYER: So PDO is just luck?

COACH: It isn't pure luck. A team with great shooters and a great goalie can stay above one hundred for years. So if a team is winning while being outplayed on every other measure, check PDO. But treat it as a flag to go and look, not as an answer.

COACH: Three kinds of context change what almost any number means. The score. A team that's behind takes more risks and more shots. A team that's ahead sits back and gives them up. So a team that leads all season looks worse on shot attempts than it really is. Good sites adjust for the score. Raw numbers from lopsided games mostly read the scoreboard back to you.

COACH: Teammates. Who a player plays with usually matters more than who they play against. It's the one people forget. Over a whole season, the strength of the opponents evens out, because everyone plays everyone. It matters more in a short playoff series.

COACH: And where a player's shifts start. Coaches start attacking players in the attacking zone, and defensive players in their own end. So a player stuck with own-end starts will look worse than an identical player who isn't. But most shifts start on the fly, not from a faceoff, so the effect is smaller than people once thought. And sites measure it in very different ways. So never compare that figure from one site with the same figure from another.

PLAYER: Is there a quick check before I believe a number?

COACH: Ask seven things. What is it divided by? Is it all situations, or five-on-five only? How big is the sample? Who was on the ice with the player? What was the score? Whose model is it? And does it match what you see when you watch? If it doesn't, either the number is wrong or you are. Those disagreements are where you learn the most.

PLAYER: Let me try the numbers back. Time on ice first, and turn every total into a rate per sixty minutes. Never judge a defender by plus-minus. Prefer expected goals to shot attempts, and know whose model it is. Judge goalies by goals saved above expected, then save percentage, then goals against average. Before I believe a hot streak, check PDO, and treat it as a flag. And give every number its context.

COACH: That's the lot.

COACH: Eighteenth idea. Where to find good analysis, and one hour a week. Websites appear, change hands, go behind paywalls and disappear. So learn the kinds of source, not a list of links.

COACH: Public statistics sites publish possession, expected goals and goalie numbers, usually with a glossary. The glossary is the page that explains how the site works out its numbers. Read it first. Definitions differ, and knowing which one you're reading is the whole skill. Coaching video breakdowns are coaches drawing on clips, to show what each player was doing and the thinking behind it. They're the fastest way to turn watching into understanding.

COACH: Wide footage is any feed that keeps all ten skaters in the picture. Some junior and development teams stream from one fixed high camera, which is ideal. Your own team's film, shot wide from the top of the stands, belongs here too. It's worth more to you than any professional footage. And research papers and research blogs tell you the reason a number exists in the first place.

PLAYER: Anything else?

COACH: Your coach. Underrated, and free. Ask two questions. What forecheck are we running, and when do we change it? And in our own end, do you want my winger following into the corner?

COACH: Be wary of anyone who treats one number as the final word on a player. And be just as wary of anyone who says all the numbers are rubbish. Both want the answer to be simpler than it is. Let a number tell you what to go and watch. Then let the watching decide what it means.

PLAYER: How much time should I spend on all this?

COACH: Try one hour a week. Coaches judge that a deliberate hour does more for your hockey brain than several hours of ordinary watching. That's a judgement, not a measurement. One way to split it. Twenty minutes on one period of professional hockey, doing one job. Change the job each week. One player, then the defencemen only, then the weak side, then the five seconds before each goal. Watch live if you can, and sit high.

COACH: Fifteen minutes on your own video. Three or four of your shifts, each watched twice, with the three questions written down.

PLAYER: What if I don't have any video yet?

COACH: A phone at the top of the stands is enough. Ask a spare player to film one period. Then ten minutes on one team, a professional one or the team you play next. Write down two answers. Which forecheck do they run, and do their wingers follow into the corner? Ten minutes on one number. Read its definition on the site that publishes it. Then look at it for some players you know well. Where it disagrees with what you've seen, you'll learn the most. And five minutes writing down one thing to try at practice.

PLAYER: And if I've only got half an hour?

COACH: Cut the professional hockey and keep your own video. That's where the improvement is.

COACH: Let me pull it all together.

COACH: Stop following the puck. Give your eyes one job per period. One player for a whole shift, the two defencemen, the weak side, or the five seconds before a goal.

COACH: Read a team's system from the players away from the puck. Watch the winger in their own end when the puck goes into the corner. Stays high on the point, it's a zone system. Follows into the corner, it's man-to-man. Hands off, it's a hybrid. Count forwards going deep for the forecheck, and players at the point for the power play. Every system is a coaching choice, so give it five or six plays.

COACH: Watch every entry at the blue line, and the defender's gap. On a breakout, find the four players without the puck. After a shot, watch the net front, not the replay. And in your own game, be the player who gets there first. Just outside the crease and off its red line, stick on the ice and clear of the goalie, under control, head up, chin off your chest, and know the post. Hold your spot without leaning or pushing, no stick across anyone, nothing into anyone's back, and never go into the goalie.

COACH: Watch live for systems and TV for technique, and sit high. Film yourself wide, and watch whole shifts, not your touches. Don't trust any number until you know what it's divided by, the situation, the sample, the teammates, the score and whose model it is.

PLAYER: What should I ask my own coach?

COACH: Much of what you'll watch depends on what a team plays. So here are three questions. What forecheck are we running, and when do we change it? In our own end, do you want my winger following into the corner? And on the breakout, where do you want me when our defenceman goes back for the puck? Ask, because these are your team's choices, not rules.

COACH: Then end every session with one thing to try at practice. Go and watch for the answers, and take them onto the ice.
