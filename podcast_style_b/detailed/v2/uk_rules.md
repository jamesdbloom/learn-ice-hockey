In the Elite League it's the League's own Department of Player Safety that acts, and the League writes its own suspensions. Under the In-House Rules it's the home association's disciplinary process.

PLAYER: What does that actually cost me?

COACH: At a club in England or Wales, outside the Elite League, some of it is written down. A game misconduct for checking from behind or a check to the head, or a major for boarding, is at least a one-game ban. And you also miss a game in every other league or age group you're registered in. A first offence can cost a lot more than one game. The range runs to eleven games or more for boarding or a hit from behind, and to twenty for a check to the head. Under sixteen, a first offence may get a written warning instead, but a second or serious one is still a ban. Games in Scotland and Northern Ireland come under their own policies, so ask your league there.

PLAYER: And if I just pick up lots of small penalties?

COACH: They add up too. At a club in England or Wales, outside the Elite League, majors and misconducts earn penalty points, and ten points is a one-game ban, with no appeal. The points follow you across every league and age group you play in. So five ten-minute misconducts, for arguing on after a call, say, or a missing neck guard, are a one-game ban on their own. Treat a major as the start of a process, not the whole bill.

COACH: Fifteenth idea. When a fight or a scuffle starts, stay on the bench, or stay in the box, until an official releases you. Picture it. You're on the bench, and a teammate gets jumped right by your door. What would you do?

PLAYER: Everything in me says go and help.

COACH: Stay put. Not to help a teammate. Not to change. Not because your penalty has just run out. This applies at every age and in every competition. It's the IIHF's own rule, and the British documents don't soften it. Playing in the NIHL changes what a fight costs you, not what leaving the bench costs you.

PLAYER: What counts as a fight, for this?

COACH: Less than you'd think. In the rulebook, an altercation needs only one penalty, not one punch. So a scrum after the whistle, where somebody takes a roughing minor, counts. From that moment the bench door and the penalty box are shut.

COACH: And leaving costs a lot. The first or second player off the bench during an altercation gets a game misconduct. Being third out isn't a free pass, because nobody is allowed to go. A penalised player who leaves the box during an altercation gets a minor and a game misconduct. That includes a player who does nothing but skate around it. Skating around it is not staying out of it.

PLAYER: What if my penalty runs out in the middle of it?

COACH: Your time running out isn't a release. You may only leave the box when your penalty is over and your team is entitled to an extra player on the ice. When both teams have penalties running, those two moments often don't line up. So stay in the box until an official waves you out. And in the Elite League, leaving the bench to join an altercation also brings an automatic suspension of at least three games.

COACH: Sixteenth idea. Don't fight, even when someone else starts it. Expect a fight to end your game. Under the In-House Rules, a fight between two willing players throws you out, with one exception. The NIHL, the SNL and university Varsity games treat that plain fight more lightly, as a five-minute major. But not in the last five minutes of an NIHL game, or in its overtime. Not in women's hockey, where you should plan on any major ending your game. And in junior hockey any major ends your game anyway. Don't hear that as permission. Under the In-House Rules, everything around a fight still keeps its full price, and that price is being thrown out. Starting the fight. Being the aggressor. A sucker punch. Fighting before or after the periods. And joining a fight that's already going, even to break it up, can get you thrown out too, at the referee's call.

PLAYER: What if I didn't want the fight? Someone grabs me and starts swinging.

COACH: Even if you didn't want it, punching back can still cost you. In the NIHL, the SNL and Varsity games, a player dragged into a fight they didn't want can still get a minor or a five-minute major, at the referee's call. And late in an NIHL game, in the last five minutes or overtime, that major throws them out too. And at a club in England or Wales, plan on a ban on top. Any fight in the last five minutes, in overtime or at the end of the game brings at least a one-game ban there, in every league outside the Elite League. So don't count on a few punches back being free.

COACH: When a linesperson steps in, stop. Carrying on after you've been told to stop, or resisting a linesperson, can get you thrown out. And under the newer edition of the rulebook, a grudge from an earlier game, even one from months ago, can be what makes you the instigator, the player who started it. So don't bring an old score into a new game.

COACH: The Elite League writes its own fighting rule, and it's lighter on the night but heavier afterwards. A first plain fight during play there need not end your game. Don't hear that as permission. It's at least a major, and "at least" means the referee can give you more. A fight in the warm-up or after the buzzer still throws you out. A second fight on the same whistle ends your game. So do two majors of any kind. And the League doesn't say whether it counts fighting majors across the whole season, so play as though it does.

COACH: And starting a fight in the Elite League goes to the League for review. Late in the game, it throws you out.

COACH: Seventeenth idea. In women's hockey and junior hockey, plan on a major ending your game. In women's hockey a major throws you out at every age, adults included. Read junior hockey the same way, from under-nineteen down to under-ten. That junior reading is the safe reading of the rule rather than its exact words. Confirm both with your league.

PLAYER: Is contact different in women's hockey too?

COACH: It is. Women's hockey has its own contact line in the IIHF book. Any step or glide into an opponent is at least a minor for an illegal hit. So is using the boards to take an opponent out of the play, to push them into the boards, or to pin them there. Here's how to play it. If an opponent is standing still, skate around them. That ice is theirs. And if you're carrying the puck straight at an opponent, avoiding the contact is your job. WNIHL games are non-checking. In any other game, ask before you hit anyone.

PLAYER: Keeping myself on the ice, then. Stay on my feet, because under the In-House Rules a dive is twelve minutes and a misconduct. A cross-check to the head called cross-checking is no cheaper, and I never shove with both hands on my stick. Play to the whistle, because in EIH and SIHA games the call on the ice is the call. A major is the start of a process, and penalties add up to bans. If an altercation starts, stay on the bench or in the box until an official releases me. Don't fight, expect a fight to end my game, and don't count on a few punches back being free. And in women's and junior hockey, plan on any major ending it.

COACH: Spot on. Now, how your age group and your competition change the game.

COACH: Eighteenth idea. Take your age group from this season's rules, and nowhere else. Under the In-House Rules, junior age groups are set by birth year. The bands move by a full year every season. So a player born in two thousand and ten was under-sixteen last season and is under-nineteen this one.

PLAYER: Why does one year matter so much?

COACH: A table copied from last season puts a child in the wrong age group. In a collision sport, the gap between a fourteen-year-old and an eighteen-year-old is the whole point of having age groups. So take the bands from the current season's In-House Rules, and nowhere else.

PLAYER: Are junior teams mixed, boys and girls?

COACH: The In-House Rules say junior age groups are the same for boys and girls, and this season's junior rules still expect girls on junior teams. But a separate UK policy on sex and gender sets male and female categories by sex recorded at birth from under-twelve, with some exceptions. It doesn't say how that fits mixed junior teams. So ask your club.

COACH: Nineteenth idea. At under-twelve in IHUK's junior leagues, play on without waiting for an icing or offside whistle, and clear the zone at once when the official calls your team out. Under-twelve is a different game, and neither British rulebook describes it. It's set out in IHUK's junior competition rules.

COACH: There are no icing calls at under-twelve. Nothing is whistled, and nothing comes back. But giving the puck away down the ice is still giving it away.

PLAYER: And offside?

COACH: An offside doesn't stop play either. Offside means an attacker going into the attacking zone ahead of the puck. Picture it. You're an under-twelve forward in a red jersey. You've drifted over their blue line ahead of the puck, and the official shouts "Reds out". What would you do?

PLAYER: Get back over the line?

COACH: Everyone does, at once. Instead of a whistle, the official calls your team out of the zone, and the whole team has to get back out over the line before going in again. Do it straight away. Not clearing quickly is a penalty for delay of game. So when you hear the call, everyone turns and skates out together.

COACH: The clock keeps running. A game is three periods of eighteen minutes on three lines, or twenty minutes on four. And a buzzer changes the lines every sixty seconds. When the buzzer goes, leave the puck where it is. Moving it on purpose gets a warning, and then a minor.

COACH: You play on the line you were put on. Your coach hands in the lines before the game, and each line is marked, with numbered jerseys, armbands or helmet tape. So wear whatever marking your team uses. Moving up a line is only allowed for an injury or an ejection. Jumping on with the wrong line is a minor the first time.

PLAYER: Do penalties work the same at that age?

COACH: Not quite, and the rules leave gaps. So ask your coach or the officials before the game how penalties are served.

COACH: Under-ten is different again. It's played across the ice or on half the ice, the lines change on a buzzer, and there's no body checking. After a goal, a line change or a frozen puck, the faceoff goes back to centre. And penalties are explained to the player rather than written down. Nothing written sets an icing or offside rule for under-ten. So ask the coach running the day how they're restarting play. From under-fourteen up, it's the ordinary full-ice, stop-clock game.

COACH: Twentieth idea. Find out before the game how a tie is settled, because overtime isn't played everywhere. Under the In-House Rules, overtime is only played in NIHL games, and in SNL games whose competition rules provide for it. University games marked Varsity OT on the schedule follow the NIHL rules for overtime too. Plenty of other games simply end level.

PLAYER: And where there is overtime?

COACH: Where an NIHL game goes to overtime, it's five minutes of three-on-three. Then comes a shootout with three shooters a side. If it's still level, it goes to one shot each until somebody wins. Whether one player may shoot twice differs between the NIHL leagues and the SNL. So name your three shooters before the game, and ask which version is yours before you plan on your best shooter taking two of the shots.

COACH: In the Elite League, plan for five shooters, not three. A level regular-season game plays five minutes of three-on-three, then a five-shooter shootout. Its knockout games have their own formats, so check yours.

PLAYER: Age groups and formats, then. Take my age group from this season's rules. At under-twelve, play on without waiting for an icing or offside whistle, and clear the zone the moment my team is called out. Leave the puck at the buzzer, and stay on my own line. Under-ten is played on a smaller sheet with no checking. And find out before the game how a tie is settled, and how many shooters we need.

COACH: That's it. Now the last part, and it's the one to learn before you need it. What to do at the rink when someone is badly hurt. You may never need this, but it's worth knowing calmly in advance.

COACH: Twenty-first idea. Before you need it, know who the first aider is, where the phone is, and what the plan is. At an England Ice Hockey game, five things are required. A first aider, though the rule sets no qualification for that person. A first aid kit, checked before each game. A risk assessment and an emergency action plan, both shared with the visiting team. And a telephone to call the emergency services.

PLAYER: No ambulance? No doctor?

COACH: A doctor or physio is only recommended, so check the team sheet to see whether one is there. And an ambulance waiting at the rink isn't required. So a game with a first aider, a phone, no ambulance and no doctor is exactly what the rule allows. That's why the phone is on the list.

COACH: Whether that rule reaches the Elite League, Scotland or Northern Ireland, the documents don't say. It sets nothing for practice either. Every registered coach must hold a basic first aid certificate, but that tells you nothing about the building. So wherever you play, ask your club what cover your rink actually has. Before the game, find out who the first aider is, where the phone is, and whether your team has been given the rink's emergency action plan. Ask the same at an away rink.

COACH: Twenty-second idea. A knock to the head. Picture this. A teammate takes a hit, their head snaps back, and they're down on the ice. They're moving, and they're telling you they're fine. What would you do?

PLAYER: Help them up and get them to the bench?

COACH: No. Don't help them up. Treat every knock to the head as a possible neck injury too. Don't move them, unless you must, to keep their airway clear. Leave the helmet on. Then look for red flags. A red flag is a sign that means you call an ambulance straight away. Any one red flag is enough, even if it seems mild. In the United Kingdom, that's nine nine nine.

PLAYER: What are the red flags?

COACH: The UK concussion guidance for grassroots sport, which England Ice Hockey has adopted, lists fourteen. They apply to a player who has taken a blow to the head, or a blow to the body that moved the head. Learn all of them, because a partial list teaches you to stop looking. Any one of them, reported or seen, means call nine nine nine. One. Any loss of consciousness because of the injury. Two. Consciousness getting worse, the player getting more drowsy. Three. No memory of what happened before or after the injury. Four. Confusion or irritability that keeps increasing. Five. An unusual change in behaviour.

COACH: Six. Any new problem with how the brain or nerves are working. Trouble understanding, speaking, reading or writing. Less feeling. Loss of balance. Weakness. Or double vision. Seven. A seizure or a fit, twitching arms or legs, or lying rigid or motionless because of muscle spasm. Eight. A severe headache, or one that keeps getting worse. Nine. Being sick more than once. Ten. Severe neck pain. But don't wait for severe. Call for any neck pain at all. Eleven. Any suspicion of a broken skull. A cut, a bruise, swelling or severe pain where they were hit is enough to suspect one.

COACH: Twelve. A history of brain surgery, or a bleeding disorder. Thirteen. Being on blood-thinning medication. Fourteen. Being drunk or on drugs right now. Any one of those fourteen is enough on its own.

PLAYER: The last three aren't really about the hit.

COACH: No. They're facts about the player, not about the injury. They matter because they fire when nothing else has. A teammate on blood thinners who takes a knock to the head is a nine nine nine call on that alone. That holds even if they get up and tell you they're fine. So find out in advance who on your team is on one.

COACH: There's also an international list, from the Concussion Recognition Tool, and in places it reaches further. It counts any neck pain or tenderness, not just severe. Numbness or tingling in more than one arm or leg. Losing sight, as well as double vision. And getting more restless, agitated or aggressive. Where the two lists differ, act on whichever fires first. Neither list says that. It's what reading them side by side leaves you with, and it can only make you call sooner.

PLAYER: So which of these are easy to miss?

COACH: Four. Call nine nine nine for any neck pain at all, without waiting for it to be severe. Don't wait for a dent in the skull, because any suspicion of a break is enough. And a player who goes stiff and still after a hit to the head needs an ambulance, even without a fit. It can look like being knocked out. And any symptom that's getting worse, like a headache or confusion, counts as a red flag too. Call nine nine nine.

COACH: If a player is lying still, check whether they respond. If you can't get a response, call nine nine nine. You don't have to tell a rigid player from an unconscious one, because both are on the list.

PLAYER: And if they're lying still but awake and talking to me?

COACH: They still need watching. Call nine nine nine for neck pain, stiffness or any other red flag. If there's none, they may not need an ambulance. But don't move them or get them up. Let the rink's first aider or medical cover assess them where they lie.

COACH: If a neck injury is suspected, phone for help first. Only trained healthcare professionals should move the player. Don't move them yourself, except as far as keeping their airway clear needs. And leave the helmet on.

PLAYER: And if there's no red flag, and it's just a knock?

COACH: Then it's still a suspected concussion. The player comes off, and doesn't come back that day. Not that shift, not that period, not that game. A medical professional decides when they play again, not the player. A concussed player will often tell you they're fine. Call one one one, the NHS line, for an assessment within twenty-four hours. Don't leave them alone for those twenty-four hours. They don't drive, and they don't go home on their own. And if they get worse, or their neck starts to hurt, call nine nine nine, even though they got up.

COACH: Now coming back. The British guidance says no return to competition before twenty-one days from the injury. The day of the injury counts as day zero. That's a floor, not a date, and not a clearance. The medical assessment still has to happen. And a clearance that arrives sooner doesn't put you in a game sooner. Training with a risk of a head injury waits until you've had no symptoms at rest for fourteen days, and not before day fifteen. The only players who can be cleared to compete sooner are elite athletes, managed by sports concussion specialists. Grassroots hockey isn't that.

COACH: Let me pull it all together.

COACH: Your rulebook is the IIHF's, with the In-House Rules or the Elite League's Casebook on top. Ask which edition your officials carry, and play to the stricter one.

COACH: Wear your neck guard every time you go on the ice, and assume there's no warning. If it comes off during play, leave the ice without picking it up. Tighten your chin strap to one finger. Leave the ice if your helmet comes off, or your visor or cage cracks. And wear a mouthguard.

COACH: Don't hit anyone until your league tells you your age group checks.

COACH: Play as though your first major ends your game, adult or not, and once you have one misconduct, as though the next one does. Never hit a player who can't see you coming, and never from behind. Never hit a player in the head. Never shove with both hands on your stick. Keep your stick below every shoulder. Stay on your feet, because under the In-House Rules a dive is a misconduct. And remember that a major can bring a ban after the game, and penalties add up to bans.

COACH: Keep your wingers out of the circle, and have a second player ready for the draw. Stay out of the blue paint and off the goalie, in the crease and out of it. Goalies, play as though the trapezoid binds.

COACH: Play to the whistle. If an altercation starts, stay on the bench or in the box until an official releases you. Don't fight. Expect a fight to end your game, and don't count on a few punches back being free.

COACH: After a knock to the head, treat it as a possible neck injury. Don't move the player unless you must to keep their airway clear, and leave the helmet on. Learn all fourteen red flags. Any one red flag means nine nine nine, and that includes any neck pain at all. With no red flag, it's still off for the day. Call one one one, and don't leave them alone for twenty-four hours. A medical professional decides when they play again. They don't drive or go home alone. If they get worse, or their neck hurts, call nine nine nine, even though they got up.

PLAYER: What should I ask my own coach, and my league?

COACH: Much of this depends on where you play. So three questions for your coach. Who takes the draw if our centre is replaced? Who is our first aider, where is the phone, and what cover does our rink have? And how are ties settled in our games, and who are our three shooters?

COACH: And three for your league. Which British rules do we play under, and which edition do the referees carry? Is my age group checking? And has a rule bulletin been issued this season? A bulletin can change a rule without changing the published documents. In the Elite League, the Casebook itself is updated during the season, too. Their answers tell you which rules are yours, and now you know why each one changes how you play.
