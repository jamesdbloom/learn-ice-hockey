It grows out of an ordinary battle position, so watch for it in yourself. In most leagues, even the smallest penalty for it gets you thrown out, because the player goes down violently.

PLAYER: If someone does go down hard like that, what should we do?

COACH: If anyone goes down after a knock to the head, treat it as a possible neck injury. Don't help them up or move them, unless you must, to keep their airway clear, and leave their helmet on. No exceptions. Call an ambulance straight away for any one of these, even if it seems mild. Knocked out. A fit, twitching or going rigid. Any neck pain. Weakness, numbness or tingling in their arms or legs. Double vision or losing their sight. Sick more than once. A bad or worsening headache. Getting more confused, drowsy, restless or agitated. A dent in the skull. And a symptom that's getting worse means an ambulance too.

COACH: Holding is any grab that holds a player back. Some leagues let you block with an arm if you have position and aren't holding on. Others don't. So the habit that's safe everywhere is to win the wall with your feet and your position, not your arm. Keep your free hand on your own stick. Holding isn't capped at two minutes either. In some leagues, a hold that hurts someone gets you thrown out.

COACH: On the wall, the same rules as any chase still hold. Stick on the puck, not a hit. Whether you may hit at all depends on your league, your age group and the game you're in, and if you can see their numbers, pull up. The boards position. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to anyone coming at you, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly. Stuck facing the boards? Stick and gloves up and spread on the glass, head off it.

COACH: And never touch an opponent's cage, the face guard on their helmet. No league lets you count on two minutes for that. It can get you thrown out.

PLAYER: Let me try the contact part. No checking doesn't mean no contact, but steer with my skating and keep my arms, shoulders and hips in. Where checking is allowed, hit with my hips and shoulders, while they still have the puck, never low and never at the head. Never into a battle, never a player facing the boards, and never anyone from behind.

PLAYER: Arrive under control, and never leave my feet. Keep my stick below my shoulders, never shove with both hands on it, and never chop. Control their stick with a low lift instead. And on the wall, win it with my feet, free hand on my own stick.

COACH: Good. And finish it the way you'd finish any shift on the wall. Stick on the puck, not a hit, and never push or hit anyone from behind. The boards position. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to anyone coming at you, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly.

COACH: Now the two nets.

COACH: Thirteenth idea. The front of their net. Screen the goalie, and stay off the goalie. The crease is the painted blue area in front of each net. It's the goalie's area, and the red line around its edge counts as part of it. A screen means standing in the goalie's line of sight, so they can't see the shot.

PLAYER: Is that even allowed? It feels like it shouldn't be.

COACH: For a forward, it's the most useful rule there is. From outside the crease, without touching the goalie, a screen is legal, and referees allow it all the time. It's still the referee's call. So get in the goalie's eyes. Keep your skates out of the blue paint, and off the red line around it, because every league that settles it counts the line as part of the crease. Standing in the crease can cost your team the goal, so stay out of it.

COACH: Head up, chin off your chest. The goal post can hurt you the same way the boards can. Stand your ground without pushing. If a defender leans on you, don't push back or lean, and never put your stick across them, or anything into their back.

PLAYER: And if the puck's loose at the goalie's feet?

COACH: Never go into the goalie. Any contact with a goalie can be a penalty, and it can get you thrown out of the game. A goalie who is down or turned can't protect themselves, so contact can hurt them. It can also cost your team the goal. So make a real effort to go around the goalie, every time, and treat any contact you had time to avoid as a penalty you've bought.

COACH: That holds outside the crease too. A goalie out of the crease is not fair game, even in a corner. Take the puck, not the body. And never stand in a goalie's way while they're getting back to their crease. That can be a penalty even if you never touch them.

PLAYER: What if a defender shoves me into the goalie? That can't be my fault.

COACH: Get out, and go around, not through. Being pushed in is a defence, but never a complete one. You still have to make a real effort to avoid the goalie.

COACH: Fourteenth idea. The front of your own net. Never close your hand on the puck, or gather it into your body, when the puck is in your own crease. That can be a penalty shot in every league, a free shot alone on your goalie. And with your net empty, play it as a goal against you.

COACH: One rule here works in your favour. In your own end, you can bat the puck to a teammate with an open hand, and a hand pass isn't called. That's a free tool for a defenceman under pressure.

COACH: Fifteenth idea. For goalies. Some leagues paint a trapezoid, a shape on the ice behind your net. Behind your goal line, you can only play the puck inside the trapezoid, or with a skate still touching your crease. Where the puck is decides it, not where you are. And there's no exception because a teammate is coming.

PLAYER: Why have it at all?

COACH: The international federation said it brought the trapezoid in to create more attacking play. Without one, a goalie can be a third defenceman on every dump-in, fetching pucks from the corners. But in some leagues you must play those corner pucks, not smother them. Move them on quickly. Out in the corner, a goalie has less protection than in the crease, so don't sit on the puck there.

COACH: Skaters, that's not an invitation. Go for a puck the goalie is playing outside the crease with your stick, never with your body. In Britain it isn't clear whether the trapezoid is enforced everywhere. So assume your own goalie is bound by it, and assume theirs might come for the puck anyway. Then ask your league.

PLAYER: When should a goalie freeze the puck?

COACH: Only under real pressure. In many leagues a soft freeze on a routine dump-in from beyond the red line costs you like an icing. The faceoff is in your end, they pick the dot, and your team can't change. Keep both your skates and the puck on your own side of the centre red line. That's legal everywhere.

COACH: If your stick breaks, don't go to the bench. Call for one from a teammate on the ice, and play with your blocker, glove and body until it arrives. And when a fight breaks out near your crease, stay in your net. If you must move, go only where the referee sends you. Never head to your bench on your own, and never join in, even to break it up.

COACH: Sixteenth idea. Broken sticks, for skaters. Your stick snaps in the corner. What's the first thing you do?

PLAYER: Try to poke the puck out with what's left?

COACH: That's the minor. Open your hand. Drop it straight away. You can keep playing without a stick, but playing on with a broken one is a minor. Don't poke at the puck with what's left. Don't carry the broken one to the bench, either. Drop it where you are. Get a new one handed to you, from your bench or by a teammate. Never have one thrown or slid to you.

COACH: And here's a habit the rule creates. If you're a defenceman who loses your stick in your own end, drop it, get your body into the passing lane, and let a forward hand you theirs. Forwards, hand yours over. You can get to the bench far more easily than a defenceman can.

COACH: Seventeenth idea. Faceoffs. If you're not taking the draw, keep both skates outside the circle and off its line. At a dot in either end, stay on your own side of the hash marks, the short lines beside the circle, until the puck drops. Touching the line is fine. Crossing it isn't.

PLAYER: I'm a winger. If I lean in early, isn't that just a re-drop?

COACH: Worse. If you move in early, your centre gets thrown out of the draw, not you. Your team then takes the draw with its second choice. A second violation by your team on the same draw can cost you two minutes. Losing your best faceoff player because a winger leaned in early is one of the most costly rules mistakes there is, and it's completely avoidable.

COACH: Centres, win the draw with your stick. Closing your hand on the puck can be a penalty, and in some leagues batting it with your hand to win the draw is a minor. In some leagues, winning the draw with your skates is a violation as well.

COACH: Two more things change how your team plays faceoffs. In some leagues, after your opponents take a penalty or ice the puck, your team picks which dot in their end. Where it does, somebody on your team should be making that choice on purpose. And if your team has just earned a draw in the other end, don't go and join a scrum there. If players from the bench or the points get stuck in, the draw can be moved out to the neutral zone.

PLAYER: Let me do the two nets. Screen from outside the paint, off the red line, head up, without pushing. Never into the goalie, in or out of the crease, and never in their way back. Shoved in, get out and go around. In my own crease, never close my hand on the puck.

PLAYER: Goalies, know whether you have a trapezoid, freeze only under pressure, and stay in your net in a fight. When my stick breaks, drop it. And on the draw, skates outside the circle and behind the hash marks until the puck is down.

COACH: That's it. The last part is the one that happens when play stops.

COACH: Eighteenth idea. Play as though one major ends your game. First, the sizes of penalty, all in one place. A minor is two minutes, with your team a player short. A major is five. A misconduct is ten minutes for you, but a teammate comes straight on, so on its own it doesn't put your team a player short. Being thrown out is the rest of the game, and sometimes more.

PLAYER: So a major's five minutes and I'm back on?

COACH: Often not. In a lot of hockey, one major ends your game. In many leagues, every major comes with being thrown out. In Britain's book, a first major for a long list of fouls throws you out straight away. And in British women's hockey, every major throws you out. Hits from behind, cross-checks, slashes, hooks, high sticks and hits to the head are all on that list. And a second major, or a second misconduct, in one game throws you out too. So play as though one is enough.

PLAYER: Why does that change how I play, rather than just what it costs?

COACH: Because a major leaves your team a player short for five full minutes, and a goal against doesn't end it. Then you're gone for the rest of the game. And it costs more than tonight. Many leagues suspend you for your next game, or report it so you can be banned for longer. In some, you can be banned for something the referee didn't even call.

COACH: Penalties add up as well. In some leagues, as few as three penalties of any kind in one game throw you out. A misconduct often comes with a minor, and then your team is short. So once you're given one, add nothing to it. No word to the referee, and no shove on the way to the box.

COACH: In Britain, wear your neck guard every shift. Outside the Elite League, going on without one is a ten-minute misconduct straight away, with no warning. A second one throws you out.

COACH: And if you play adult recreational hockey in North America, ask whether your league belongs to CARHA, the adult recreational body. Where it does, the rules change what you do. The slap shot is banned outright. Deliberate body contact is a penalty in every game. And almost every major throws you out, and costs you at least your next game. Many adult leagues don't belong, so ask.

COACH: Nineteenth idea. After the whistle. The whistle doesn't end your shift. The scrum around the net and the faceoff both carry on after it, and that's where undisciplined players give away penalties.

PLAYER: The puck's right there after the whistle. Can I just flick it away?

COACH: Leave the puck where it is, or pass it gently to the official. Never shoot it away. That's usually two minutes, and in some leagues it can be more. Shooting it toward an official or the crowd can get you thrown out, and can mean a ban for several games.

COACH: Now picture someone giving you a shove and a punch in that scrum. What would you do?

PLAYER: Everything in me says hit back.

COACH: Don't throw a punch, and don't answer one. Turn away, get to an official or your bench, and let the referee sort it out. A punch with your glove on isn't a mutual two minutes. In some leagues even a punch that misses counts as fighting. Assume a punch gets you thrown out.

COACH: And if a punch lands on your head, come off and say so. You're off for the rest of the day, and a medical professional decides when you play again. Someone should stay with you, and you shouldn't drive or go home alone. If any of the red flags we covered earlier shows up, or a symptom gets worse, or your neck hurts, that's an ambulance, even though you got up.

COACH: Assume a fight gets you thrown out. And stay out of anyone else's fight, even to break it up. In some leagues the first player to join a fight that's already going is thrown out, peacemakers included.

COACH: Do your chin strap up properly. If more than one finger fits between your jaw and the strap, it's too loose. And keep your hands off anybody's helmet, your own included. In some leagues, taking a helmet off in a scuffle, yours or theirs, gets you thrown out, and can mean a ban for several games. Knocking theirs off in a scuffle, or losing your own because the strap was loose, can get you thrown out too. And a scuffle only needs one penalty, so a shove after the whistle can count.

COACH: Never spit at anyone. It can get you thrown out in every league, and in some it means a ban for several games. In some leagues, threatening an official or a player with harm does too.

COACH: Racial slurs, taunts and sexual remarks can get you thrown out in every league, and can mean a ban too. In most leagues an obscene gesture gets you thrown out the first time.

COACH: Don't dive, or make a fall look worse than it was. That's a penalty too. In British hockey outside the Elite League it now comes with a ten-minute misconduct as well, and in Britain's book a second misconduct in a game throws you out.

PLAYER: What if the referee's just got it wrong? Can I say something?

COACH: Leave the talking to your captain. Only the captain, or an alternate when the captain isn't on the ice, can discuss a rule with the referee, and only when invited. Coming off the bench to protest is two minutes. Disputing a call from the penalty box after play restarts gets you ten minutes. And complaining never gets a call reversed. If your captain is invited to talk, ask, don't tell.

COACH: And never put a hand on an official. Deliberate force against one gets you thrown out in every league, and expect a ban on top.

COACH: Twentieth idea. Delayed penalties. Sometimes the referee raises an arm and lets play carry on. That means a penalty is coming against the other team. Play stops when they get control of the puck. Control, not a touch, so a puck off one of their skates doesn't end it.

PLAYER: How do I tell that arm from the offside one?

COACH: Remember whose arm it is. A linesperson's arm at the blue line means offside. The referee's arm means a penalty.

COACH: Because play stops as soon as they control the puck, they can't score against you from open play while the arm is up. So many teams pull their goalie for an extra skater. The goalie skates to the bench, and a sixth attacker jumps on. You're sure to get the puck or a whistle, so there's no risk of them scoring from open play.

PLAYER: So we should always do that?

COACH: It's a tactic, not a rule. In recreational hockey plenty of goalies stay in their net, because the trip is longer than it looks. And a sixth attacker only helps if somebody comes off for the goalie. So agree in the room what your team does. The thing that goes wrong is a goalie setting off while nobody on the ice expects it. Ask which yours plays.

COACH: When the arm goes up and you're the closest player to the bench, and not in the play, you're the one coming off. Then keep the puck. No risky passes across the middle, and no forced shot from a bad angle. A miss that they collect ends your six-on-five, and you get a five-on-four instead. If the zone isn't open, take it back and regroup. They can't force a whistle without taking it from you.

COACH: One way it goes badly wrong is your own team putting the puck into your empty net. That's a goal against you, and the penalty is still given. So with your net empty, no blind passes.

COACH: If you score during the delay, a minor that was coming is wiped out. A major still goes on. And if your team is the one about to be penalised, get control of the puck as fast as you can. In your own end, a hand pass is legal, and it counts.

COACH: Let me pull it all together.

COACH: Before your first game, find out which rulebook your league plays under, and whether your game allows body checking. Play as though one major ends your game, and as though being thrown out costs you more than tonight.

COACH: Never hit anyone in the head or neck. Carry the puck with your head up, and never duck. At the boards, it's the same every time. The boards position. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to anyone coming at you, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly.

COACH: If you can see a player's numbers, whether they have the puck or not, pull up and play the puck. In any chase, go for the puck, not the player, and never hit anyone from behind. Keep your stick on the puck, never on the player, and below your shoulders. Never shove with both hands on it, and never chop.

COACH: Drag your back skate on the blue line. Get the puck past the red line on your stick before you send it away. In your own end, one look, one decision, move it. Don't ice it, or knock the net off, to buy a rest. Screen the goalie from outside the paint, and never go into the goalie. After the whistle, leave the puck alone, keep your hands to yourself, and let your captain do the talking.

COACH: And if a hit lands on your head, come off and say so, and let a medical professional decide when you play again. If a player is down after a knock to the head, don't move them and leave the helmet on. Any one red flag, or a symptom getting worse, means an ambulance.

PLAYER: And the questions for the coach?

COACH: Many of these rules change from league to league, so here are three. Which rulebook do we play under, and is this a checking game? Is our icing hybrid or automatic, and can we ice the puck on the kill at our level? And on a delayed penalty, does our goalie come off, and where does the extra skater go?

COACH: Go and ask. Their answers tell you which rules you play under, and now you know why each one changes how you play.
