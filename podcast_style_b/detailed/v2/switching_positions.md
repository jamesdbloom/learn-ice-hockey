PLAYER: And in a league where checking is allowed?

COACH: The ride still has a limit. The player giving the check is always the one responsible. Sending a player dangerously into the boards is boarding, even when they saw you coming. That includes a player racing for the puck on an icing. Boarding can get you thrown out of the game. For a reckless one, plan on a match penalty, and in some leagues a likely suspension of several games.

COACH: Now the most dangerous moment. You've steered a carrier to the boards. By the time you get there, they're very often shielding the puck, half turned away from you. You can see their numbers. What would you do?

PLAYER: Pull up. Take the puck, not the player.

COACH: Exactly that, and it's the rule that matters most here. A hit from behind is never just two minutes. In almost every league, it throws you out of the game. A hard one on a player standing at the boards throws you out in every league. In some leagues, for that one, plan on a match penalty and a likely suspension of several games. Sometimes the player you hit from behind goes into the boards unable to protect themselves. Then, in several leagues, plan on a match penalty, with no choice for the referee. If the hit reaches their head as well, you take whichever penalty is harsher. So steer with your skating. When you see their numbers, pull up, and play the puck.

PLAYER: And if they've already got past me, and I'm chasing?

COACH: Then your stick goes on the puck and nothing else. A hook, a trip, a hold or a slash from behind that stops a scoring chance gives them a penalty shot. That's a free shot, alone against your goalie. And if your net is empty, the goal can simply be given to them.

COACH: Thirteenth idea. Getting the puck out, and when to go deep. Never shoot the puck over the glass from inside your own end. Off the glass and along the boards is fine. Over it is two minutes in many leagues, including all hockey in Britain, whether you meant it or not. New defencemen take that penalty all the time. And putting the puck out of play on purpose, to stop the game, is two minutes everywhere, from anywhere on the ice.

PLAYER: I'll be icing it more too, won't I?

COACH: More than you used to, yes. In many leagues, the team that ices it can't change lines before the next faceoff, and that faceoff is in its own end. So an icing keeps a tired pair out there, facing a draw by its own net. An injured player can still come off.

COACH: In their end, you have three depths. Just inside the blue line is your default. The top of the faceoff circle is a step down, and you can still get back from there. Below the circles is a commitment you can't recover from. Only go that deep if a forward has already taken your spot at the blue line.

PLAYER: And pinching?

COACH: Pinching means stepping down from the blue line to keep the puck in. Only one defenceman pinches at a time. That's a coaching habit rather than a rule, and coaches do call exceptions. When you pinch, it's stick on the puck, not a hit. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call. If you see their numbers, pull up. And along the boards, the boards position. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to anyone coming at you, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly. If you end up facing the boards, stick and gloves up and spread on the glass, head off it.

COACH: And stop worrying about offence for now. Your shot and your vision will show up on their own once the defending is automatic. Jumping into the rush before you can hold a gap just gets you back to your own blue line late and out of breath.

COACH: Let me pull defence together. Book the power skating first, because a gap you can't hold is a skating problem, not a bad read. Be goal side. Never move the puck across the front of your own net. Go behind it, or high if your team does. In your own end, one look, one decision, move it. Play as a pair, one on the puck and one at the goalmouth, and talk. On a two-on-one, find out whether you sit in the lane or slide. Force the carrier outside with your route, not your body. When you see their numbers, pull up. Never put the puck over the glass from your own end. And go deep in their end only when somebody has taken your spot.

COACH: Fourteenth idea. Moving from defence up to forward. This is the change people overlook. It's often the harder one in your head, because you're unlearning caution you were praised for, for years.

PLAYER: So what's a forward's job, put simply?

COACH: The mirror of what you did. As a defenceman, you closed space down and took options away. A forward finds the space nobody is defending. Arrives in it late and at speed. Makes somebody decide who's taking them.

COACH: The habit that has to go is waiting until the play is obviously safe before you move. Think about it. By the time it's obviously safe, what's happened to the space?

PLAYER: It's gone. Someone's closed it.

COACH: So go to the open ice before it looks open.

COACH: Your old job helps you on the forecheck. Forechecking means pressuring the other team in its own end, to win the puck back. As a defenceman, you read every forecheck that came at you. How many came in. Which side the first one pushed you to. Whether the pressure sealed you in or overcommitted. Now you're the one applying it, and that knowledge is worth a lot.

COACH: The forwards on a forecheck are called F one, F two and F three. They're roles, set by who gets there first. They're not positions, and they're not people. Say the puck goes in deep, and you're first to it. Who's F one?

PLAYER: Me? Even though I'm really a defenceman?

COACH: You, whatever your jersey says. Go. If you hesitate, nobody is F one. And when you go, go for the puck. Stick on the puck, not a hit. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call. If you see their numbers, pull up and play the puck. And at the boards, the boards position. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to anyone coming at you, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly. If you end up facing the boards, stick and gloves up and spread on the glass, head off it.

COACH: How many forwards go in deep is your team's system. A two-one-two sends two in. A one-two-two sends one. And which side F three sits on is a real coaching split. The common default is the far side, away from the puck. The other version, called stacked, puts F three on the puck side. It crowds one corner on purpose, and gives up the pass out to the far side. Ask which one you're running.

PLAYER: And coming back the other way?

COACH: On the backcheck, the usual plan is the centre in the middle and the wingers in the outside lanes. That's a coaching choice, not a law. Some teams send the first player back to the middle instead. Others pick up man-on-man, starting from the puck and working outward. Whichever you play, somebody owns the middle, and nobody assumes it's somebody else.

COACH: Crossing their blue line, keep your trailing skate down on the ice. You're only offside when both skates are completely over the line before the puck. So a trailing skate still touching the line keeps you onside. Keep the blade on the ice, and you're onside wherever you play. If you lift it, some leagues call you offside.

PLAYER: Anything to work on in my skating?

COACH: Your first three strides and your stop-and-start. A forward's shift is one burst after another. Forecheck, reverse, backcheck, reverse again. A defenceman's skating has more gliding and more backward work. Straight-line speed matters less than how fast you get to it.

COACH: And stop worrying about staying above the puck, on your own net's side of it. That was the right instinct, and it isn't yours to have any more. Somebody is above the puck. On most shifts, it isn't you.

COACH: Fifteenth idea. Playing your off wing. That means a left shot playing right wing, or a right shot playing left wing. The puck sits on the inside of your body, toward the middle of the ice. It isn't a demotion or a promotion, and it's more common than new players expect.

PLAYER: Why would a coach put me there?

COACH: Coming into their end with your stick toward the middle, you have more options with the puck. And you get a better forehand shooting angle from the middle of the ice. A winger on their strong side has the stick along the boards, and needs more time to get into a shooting position. On the power play, the off-wing half-wall is standard. The half-wall is the stretch of boards level with the faceoff dot, between the corner and the point. With the puck on your inside there, the cross-ice one-timer stays ready. A one-timer is a shot taken straight off the pass.

PLAYER: What does it cost me?

COACH: In your own end, a breakout pass up the boards now arrives on your backhand. That costs a beat, and it can weaken your exit. So the pass is coming up the boards to your backhand. What would you do?

PLAYER: Turn and face the boards, so I get it on my forehand?

COACH: Don't. Turning to face the boards puts your back to anyone coming at you. Receive it open to the ice. As the puck comes, open your hips toward the ice, so you can see the rink and the puck at once. And take a step or two off the boards. That space lets you skate it, pass to the centre, pass back to your defenceman, or attack the middle. Flat against the boards, you get one of those. If the puck arrives hard, angle a skate to kill it onto your stick, rather than reaching for it.

COACH: And because you're on the boards, the boards position comes with you. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to anyone coming at you, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly. And don't turn to face the boards to find your forehand. Looking first and seeing nobody coming doesn't make any of it safe.

COACH: A defenceman on their off side is different again. Pucks behind your own net, and passes from your partner, now come to your backhand. It's common, and it isn't a disaster.

COACH: Sixteenth idea. Going in goal. Playing in goal is really a different sport that shares the rink. You spend the whole game in one zone, you almost never carry the puck, and you pressure nobody. It isn't a position you drift into for a few weeks to see how it goes.

PLAYER: How different is it?

COACH: The skating, for a start. It's short pushes sideways, in and out along your angle, post to post, and back up onto your feet. Almost all of it is on your inside edges, in a deep stance. And the technique is very hard to correct on your own, because you can't see yourself, and a goal going in doesn't tell you why. So it needs its own coaching from the start.

PLAYER: So how would I try it?

COACH: Before you buy anything, ask your club whether it has house goalie gear and a goalie coach. Go to a goalie session, not a team practice, where you'll just get shot at. And give it a block of weeks, not one night, because the first night tells you little in either direction.

COACH: Now picture a game. Your goalie is hurt and can't carry on, and there's a spare set of pads on the bench. What would you do?

PLAYER: I'd want to just put them on. I'm guessing it's not that simple.

COACH: It isn't. Not on your own. In most leagues, a skater isn't allowed to wear goalie gear at all, and the exceptions are narrow. Where a league doesn't ban it, a skater who goes in without being listed as the goalie gets none of a goalie's protection. Who goes in net is settled between your bench and the officials, on the scoresheet. Never by you grabbing a spare set of pads. Going back out works the same way. It's paperwork, not just a decision.

COACH: Wear a neck guard from your first time in net, whatever your age. Across the UK it's compulsory for every player at every age, adults too. In England and Wales you need it at practice as well, and wear it at practice in Scotland and Northern Ireland too. The adult exemption people quote is American. And in Canadian minor and women's hockey, a throat guard hanging off your mask doesn't count on its own.

COACH: And one thing for every skater, whether or not you ever play in goal. Most goals your team gives up from the front of the net aren't your goalie's fault. They come from skater habits. So learn what your goalie needs from you.

PLAYER: Let me try those last three. Moving up to forward, go to the space before it's obviously safe. First to the puck on the forecheck, I'm F one, so I go, stick on the puck. Trailing skate on the ice at the blue line. On my off wing, take the pass open to the ice, a step off the boards, and if contact's coming, the boards position. And going in goal, a goalie coach and house gear, settle it on the scoresheet, and a neck guard from my first time in net.

COACH: That's it.

COACH: Seventeenth idea. How long it takes. Nobody has measured how long a switch takes, so this is coaching experience, and people vary a lot. A few shifts to stop feeling lost. You'll know where to stand long before you know when to move. A handful of games before the everyday reads are automatic. Who you pick up. Where you go when the puck goes to a particular place. A season before the position feels like yours, not like a job you're doing carefully. And longer, maybe much longer, for forward to defence, because that one depends on skating.

PLAYER: And in the meantime I'll be worse than I was.

COACH: Expect a real dip. New techniques feel worse before they feel better, and a switch is a big version of that. It's a bit like moving house. For weeks you reach for the light switch on the wrong wall, even though you know where it is. The dip will feel like proof you can't play the position. It isn't.

PLAYER: When do people usually give up?

COACH: Coaches see most people give up somewhere between week two and week five, right at the bottom of the dip. That's what coaches see, not a measurement. So judge the switch over at least a couple of months, not a couple of skates. Longer for defence, and longer again for goal. And improvement comes in steps, not a smooth line. Flat spells are normal.

COACH: Eighteenth idea. Asking for a switch, and being moved without asking. Say you want to try centre. When do you ask?

PLAYER: Straight after a good game, while the coach is happy?

COACH: Not then. Not on the bench. Not straight after a game. Not in front of the group. A day or two later, in private. And ask a specific question. What would I need to show you to get a look at centre? That's hard to dodge, and it gives you something to work on. Can I play centre just gets a shrug.

COACH: Ask to try it in practice first. That's much easier for a coach to say yes to than a change on the game sheet, and it costs them nothing. Then do the work where the coach can see it. If the answer is, I need to see you win draws, take draws at the end of every practice. And accept that some answers are about the roster, not about you. A team with four centres and no left wingers is going to keep you on the left.

PLAYER: And if I'm moved without asking? That feels like a punishment.

COACH: It usually isn't one. The commonest reasons are the numbers on the roster, an injury, a line that needs a reliable defensive player, or a spot that suits you better. Some of those are compliments. Moving a forward to defence is often what happens to the player a coach trusts most with the puck in their own end.

COACH: So find out the system before you find out your feelings about it. Ask what a good shift from you looks like in that spot. Coaches rarely get asked, so the answer is more useful than you expect. And if you were told bluntly, that doesn't make the decision wrong.

PLAYER: What if I just want my old spot back?

COACH: Don't play the new position badly to win the old one back. It looks like sulking, even when it isn't. And it's the surest way to make a temporary move permanent. And tell your linemates you're new to it. A line where nobody has said the centre is new will make the same mistake for three games. Thirty seconds of talk before the shift fixes most of it.

COACH: Let me pull it all together.

COACH: When you're moved, your skills come with you. What you rebuild is the map. Where to look, where to drift, and who to pick up.

COACH: Your old good habits are the problem. Name the one you're fighting, out loud, before the shift. Ask your coach which system you're playing, before you play in it. And when you're lost, go to the safe spot and read the play from there.

COACH: At the boards, it's always the boards position. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to anyone coming at you, never duck. At the dot, get low from your knees and hips, never with your head.

COACH: Moving to centre, go to the puck and swing low. Moving to the wing, hold your lane, and stand still on the hash marks. Moving to defence, fix your backward skating first. Be goal side, and never move the puck across the front of your own net. Steer the carrier with your route, not your body, and when you see their numbers, pull up. Moving up to forward, go to the space before it's safe. On your off wing, take the pass open to the ice. And going in goal, settle it on the scoresheet, and wear a neck guard from day one.

COACH: Then expect the dip, and keep going through it.

PLAYER: What should I ask my own coach?

COACH: Three things. What system do we play in our own end? Which forecheck and which breakout do we run? And what does a good shift from me look like in this spot? And one for your league. Is body checking allowed in our games? The first three are your team's choices, not rules. The last one decides whether you may hit anyone at all. Their answers tell you how your team wants you to play, and now you know the reason behind each one.
