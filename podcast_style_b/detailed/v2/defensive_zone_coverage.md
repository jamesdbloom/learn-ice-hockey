PLAYER: What if I push them onto our goalie? Then the goal gets called off, doesn't it?

COACH: Never shove an attacker into your own goalie. Your goalie is usually down, square to the shooter, and can't see the attacker coming. The attacker lands backwards on your goalie, with the post right beside them. That can hurt your goalie, and it can be a penalty on you in every league. And the goal usually still counts, because you pushed them there. The injury part is coaching caution, not a counted figure.

PLAYER: And if the shot comes while I'm standing in my goalie's way?

COACH: Don't try to block it from there. A half block on a shot your goalie can't see is worse than no block, because you change the direction of a puck they never saw. Get out of the line of sight. Or take the inside on the player you're covering, without pushing them, and let the puck through. If you block at all, block from close, about ten feet from the shooter. And the whole method goes together. Close with your feet, don't lunge. Get square, with your shin pads to the puck. Head up, and out of the shot's path. Hands behind your body, backs of the gloves out, stick down beside you. Never lift a leg. Never turn sideways or turn your back on it. And stay on your feet until the puck has left the shooter's stick.

COACH: And talk to your goalie. Screen left. I've got the tip. Your goalie is trying to find the puck through a crowd of legs.

COACH: Eleventh idea. The scramble at your own net. A rebound, a loose puck at the top of the crease, four bodies, two seconds. What's the first thing you go for?

PLAYER: The puck. Get it out of there.

COACH: Body first, puck second. Not a hit. Before you look for the puck, take away the most dangerous stick. A player with a free stick at your doorstep will score before you get to the puck. As the puck arrives, lift that stick. Up, not sideways, low on the shaft, well below the bottom hand. Stop at the stick, and let it go. Or press their blade down to the ice with yours as the shot comes, and let go once the shot is through.

PLAYER: Can't I just hang on to their stick?

COACH: Never hook the stick, keep it pinned, or grab it with your hand. Holding a stick is a penalty, and if it hurts someone, it can get you thrown out. Never at the hands, and never a chop. And no shove with your stick in both hands. That's a cross-check, whatever your blade is doing.

COACH: The bodies there follow the net-front rules. Hold your spot. No push, no lean, and no cross-check, so never your stick across anyone. Never hit or push into a back, and if you see their numbers, pull up. Keep their skates on the ice. And if a battle moves a body there, never let it cross the front of your net. The corner side is the safe one.

COACH: Don't lunge at the puck through your goalie. You'll knock the puck loose, or knock your goalie off their post. Trust your goalie to cover it, and protect them while they do. Move the puck with a low jab of your stick, not a swing.

PLAYER: Why not one big swing to clear it?

COACH: A wild swing at a bouncing puck can catch someone high, and that can be a penalty even by accident. It can get you thrown out. And a hard chop that lands on someone's hand can be slashing, even if you were going for the puck.

COACH: Stay on your feet as long as you can. A defender lying on the ice is out of a scramble that only lasts two seconds. And going down hands and head first at the doorstep puts your hands and head under four sets of sticks and skates. That's coaching caution, not a rule. It's the reason to use your stick.

PLAYER: Can I use my hand?

COACH: For one thing. Knock a loose puck down, or push it along the ice, with an open hand. That's never a penalty. Catching a puck out of the air is fine too, as long as you put it straight down. But never hold it, skate with it, turn with it or throw it. Never close your hand on a loose puck, and never smother it under your body. That can be a penalty, or at least stop play. Outside your crease, if you block a shot and the puck ends up under you without you trying, that's fine. Reaching for it with your hands to keep it there is not.

PLAYER: And inside the crease?

COACH: Much worse. If you hold or cover the puck while it's inside your crease, the other team gets a penalty shot. That's a free shot, alone against your goalie. With your goalie pulled for an extra attacker, in most leagues they're simply given the goal. And in at least one league, standing in your crease when you cover it can cost the same, even with the puck outside. The crease goes all the way up to the top of the net. So don't reach up to hold a puck in the air either. Knock it straight down and play it with your stick. Covering the puck in the crease is your goalie's job, and nobody else's.

COACH: Let me pull the front of your net together. Get there first, and hold your spot with no push, no lean and no cross-check, and never hit or push into a back. If a battle moves a body there, never let it cross the front of your net. The corner side is the safe one. As the puck arrives, lift the stick low, up not sideways, and let go. Never at the hands, and never a chop. Never walk a screener out, and never shove anyone into your goalie. In a scramble, body first, and jab, don't swing. And never hold the puck in your crease.

COACH: Now the plays you'll face most, and what to do about each. Twelfth idea. The cycle. That's the attackers passing the puck around the corner, the half-wall and behind your net. The half-wall is the stretch of boards roughly level with the faceoff dot, between the corner and the point. Each pass goes to a teammate arriving in the space somebody just left. The aim is to make you chase until somebody is out of position.

PLAYER: It's hard not to chase when the puck keeps going round.

COACH: That's exactly how the cycle wins. When you chase, your five get strung out. Let it come to you, and defend the ways out, not where the puck is now. Take away the next pass, because the player arriving is the threat.

PLAYER: Where do I break it, then?

COACH: At the goal line. The dangerous carrier is the one trying to come out from below the goal line to the front of your net. Meet them at the post, not in the corner. Stick on the puck, not a hit. Whether you can hit at all depends on your league, your age group and the game, so find out first. And if you see their numbers, pull up.

COACH: In the collapse, be patient. The house is full, and they aren't threatening you. Time is on your side, so let them grind. In man-on-man or the hybrid, stay on bodies. That means goal side, within a stick's length, with no holding, no hooking and no bumping. Man coverage low is the strongest answer to a cycle.

COACH: Cycles end when somebody wins a puck battle. So send two to the carrier. In the collapse that's the strong-side defenceman and the centre. Stick on the puck, not a hit, and if you see their numbers, pull up. The second one arrives late and unseen. So arrive on the puck, or on the ice they want, never on their back. And arrive under control.

COACH: And whoever ends up on the boards with the puck, the boards position. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to anyone coming at you, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly. If you're already facing the boards, get your stick and gloves up on the glass, spread wide, head off it, and only when things have gone wrong.

COACH: Thirteenth idea. The low-to-high play. They put the puck low, into the corner or behind your net, to drag your coverage down. Then they pass it back up to the point or the high slot, for a shot with your backs turned and traffic in front. It exists to beat the collapse. Coaches call it one of the most common ways teams score now. That's coaching agreement, not a count.

PLAYER: So who stops it?

COACH: The strong-side winger, by being disciplined. Stay high, in the lane, between their defenceman and your net. A winger who gets pulled low has handed them the play for free. And know the trigger. When the puck goes behind your net, expect the pass back up. Wingers start closing on their points as the puck disappears behind the goal line, not when it comes out.

PLAYER: Then I just stand in front of the shot?

COACH: Get out to the shot. Don't sit under it. A winger standing back near the faceoff dot blocks nothing. One closing hard at the top of the circle does. You're closing to block the shot, never to hit the shooter. And the whole method goes together. Close with your feet, don't lunge. Get square, with your shin pads to the puck. Head up, and out of the shot's path. Hands behind your body, backs of the gloves out, stick down beside you. Never lift a leg. Never turn sideways or turn your back on it. And stay on your feet until the puck has left the shooter's stick.

PLAYER: What's everyone else doing while I close out?

COACH: Boxing out. Eyes on the puck, body beside the player in front of you. The shot is only dangerous because of the tip and the rebound, and you can't beat anyone to a rebound you didn't see leave the stick. So get to the inside, then the net-front rules. Hold your spot. No push, no lean, and no cross-check, so never your stick across anyone. Never hit or push into a back, and if you see their numbers, pull up. Keep their skates on the ice. And if a battle moves a body there, never let it cross the front of your net. The corner side is the safe one. Never walk anyone off a spot they already hold. That can be a penalty in any league. And in some leagues you hold that spot without putting your body on them at all, so check yours.

COACH: Fourteenth idea. The back door. While everyone watches the battle in the strong-side corner, an attacker drifts to the far post. One pass across, and it's an open net. Every system fears this chance most. It's worth giving up almost anything else to stop it. You're the weak-side defenceman. Where are your eyes, and where's your body?

PLAYER: Eyes on the puck, I suppose.

COACH: Your eyes follow the puck, but your body stays between that attacker and the net. Use your position, and the net-front rules. Hold your spot. No push, no lean, and no cross-check, so never your stick across anyone. Never hit or push into a back, and if you see their numbers, pull up. Keep their skates on the ice. And if a battle moves a body at your net, never let it cross the front of your net. The corner side is the safe one. If you find yourself facing the corner, you've already lost the attacker.

COACH: Lay your stick flat in the seam across the slot. The pass has to cross the slot, so a stick lying there takes it away even when the passer is open. The weak-side winger helps too, by dropping into the middle rather than standing out at the point looking tidy. A body in the high slot is worth more than perfect point coverage. And call it the moment the attacker appears. Back door! Not when the pass is already on its way.

COACH: Fifteenth idea. The walk-down. Instead of shooting, their defenceman skates down from the blue line toward the top of the circle. That's about thirty-five feet from your goal line. They've roughly halved their distance to your net, and nobody has touched them. From there they can shoot, or feed the slot.

PLAYER: Isn't that the winger's player?

COACH: That's the trouble. This, not the point shot, is the collapse's real weakness. A winger guarding an area will often let them go, because they've walked out of that winger's area and into nobody's. How you answer it is a coaching choice, so know your team's answer before you need it.

PLAYER: What are the usual answers?

COACH: In the collapse, the usual instruction is to follow them a few feet and stay connected, but not to chase them into the corner. Call it loudly, so everyone knows a fourth attacker is arriving low. In man-on-man, you go with them all the way. That's why the walk-down doesn't trouble man-on-man teams. In the hybrid, you pick them up as your player the moment they cross the dots. That's the cleanest answer of the three, and the main reason hybrids exist. Whichever it is, picking them up means staying goal side. Stick on the puck, not a hit. Whether you can hit at all depends on your league, your age group and the game, so find out first. And if you see their numbers, pull up.

COACH: And when a fourth attacker arrives low, you're outnumbered down there. Somebody has to give up their area. It's never the doorstep. Usually the centre takes the extra player, and the weak-side winger takes over the high slot.

COACH: Sixteenth idea, and it's short. Pinned in your own end on a long shift. Forty seconds or more, legs going, and they're working the puck around your boards. This is when the shape breaks, because tired players start chasing.

PLAYER: So what do I do with nothing left in my legs?

COACH: Shrink, don't chase. Stay in your area or on your player, keep your stick in the lane, and let them work the boards. Chasing costs the most energy and opens the most ice. Take the first clean chance to get it out, and get it out where it stays out. Off the glass and out, or chipped into the neutral zone, the middle of the rink between the blue lines. Not fired the length of the ice.

PLAYER: Why not just fire it down? At least it's gone.

COACH: A long, flat clear can be icing, and an icing brings the faceoff straight back to your end. In some leagues your tired line can't change for that faceoff, so check yours. A hurt player can always be replaced.

COACH: Change only once the puck is out and heading the other way. A change with the puck loose in your end is one of the commonest ways to give up an odd-man rush. That means more attackers coming at you than you have back. The common approach is that whoever is nearest the bench goes first as the puck leaves. Some coaches want it dumped deep before anyone moves. So ask your bench which yours wants. And say it as the puck leaves. Out, change!

COACH: Seventeenth idea. When the system breaks. A bad change, a puck bouncing off shin pads, three of you below the goal line and nobody where the plan says. What do most players do?

PLAYER: Freeze. I know I have.

COACH: And freezing is what turns a scramble into a goal. So here's the order. Work down it, and don't skip a step. One. Bodies to the front of the net, right away. Not to the puck. The most dangerous thing in a scramble is an attacker free at your doorstep. If you're nearest and unsure, go and stand between your goalie and the nearest opponent.

COACH: Two. Sticks in lanes. If you can't reach anyone, lay your stick flat in the passing lane through the slot. Broken plays are decided by passes that should never have got through. Three. Find the most dangerous free attacker. Look at the house, not the puck. Ask who could score in the next two seconds, and go and stand with them. It's almost never the one with the puck.

COACH: Four. Count. Outnumbered down low? Somebody high comes down, and says so. Even numbers? Match up, loudly. I've got nineteen. You take the net. Five. Get the puck out any way you can that keeps it in the rink. Off the glass, never over it, around the boards, off a shin pad. A clear that gives the puck away has still bought your team a line change, and a reset.

COACH: Six. Reset before you attack. The most common second goal is the one given up straight after surviving the first, because everyone relaxed while three attackers were still in your end. And the habit underneath all of it. The moment you don't know who's yours, go to the middle.

PLAYER: Let me try the plays. Against the cycle, don't chase, send two, and never hit or push into a back. Against the low-to-high, the strong-side winger stays high and gets out to the shot. Against the back door, body between the attacker and the net, and stick in the seam. Against the walk-down, know our team's answer. And when it all breaks, bodies to the net, sticks in lanes, and go to the middle.

COACH: Spot on. Last part. Getting the puck out.

COACH: Eighteenth idea. Win it, and leave as a group. Picture yourself in that corner battle. The puck is about to squirt loose, and you're the one who'll get it. When do you decide what to do with it?

PLAYER: Once I've got it?

COACH: Before it's yours. Scan while the battle is still going. Then when the puck arrives, you already know whether it's going to the winger, the centre, or off the glass. Taking the puck and only then starting to look is how a zone exit gets turned over. In your own end, don't hang on to it while you think. One look, one decision, move it.

PLAYER: And if it's on my stick and I've got no idea where it's going?

COACH: That's where the panic pass comes from. A panic pass is a pass made without looking, just to get rid of the puck, and in your own end it usually goes straight to them. The fix is the early look. Get one more look before the puck arrives, so you know the answer before it gets to you. Then one look, one decision, move it, to a teammate you've seen or off the glass and out. And never across the front of your own goal.

COACH: In the collapse, your wingers are high while the other team has the puck. As you win it, they come down the boards to between the hash marks and the goal line. That's where the pass goes, not the blue line.

COACH: Now say you're the defenceman going back for a puck at the boards. Then you're the one with your head over the puck and a forechecker coming behind you. A forechecker is an opponent chasing to win the puck back in your end. This holds in every league, checking or not. The boards position. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to anyone coming at you, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly. If you're already facing the boards, get your stick and gloves up on the glass, spread wide, head off it, and only when things have gone wrong.

COACH: Then pick your exit, in this order. First, a controlled exit, a pass and out with the puck, whenever it's really there. Second, a safe exit without the puck. Rim it, which means sending it hard around the boards to the next teammate along. Or chip it off the glass. Either way there's no chance against you, and your team gets to change. That's a good play, not a failure. Third, when it's bad, anything that ends the shift.

PLAYER: Should the forwards head up the ice as soon as we win it?

COACH: Leave as a group. Forwards leaving early is how a cleared zone turns into an odd-man rush the other way. The puck should be out of the zone before your third forward is. Some breakouts send a forward early on purpose, with names like a centre fly, a slash or a stretch. Find out if yours does, before you treat an early exit as a mistake.

COACH: Nineteenth idea, and it's short. How you clear the puck. Use the glass, and keep the puck in the rink. Off the glass and out is a good play. So is a puck that dies in the neutral zone.

PLAYER: What's wrong with just firing it out over the glass?

COACH: Putting it straight over the glass from your own end is two minutes in some leagues, even by accident, and that includes hockey in Britain. Flipping it out on purpose, from anywhere, is two minutes in every league. So aim high and wide off the glass, never over it.

COACH: Let me pull it all together.

COACH: Find out your system, and ask the corner question. Defend the middle before the outside, and stay goal side. Somebody always has the doorstep, and you both know who. Say what you're about to do, early. Don't chase. Take away the next pass with your stick flat in the lane. When you don't know who's yours, go to the middle.

COACH: At your own net, get there first and hold your spot, with no push, no lean and no cross-check, and never hit or push into a back. If a battle moves a body at your net, never let it cross the front. The corner side is the safe one. Lift the stick low, up not sideways, and let go. Never walk a screener out, and never shove anyone into your goalie. In the corner, pin the puck, not the player. If you see their numbers, pull up.

COACH: When you win it, decide before it's yours. One look, one decision, move it. Never pass it or skate it across the front of your own goal, and leave as a group. At the boards, the boards position. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to anyone coming at you, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly. Clear it off the glass, never over it. And at your own crease, knock the puck down and play it with your stick.

PLAYER: What should I ask my own coach?

COACH: Three things, and one for your league. When the puck is in our corner, does the winger stay up or come down? Who has the doorstep when both defencemen go low? What do we do when their defenceman walks down from the point? And for your league, is body checking allowed in our games? The first three are your team's choices, not rules. The last one decides whether you can hit at all. Their answers tell you where to stand, and now you know the reason behind each one.
