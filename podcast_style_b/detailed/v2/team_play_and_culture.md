COACH: The cost, kept short. In most leagues, just disputing a call is a two-minute penalty. A complaint about a penalty is not a question about the rules, and in most leagues it draws a penalty of its own. And coming off the bench to argue can be a penalty in its own right, so stay on the bench.

PLAYER: Do you get a warning first?

COACH: Don't count on one. Some things skip straight to the top. Never put a hand on an official. Never spit at one, swear at one, use a slur at one, or make an obscene gesture. Never throw anything toward one. And never leave the penalty box to argue. Any of those can end your game with no warning, and putting a hand on an official or spitting can mean a ban for games after this one, too.

COACH: And keep your stick off the boards. Tapping the boards from the bench is the bench's applause, for a goal or a good shift, so keep it a tap. Never bang the boards or the glass at a call, or to cheer a hit. That can be a penalty too.

COACH: The one most likely to catch you comes just after you've been called. When an official tells you to go to the penalty box, go straight there. And stay out of the referee's crease, the marked area where the referee stands to report the call. Get either wrong and it can add a misconduct penalty on top, with no warning.

COACH: That's keeping your head. Give it back to me.

PLAYER: After a soft goal, say "next one" and play a simple shift. Keep giving a struggling teammate the puck, and an easy one first. Keep my frustration off my face, and sort it out after the game, one play, once. Say nothing to the referee, and let the captain ask one calm question. Never touch, spit at or gesture at an official. And when I'm told to go to the box, go straight there.

COACH: Spot on. Now the customs of the game.

COACH: Fifteenth idea. The handshake line, after the game. Whether it happens at all is a custom, not a rule. A referee can call it off after a rough game, to keep the two teams apart, not to punish them.

PLAYER: Is there a right way to do it?

COACH: How you go through it is what matters, and it's simple. Helmet on and done up properly. Gloves and stick left at the bench. Shake every hand, say "good game", and keep moving. Say nothing else, and keep your hands to yourself. Check your chin strap at the bench before you go. If more than one finger fits between your jaw and the strap, it isn't done up properly.

PLAYER: Why does the helmet matter once the game's over?

COACH: Because the game isn't over for the rules just because the buzzer went. In some leagues going down the line without a helmet is a penalty on its own. And if anything starts, even just a shoving match, a helmet coming off can get you thrown out of the game. That means taking your own off, pulling theirs off, or losing yours because it wasn't done up. In some leagues, pulling a helmet off, yours or theirs, also means a ban, likely for several games, and grabbing their cage can mean one too. So keep your hands off their head, which means their helmet, their cage and their chin strap.

PLAYER: What if it does go up in the line?

COACH: Back off, and let the officials in first. In most leagues a fight there ends your game, and it can mean a ban for games after this one. And hold back however obvious it looks that somebody should pull them apart. Going in to break it up can get you thrown out in every league, even if you were only trying to make peace. In some leagues it's automatic for the first player in. Then go where the referee sends you, and leave your stick and gloves alone. They go back to the bench, not onto the ice. If you're watching from the bench, stay on it. Coming over the boards after the handshake costs the same as it would in the first period.

PLAYER: And if someone says something to me on the way down?

COACH: Say nothing back. Abusive words after the game can get you thrown out. A slur, any hateful or discriminatory word, can get you thrown out wherever you are in the rink, before, during or after the game. In some leagues it means a ban of at least one game, often several. A shove after the whistle can be a penalty too, and a reckless one can get you thrown out and banned for games after this one. So shake every hand, say "good game", and keep moving. If you lost badly, or something happened in the game, that's exactly when doing it well matters.

COACH: Sixteenth idea. Your own goalie, in the warm-up and in drills. In the warm-up, don't shoot at your own goalie's head. In a drill, don't fire pucks at them from ten feet. Aim at the pads, the glove and the blocker, and shoot from where the drill puts you.

PLAYER: Nobody means to go high, though.

COACH: That's exactly how it happens so often. The warm-up is where shooters are loosest and least aimed. And in a drill line, the distance quietly shrinks each time round.

PLAYER: Is it against the rules?

COACH: No rule makes shooting at your own goalie an offence, and that's a reason to keep to it, not to relax it. Play only stops for a hurt goalie once an official judges that the goalie is hurt. A cage hides most of what they'd judge it by. So a teammate who doesn't shoot there is the one protection that doesn't depend on somebody noticing.

COACH: And stay in your own end during the warm-up. Every league requires it. Shooting at the other team's goalie then is what usually starts a fight. It can cost you a penalty on the spot, or the league can decide afterwards whether you play next week.

COACH: Seventeenth idea. Their goalie, and their crease. After a whistle, don't skate through their crease, don't stand in it, and don't flip the puck at their net.

PLAYER: What's the harm, if play's stopped?

COACH: Goalies treat that space as theirs, and standing in it after a whistle is the standard way to start something. Flipping a puck at the net after the whistle can be a penalty as well.

PLAYER: But I'm meant to screen during play.

COACH: During play, screening the goalie, standing in their line of sight, is normally fine. Do it from outside the crease, with your skates out of the blue paint, and without contact. Hold your spot without leaning or shoving, and never put your stick across anyone. And keep your head up, chin off your chest. The goal post can hurt you the same way the boards can. Getting into the crease, or stopping the goalie moving in it, is what costs your team the goal. And never go into the goalie. Any contact with a goalie can be a penalty, and it can get you thrown out of the game. A goalie who is down or turned can't protect themselves, so contact can hurt them.

COACH: Now a moment people get wrong. Their goalie has come out of the crease to play the puck, and is skating back to the net. You're standing in the way. What would you do?

PLAYER: Stand still? If I don't move, it's not my fault.

COACH: Standing still doesn't automatically win you the call. Get out of their lane, even if you never touch them. Moving into their path can be a penalty, and it can cost you the goal. In some leagues it can get you thrown out. Just get out of the way.

COACH: That's the customs. Let's hear them.

PLAYER: In the handshake line, helmet on and done up, gloves and stick at the bench, hands off their head, and nothing but "good game". If anything starts, back off and let the officials in. Shoot at my own goalie's pads, glove and blocker, never the head. Stay in my own end in the warm-up. After a whistle, stay out of their crease. Never into the goalie. And get out of the way of their goalie going back to the net.

COACH: That's all of it. Now rec hockey.

COACH: Eighteenth idea. Rec and beer league hockey, where most adults play. A single bench can hold a former junior player, someone who learned to skate at thirty-five, and everyone in between.

PLAYER: So how does that change what I do?

COACH: Adjust your pass to the person receiving it. A hard, flat pass to someone who can't take it isn't a good pass. It's a turnover you caused. Cover more ice than your job if you can. If you're the strongest skater on your line, you're the one who backchecks. Backcheck for the puck, not the player. Whether you can hit at all depends on your league, your age group and the game, so find out first. Never hit a player who can't see you coming, never from behind, and if you can see their numbers, pull up.

PLAYER: Can I help the weaker ones out from the bench?

COACH: Don't coach your teammates from the bench. Nobody paid league fees to be taught by a teammate. If someone asks, help. Otherwise, tell them what you'll do, not what they should.

COACH: There's one sentence that sums up most of rec hockey. Everyone has work in the morning. A late game isn't worth an injury. Nobody is getting a contract. So no retaliation, because retaliation is what makes things worse, every time. Never slash. Never shove anyone with both hands on your stick, and keeping your blade on the ice doesn't make it legal. Sticks down, blades on the ice, and no follow-through above the waist.

COACH: Let the referee referee. They're often a teenager or a volunteer, on their fourth game of the night. And be somebody people are pleased to see on the ice. Rec teams fill their rosters by word of mouth, so that's how you get invited back.

COACH: Nineteenth idea. Find out whether you may body check at all, and what non-check really means. Body checking is using your body to knock a player off the puck. Some games allow it and many don't. A game that doesn't is called non-check.

PLAYER: Isn't that just down to age?

COACH: Your age alone doesn't answer it. It depends on which category your league plays in. And in some leagues it can change from one game to the next, depending on who you're playing. So ask your league, and get the answer in writing. In a non-check game, a body check is a penalty however gently it lands. If the referee judges it reckless, it can get you thrown out, and it can mean a ban for games after this one.

PLAYER: So non-check means no contact at all?

COACH: That's the part that causes most of the arguments. Non-check does not mean no contact. Some contact is part of the game everywhere. You may hold the ice you already have, meaning ice you got to first, not ice you step into their path to take. And you may angle a player off. Angling means steering a player with your skating route, not your body. Pick your line early, keep it, and add nothing when you arrive.

COACH: So here's what you never do in a non-check game. Never make contact without a try for the puck. Never step or glide into a player. Never throw a hip, a shoulder, a forearm or your body into them. Never use the boards to push or pin them, even while you're going for the puck. No shove, and never your stick across anyone. Never into anyone's back. If you can see their numbers, pull up. And remember that even going slowly, a crash into the boards can hurt. And finishing the check, hitting someone to punish them rather than to win the puck, has no place in a non-check game.

PLAYER: What about leaning on someone while we both go for the puck?

COACH: That's where leagues differ. Some allow it. Some don't. In some Canadian adult rec leagues, called CARHA leagues, you never use your body on anyone. There, don't lean, and don't plant yourself in a player's path. Get out of the way of contact you can avoid. And when your team has the puck, don't skate into a defender's way to free up your carrier. In British women's league hockey, the WNIHL, play it as no body checking at all. So unless you know your league allows leaning, don't lean. And even where it's allowed, it's only while you're both playing the puck. Never lean on a player who has already passed or shot it.

COACH: Whatever your league, there's one custom on top of the rules. How physical a battle gets is set by the least willing player in it, not the most.

COACH: Twentieth idea. When the game is badly one-sided. Say you're playing a much weaker team, and you're well ahead. What would you do?

PLAYER: Take my foot off the gas?

COACH: Not quite the way you mean. There's no rule against scoring, though many local leagues have a mercy rule, so check yours. The custom, once the game is out of hand, is to stop trying to add to the margin. Roll all your lines evenly, and give the least-used players more shifts. Stop forechecking hard, chasing the puck in their end, and play in the neutral zone instead, the middle of the ice between the blue lines. Work on things, like completing passes and cycling the puck, working it along the boards in their end, rather than hunting chances. Don't celebrate goals showily. And don't pull anyone's stick, and don't hit anyone you don't need to.

PLAYER: That's not easing off?

COACH: What you must not do is visibly ease off. That insults the other team more than a goal does. The point isn't to play badly. It's to stop chasing a bigger score.

PLAYER: And when we're the weaker team?

COACH: Keep it simple. Keep your shifts short, and take away the middle. Trying to trade rushes with a much better team is how a close game turns into a rout. And whichever side of the mismatch you're on, keep your stick down, and remember that whether you can hit at all depends on your league, your age group and the game. A mismatch is exactly when injuries happen.

COACH: That's rec hockey. Give it back.

PLAYER: Adjust my pass to the person receiving it. If I'm the strongest skater, I backcheck, for the puck, not the player. Whether I can hit at all depends on the league, and if I see their numbers, pull up. Never slash, and never shove with both hands on my stick. Find out whether our game is checking or non-check, and get it in writing. In a non-check game, hold my ice, angle with my skating, and never step into anyone. And in a one-sided game, stop adding to the margin without visibly easing off.

COACH: Spot on. Last part, being new to a team, and leading.

COACH: Twenty-first idea. Being new to a team. Being reliable matters more than skill, and it isn't close. Your skill decides which line you play on. Whether people can count on you decides whether you're on the team next season.

PLAYER: So what does reliable look like, before I've even played?

COACH: Turn up on time, every time. In hockey, on time means dressed and ready before the stated time, not arriving at it. That's custom, not a rule. But the ice is paid for, and it starts on the minute. If you'll be late or absent, tell someone. Answer the team's messages quickly. And pay your fees before you're asked.

PLAYER: And in the dressing room?

COACH: Say hello when you walk in. One sentence. Listen more than you talk for the first few weeks, because they're for finding out how this team does things. Don't criticise anything, not the systems, not the lines, not the goalie. Don't compare it to your old team. Ever. And leave the goalies alone in the last stretch before the warm-up. Many of them want that time to get their head right.

COACH: In your first week, ask what the systems are. What forecheck, what coverage in your own end, and who calls the breakout. Then play them, even if you think a different one is better. Ask which bench door is on and which is off, and where you sit.

PLAYER: Should I try to show what I can do early on?

COACH: Play simple for the first few games. Complete your passes, and finish your shifts. A team forms its first impression of you fast, and safe is a better first impression than flashy. Say yes to the drink after the game, at least sometimes. Half of a team's shared assumptions get built there. And don't apologise for every mistake. Make the mistake, reset, next shift.

COACH: Twenty-second idea. Leading, with or without a letter. The captain's real job is mostly off the scoresheet. They set the standard for what a normal shift looks like. Not the best shift, the normal one, because teams match their captain's floor, not their ceiling. They're the link to the coach. They handle the officials, so nobody else has to. And they notice who isn't talking.

PLAYER: I'm not a captain, though.

COACH: Most leadership on a team has no letter at all. Think about who really shapes a room. The player who is always first dressed and last off the ice. The one who talks on the ice. The one who welcomes new people. The one who doesn't sulk after a bad shift, because a mood spreads both ways. And the one who does the unglamorous jobs without being asked, and never mentions it. Taking the faceoff in their own end. Killing the last minute of a penalty. None of that needs skill, or being senior, or permission. If you want to lead, be reliable where everyone can see it.

COACH: Twenty-third idea. What coaches notice, and how to ask them things.

PLAYER: What are they actually watching?

COACH: Coaches, in their experience, watch effort and positioning far more than skill. That's coaching opinion, not a measured fact. The reasoning goes like this. Skill hardly changes within a season, and a coach can see it in the first ten minutes. Effort and position are what a coach can change, so that's what they watch for.

COACH: So they notice whether you backcheck all the way, or coast in at the top of the circles. Backcheck for the puck, not the player. Whether you can hit at all depends on your league, your age group and the game, and if you can see their numbers, pull up. They notice where you are when the puck is on the other side of the ice, because that shows whether you understand the system. They notice how long your shifts are. A player who changes at forty seconds is easier to use than a better one who stays out for ninety. They notice whether your head is up on the bench. And whether a bad shift changes your next one.

PLAYER: And if I want feedback?

COACH: Ask at a good time. After practice, not during a game, and not just after a loss. Ask a narrow question. "How am I doing?" gets you "yeah, good". Ask about a specific shift instead. "Third period, the shift where they scored, where should I have been?" Ask what would get you more ice time. Then do it, and don't mention that you're doing it.

PLAYER: What about asking why we play the system we do?

COACH: When you ask about systems, ask what and when, not why not something else. Asking whether the coach wants you to chip it or hold it when their defenceman steps up is a question. Asking whether a different system would work better is a challenge, whether you meant it as one or not. And accept the answer. You can disagree, and you still play the system.

COACH: Let me pull it all together.

COACH: When a teammate leaves their spot to go after the puck, fill it, and get back to yours when somebody has filled it. That's what trust looks like on the ice.

COACH: Treat the bench as a working position. Know the game, name the player you're replacing out loud, and step on only once they're at the bench and out of the play. Change while you have legs, never while they have the puck in your end, and don't ice it to get off.

COACH: Talk early, in your team's words. Learn your linemates' habits, adapt to them, and be in the same place every time.

COACH: Keep giving a struggling teammate the puck, and keep your frustration off your face. Never retaliate, never leave the bench during a fight, and stay in the box until an official lets you out. Plan on a fight ending your game. Say nothing to the referee, and never touch one.

COACH: In the handshake line, helmet on and done up, hands off their head, and back off if anything starts. Shoot at your own goalie's pads, never the head, and never go into theirs. In a non-check game, play the puck, never step into anyone, and find out whether your league allows leaning before you lean.

COACH: And when someone is down, treat a head knock as a possible neck injury. Somebody phones for an ambulance. Nobody moves them except to keep their airway clear, and their helmet stays on. Any one of the red flags we went through means an ambulance straight away, even if it seems mild. After any knock to the head, they're done for the day, a medical professional decides when they play again, and they don't drive or go home alone. If anything gets worse, call an ambulance, even if they got up.

PLAYER: What should I ask my own coach?

COACH: Three things, because these are your team's choices, not rules. Which system do we play, and which spot do I fill when a teammate goes? Who on our team talks to the referee? And can we agree, out loud, before the season, that nobody leaves the bench?

PLAYER: And anything for the league?

COACH: One, because only they can answer it. Is our game checking or non-check, and is leaning allowed? Their answers tell you how your team wants you to play together, and now you know the reason behind each one.
