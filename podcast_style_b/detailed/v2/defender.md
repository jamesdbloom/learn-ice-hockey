COACH: Then expect it to take time. Moving to defence is a rebuild of your skating, not just a set of new decisions.

PLAYER: Which way should I pivot?

COACH: This one has a real choice in it. The usual coaching default is to pivot toward the boards side. When you have to turn, you open up toward the boards, not toward the middle. If you're beaten, you're beaten toward the wall, not into your own slot. Some coaches want a middle pivot in certain situations. One is when turning away would take your eyes off the puck. Another is when you have help on the inside, and the bigger danger is the attacker cutting in behind you. So ask which your coach wants, and practise both. The right pivot depends on where the attacker is, not on which way you happen to be good at.

COACH: Eleventh idea. Forcing a carrier outside, and where it stops. Forcing a carrier outside means steering them toward the boards with the route you skate. It doesn't mean hitting them into the boards. The boards take away their ice, not them.

PLAYER: So once they're on the wall, can I lean on them?

COACH: No bump, no push, no lean, and no ride along the boards. Never your stick across them. Play the puck. Steering with your route is allowed everywhere. Riding a player along the boards with your body is a different act. Where checking isn't allowed, that ride is an illegal check. And checking isn't allowed in a lot of age groups and leagues. So check the game, not the age group. A team old enough to check can be in a no-checking game for the night, just because of who it's playing.

PLAYER: And where checking is allowed?

COACH: The ride still has a limit. Put a player dangerously into the boards and it's boarding. That includes a player racing you for a loose puck shot down the ice, who can see you perfectly well. Boarding can be far more than two minutes, and it can get you thrown out of the game. In women's hockey, the rulebook spells out where steering stops. Steer with your skating only. Stepping or gliding into a player at the end of it is a penalty. And using the boards to pin a player isn't allowed either.

COACH: Now the trap hidden in this idea. Forcing a carrier outside turns them. They shield the puck on the boards, so by the time you arrive their back is half turned to you. What would you do?

PLAYER: Finish the check. They're right there.

COACH: Finish a check on their back, on the numbers on their jersey, and that's checking from behind. So when you can see their numbers, pull up. Take the puck, not the player. Your steering has already done its work, and the boards are holding them there for your stick. Don't finish the hit because they turned into you, either. In some leagues the hit can still be a penalty, so it has to be fine on its own. A hit from behind gets you thrown out of the game in most leagues. If you drive them into the boards when they can't protect themselves, you'll almost certainly be thrown out. In some leagues that's automatically a match penalty. Never lift anyone off their feet against the boards. And even going slowly, a crash into the boards can hurt.

COACH: Twelfth idea. Stepping up and pinching are two different plays. You step up in the neutral zone, the middle of the ice between the two blue lines, or at your own blue line. You skate forward to meet an attacker early, instead of backing up. You close the space, and you never finish it as a hit. Miss, and what's behind you?

PLAYER: Open ice.

COACH: And your partner, alone against two attackers. That's called a two-on-one. You pinch at their blue line. You step down into their end to stop the puck coming out. Miss, and the puck is behind you, going the other way. But your forwards are still in their end to help. So a pinch is usually the safer of the two. But safer here only means less chance of a rush back at your net. It says nothing about anyone's body.

PLAYER: When should I step up, then?

COACH: Only when you're confident you can win the puck or force a turnover. Confident, not hoping. Otherwise, back up under control, so they can't get into your end cleanly. How confident is enough is your coach's call, and it depends on your team's system. A team that presses in the neutral zone wants you challenging at the line. A team playing a trap, which sits back and clogs the middle of the ice, wants you holding and steering the carrier wide. Ask which risk your coach wants to take.

PLAYER: And when I do step up, how hard do I go in?

COACH: A step-up closes the space. It isn't a hit. Your feet keep moving, you don't speed up into them, and the attacker stays in front of you. Close the space, and play the puck. Now picture a carrier coming at you with their head down.

PLAYER: That's a free hit, surely.

COACH: It isn't. A carrier with their head down can't change direction, so you can get to the spot in front of them first. That's all it tells you. It isn't a chance to hit them. Speed up into them instead, and it's charging, which means building up speed to drive into a player. Charging counts whether your league allows checking or not. No number of strides makes it safe, and it can get you thrown out of the game.

COACH: Thirteenth idea. Defending the rush, when they attack up the ice at you. A rush where the attackers outnumber the defenders coming back is called an odd-man rush. There are three situations you need to have automatic. One attacker against you is a one-on-one. That's gap control. Force them outside, keep your stick in the passing lane, and don't lunge. Steer them to a bad angle for a shot, rather than going for a hit. Take the angle, not the player.

COACH: Two attackers against you alone is the two-on-one. One has the puck, one is wide. What would you do?

PLAYER: Go at the puck carrier?

COACH: That's the instinct, and it's usually wrong. The usual way is to take away the pass and leave the shot to your goalie. Sit in the passing lane between the two attackers, with your stick flat on the ice on the passing side. Stay on your feet until the pass has gone.

PLAYER: So I'm letting them shoot?

COACH: On purpose. Your goalie can see the shot, and is set for it. A pass across to an open forward is a chance your goalie can't stop. So you take away the thing your goalie can't handle, and leave the thing they can.

PLAYER: I've seen defencemen slide across on the ice.

COACH: That's the other real option, a timed slide. You slide flat across the passing lane, to take the pass away and force the shot. Go too early, and you're out of the play and can't get to the rebound. Even when your coach asks for the slide, it can cost you. A slide that takes the attacker down is tripping, and in most leagues getting to the puck first doesn't excuse it. A slide that catches them low, in the legs, can be called clipping, and clipping can get you thrown out of the game. And never slide into anyone from behind. That's checking from behind, and it can get you thrown out. If you do go down, keep your head up and out of the shooting lane. A slide across a passing lane isn't the same move as dropping to block a shot. This episode doesn't teach how to go down for a slide, so ask the coach who wants it to show you.

COACH: So ask your coach which way your team plays the two-on-one, and make sure your goalie gives the same answer. One exception. Say a teammate racing back, called a backchecker, has picked up the second attacker. The shooter is walking in with nobody on them and no real pass to make. Then it isn't a two-on-one any more. Close on the puck.

PLAYER: And three of them against the two of us?

COACH: A three-on-two. This one really has no single answer. There are two named ways to play it. The common one is called two wide, middle to the backchecker. Each defenceman takes an outside attacker. Nobody chases the carrier. The middle attacker belongs to whoever is backchecking down the middle, usually your centre. The other is called strong side takes the puck, weak side splits. The defenceman on the puck side plays the carrier one-on-one. The other drops back between the middle attacker and the far one, sitting in the passing lane, the way you would on a two-on-one.

PLAYER: Why would a team pick one over the other?

COACH: Each one gives something up. The first gives up the middle if the backchecker doesn't arrive. The second never gives up the middle, but it gives the far attacker more room. So ask which your team plays. Two defencemen playing different versions on the same rush is worse than either one. Whichever it is, back up together, and keep the middle closed as long as you can. And under all three of those sits the first idea. Stay on the defensive side of the puck.

COACH: Fourteenth idea. The neutral zone, with your own team going forward. When you have the puck with space, carry it with confidence and look to pass to a forward. Or chip it off the boards ahead of a forward skating onto it. Under pressure, move it quickly. A bank off the boards, a pass to a forward, or a dump into their end are all good options. Don't try to carry it through heavy pressure, and don't stickhandle through the middle.

PLAYER: Even if I'm decent at stickhandling?

COACH: Even then. The middle of the ice is an expensive place to lose the puck. Coaches say that's where odd-man rushes against you get made. That's coaching agreement, not a count, and not every one starts there. So simple and safe beats clever and risky there. When in doubt, go simple.

PLAYER: What if every forward is covered?

COACH: Pass it across to your partner. That's called a regroup. It resets the play. It gives your forwards time to swing back and come again with speed. And it makes their forecheckers turn and skate. Forcing a pass into a lane that's already shut is how a turnover becomes a rush against you, with you as the last player back. Where your forwards swing on a regroup is a coaching choice, so ask what your team runs.

COACH: When a forward has the puck, you and your partner hold your own blue line. One of you on each side of the middle, roughly between the two faceoff dots in the neutral zone. You're the safety valve, the pass back if a forward needs one. Keep your stick ready to cut out passes in your lane. And don't drift too far forward. If your team loses the puck, you need to be in place to stop the rush.

COACH: Let me pull the middle of the ice together. Win the gap with your feet, and pivot late. Steer the carrier wide with your skating. Don't ride them into the boards, and if you see their numbers, pull up. Step up only when you're sure, and close the space, never charge. On a two-on-one, stay on your feet in the passing lane until the pass has gone, unless your coach wants the slide. On a three-on-two, know which version your team plays. And with the puck, keep it simple through the middle.

COACH: Fifteenth idea. Their end. In their end you have two jobs at once. Keep the puck in, and stay able to get back. Keeping it in comes first. Hold the blue line, and don't panic and back off it. If they try to clear it up the boards, step in and stop it. And keep your own skates inside the blue line too. If you drift out while the puck stays in, you're no longer an option for your forwards.

PLAYER: Why does the line matter so much?

COACH: Lose the blue line, or get caught deep in their end, and they get out cleanly. So if you really can't hold the line, get out and reset rather than gamble. Under heavy pressure, a pass back to your partner, or a chip out of the zone, resets the play.

COACH: Now the shot from the point. You've got the puck at the line, and there's traffic in front. What's your first decision?

PLAYER: Where to aim?

COACH: Close, but it comes before that. The first decision isn't the shot. It's the lane, a clear path to the net. Walk the line, which means skating sideways along the blue line, to open up a better angle. Once you have a lane, get the puck through rather than shooting it hard. Keep it on net at knee height or below, with enough pace that a small touch still carries it.

PLAYER: Not hard? I thought a big shot was the whole idea from out there.

COACH: A shot from the point is there to make a tip, a rebound or a scramble, not to beat the goalie clean. A low shot lets your teammate at the net tip it, and the rebound comes out to them, not into the goalie's glove. That's coaching know-how, not a rule.

PLAYER: And if there's no lane?

COACH: Move the puck until there is one. Pass across to your partner, or down to the half-wall and back. The half-wall is the stretch of boards roughly level with the faceoff dot, between the corner and the point. You can also pass to a winger in the faceoff circle, or set up your partner for a one-timer. A one-timer is a shot straight off a pass, without stopping the puck. If you play adult rec hockey, ask whether your league bans slap shots. At least one adult rulebook counts a one-timer with a big backswing as a slap shot, and a hard rim with a big backswing too.

COACH: Now picture three depths in their end. Just inside the blue line is the normal spot. The top of the faceoff circle, the edge nearest the blue line, is more active. The strong-side defenceman can step down there to support a cycle, where your forwards keep the puck by passing it round the boards and behind the net. Only do it when there's no immediate threat of a turnover. From there, you can still get back.

PLAYER: And deeper than that?

COACH: Below the faceoff circles is always a commitment. Down there you're a fourth forward. If the puck turns over, you can't get back. That's fine if somebody has already taken your spot at the blue line. If nobody has, it's an odd-man rush against you. So only one of you goes below the puck at a time. Below the puck means deeper into their end than the puck is. The other one holds the blue line. If you both go in and the puck turns over, there's nobody in position.

PLAYER: Is that a rule?

COACH: Nearly every coach teaches it, but it isn't a rule, and coaches do call exceptions. A full-ice press is one. Pulling your goalie for an extra attacker is another. Unless you've been told one is on, assume it.

COACH: Sixteenth idea. When they win the puck back in their end. Their player picks up a loose puck down low, and you're at the line. What would you do?

PLAYER: Jump on them before they can move it.

COACH: That can be right, for one of you. The strong-side defenceman can pinch, to challenge the player with the puck. Challenge the puck with your stick. A body check is an option only where your game allows checking, and never into a back turned to you. If you see their numbers, pull up. The weak-side defenceman holds back, against an odd-man rush. If the puck is deeper in their end, both of you hold at the top and cut out passes, rather than chase. And if you'd stepped down when it turned over, get back to the line first. Read it, then decide. Pressure from the top, not from below.

PLAYER: How do I know the pinch is on?

COACH: Here's the timing test most teams use. Go only if you'll arrive as their player gets the puck. And go only when a forward is high enough to cover the point you're leaving. Ask what your coach wants.

PLAYER: And when it goes wrong?

COACH: The commonest way is their winger chipping the puck past you. Then turn and skate after the puck. Never cut across into the winger, and never hit them once the puck has gone. The hit doesn't get the puck back, and the window for a legal hit then is almost nothing, even where checking is allowed. Where it isn't, and in women's hockey, there's none at all. And if you do meet their winger on the boards, it's the same as in your own end, the boards position. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to anyone coming at you, never duck.

COACH: Seventeenth idea. Joining the attack, which is called activating. Staying back is the default, not a ban. Modern defencemen create a lot of offence by jumping into the play. The difference between a good jump and a bad one is the read. Picture your team working the puck low in their end, and the slot opening up. When do you go?

PLAYER: When I see the space?

COACH: Space is the start. Only go when a forward can fill in above the puck for you. Somebody has to be the safety valve. If all three forwards are below the circles and you jump, there's nobody home. Go when your team clearly has the puck, low in their end. Not while the puck is still up for grabs in a corner. Call it. Shout, so your partner slides across to cover the middle, and your forwards know to rotate high. And arrive late, as the second wave.

PLAYER: Late? I'd have gone early.

COACH: Arriving late into the slot is what beats their coverage. Arrive early, and you're just one more player for them to mark.

PLAYER: Which side do I come down?

COACH: The common way is the weak side, into the back door, the far side of their net. The player arriving unmarked is the dangerous one, so coming down the weak side usually beats driving into a crowded battle on the strong side. Some teams activate on the puck side on purpose. Teams that rotate all five players do it, and so do teams that use the defenceman as support on the boards where the puck is. Ask which your team does.

COACH: The rule of thumb is that somebody is above the puck. On a pair, that's normally one of you two. But think of it as a person, not a position. Some teams rotate all five players and cover the line with whoever is highest. Some keep only one player high, and make that the far-side winger. So before you go below the puck, know exactly which teammate is above it.

PLAYER: Let me try their end. Keep the puck in, and keep my skates inside the line. Find a lane, then get the shot through, low, for a tip or a rebound. Below the circles is a commitment, so go only once somebody has my spot. Only one of us below the puck. If the winger chips it past me, chase the puck, not the winger. And before I join the attack, know who's above the puck.

COACH: That's it exactly.

COACH: Eighteenth idea. Blocking shots. Blocking shots is part of your job. It's a technique, not an act of courage. Done badly, it hurts you and doesn't stop the puck. So here's the whole of it, and it goes together. First, your gear. Blocking puts your shins, feet and hands in the puck's way on purpose. So wear shin guards that cover your shin and your ankle, and gloves that aren't worn through in the palm.

PLAYER: Where should I be?

COACH: Close. Ten feet beats thirty. The further away you are, the more the shooter can adjust around you, and the more time the puck has to rise or change direction. Close in, you take away more of the net. That it's also safer for you is coaching know-how, not measured. Be big and square to the shooter. Shin pads face the shot, and your stick is down on the ice beside you, to seal the gap low. Stay on your feet if you can. A standing block keeps you in the play. And stay up until the puck is actually shot.

PLAYER: Even when they're winding up?

COACH: Especially then. Going down early is exactly what a fake shot is trying to get you to do. Close the distance with your feet. Don't lunge. Keep your head up and out of the shooting lane, standing or down. Hands behind your body while you're standing. If you do go down, go down toward the shooter, not sideways. Head still up and out of the lane. Hands behind your shin pads once you're down, with the backs of your gloves out. Feet on the ice. That direction is coaching know-how, not measured.

COACH: Four things you never do. Never turn your back to the shot. Never turn sideways. Never lift a leg. And never lead with your stick.

PLAYER: Turning away feels like the natural thing to do.

COACH: It is, and it's what gets players hurt. Turning your back or your side opens up the parts your pads don't cover, and you lose sight of the puck. A lifted leg puts the gap between your shin pad and your pants in the puck's path. And a stick pushed out in front can send the puck up, at your own face and at your goalie. That last part is coaching know-how, not measured.

COACH: Never block from a screening spot, in your goalie's line of sight. Half blocking a shot your goalie can't see is worse than not blocking it at all, because you've changed the path of a puck they never saw. So if you're in that line, either step out and block square, or get out of your goalie's eyes. And know when not to block. On a two-on-one, blocking the shot means leaving the pass open, and your goalie wants the shot.

PLAYER: Some of my teammates hardly ever go down to block. Are they being soft?

COACH: Don't decide that yet. How much your team blocks at all is a coaching choice. Some teams want defencemen dropping and blocking a lot in front of the goalie. Others want the lane taken away on your feet, with the goalie seeing everything. Neither is right on its own. So ask your coach which, and ask your goalie what they want, before you decide a teammate who stayed up was being soft.

COACH: Nineteenth idea. Faceoffs. Your centre takes the faceoff. Where you line up depends on the zone and on what your team runs. These are common setups, not rules, so ask what yours are. In your own end, one defenceman lines up near the hash marks on the boards side, backing up the winger there. The hash marks are the short red lines on the outside of the faceoff circle, roughly level with the dot. That defenceman is ready to chase the puck into the corner, or get behind the net. The other defenceman is in the slot, ready to block a point shot or cover the front of the net.

PLAYER: And in their end?

COACH: One of you is at the point on the side of the draw, ready to shoot if your centre wins it cleanly. A set play off a clean draw is one of the few times you get to shoot with your feet already set and traffic already in front. The other defenceman holds the far point. At centre ice, you both stay back at your own blue line, in case they win it forward and come at you. Whatever the draw, be ready to react straight away to a tie-up or a loose puck.

COACH: Twentieth idea. The power play, when your team has an extra player. Your team's setup decides whether you're even on the ice, and whether you have a partner up there.

PLAYER: How would I not be on the ice?

COACH: In a one-three-one, there's only one defenceman, up at the top. That setup is widely described as the modern default. If that's you, you're the passer who runs the play. Move the puck quickly. Walk the line to open shooting lanes. And use the threat of your shot to pull their four players out of shape. If you're the second defenceman, you're on the second unit, not on the ice. In an umbrella, there are two defencemen up top, so you have a partner to pass back and forth with. In a two-three, both defencemen are at the points. It's the older setup, and it's still common in rec and youth hockey, though that's an impression, not a count. In an overload, you're usually the lone outlet on the far side. Your job is to be the release when the near side gets sealed off.

PLAYER: Whatever the setup, what's my main job?

COACH: Keeping the puck in their end. A power play that gets cleared has to come back in against four players already set up, and that's much harder than holding the line was. Your second job is shooting for tips and rebounds, not trying to beat the goalie clean through four bodies.

COACH: Twenty-first idea. The penalty kill, when your team is a player short. A penalty kill is four skaters, usually two forwards and two defencemen. Where you stand depends on your team's shape. In a box, you and your partner are low, owning the front of the net and the two low corners. It's the shape almost everyone is taught first. In a diamond, one of you usually takes the bottom point, at the front of the net, and the other takes a middle spot. Which spots go to the defence is your coach's call.

PLAYER: Why use a diamond instead of a box?

COACH: Against a one-three-one power play, a box leaves their middle player, called the bumper, unmarked. The diamond covers that player. Then there's the wedge plus one. Three players sit in a tight triangle around the net, and the fourth, the plus one, pressures whoever has the puck. You're almost always in the triangle. But when the puck moves, the nearest triangle player steps out as the new plus one, and that can be you. The old plus one drops back in. Following the puck around the outside is how this shape gets beaten, so the job is handed on instead. The route the plus one takes out is a coaching choice, so ask which yours runs.

COACH: In all of them, stay tight, hold the inside at the front of the net, block shots, and clear the puck every chance you get. Don't chase the puck around the outside. Let it come to you.

PLAYER: That's hard when they're just passing it round and round.

COACH: That's exactly what they want. It's like piggy in the middle. Chase every pass, and you never touch the ball and you're soon worn out. The power play wants you to chase, because that's what opens the gap they're waiting for.

COACH: In many leagues, while your team has fewer players on the ice, icing isn't called against you. Icing is shooting the puck from your own half all the way across their goal line, with nobody touching it. Some adult leagues count it from the blue lines instead. Play stops, and the faceoff comes back to your end. Where you're allowed to ice it, use it, rather than trying to make a play you don't need. But at some younger levels a team that's a player short is still called for icing, so check your league. And on the last shift of a kill, get the clear away before the penalty ends.

COACH: Twenty-second idea, and it's short. Line changes and tired clears. Your shift can be a little longer than a forward's. More of it is gliding, reading and skating backwards, rather than sprinting.

PLAYER: So it's easier work?

COACH: Longer doesn't mean less work. One study tracked professional players through a game. The defencemen covered more ice in total, and the forwards did more hard skating per minute. And the difference in shift length is small, only a second or two. Your pair changes on its own timing, not with a forward line. If all five players step over the boards at the same moment, nobody is there to stop a rush. So let the forwards go, and change at the next safe moment. And if you're the last player back, you don't change until somebody takes your place. Say it out loud. Last man.

COACH: Now picture the end of a long shift. You're gassed, the puck comes to you in your own end, and all you want is to get it out and get off. What would you do?

PLAYER: Fire it up the middle as hard as I can.

COACH: That's the one not to fire. A flat clear up the middle is the one that gets picked off at the blue line, and it's the one that ices. Aim wide and high off the glass instead. A glass clear rarely ices, it's hard to pick off, and it gets the puck out even when it doesn't get it out cleanly. Off the glass, never over it. If you do ice it, the faceoff comes back to your end. And in many leagues, after your own icing, your team can't change players. So a tired clear can keep you out there, tired, for a faceoff in your own end. When you're gassed, the glass is the answer.

PLAYER: What if one of us is hurt?

COACH: That's the exception that matters. An injured player can be replaced, so a hurt teammate never has to stay out for that faceoff.

COACH: Let me pull it all together. Stay on the defensive side of the puck. Leave it only on purpose, after calling it, with a teammate you know is covering. Play as a pair. One on the puck and one at the mouth of the goal, swapping out loud every time the puck crosses the middle. And know which system your team plays.

COACH: At your own net, get inside first, lift the stick low, and let go. Never shove, never lean, never your stick across anyone, and never into a back. If a body moves there, keep it to the corner side, with their skates on the ice. And never take the puck across the front of your own goal.

COACH: On the way back for a puck, glance over your shoulder, so you're not panicking when it arrives. Use the boards position. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to anyone coming at you, never duck. Look up the ice first. Off the glass, never over it.

COACH: Win the gap with your feet, not with a hit. Steer the carrier wide, play the puck, and if you see their numbers, pull up. On a two-on-one, take away the pass and leave the shot to your goalie. In their end, keep the puck in, get your shot through low, and know who is above the puck before you go below it.

PLAYER: What should I ask my own coach?

COACH: Three things. What system do we play in our own end? How aggressive do you want me at the blue lines? And on a two-on-one, do you want me in the passing lane, or sliding? Then two for your league. Which rulebook do we play under? And is body checking allowed in our games? Ask, because much of this depends on what your team plays. Their answers tell you how your team wants you to play, and now you know why each answer makes sense.
