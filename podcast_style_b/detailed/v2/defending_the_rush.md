PLAYER: Can I just slide across and take the whole thing away?

COACH: Not until the pass has gone. A slide is a one-time commitment. If you go down before the pass, you've taken yourself out of the play, and both options are open. If the pass has gone and you can really reach it, sliding into the lane is right. Keep your head up and out of the shot lane, don't take their legs, and ask your coach to show you how to go down.

PLAYER: What about blocking the shot?

COACH: Don't try. That means leaving the pass, and worse, a half block can deflect a puck your goalie was tracking cleanly. There's one honest exception. If a backchecker has picked up the second attacker, and the shooter is walking into the slot with no real pass left, it isn't a two-on-one any more. Close on the puck, with your stick, not a hit. If you see their numbers, pull up.

COACH: Now the other way to play it, the timed slide. Instead of keeping the lane and living with the shot, you time a slide across the passing lane, to take the pass away completely and force the carrier to shoot. That's a different system, not a mistake. Notice what doesn't change. Your goalie still owns the shooter, and you still own the pass. What changes is how you take the pass away, and what it costs if you get it wrong. If you go early, or slide past the lane, you're on the ice and out of the play, and you can't fight for the rebound. If your team slides, your head stays up and out of the shot lane. This system exists to force a shot, so a shot is what you're going down in front of. Don't borrow the way you drop to block a shot. A slide across a passing lane is a different move, in a different direction. How you go down is the part that hurts you, and this episode doesn't teach it. So ask the coach who wants you to slide to show you exactly how.

PLAYER: And if I slide and they go over me?

COACH: Then it isn't free. If the attacker goes over your body, that's a trip, and touching the puck first won't always save you. A slide that catches them low, on the legs, can be clipping, which costs more. In some leagues a reckless one throws you out of the game even if nobody is hurt. The worst outcome isn't picking the less popular system. It's you and your goalie having different answers. So ask your coach which one your team plays, and settle it with your goalie before the game.

PLAYER: Let me pull the first numbers together. One-on-one, steer them to a bad angle and let them run out of ice. One against two, one takes the puck and the other stays back. Two-on-one, keep the lane until the pass has gone, unless we slide, and settle it with my goalie.

COACH: Right. Fourteenth idea. The other numbers. Two-on-two. It should be routine, but coaches say it's the one most often messed up, because even numbers make people relax.

PLAYER: Who takes who?

COACH: The usual way is man-on-man. Each defender takes an attacker and keeps a tight gap on them. The alternative is zone. The defender on the puck side takes whoever carries the puck into their half, and the other holds the middle. Zone copes better when the attackers cross. Man-on-man is tighter when they come straight. Find out which your team wants, because mixing them on one rush leaves an attacker free. Either way, never both drift to the puck. The puck pulls you in. Two of you go to the carrier, they make a simple pass, and their partner has the net. If the attacker you're covering isn't the carrier, stay with them and let your partner do their job. The defender on the far side isn't free. Being ready to help isn't the same as leaving your player.

PLAYER: What if they cross on purpose?

COACH: They do, to make two defenders swap badly. So either follow your player through the crossing, or call a clean switch, out loud. Never half of each.

COACH: Three-on-two. You take the chaos out by agreeing beforehand which of two systems you're playing. The first is two wide, middle to the backchecker. Each defenceman takes an outside attacker, and nobody chases the carrier. A carrier in the middle belongs to the backchecking centre. A carrier out wide belongs to the defenceman in that lane. If your backchecker really isn't coming, the defenceman on the puck side squeezes the middle and gives up the far attacker. But look before you assume it.

COACH: The second is strong side takes the puck, weak side splits. The defenceman on the puck side takes the carrier as a one-on-one, stick on the puck, not a hit. If you see their numbers, pull up. The other defenceman sits in the passing lane between the middle attacker and the far one, like a two-on-one.

PLAYER: Which is better?

COACH: It's a trade. The first keeps three defenders on three attackers if the backchecker arrives, but gives up the middle if they don't. The second never gives up the middle, but one defender is covering two people. Two defencemen playing different versions on one rush is worse than either, so ask your coach which you play. Whichever it is, back up together. Both defencemen go back at the same speed, level with each other across the ice, with the middle sealed between you. A staggered retreat opens a diagonal seam straight to the slot. And don't back all the way to the goal line. Collapsing into your own crease gives up the whole slot. Back up under control, and force the shot from outside.

PLAYER: And three-on-one?

COACH: That's a bad situation. Picture it. Three of them, just you. What would you do?

PLAYER: Go at the carrier and hope?

COACH: That's the one thing not to do. If you go at the carrier, it turns into two attackers alone on your goalie. Take the middle, goal side of the puck, with your stick in the most dangerous lane. Your job changes from stopping it to making it take longer, because every extra second brings another backchecker. Back up slowly, and make them make an extra pass. Never commit to one attacker. Give your goalie the shot from distance. And call for help, loudly.

COACH: Fifteenth idea. The late attacker, and two passes to watch for. The player who arrives second is the one who gets forgotten. The trailer is the attacker following the rush a few metres behind. They arrive in the high slot, the top part of the slot from the faceoff dots up to the tops of the circles, just as the first wave has pulled everyone low. And they arrive with a clean look.

PLAYER: Whose job is the trailer?

COACH: In the usual setup, the backchecking forwards', not the defencemen's. Your defencemen are busy with the first wave, and if they step up to the trailer, the first wave gets the net. Some teams give the trailer to the weak-side defenceman instead, and drop the third forward to the front of the net. That's tighter on the trailer and thinner in front. Ask which your team runs. A trailer covered by nobody and a trailer covered by two are both goals. That's where skating first pays off. A backchecker who's several metres behind can't catch the trailer. And if you're the last one back and everyone else is covered, the trailer is yours. Don't skate into the pile. Defencemen, you'll see the trailer first, so call it. Trailer, or high.

PLAYER: And the two passes?

COACH: First the drop pass. The carrier crosses your blue line, leaves the puck behind for a teammate, and keeps driving at the net, often straight through you. You're the defenceman on that carrier. Do you go back for the puck?

PLAYER: Feels like I should. That's where the puck is.

COACH: The common answer is no. The defenceman on the carrier stays with the player driving the net. The dropped puck belongs to the backchecking forward who has the trailer. If you follow the puck back, you leave the player at your net free, and you hand the shooter a screen. So stay goal side of the driver, stick in the lane to the slot, and shout drop. Keep your spot with your feet. No shove, no lean, never your stick across them, and never into their back. Some teams have the defenceman nearest the dropped puck step to it, while the partner picks up the driver, with the switch called out loud. Ask which yours plays, because both defencemen going to the puck is the version that loses.

COACH: Then the stretch pass. An attacker leaves early, and a long pass from deep in their end finds them behind your defence. The fix is getting depth before the pass, not speed after it. While they have the puck in their end, look over your shoulder for the forward leaving early, and start giving ground in time to keep them in front. Some teams would rather stand higher and accept the odd long pass, trusting the backcheck and the goalie. How high your defence stands is your coach's call, so ask.

COACH: Sixteenth idea. Chasing a breakaway. A breakaway is an attacker alone with the puck against your goalie. This is your goalie's save, and goalies usually make it. Your job is to do the one useful thing that's legal, and then get out of the way.

PLAYER: What's the one useful thing?

COACH: Your stick on the puck. A poke at the puck itself, or a lift of their stick as they shoot. Keep a lift low on the shaft, up, not sideways, and let go. Never at their hands, never a chop, and never a two-handed shove. What you must not do is a hook, a trip, a hold or a slash from behind that takes away their chance. That gives the other team a penalty shot instead of two minutes, in every league. In one, their team can take the two minutes instead. That's their choice, never yours. In some leagues, a foul from the side and a little behind counts too. If your goalie has been pulled for an extra attacker, the same foul gives them a goal. With your net empty, in some leagues a foul from the front does it too.

PLAYER: And if I can catch them?

COACH: Skating to catch them is fine, and worth doing if you have the speed. If you get alongside, angle them wide with your route. Turning a breakaway into a shot from a bad angle is a win. But the angle ends where contact begins. Steer with where you skate, and don't add an arm, a shoulder or a hip at the end. No shove, no lean, and never your stick across them. You're arriving from behind, so never into their back. If you see their numbers, pull up. Never lift anyone off their feet. Even going slowly, a crash into the boards or the net can hurt.

PLAYER: And if I poke the puck first, and they go down anyway?

COACH: Don't let that thought talk you into a reach or a slide. Touching the puck first can save you the penalty shot in some leagues, but you still take a tripping penalty there. And in some leagues it never saves a slide that takes someone down. If you catch them low, on the legs, it can be clipping. Sweeping their feet out from under them is slew-footing, and in most leagues that throws you out of the game. So stay on your feet, and put your stick on the puck and nothing else. If your blade isn't touching the puck or their blade, you're probably committing a penalty. Take your goalie's odds over a penalty shot.

COACH: And don't stop and look at the referee. Play goes on until their team loses the puck, so keep defending.

PLAYER: Any other desperate moments like that?

COACH: One. The puck is in the air at your own crease. Knock it straight down and play it with your stick. Never close your hand on it, skate with it, or fall on it. Smothering the puck is your goalie's job. Anywhere, closing your hand on it can be a penalty. If the puck is in your crease and you fall on it or hold it, that's a penalty shot. So is throwing your stick at the puck or the carrier in your own end.

PLAYER: Let me try the rest of the numbers. Two-on-two, never both to the puck. Three-on-two, know our system and back up level. Three-on-one, delay. Somebody takes the trailer. And chasing a breakaway, stick on the puck.

COACH: That's them. Seventeenth idea. Talk. A rush is loud and fast, and it comes at most of your team from behind them. Talking is the only way anyone knows what's happening. Keep it short, early and loud.

PLAYER: What makes a call actually useful?

COACH: Three habits. Call early. A call as it happens is information. A call after it happens is only commentary. Call the answer, not the problem. Back door, for an attacker arriving unmarked at the far post, tells a teammate where to go. Watch out doesn't. And everyone talks. Your goalie has the best view of the whole rush, and should be the loudest voice on the ice.

COACH: Eighteenth idea. When the rush is stopped, keep working until everyone has a player to cover. Picture it. Your goalie makes the save, the puck's loose in the corner, and everyone breathes out. What's wrong with that picture?

PLAYER: Nobody's picked anybody up.

COACH: Right. Stopping the rush is only half of it. The seconds straight after are when good teams still give up goals. Everyone relaxes, and nobody picks anyone up. So here's the order. First, notice when the attack loses its speed. A dump-in picked up in the corner, a shot saved, or a pass back out toward their blue line. Second, find the front of your net before anything else. During a rush, nobody was assigned there. Third, switch from lanes to players, from who is in my lane, to who is my player. Do it in about a second.

PLAYER: And then?

COACH: Fourth, backcheckers go to their spots in your team's own-end system, not to the nearest puck. In one common system, a winger who followed an attacker into the corner lets them go and gets back out toward the blue line. Under man-on-man, the winger stays. Those aren't the only two, so know yours. Fifth, talk it through. I've got the corner. You take the point. Net front is mine. The point is the area just inside your blue line, where their defencemen play. A rush isn't over when the shot is saved. It's over when everybody has someone to cover.

COACH: Nineteenth idea, and the last. The best rush defence is not creating the rush. Almost every rush where they outnumber you is made by your own team. Coaches point to five causes. That's what coaches stress, not a counted list, because nobody publishes data on what starts these rushes. But it's the list worth drilling.

PLAYER: Go on, then.

COACH: One. A pinch that misses. A defenceman steps down at the other team's blue line to keep the puck in, and doesn't get it. The fix isn't, never pinch. It's, pinch on a read. Pinch when you'll clearly win the puck, when a forward can cover for you, and when the score and time say it's worth it. A pinch is a challenge with your stick on the puck, not a hit. If you see their numbers, pull up.

COACH: Two. Forwards leaving early, hunting a breakaway pass before your team actually has the puck. When the puck doesn't come out, that forward is no use at either end. The test is whether you can still get back if you're wrong.

COACH: Three. Turnovers in the middle of the ice, the most direct cause of all. A turnover there hands the puck to a team already facing the right way. So don't stickhandle through the middle under pressure, and don't force passes across the neutral zone.

PLAYER: Most of my forced passes are me just getting rid of it.

COACH: That's a panic pass, a pass made without looking, just to get rid of the puck. Panic is normal while you're still learning, and it spends time you actually had. So after a shift, ask yourself, did I have more time than I thought? The honest answer is almost always yes. The fix isn't being braver. It's one more look before the puck reaches you, so you know where it's going before it arrives.

COACH: Four. A bad line change. Change when the puck is in their end, or heading away from yours. A change with the puck loose at your own blue line is how a three-on-two is born.

COACH: Five. Nobody left above the puck in their end, meaning nobody between the puck and the blue line. Nothing looks wrong until the puck turns over. It usually happens when both defencemen go deep at once. Some teams cover the line on purpose with whoever is highest, or with a winger. Unless your team plays that way, one of the two defencemen stays above the puck. So before you go below the puck, know who's above it.

COACH: Let me pull it all together.

COACH: Stay goal side of every attacker, all the time. When a rush goes wrong, somebody lost goal side of somebody. Start skating backwards early, and keep the gap closing all the way in. Watch the chest, not the puck.

COACH: Take the middle and give the carrier the wall, with your route, not your body. Pin the puck, not the player. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call, and if you see their numbers, pull up. On a two-on-one, take away the pass, give up the shot, and tell your goalie the pass is yours.

COACH: At your blue line, step up or back off, never half. When in doubt, back off with a tight gap. On a backcheck, sprint first, come back to the inside, and make sure somebody owns the middle. Know your team's system. And never let a chase end in a player's back.

COACH: Know your job on every rush before it arrives. Chasing a breakaway, stick on the puck. Talk early. After the save, keep going until everyone has a player. And best of all, don't create the rush.

PLAYER: So what should I ask my own coach?

COACH: A lot of this depends on what your team plays. So three questions. Which backchecking system do we play, and where is the hand-off? On a two-on-one, do you want me in the lane or sliding? And on a three-on-two, is it two wide, or strong side on the puck? And one for your league. Is body checking allowed in this game? Ask, because these are your team's choices, not rules. Their answers tell you how your team wants you to defend the rush, and now you know why each answer makes sense.
