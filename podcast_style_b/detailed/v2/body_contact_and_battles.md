COACH: Eleventh idea. The loose puck. That's a puck nobody has and both teams can reach. A puck sent round the boards, a puck shot into the corner, a rebound. Picture a puck rolling toward the boards. You and an opponent are both going for it, and they're half a step quicker. What would you do?

PLAYER: Try to beat them to it?

COACH: Winning the race is not the same as winning the puck. The player who arrives half a step behind, but on the inside, usually ends up with it. On the inside means between the other player and the puck. So skate the inside lane, and make them go the long way round. Hold it with your body, no shove and no lean, and never use it to deliver a hit. Under Hockey Canada, and in the CARHA adult leagues, you hold only a lane you already have, and never lean, bump or use your body to keep it. Arrive on a curve, so you get there already turned. Turn your hips so your backside is between them and the puck. Get low and wide, and keep your feet moving, because a player standing still on the wall gets pinned.

COACH: Never hold the lane with your hands, your stick or your skates. Your stick goes on the puck, and whether you may hit at all is your league's call. And never hold a lane just to clear the way for a teammate with the puck. That's interference. Same limits here too. No shove, no lean, no stick across anyone, never into a back, pull up on the numbers, never lift anyone off their feet. Even going slowly, a crash into the boards can hurt.

PLAYER: And when I get there?

COACH: Get there early, then decide late. That's a coaching rule of thumb, not a law. A player who arrives flat out, already committed to one move, has given away their options. A player who arrives early, slows into a wide balanced stance, and then reads the pressure can do whatever the play needs. The commonest way to waste a won race is to swing at the puck straight away and put it into a shin pad. So, outside your own end, take a beat while your support arrives. In your own end, or with a player bearing down on you, there's no beat. One look, one decision, get it out.

COACH: It's also the safest way to arrive. A player who's upright, balanced and looking around is far harder to hurt than one stretched out at full reach with their head down.

COACH: Decide before you get there what you'll do with it. Take it off the wall, chip it along, or protect it. The best play is often a single touch. Chip it past them and skate onto it, bank it off the boards to a teammate, or rim it. A single touch turns a coin flip into a possession, and it takes you out of the collision. Race to the puck, not the player. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call, and if you can see their numbers, pull up.

PLAYER: Rim or reverse, how do I choose?

COACH: A rim sends the puck on round the boards. It beats a player who's sealing you against the wall, because it goes past them. A reverse sends it back the way it came, into the space a player has just left. It beats one who has come in too hard and gone past you. So pick by where they're going, and pick before you arrive.

COACH: Puck races to the wall are where hits from behind and boarding injuries happen. So never arrive bent over with your back to the play. Get side-on, in the boards position. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to anyone coming at you, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly.

COACH: One more. Picture a player racing for the puck on an icing, the long clearance down the ice. Hitting them into the boards counts as boarding. That protects you when you're the one chasing it. But don't rely on it.

PLAYER: Let me try the battles. On the wall, arrive with position, low and wide, and the puck between my skates and the boards. Feet on the puck, never on a player. Pin the puck to wait for help, never for a whistle. Then move it with my eyes up, never a panic pass. In the corner, the first player stops the puck and the second reads where it comes out. Steer the carrier up the wall, never to the slot. On a loose puck, take the inside lane, get there early and decide late, and pick rim or reverse before I arrive.

COACH: That's the battles. Now the net, both ends.

COACH: Twelfth idea. The front of their net. Goals come from there, so both teams send bodies to it. Attackers go to screen the goalie, tip shots and bury rebounds. A screen means standing where you block the goalie's view. Defenders go to hold their ground and take away sticks. In one sample of top professional games, about a quarter of all goals came from within two stick lengths of the net.

PLAYER: So I just go and stand there?

COACH: Get there first. A coach's way of putting it is that you have to commit to getting there before you can play there. Then stand in front of the goalie, in the line the shot travels, not beside them. You're there to take the goalie's eyes, and the goalie is looking down the line the shot will come along. Two hands on your stick, blade on the ice. A tip, a screen and a rebound all need your blade ready. Eyes on the puck, and know where the defender is without looking. Hold your ice and let them come to you, leaning on nobody.

COACH: Now, where your skates go. The crease is the goalie's area, the blue paint in front of the net. Stand outside it, and off its red outline too. In many leagues that red line counts as part of the crease, and a skate on it can cost your team the goal. So stand a skate's width further out. That costs you nothing anywhere.

COACH: Keep your hands, glove and stick blade down. The screen is your body in the goalie's line of sight, and nothing else. A hand or a blade up in front of the goalie's face can be a penalty with no contact at all. The screen is a place you stand. You never need to touch anybody to set it. And if the goalie came out to play the puck, never get in the way of them heading back to the crease.

COACH: Standing in the shot's path is the job here, so know when the puck is about to be shot. Have your helmet and cage on properly. Chin strap done up, and the chin cup of a full cage touching your chin, not hanging below it.

PLAYER: The defender always seems to be shoving me around in there.

COACH: The net front is the hardest place on the ice to take contact. Your eyes are on the puck, so a defender can move you from behind or the side without you seeing it. And the path they move you along has a steel goal post at one end and the goalie at the other. So do two things. Know where the near post is before you plant. And take the shove braced. Wide base, knees bent, weight low, giving with your knees and hips rather than locked stiff. Head up, chin off your chest, never duck. The goal post can hurt you the same way the boards can. That holds in every league, because a defender can push you along that path whether the push is legal or not.

COACH: Keep your spot against the defender, but don't push back or lean, and never your stick across anyone.

COACH: Now picture the goalie leaning into you to get their position. What would you do?

PLAYER: Hold my ground? That's my spot.

COACH: Not against the goalie. The goalie is different. If a goalie leans into you to get their position, don't brace against them and don't hold your spot. Give ground straight away. Then set the screen up again, outside the paint and off its red line. Holding your spot against the goalie costs your team the goal or puts you in the box, never nothing.

COACH: Now contact with the goalie itself. Any contact with a goalie can be a penalty, and it can get you thrown out of the game. A goalie who is down or turned can't protect themselves, so contact can hurt them. It can also cost your team the goal. So avoid the goalie, in the crease or out of it. A goalie who has come out of the crease is still not fair game. And charging a goalie, building up speed into them, can get you thrown out in every league, and in some it always does.

PLAYER: What about going for a rebound? Sometimes the goalie's right there.

COACH: Play the puck, and make a clear, obvious effort to avoid the goalie. Go round the goalie, never through. Pull up short. Head up. If a defender shoves you into the goalie, being shoved is not your defence. What protects you is clearly trying to avoid the goalie. So go to the net under control.

COACH: Thirteenth idea. The front of your own net. Here's the order to do it in, and the order matters. First, body position. Inside shoulder ahead of theirs, hips between them and the puck, beside them rather than on them. If you're established inside, they have to come through you. Hold that ground, low and wide. No shove, no lean, no stick across anyone, never into a back, pull up on the numbers, never lift anyone off their feet. Even going slowly, a crash into the post can hurt. And head up, chin off your chest. The goal post can hurt you the same way the boards can.

PLAYER: Why position first?

COACH: The rules protect a spot you already hold, not one you take by moving someone. And in at least one league even holding a spot can be called interference, so get there early and quietly.

COACH: Second, take their stick, not their body. As the puck arrives, lift their stick up, low on the shaft, well below their bottom hand. Or press their blade lightly to the ice. Then let go. Never at the hands, never a chop. That takes away the tip and the rebound. It does the job a cross-check was meant to do, at far less risk. The timing matters. A press kept on their stick before the puck is anywhere near can be called holding. One coach's warning is worth keeping. If you get physical before the puck is on its way, you're going to get a penalty.

COACH: Third, never walk the screener out. Never try to push them off their spot, in any game, checking or not. Moving them can be called interference in every league. It's usually two minutes, but a violent or reckless one can be a major that gets you thrown out. Where checking is banned, it's an illegal check too.

COACH: Fourth, eyes on the puck, body beside the player. You can't beat them to a rebound you didn't see leave the stick. A defender who turns to look at the player they're boxing out has stopped watching the dangerous thing.

COACH: Fifth, don't get pushed back into your own goalie. You end up screening your goalie, and saves turn into goals. And you land backwards on a goalie who is usually down and can't see you, right beside the post. So fight the shove early, rather than ride it into the paint. That collision risk is coaching caution, not something anyone has counted. Head up and chin off your chest there too, because the post is right beside you.

PLAYER: What about a cross-check to clear them out? Everyone does it.

COACH: Never cross-check to make space. A cross-check means hitting or shoving with the shaft of your stick, held in both hands. Keeping your blade on the ice doesn't make it legal. No league caps a cross-check at two minutes, and it can get you thrown out even if nobody is hurt. If it rides up above the shoulders, some leagues throw you out automatically. And never put your shaft into their numbers. In some leagues that counts as a hit from behind, with no two-minute option.

COACH: One more thing, about where a body goes. A scrum, a lost edge, or a push that went further than you meant can move a player without anyone trying to. Never move a body across the front of your own net. That path has a steel goal post at one edge and your goalie at the other. The player moved along it is going sideways or backwards, and can't see either. That's coaching caution drawn from the neck injury danger, not a written rule.

COACH: Never drive a player from behind into the goal frame. That's a hit from behind, so plan on being thrown out. If they couldn't protect themselves, some leagues make it a match penalty, and the referee has no choice. And never take a player's skates off the ice there. A player whose edges are gone can't brace, and can't choose what meets the post first.

COACH: Whose job the front of your own net is, is also your coach's choice. In the low zone collapse it's usually the defenceman on the far side from the puck. In man-on-man it belongs to whoever's player went there, which can just as easily be a forward. Ask which yours plays.

PLAYER: Let me try the net front. At their net, stand in the shot's path, outside the crease and off its red line, hands and blade down. Know where the post is, take the shove braced, head up, chin off my chest. Give ground to the goalie, and never go into the goalie, in the crease or out. At my own net, get inside first, beside the player, lift or press their stick as the puck arrives, and let go. Never walk the screener out, never cross-check, never move a body across my goalmouth, and head up at the post.

COACH: Spot on. Now contact itself, taking it and giving it.

COACH: Fourteenth idea. Taking a hit, anywhere on the ice. Get a wide base, knees bent, weight low. A stiff, upright player gets knocked over. A player in an athletic stance rides the contact. Standing upright with your feet together is the mistake to avoid. And skate through it. Keep your legs moving through the contact, rather than slowing down or stopping.

PLAYER: Why keep moving? Wouldn't I be steadier standing still?

COACH: Moving keeps you balanced and in the play. Meeting contact with your body ready and your feet moving costs far less than standing still and being hit. But meet it, don't go and get it. Never turn and skate at a player who is coming at you. That adds your speed to theirs, and then neither of you controls the collision. Keep moving on your own line instead.

COACH: Keep your hands and stick in front of your body, not out to the side. That keeps you ready to play the puck the moment the contact ends. And let your knees and hips give a little, rather than taking it all through a locked, stiff frame. If you can't skate through it, keep your arms up, your knees bent and your head up. For a hit with no warning at all, there isn't much technique. Your best protection is knowing it's coming. So check over your shoulder to see where opponents are.

COACH: The best protection on the wall is not getting pinned at all. Know what's around you before the puck arrives, and have a plan before you get there. A chip, a rim, a reverse, or a spin off the wall with your head up, never turning your back to anyone coming at you. Players who arrive with no plan get pinned. Players who arrive with a plan get out.

COACH: And talk to each other. When a teammate goes back for a puck with their head down and pressure coming, call it, loud and early. Time means they have space. Man on, or their name and behind you, means they don't. Only say time when you're sure. If you're not sure, say nothing. A wrong time sends a teammate into a hit they weren't ready for. Those calls are common ones, not rules, so agree yours as a team. A player can't brace for a hit they can't see, and your call gives them the chance to.

COACH: Fifteenth idea. Giving a legal body check, but only where your game allows one. First, the gate. If checking is banned in tonight's game, everything in this idea is a penalty, and not a cheap one. Ask your league whether your game allows it, and get it in writing.

PLAYER: And if it does allow it?

COACH: Then a body check is the last step, not the first. One national coaching manual teaches it at the end of a ladder. Skating, angling, stick checks, being comfortable with contact, blocking a player's path with your body, then the in-between checks it calls take-outs and rub-outs, and only then a solid body check. The earlier steps are what make the last one safe. If you can't yet angle a carrier, lift a stick cleanly or stay balanced through contact, that's the step you skipped.

COACH: Here's what a legal check is, where your game allows one. Its purpose is to win the puck, not to punish or scare anyone. It goes on a player who has the puck. It comes from the front, diagonally from the front, or straight from the side. You hit with your hips and shoulders only. Hands, forearms, stick and elbows are all illegal. It lands above the knees and at or below the shoulders, and in at least one league it must land above the hips.

COACH: Start the check with your stick on the puck, blade low. That keeps your hands and elbows down. If your stick is up, you're about to commit a cross-check, a high stick or a hit to the head. An elbow pushed out is elbowing, hurt or not. Play the body, not the head. Aim for the chest, square. Don't stretch up or out to reach.

PLAYER: Coaches always say get low, though.

COACH: Getting low means your own base, not hitting them low. If you drop your body to hit them low, on the legs, that's clipping, in every division. If your knee is what lands on them, that's kneeing. Neither stops at two minutes. And never go in low at a turned back. That counts as a hit from behind. Hit through, not at. Keep your feet moving through the contact, so you finish standing over the puck.

COACH: Time it, don't chase it. Picture a carrier across the ice from you, not looking your way. You've got a run at them. What would you do?

PLAYER: Isn't that the big hit everyone wants?

COACH: And that's charging. Building up speed into contact is charging. In some leagues, two or more strides is already enough. A running or jumping hit can be charging too. For an open-ice hit, where your game allows one, the player should be one stride away. Drive your shoulder into their chest, and don't lunge. If you need more strides, you don't have the gap, so angle them instead. Never leave your feet. A jump into a check is charging however short your run-up. No league caps charging at two minutes, and a violent or reckless charge can get you thrown out.

COACH: And everything we covered earlier still holds. Never a hit from behind, to the head, after the puck has gone, or on a player already battling a teammate. If you can see their numbers, pull up.

COACH: Sixteenth idea. Blocking shots. It's useful and it's dangerous, and both matter. How much your team blocks is a coaching choice. Some teams ask defenders to block a lot and collapse in front of the goalie. Others ask them to take away the lane on their feet, and let the goalie see everything. Ask your coach which yours plays, and ask your goalie what they want.

PLAYER: And if we do block?

COACH: Then here's the whole method, and it goes together. Head up and out of the shot's path the whole time. Get close to the shooter. Close up, you take away more of the net, and the puck has less time to rise or change direction. Coaches say closer is also safer. That's coaching know-how, not measured. Stay on your feet if you can. A standing block keeps you in the play, and a puck blocked on your feet often turns into a breakaway the other way. A dropped block takes you out of it, whether it works or not. And if a dropped block misses, the shooter walks round you.

COACH: Square up to the puck, with your padded parts facing it. Shin guards front-on, thigh, hip. Head up and out of the shot's path. Feet on the ice. Hands behind your body, with the backs of your gloves out. Tucking your hands behind your shin pads is for once you're down on the ice. Standing, it makes you bend forward, which brings your head down into the shot.

COACH: Never turn your back, never turn sideways, never lift a leg. All three put the parts your pads don't cover in the puck's path, and turning away means you can't see it. Don't lead with your stick either. Keep it flat on the ice beside you, or out of the lane. Coaches say a puck off a blade held out in front goes up, often into your own face. Close the distance with your feet. Don't lunge, because a lunge that misses leaves you out of the play.

PLAYER: Are there times I shouldn't block at all?

COACH: Yes, and it's the part that gets skipped. It's where blocking costs goals. On a two-on-one, that's two attackers against you alone, your job is the pass, and the goalie has the shooter. Don't block from right in front of your own goalie, where they can't see the puck. A shot deflected off a body they couldn't see is a goal. Either commit fully and take the shot, or get out of the lane so they can see it. Never stand half in the way. Don't dive at a point shot while their player stands unmarked at the side of your net. And stay up until the puck is actually shot, because going down early is exactly what a fake shot wants.

COACH: Because blocking puts you in the puck's path on purpose, wear shin guards that cover your shin and ankle, and gloves that aren't worn through in the palm.

COACH: Let me pull this part together. Taking a hit, stay wide and low, skate through it, and never turn into it. Have a plan before you reach the wall, and call time only when you're sure. Giving a check, only where your game allows it, only on the puck carrier, chest square, stick down, feet on the ice, and no run-up. Blocking, get close, stay standing and square, head out of the lane, and never block in front of your own goalie's eyes.

COACH: Seventeenth idea. Staying out of the box. Coaches say most amateur penalties aren't dirty. They're late, frustrated or lazy. Nobody has counted that, so use it as a way to look at your own game.

PLAYER: What do they mean by late?

COACH: Late means you were beaten, so you reached. Hooking, holding, tripping and interference are what a beaten player reaches for. Frustrated means the shove back after the whistle. Lazy is the stick left on a player's hip because you couldn't be bothered to skate. So skate more, and reach less. Keep your blade on the puck, never on the player. A stick that has to reach is a stick that hooks, holds or trips. If your first thought is their body, your stick is somewhere it shouldn't be.

COACH: Hooking, tripping and interference are usually two minutes, but not always. A violent or reckless one can be a major that gets you thrown out. Play the whistle, then stop. Don't retaliate. That turns one penalty into two. And penalties add up. In some leagues, three penalties in one game throw you out, even if each one was only two minutes.

COACH: Three habits for a scrum. First, never put your hand on an opponent's cage or facemask. It's an easy reflex, and it can get you thrown out of the game. Second, keep your head out of theirs. Leaning cage against cage in a scrum counts as head-butting, even if nobody swung. In some leagues head-butting throws you out with no lighter option. So back your head away.

COACH: Third, keep your leg in front of you, never behind theirs. Putting your leg behind a player and taking their feet away is slew-footing. They go down backwards with nothing to break their fall. In most leagues even the lightest call for it gets you thrown out. And saying you played the puck first is no defence anywhere.

COACH: Don't take a penalty out of frustration at the referee. It's the cheapest goal you'll ever give away. Every league calls the game a bit differently, so watch how the first five minutes are being called, and adjust.

COACH: Eighteenth idea. When it boils over. This episode is about legal contact, and fighting isn't it. But once a battle turns into a shoving match, the rules you play under are not the ones you see on television. The top professional rules are the lightest. The amateur rules are much harsher.

PLAYER: So what do I do when it starts?

COACH: Gloves on, helmet on, hands down, skate away. That isn't weakness. It's the only way to stay on the ice. In most amateur leagues a fight gets you thrown out of the game, and in at least one, a punch that misses still counts as a fight.

COACH: Now picture your teammate in a shoving match that's turning into a fight. You're the nearest player. What would you do?

PLAYER: Go in and pull them apart?

COACH: Don't. Don't skate in to help a teammate, and don't skate in to break it up. The third player in can be thrown out. In some leagues the player who comes in to make peace is thrown out too, whatever their reason. Don't count on your reason protecting you. Leave it to the officials.

COACH: And if it kicks off while you're on the bench or in the penalty box, stay there. Every league penalises leaving. In some, every player who leaves is thrown out, even one whose penalty has just run out and who only skates to their own bench. This is the one place where skating away is itself the offence. The rule is about leaving at all, not what you do once you're out. So wait until the officials wave you out.

COACH: Keep your helmet on, and never touch theirs. In some leagues taking your own helmet off in a scrap, or pulling theirs off, gets you thrown out. And do your chin strap up properly, so it stays on. And being thrown out of the game, a game misconduct, is never only tonight either. Every league can make it cost you the next game or more.

COACH: A team that plays hard, wins battles and takes no penalties is far more physical than one that hits everything and spends nine minutes a game a player short.

COACH: Nineteenth idea, and the most important if it ever happens. When a player is hurt. Picture a teammate going head first into the boards. They're down and not getting up. What would you do?

PLAYER: Go and help them up, I suppose.

COACH: That's the most common and most damaging mistake. Treat every knock to the head as a possible neck injury. Don't help them up, and don't move them. Somebody phones for help first. Make sure they can breathe and their airway is clear. Reassure them, keep them warm, and tell them not to move. Nobody moves them, except to keep their airway clear, unless they're trained to do more. And their helmet stays on. No exception.

PLAYER: When do we call an ambulance?

COACH: Any one of these means call an ambulance straight away, even if it seems mild. They were knocked out, or you can't get a response from them. A fit, twitching, or going rigid. Any neck pain or tenderness. Weakness, numbness or tingling in their arms or legs. Double vision, or losing their sight. Being sick more than once. A bad headache, or one getting worse. Getting more confused, drowsy, restless or agitated. A dent in the skull, or any sign it might be broken. And if any symptom is getting worse, that's an ambulance too. But don't wait for any of those before treating it as a neck injury. That starts with the knock. If you play in Britain, the official list there is a little longer. Learn that one too, and if either list says call, call.

COACH: The commoner case is a player who takes a knock to the head and gets up. If a concussion is suspected, they come off the ice, and they don't come back that day. A medical professional decides when they play again. They don't have to have been knocked out. And don't let the player decide. Judgement is one of the things a concussion affects, and a concussed player will tell you they're fine.

PLAYER: What if they feel better by the end of the game?

COACH: Feeling better is not the all-clear. There's no return to anything with a risk of a knock, a fall or a collision, sport included, until they've been checked by a medical professional. That holds even if the symptoms clear up. Don't leave them on their own. Somebody else drives them home, and somebody stays with them. And if their symptoms get worse, call an ambulance.

COACH: Let me pull it all together. Before you play, find out whether your game allows checking, and get it in writing. Until you know, don't hit anyone. Take the puck with your route and your stick. Pick your line early, add nothing at the end, and lift the stick low and let go.

COACH: Hold the ice you're standing on, with no shove and no lean. Arrive at every battle with your body between the other player and the puck, then get low and wide. Never hit a player who can't see you coming. If you see their numbers, pull up and play the puck. And where your game bans checking, no hit is yours.

COACH: At the wall, head up always, and the boards position. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to anyone coming at you, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly.

COACH: In battles, use your feet on the puck, never on a player, and pin the puck, not the player. At their net, screen from outside the crease line with your hands down, and give ground to the goalie. At yours, lift their stick, don't cross-check, and never move a body across your goalmouth. Head up at the post, at both ends. When it boils over, gloves on, helmet on, skate away, and stay on the bench. And if someone may have hurt their head or neck, nobody moves them, their helmet stays on, and somebody phones.

PLAYER: That's a lot of it depending on my team and my league.

COACH: It is. So here are three questions for your coach, and one for your league. Whose corner is it in our own end? Whose job is the front of our net? How much do you want us blocking shots? And for the league. Do we play checking or non-checking, and can we have that in writing? Their answers tell you how your team wants you to play, and now you know why each answer makes sense.
