The defenceman walks the puck toward the middle before shooting. That changes the angle, makes the goalie move, and often opens a lane. One forward goes to the net front to screen and tip. One supports in the high slot, so the puck can move again instead of going into a shin pad. And the defenceman on the far side watches for a rebound squirting their way.

COACH: A common add-on is a pass across the top first, from one defenceman to the other. Coaches call it D to D. The goalie has to slide across, and the shot comes from the other side.

PLAYER: Why go low to high at all, if the point's so far out?

COACH: Because a point shot with a screen in front makes things a cycle never does. A rebound. A tip. A scramble. And a whistle in your favour. But here's the catch. At even strength, a point shot is one of the shots least likely to go in. It's blocked more often than shots from anywhere else, and it makes fewer rebounds. It scores rarely, and less often still with nobody in front. On the power play a point shot is worth more, so this is an even-strength caution.

PLAYER: So the screen is what makes it worth it.

COACH: Right. Only go low to high with the net front filled. A point shot with a screen, a tip threat and two players hunting the rebound is a good play. The same shot with nobody in front just gives the goalie the puck.

COACH: Eleventh idea. Shooting from the point. Get it through, don't shoot harder. This one is mostly for defencemen. Your job isn't to shoot harder. It's to shoot through. You get the puck at the point, and a forward is already dropping into the lane in front of you. What would you do?

PLAYER: Shoot quickly, before they get set?

COACH: Walk the line first. Skate sideways along the blue line toward the middle, until the shot-blocker has to move their feet. That's usually a step or two. A defenceman who shoots from the spot where they got the puck is shooting at a wall that is standing still.

COACH: Then change your release spot. A quick snap shot from somewhere the blocker didn't set up for beats a blocker who has already dropped. Take something off it. A slower, lower shot that gets through and gets tipped beats a big slap shot into a shin pad, every time. Aim low, at a tipper's stick or the goalie's far pad. And keep the backswing short. In at least one adult league, a backswing past about fifteen inches counts as a slap shot, and that's a penalty there. If it hurts someone, it ends your game there too. So check your league.

PLAYER: Can I fake it?

COACH: Yes. A fake that puts a blocker down on the ice opens the lane for the real one, or opens the pass across. Sell it with your shoulders and eyes, not a big backswing. Or go D to D first. That drags the blocker and the goalie across, and you shoot into a lane that has just been emptied.

PLAYER: And if it does get blocked?

COACH: A blocked point shot usually goes the other way, with your team spread out. So if nobody is in front, hold it.

COACH: Twelfth idea. Know when to shoot, when not to, and what you're shooting for. Start with when. Shoot when you're inside home plate with any kind of look. Almost any shot from there beats almost any pass from there. Shoot when the goalie is moving, not set, or screened. Shoot when you have a real lane, not the memory of one. Shoot when somebody is at the net front, because a tip threat and a rebound hunter make it a different play. And when there's a rebound and the goalie is down, shoot at once.

COACH: Now a test. You're down by the boards at a sharp angle. Nobody's in front of the net, but you've got a clear look at the goalie. Shoot?

PLAYER: It's a shot on goal. It can't hurt.

COACH: It can. The goalie seals the post, there's nothing to tip, and there's no rebound to chase. You're just giving the goalie the puck and starting their breakout. So don't shoot from a sharp angle with no traffic. Don't shoot when nobody is in front. Don't shoot when the lane is blocked and you'd be firing into a shin pad. And don't shoot when a better shot is one pass away, especially a pass across the middle. The half-second it takes is usually worth it. A shot after the goalie has had to move across is worth a lot more.

PLAYER: So what should I actually be aiming for?

COACH: This is the part most players never think about. From out on the edges, you aren't really trying to score. You're trying to make a second chance. Shoot for a rebound. Aim low, at the far pad. Shots at the goalie's chest and glove get caught, and the whistle goes. Shoot for a tip. Low and hard along the ice, through the screen, at your tipper's stick. Say who you're shooting at. The tipper shows you a blade, and your job is to hit it. You can even shoot to miss on purpose. A hard shot off the end boards to a spot for a teammate is a real pass. Say it first.

PLAYER: When do I go for a corner?

COACH: Only when the goalie can't see the puck or can't get there. Picking corners on a set goalie who can see the shot is about the worst bet you can make.

COACH: Thirteenth idea. Tipping the puck. A tip is changing the direction of a shot with your stick blade.

PLAYER: Why is a tip so hard to stop?

COACH: The goalie has tracked a puck going one way and set for it. Now it's going another way, from a few feet out. Keep your blade on the ice, always. Almost every puck you can tip comes in low. A stick held off the ice at the net front is doing nothing.

PLAYER: Do I swing at it?

COACH: No. You're redirecting, not shooting. A small change of angle is enough to beat a set goalie. A big swing usually misses the puck. So angle the blade, give a little, and don't follow through. Soft hands change its direction. Hard hands send it into the glass. And give your shooter a target. Hold your blade out in the lane where you want the puck, and shout for it.

PLAYER: Does it matter how high I tip it?

COACH: It does. A tip that's too high can cost you the goal. Depending on your league, the limit is the height of the crossbar or the height of your shoulders. Keep it low and you're fine everywhere. And watch where your blade goes in a crowd. The moment it catches a player above the shoulders, it's high-sticking, and no league caps that at two minutes. If you hurt someone with your stick on purpose, or try to, plan on a match penalty in most leagues. That goes past being thrown out. So keep your blade down after the tip as well as before it.

COACH: Fourteenth idea. Hunting the rebound, and the high forward's three jobs. We covered earlier that you decide to go before the shot. Now, where will the rebound go?

PLAYER: You can't really know that.

COACH: Not for sure. But guess before it happens, rather than react after. Roughly, a puck off a pad tends to squirt back toward the corner it came from. One off the blocker tends to go to the shooter's off side. One off the chest tends to drop in front. Those are tendencies, not rules. A good goalie angles their pads to break all three on purpose. So know where the shot is coming from, lean your feet that way, and be ready to be wrong.

COACH: When the shot goes, two forwards go to the net. One goes to the front for the screen and tip. One hunts the rebound on the puck side. The third forward is F three, the high forward. If that's you, you have three jobs, in order.

PLAYER: Go on.

COACH: First, be the safety at the top of the circle. A rebound straight out is yours, and a counter-attack has to go through you. Second, if a defenceman has stepped down or cut to the middle, take their spot on the blue line. Third, if the rebound squirts out to the high slot, jump on it. You're the closest player moving toward the net, and that's a great chance handed to you. That's the common setup, and some teams cover the line differently, so find out which yours plays.

COACH: Fifteenth idea. The goalie and the crease, in more detail. What you do here is the same in every league. Screen from outside the crease. Never start contact with the goalie. Give ground when they set their position. What changes from league to league is how much a mistake costs. So play it the strict way, and you're as safe as any league allows.

PLAYER: Why stay out of the paint, if I'm not touching anyone?

COACH: Because inside it, your position alone can take the goal off, in every league, even with no contact.

PLAYER: What if the goalie leans into me?

COACH: Give ground at once. Then set the screen again outside, with both skates off the line. Holding your spot pays nothing. If your team scores, you can lose the goal, and you can take a penalty too.

COACH: Here's another. The goalie comes out and plays the puck outside the crease, and you're standing between them and their net. What would you do?

PLAYER: Stay where I am? I was there first.

COACH: Get out of their road back. Blocking it on purpose can be a penalty, even with no contact at all. And a goalie who has come out of the crease is still not someone you run into. Unnecessary contact with them can be a penalty in every league.

PLAYER: What about a scramble, when everyone's going for it?

COACH: Play the puck. The most any league forgives is contact you couldn't avoid while you're both going for the same loose puck. Some leagues don't forgive even that. And if you push the goalie into the net with the puck after they've stopped it, the goal comes off.

PLAYER: And if a defender shoves me into the goalie?

COACH: Still try to avoid the goalie, early and where everyone can see it. Being shoved isn't a full defence in any league. What decides it is your effort. In at least one league it has to be every effort you can make, not just a try. Speeding up through the contact, putting your arms out or dropping your shoulder is the opposite of an effort. If you do any of those, plan on being thrown out of the game, and in some leagues on a match penalty.

COACH: And never arrive into the goalie. That's charging the goalie, and no league caps it at two minutes. It can get you thrown out in every league, and in some it's automatic. In some leagues a reckless one is a match penalty, which goes past being thrown out. A goalie watching the puck can't protect themselves from a hit they don't see coming, and it can hurt them.

COACH: Shooting and the net. Your turn.

PLAYER: Shoot from home plate, or when the goalie's moving or screened, with somebody in front. From the edges, shoot low for a rebound or a tip. Tip with my blade on the ice, and keep it low. Guess where the rebound goes, and shoot it straight back. If I'm the high forward, be the safety, fill the line, and jump on the high rebound.

PLAYER: At the net, stay out of the paint and off the line. Give ground when the goalie leans in, get out of their road back, and in a scramble, play the puck. Shoved toward the goalie, try to avoid them anyway. And never arrive into the goalie.

COACH: Sixteenth idea. Off the rush or off a set play. Have both. There are two ways to attack in their end, and most amateur teams only have one. Off the rush, you come in with speed before the defence is set. The chance comes from numbers and momentum, like two of you against one defender. It shows up in the first three or four seconds, then it disappears.

PLAYER: What are the good plays off the rush?

COACH: The late player arriving into the high slot. The drive to the net, under control, that pulls a defender with you. And the delay, which is curling back toward the blue line, so a second wave of your players can arrive. A shot off the rush is worth more than the same shot later, because the goalie is still moving too.

COACH: Off a set play, the defence is back and nothing is open. Now the chance comes from patience. That's the cycle, the low to high with a screen, the pass across the middle, the net front, and the pass from behind the net. You hold the puck until a tired defence makes a mistake. A defence that has chased for forty seconds is about to.

PLAYER: What goes wrong if you've only got one of them?

COACH: A team that can only rush has nothing when the rush is stopped at the blue line. They come in, lose it, and go back the other way. A team that can only cycle never catches the defence before it's set, so every chance it gets comes from the outside. So have a plan for the second wave when the rush is stopped. Chip it past the defenceman, put a soft dump into open ice, or pull back and come again. And when a cycle turns the puck over, somebody should already be reading it and going the other way with speed.

COACH: Seventeenth idea. For defencemen, where to stand, and keeping the puck in. You're always at one of three depths, and the difference matters a lot. Just inside the blue line, at the point, is the default. You're the outlet for low to high, the shooting threat that stops the defence collapsing, and the safety against a counter-attack. Stay a stride inside the line, not on it.

PLAYER: Why a stride inside?

COACH: If the puck goes all the way out, the zone is gone, and your whole team has to leave and come back in. Down to the top of the circle is the active spot. You step down to shoot from a better angle, walk the line, or take a pass in the high slot. And you can still turn and get back before most counter-attacks get going. Below the circles, past the hash marks, is always a commitment. You're a fourth forward now. So somebody must take your job at the blue line before you go, not after.

COACH: Now keeping the puck in. Their way out is usually a rim along the boards or a clear up the middle. The point is where it stays in or leaves. Their defenceman has the puck in the corner, and you're at the point. What are you watching?

PLAYER: The puck.

COACH: Watch the defender about to move the puck, not the puck already on the wall. Read it before it comes, and be a stride inside the line before it gets there. Then stop it first, and play it second. On a rim, get your stick blade, a skate turned along the boards, or your body against the wall in its path. If you use your body, the boards position goes with it. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to anyone coming at you, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly. On a clear up the middle, square up in front of it. Head up and out of its line, the padded parts of you toward it, and your stick out of the lane.

PLAYER: Why stop it first? Why not just play it?

COACH: A puck you settle at the line is a new possession. A puck you swipe at and miss can be an odd-man rush the other way. That means more of them coming at your net than you have back. If it's hard or bouncing and you can't settle it, chip it back down the wall to the corner, and keep the shift alive. And if it's already past you, turn and skate back. Don't chase it. The forward covering for you should already be on the way.

COACH: How hard your team wants you to fight for the line is the coach's call. The common way is to hold anything you can reach, and get back on anything you can't. Some teams would rather you let a hard rim go than risk it. Ask which yours wants.

PLAYER: And what if my partner and I both want to go?

COACH: Then you need the rules for covering each other. In the usual setup, if one defenceman leaves the line, the other stays. If the one who went gets the puck, they play as a forward until the play ends, and F three fills the line. If they don't get it, their first job is to get back into position. Chasing after a failed attempt is how odd-man rushes start. And say it out loud. I'm going. I've got you. Hold. Silent rotations are the ones that leave the blue line empty.

PLAYER: Is it really that bad if we both go?

COACH: Two defencemen gone at once, when nobody called for it, is a quiet way to give up an odd-man rush. Nothing looks wrong until the puck turns over. Some teams do send both on purpose, in a five-player rotation or with a green light from the coach. So ask what yours does.

COACH: Eighteenth idea. The pinch. A pinch is a defenceman stepping down from the blue line to keep the puck in, or to win it on the wall. It isn't a step-up. A step-up is challenging an attacker in the neutral zone, the middle of the ice between the blue lines, or at your own line. Before anything else, the pinch is about the puck. Stick on the puck, not a hit. Whether you may hit at all depends on your league, your age group and your game, so find out first. And if you see the winger's numbers, pull up.

PLAYER: How do I know whether to go?

COACH: The test is short. If you can't arrive on contact, don't go. Arriving on contact means you and the puck get there at the same time. On a loose puck nobody else is racing for, the bar is higher. You need to be clearly first, because second place just leaves your back to the play.

COACH: Let's try one. Their winger is flat-footed on the wall, facing the glass, and your forward is right there to cover your spot. Do you go?

PLAYER: Go. Facing the glass, they can't get away with the puck.

COACH: Go, for the puck. That's exactly what facing the glass tells you, that they can't get away with it. It does not mean you may hit them. Go when their winger is flat-footed or facing the glass. Go when a forward can rotate up to cover your spot. And go on a loose puck, a bobble or a tired line. Those are the best windows.

PLAYER: And when shouldn't I?

COACH: Don't go when your forwards are already below you and nobody can cover the line. Don't go when their player has already turned up the ice with speed. Don't go when you're protecting a narrow lead late. And don't go when your partner hasn't seen you go. A late pinch leaves the puck behind you, with your forwards below it. That's how three-on-twos against you start, three of them coming at two of yours.

PLAYER: So how do I actually do it?

COACH: Get your stick into the passing lane before your feet commit. Angle the winger toward the boards, not the middle. Close with short, quick steps, and get your feet under you for the last two or three strides. Don't speed up into the arrival. Building up speed into contact, even with two hard strides, can be charging. And jumping into a check is charging everywhere. Then take the puck.

COACH: Or seal the wall. Sealing means getting your body between the winger and the puck along the boards. You do it with position alone, and you pin the puck, not the player. No shove, no lean, no arm round them, never your stick across them, and never into their back. Keep your skates out of it, never pin them on the boards, and never lift anyone off their feet. Even going slowly, a crash on the boards can hurt. A pinch takes you to the wall, so the boards position is yours too, in every league. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to anyone coming at you, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly.

PLAYER: And if I get there and it fails?

COACH: Get back toward the slot, not down the boards. Following the puck down the wall leaves the middle open. Cutting to the slot gets you back between them and your net, and slows them down until help arrives.

COACH: How often to pinch is your coach's call. Some let both defencemen go, some only the one on the puck side, and some say never below the tops of the circles. Coaches see a pinch the system doesn't expect as one of the commonest causes of odd-man rushes against amateur teams. That's coaching experience, not a count. Ask.

COACH: Nineteenth idea. The hits you must never make on a pinch. First, find out whether you may hit at all. Check the game, not just the age group. Body checking isn't allowed in younger age groups, in most girls' and women's hockey, or in most adult leagues. And a local league can ban it at any level. Where checking isn't allowed, the pinch is angling, body position and playing the puck. Some leagues that allow a little contact for the puck still make any step or glide into a player a penalty, and a hard one can get you thrown out.

PLAYER: And where checking is allowed?

COACH: Where checking is allowed, the gate is the same. Stick on the puck first. Arrive while the winger still has the puck. If you decide on a hit after the puck has gone, that's a late hit. No league caps a late hit at two minutes. In some leagues it can throw you out even if nobody is hurt. And in at least one league, any check you could have avoided on a player who has just passed or shot is a penalty.

COACH: Here's the one that catches people. You pinch, and just as you arrive, the winger chips the puck past you. What would you do?

PLAYER: I'm already there. Finish the check?

COACH: No. Go for the puck, not the winger. Turn and skate to it. Never cut across into the winger, and never hit them. They don't have the puck any more. Moving sideways into a player without the puck can be a penalty in every league, and the same hit can cost more than two minutes.

COACH: A winger facing the glass can't see you or protect themselves. It's on you to avoid the contact. If you can see their numbers, pull up. Take the puck, seal the wall, or angle them off it.

COACH: A hit in the back is checking from behind. No league lets that cost just two minutes. Plan on being thrown out of the game. If you drive a player from behind into the boards or the net when they can't protect themselves, plan on a match penalty. In some leagues the referee has no choice. A match penalty goes past being thrown out.

PLAYER: What if my teammate's already battling someone on the wall? Can I come in and help?

COACH: Never pinch in as a check onto a battle your teammate is already in. Arrive beside your teammate. Seal the wall, not the other player's body, and keep your arms, your stick and your skates out of it. If you end up behind them, play the puck. In at least one league, a player already battling for the puck can't be body checked at all, anywhere on the ice. A reckless one throws you out. And in at least one league, any reckless hit is a match penalty, so plan on that.

COACH: Seal the wall with your position and pin the puck, not the player. Never hold with an arm or push off with a skate. If you wrap an arm round a player on the boards, that's holding. If you use your skate to push them off, in at least one league that's a major, you're thrown out, and you miss the next game too.

COACH: Give me the pinch back, because this one matters.

PLAYER: Go only when I'll arrive as the puck does and a forward can cover my line. Stick on the puck, not a hit. Find out first whether I may hit at all. Feet under me, and no speeding up into it. Seal with position alone. Never pin them, and never an arm, a skate or a stick across anyone.

PLAYER: Never hit the winger who chipped it past me, never hit a winger facing the glass, and never check into a battle my teammate's already in. If I see their numbers, pull up. And if it fails, get back to the slot.

COACH: That's all of it. Twentieth idea, and the last. Faceoffs in their end. A faceoff in their end is the one time you can put all five players exactly where you want them, with the puck about to arrive at a known spot. Most amateur teams waste it.

PLAYER: Why does a set play off the draw work so well?

COACH: Four players start moving on the drop, before anyone knows who won it. So the shooter and the screen are already on their way. Here are the common ones. Win it back to the point and screen at once. The inside winger goes straight to the net front, and the outside winger goes to the rebound spot. It's the simplest and the most reliable, because everyone was already moving.

COACH: Win it back to a winger set up for a one-timer, a shot straight off the pass. That winger has to line up as a shooter before the drop, not as a defender. Keep that backswing short if your league limits slap shots. Or win it forward and attack straight away. That's high reward if their centre is slow to react. But it puts your centre below the puck, and it goes badly if you lose the draw.

PLAYER: What if their centre beats ours clean every time?

COACH: Then tie up their centre's stick, and let a winger take the puck. Your centre lifts or presses the other centre's stick with their own, for an instant, over or under the blade, then plays the puck. Stick on stick only. Never their hands, never a hook, never holding it down, and never a hand on it. It's the usual answer to a centre who keeps beating yours clean. Nobody wins it cleanly, and your wingers are already leaning in.

COACH: Or delay for the screen. The defenceman takes the win but skates sideways for a beat before shooting, so the net-front player can get set. A shot half a second late with a screen beats one on time without.

COACH: Whatever your team runs, everyone must know it before the whistle. The two commonest failures are a net-front player who arrives after the shot, and a defenceman who fires it into the first shin pad. And keep your wingers out of the circle until the drop. A winger creeping in gets your centre thrown out of the draw.

PLAYER: And if we lose the draw?

COACH: Plan for that too, because you'll lose roughly half. One forward, usually the winger nearest the puck, pressures straight away. Stick on the puck, not a hit. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call, and if you see their numbers, pull up. The point is to stop a clean pass out and force a rim or a chip. The other two forwards turn and skate, rather than watch. A forward standing still when the puck is lost is beaten. The defencemen hold the line and read it. Is it a rim to stop, or a clean exit to back off from?

PLAYER: It feels odd to plan for losing.

COACH: Having a job for the loss is what lets you be aggressive on the win. Teams play the draw timidly because they have no plan for losing it.

COACH: Let me pull it all together.

COACH: Take your shot from home plate, or with the goalie out of position. Look across the middle before you look up the wall. Put a body in front for every shot, outside the paint and off the line. Know where the post is, head up, never duck, and hold your spot with your feet. Never start contact with the goalie. Decide before the shot that you're crashing the net, stick on the ice, and play the puck, not a body, and never the goalie. Keep somebody above the puck.

COACH: Keep the triangle, with somebody always in home plate, and end every cycle in something. On the wall in their end, keep your feet moving, and when you don't know, keep it, side-on, rather than throwing it to nobody. And use the boards position. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to anyone coming at you, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly.

COACH: Behind the net, look to pass out to the slot. Only shoot from the point with somebody in front, and shoot it low to get it through. Have a plan for the rush and a plan for the long possession.

COACH: On defence in their end, keep the puck in, and pinch only when you'll arrive as the puck does and someone can cover your line. Stick on the puck, not a hit. Find out whether you may hit at all. Never hit a player who no longer has the puck, and never hit one facing the glass.

PLAYER: What should I ask my own coach?

COACH: Three things. What shape do we play in their end, and who takes the net front? When do you want our defencemen pinching, and how deep can they go? And what's our faceoff play in their end, and what does everyone do if we lose it? Then one for your league. Is body checking allowed in our games? Ask, because these are your team's choices, not rules. Their answers tell you how your team wants you to play in their end, and now you know the reason behind each one.
