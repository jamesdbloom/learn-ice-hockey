COACH: A one-two-two sends one forechecker to steer you, with two forwards behind. It takes away the middle, and often the pass to your partner. And its two forwards sit right where a pass up the boards wants to go. One forechecker comes at you. What would you do?

PLAYER: Get rid of it before they get there?

COACH: Against this one you'll usually have time. Use it. Skate at the first forechecker until they pick a side, and then the other side is open. Your low centre is the key, because their two middle forwards sit fairly high, and the space under them is real. Don't rim into it. A rim hands the puck to the forwards waiting on the boards.

PLAYER: And if they're even more patient than that?

COACH: The most patient version is the trap. Be patient yourself and regroup, rather than skating into their wall of players. Keep the puck moving while you do, because a referee can penalise a team for delaying the game. Patient means nobody's on you. If a forechecker comes at you, it's one look, one decision, move it.

COACH: A two-one-two sends two forwards in deep, one each side, with one in the middle. It takes away your time and both corners. So play fast and simple. A rushed pass across the ice is exactly the mistake it's built to force. Reverse and rim come into their own, because two forwards coming in hard have momentum, and momentum is what the reverse punishes. Make both plays side-on, as always. The wheel is dangerous here, because there's a second forechecker on the far side. And off the glass is a fair answer.

COACH: A one-three-one puts one forechecker on you and stretches three players across the middle of the ice, with one behind. It's less a forecheck than a wall, like a defensive line in football. Every long pass is dead.

PLAYER: So how do you get through a wall?

COACH: Skate it out. One forechecker can't stop a defenceman carrying with speed, and making the carrier a threat is what forces their wall to break shape. Use short passes up the boards, not through the middle, because the boards are the softest part of their shape. That puts a receiver on the boards with pressure coming from behind, so the boards position. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to anyone coming at you, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly.

COACH: And chipping it past them and racing for it isn't a defeat. Against this shape it's often your best option, because forcing a pass into three players gives them a rush the other way. If their goalie comes out for the puck, pull up rather than finish on them.

COACH: A full-ice press is all five of them pushing up, usually late when they're chasing a goal. One clean pass through the first layer beats it. But a turnover now is a chance against.

PLAYER: So go for the long pass over the top?

COACH: Don't hunt the killer long pass. Beat the first forechecker with a short, safe play. Off the glass is completely fair, and even icing it costs you less than a turnover. An icing costs you a faceoff in your end, often with the same tired legs. A turnover against a press can cost you a goal.

COACH: Sometimes they don't come at you at all. They wait in the middle of the ice, set and patient. If you chip it out, they have it. If you skate into them, they have it. So you need a set pattern, called a control breakout. It works on two things. The four players without the puck all move, with speed. And the carrier knows where each of them will be, and picks whichever one the trap hasn't covered.

PLAYER: So that's the one time I settle it behind the net and wait?

COACH: Only while nobody is on you yet. That's the whole point of the pattern. They're standing back, so you have the time. The moment a forechecker comes at you, behind the net or anywhere else in your end, the waiting's over. One look, one decision, move it. And even with nobody on you, keep the puck moving behind your net while the pattern starts. In some adult leagues, sitting on it there with nobody pressuring you stops play for a faceoff in your end.

PLAYER: How many of these patterns are there?

COACH: One coaching manual sets out three, and says to pick one and drill it until nobody has to think. A team trying to hold all three runs none of them well. So learn your team's one, and where you stand in it. In some patterns a winger is posted up, standing still on the boards on purpose. Even then, body open to the ice and head up. If contact comes, the boards position. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to anyone coming at you, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly.

COACH: Let's hear the plays and the forechecks back.

PLAYER: Up when the boards are open. Wheel with a step. Rim when I'm sealed, reverse when they've gone past. D-to-D behind the net. Against a one-two-two, use the time and make the first forechecker commit. Against a two-one-two, fast and simple. Against a one-three-one, skate it out and go short up the boards. Against a press, beat the first layer first. And against a team waiting in the middle, run our set pattern, with everyone moving, and only wait while nobody's on me.

COACH: Eighth idea. Faceoffs in your own end. Coaches call a faceoff in your own end the most set-up moment in hockey, so it's the best chance for a real set play. If you win the draw, the classic play is a wheel off the faceoff. The centre pulls the puck back to a defenceman, who wheels behind the net. The far winger goes up the boards as the main target, and the centre curls low.

PLAYER: Why does the centre curl low and not just go?

COACH: Because if the forecheck arrives at once, the defenceman reverses it to the centre swinging low. That reverse is made on the wall, so the boards position. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to anyone coming at you, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly. The reverse is the reason the centre swings low after a draw rather than sprinting up the ice. Whatever your team runs, decide it before the puck drops. Look at your winger. Look at your partner. Know what happens next.

PLAYER: And if we lose the draw?

COACH: Then your first job is defence, not the breakout. Get to your coverage straight away. In the coverage this episode assumes, wingers go to the points, because a lost draw in your end usually goes back to the point for a shot. Never chase the puck as a group. That's exactly how the goal gets scored. And when you win it back, treat it as a quick-up. They've just committed to attack, and their shape is at its weakest.

COACH: Ninth idea. When the breakout breaks down. Picture it. Your winger gets it on the boards, and it's stripped straight off their stick. What now?

PLAYER: Try the exit again?

COACH: No. Turned over on the boards or behind your net, the breakout is over. Defend first. The nearest player pressures the puck straight away, so it can't be walked into the slot. Pressure means your stick on the puck, not a hit. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call, so find out first. If you can see their numbers, pull up and play the puck. Never hit or push anyone from behind.

PLAYER: And everyone else?

COACH: Gets back between the player they're covering and your net, with the slot filled. Which coverage you fall into is your team's call, so ask. Trying the same exit again while three of them are still in your end is how one turnover becomes two. And when you win it back, go before they reset. They've just sent players low. Look up the ice before you look down.

COACH: Tenth idea. Your goalie's calls, and where you aim a rim. Your goalie faces the play, with the clearest view of everything behind your defenceman's back. That view is worth nothing unless the words are agreed in advance and the calls come early.

PLAYER: What are the usual ones?

COACH: "Time" means nobody is near you, turn and make a play. Only say time when you're sure. A false "time" sends a teammate into a hit they weren't ready for. "Man on" means pressure is arriving now, so don't turn into it. The boards position. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to anyone coming at you, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly.

COACH: "Leave it" means the goalie has stopped a rim behind the net and left it for you. "Up", "wheel" and "reverse" name those plays. And "over", to most goalies, means the safe pass behind the net. Agree your own words before the game.

PLAYER: You said where to aim a rim depends on the goalie.

COACH: And on your league. In the NHL, and under the rules hockey in Britain uses, your goalie may only play the puck behind the net inside a marked shape. It's called the trapezoid, narrow at the goal line and wider at the end boards. Outside it is a two-minute penalty. In Britain it isn't clear that every league enforces it, so play as though it counts, and ask your league. Where it counts, your goalie can't go and get a rim that dies in the corner. A defenceman has to, under pressure, with the forecheck arriving.

PLAYER: So where should the rim go?

COACH: There are two good rims. Hard and all the way round, to where your winger meets it in stride. Or tight and short, close enough behind the net for your goalie to stop it inside the trapezoid and leave it for you. The bad one is in between, dying in the corner where nobody can get it safely. Agree which of the two good ones your team wants. And whoever goes into that corner goes in at an angle, in the boards position. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to anyone coming at you, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly.

PLAYER: And with no trapezoid?

COACH: Then your goalie becomes a third puck-handler, who can play a rim anywhere behind the net and pass it to your defenceman. But once your goalie leaves the crease to get it, they can't sit on the puck to get a whistle. That's a penalty too. So don't aim a rim expecting your goalie to hold it for you. Look behind your net before your first shift to see whether the lines are painted, and ask if you aren't sure.

COACH: Give me those set moments back.

PLAYER: Off a draw, decide the play before the puck drops. Lose it, defend first. When the breakout breaks down, defend first, stick on the puck. Agree the goalie's calls. And rims go hard all the way round, or short for the goalie, never into the corner.

COACH: Eleventh idea. When the numbers are uneven. On the power play, one of theirs is in the penalty box. It's the same breakout. You still have five skaters and five jobs. It's their forecheck that's a player short.

PLAYER: Does anything change about who does what?

COACH: Power-play units often play four forwards, so one of the two defence jobs may be a forward's. Settle whose it is before the puck is behind your net, not while a forechecker is arriving.

PLAYER: Do we take our time, then?

COACH: That's your coach's choice. One way is to take the beat the missing forechecker gives you and leave with control, because a controlled exit gives you a controlled entry. That beat is only there while nobody is on you yet. If a penalty killer comes at you, behind the net included, you move it, same as at even strength. The other way is to go the instant you win it, because four penalty killers falling back are at their most disorganised in the first two seconds. Ask which your coach wants on the power play specifically.

PLAYER: What about clearing it?

COACH: That's what changes. The team killing the penalty is often allowed to ice the puck. In some youth hockey, even they can't. A man up, you never can. So don't fire it the length of the ice. Get it out off the glass and short of the red line, so it dies in the middle with a teammate skating onto it. Off the glass, not over it. Over the glass can be a penalty, and on a five-on-four that doesn't shorten your advantage. It takes it away.

COACH: With your goalie pulled for an extra skater, late in a game you're losing, it's a different problem. Now a mistake ends the game. Icing still applies to you, because six against five isn't short. The controlled exit is the only right one.

PLAYER: Where does the extra skater go?

COACH: Use them low, as a short, safe pass, not standing in front of their net. The long clear is off the table, but the glass clear isn't. Aim it to die in the middle of the ice with a teammate skating onto it.

COACH: Twelfth idea. When an official's arm goes up. A delayed penalty against the other team means the referee's arm is up. Play stops when they get control of the puck. You've got the puck behind your net with the arm up. What would you do?

PLAYER: Clear it and let them chase?

COACH: Keep it. Don't clear it. The moment they control it, your free spell is over. Make the safe play every time. D-to-D behind the net, back to the point, protect it in the corner, side-on, puck moving on your stick, never your back to anyone coming at you. If your goalie comes off for an extra skater, buy them time to reach the bench. And never make a blind pass back through your own crease with the net empty behind it. Putting it in your own net is the only way you can lose from there.

PLAYER: Should we always pull the goalie then?

COACH: It's a coaching choice, not automatic. Plenty of recreational and youth teams don't, and often shouldn't. A slow goalie, a far bench, or a team that can't keep the puck for four seconds can turn a free attacker into an own goal. With no set routine, just keep the puck and let the whistle come.

COACH: Then the delayed offside. The linesperson's arm is up because one of their players went into your end ahead of the puck. It feels like free time.

PLAYER: Isn't it? Play's going to stop.

COACH: The raised arm isn't a stoppage. The forecheck is still coming, so keep your head up and expect to be hit. Some leagues stop play if an attacker is about to hit you while you still have the puck. Others don't. And even where they do, the protection ends the instant you pass. A whistle stops the play, not a body already moving. So play it as an ordinary breakout under pressure. Take your look with your head up, then move it. Don't stop and wait for a whistle. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to anyone coming at you, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly.

COACH: Give me the special moments.

PLAYER: A man up, any clear goes off the glass, short of the red line. Goalie pulled, keep the puck. Arm up for a penalty, keep the puck and never pass blind through our crease. Arm up for offside, play on with my head up, and move it.

COACH: Let me pull it all together.

COACH: Never move the puck across the front of your own net, by pass or by skating. Go behind it. Keep the puck by default, and when you clear it, off the glass, never over it.

COACH: Look before you have the puck. Count the forecheckers, watch their feet, and decide early. Going back for the puck, angle in. If contact comes, the boards position. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to anyone coming at you, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly.

COACH: In your own end, under pressure, behind the net included, one look, one decision, move it. You only settle it and wait while nobody is on you yet. And if nothing's open, rim it or put it off the glass. Don't panic pass it to nobody.

COACH: Know your job. Partner, be where the pass behind the net reaches you, and make the calls. Winger, come down the boards moving, open to the ice, and never give it away at your blue line. Centre, stay low inside the dots, and leave with speed. Then pick the play the forecheck leaves open, and aim your rims where your goalie can help.

PLAYER: What should I ask my own coach?

COACH: Three things. Which breakout do we run, and where do I stand in it? What does over mean on our bench? Do we pull the goalie on a delayed penalty? And two for your league. Does the trapezoid count here? And can our partner stand in front of the net on a wheel? Ask, because these are your team's choices and your league's rules, not things you can guess. Then all five of you can run the same play, and now you know the reason behind each part of it.
