COACH: They're the link between you and the only surface you play on. Every skating skill you learn, the knee bend, the edges, the push, goes through the boot. A boot that lets your foot move about inside it can't pass any of that on.

COACH: Players in skates that are too big usually decide that. What they usually have is loose boots. And a foot sliding about inside the boot also breaks the boot down from the inside. So a size too big costs you twice.

COACH: Match how stiff the boot is to how often and how hard you skate, not to how good you want to be. COACH: Next, flex. That's how stiff the shaft is. The number is roughly the pounds of force it takes to bend it an inch. Start at about half your body weight in pounds. Go up a bit if you're tall or strong for your weight, and down if you're not.

PLAYER: Isn't a stiffer stick a harder shot?

COACH: No. You shoot by bending the shaft like a spring, then letting it snap back into the puck. If you can't bend it, you get less power, not more, and a slower release. Too whippy is wrong as well. You overpower it, and the puck goes somewhere you didn't choose. You want a flex you can bend on every shot.

COACH: Here's one for you. You've worked out your flex from your body weight, bought exactly that stick, and you're about to saw four inches off it. What have you just done?

COACH: Curve. Start with a mid curve, with the face of the blade not too open. An open face is tilted back, the way some golf clubs are, so it lifts the puck. That makes lifting the puck on your forehand easy. But it makes your backhand and your passing worse, because the back of the blade has nothing to cup the puck. For a developing player, a less open curve is better. It makes you learn real shooting and stickhandling, instead of leaning on the curve. And some leagues also limit how deep the curve can be, so check that too.

PLAYER: And lie? I've never really understood lie.

COACH: Lie is the angle between the blade and the shaft. With the right lie, the whole bottom of the blade sits flat on the ice in your normal stance. With the wrong lie, only the toe or the heel touches. Passes skip off, your stickhandling gets loose, and your shots go off line. To check, turn your stick over every so often and look at the bottom of the blade. If the toe or the heel is wearing much faster than the rest, the lie is wrong for you. Try a different lie on your next stick. And cutting a stick down changes the lie you need, too.

COACH: Whatever you settle on, write the numbers down, and change one thing at a time. That way you know what made the difference.

COACH: Twelfth idea. When your stick breaks, and when your goalie's does. You already have the first part. Open your hand, leave it on the ice, and never put it over the boards. Now, getting a new one.

At the top of the stick, build a knob of tape. It stops the stick sliding out of your top hand, and it helps you pick the stick up off the ice.

COACH: Let me pull your stick together. Chin height in skates. A flex near half your body weight, and lower if you'll cut it. A mid curve with a face that isn't too open. Check the bottom of your blade for the lie. Write the numbers down. Carry a spare out to your goalie, and never slide it. And tape it smooth, then wax it.

COACH: Thirteenth idea. Your pads, and the gaps between them. Pads only protect you where they overlap. So fit each piece, then check where the pieces meet.

PLAYER: Go through them for me.

COACH: Shoulder pads first. The tips of your shoulders sit in the middle of the shoulder caps. Elbow pads, try them on with your shoulder pads and gloves on. That's the only way to see whether the three pieces meet. Gloves, the cuff should meet the bottom of your elbow pad with no gap, and your fingertips should just reach the end. If you're between two sizes, go up. Pants, standing up, should overlap the knee part of your shin guards by about a third to a half. Shin guards, your kneecap sits in the middle of the knee cup, strapped so it can't turn.

COACH: Then the three gaps. Fitters keep coming back to them. Nobody has counted which one causes the most injuries, but each is easy to check at home. One, the gap between your shin guards and your pants. It leaves a strip of bare leg just above the knee. A rising puck, a stick and the top of the boards all arrive there. Check it bent into your skating stance, not standing up.

PLAYER: And two is the elbow pad that spins.

COACH: Right. It's a strap and sizing problem, and you can stop it completely. Strap it so you can't twist it. Three, your wrist, between the top of your glove and the bottom of your elbow pad. Slashes land there, and you can't see that part of your arm. A glove cuff that meets your elbow pad closes it for free. A cut-resistant sleeve closes it properly, and two of the big rulebooks recommend one.

COACH: And tape your socks over your shin guards. Two or three wraps near the top of your calf, and two or three at the ankle. That holds the guard where it should be, so it can't turn during a game. Don't wrap it so tight that it cuts off your circulation.

COACH: Fourteenth idea. Spend where it counts, and know what never to buy used. Say you've got a set amount of money to spend on kit. Where does it go first?

PLAYER: The helmet, surely. It's my head.

COACH: The helmet's second, and close. Spend on your skates first. They make the biggest difference to how you play, and a bad pair holds you back. Then your helmet, because a mistake there costs you the most. Then your stick, but spend on getting its numbers right, not on its price. A cheap stick with the right numbers beats an expensive one with the wrong ones. Then your gloves, because you feel the puck through them, and cheap ones wear through at the palm. Never spend big on a stick and cheap on a helmet.

PLAYER: Where can I save?

COACH: Socks, base layers, the bag and tape. Fit matters more than the model for shin guards and elbow pads. And in an adult league without body checking, light shoulder pads are fine. Where checking is part of the game, they move a long way up the list.

PLAYER: What's fine to buy used?

COACH: Plenty. Sticks, if the numbers suit you and there are no cracks. Pants, shin guards, shoulder pads and elbow pads, once you've checked for cracked plastic, squashed foam and broken straps, and washed them. And skates, but only if they really fit you and the boot isn't broken down. A used boot has already moulded to someone else's foot, and that's the risk.

COACH: Never buy a used helmet, goalie mask, mouthguard or jock. Buy face guards and neck guards new every time. Never buy anything with an expired sticker. Never buy a cage or visor with deep scratches or cracks. And a sticker can be faked. Fake Canadian CSA helmets are mostly bought online, or in second-hand shops. If a helmet feels light, flimsy or modified, look the model up in CSA's own product list before you trust its sticker.

PLAYER: What if someone's just starting and money's tight?

COACH: Ask your club first. Many clubs and rinks lend gear to new players. Some beginner programmes give you kit to keep. Whether that kit is new is a separate question, so ask them. Borrow the pads, and buy the head protection new. Get fitted in person for skates and a helmet, even if you buy online afterwards. Those are the two you can't guess. And last season's mid-range skates on clearance are usually the best value in the shop.

COACH: Fifteenth idea. Trying out in goal. Goalie gear is a different world. It's sized differently, it costs several times what a skater's kit does, and it's harder to find second-hand in the right size. That's why many clubs own a set.

PLAYER: So I can borrow the lot to give it a go?

COACH: Borrow the club's pads, blocker, catching glove, chest protector and pants, and find out if you like it. But get your own mask. A goalie mask is a helmet. A club mask has been on more heads and stopped more pucks than any hand-me-down, and nobody is keeping a record of what it has stopped. Ask about its history anyway. But a mask nobody can account for is one you don't put your head in. No rulebook says that about a loaned mask. It's the same caution as for any borrowed helmet.

COACH: Sixteenth idea. Look after it. Empty your bag every single time you get home.

PLAYER: Honestly, mine stays zipped till the next session. Is that so bad?

COACH: Wet kit zipped up in a bag is warm, dark and damp, which is exactly what bacteria and fungi like. The smell is only the sign. The foam breaks down, the stitching rots, and your blades rust. And hockey gives you plenty of small cuts and blisters, which is how skin bacteria get in. Clean, dry kit and clean skin take away most of that chance.

COACH: So take everything out, hang it up, and run a fan. Moving air does more than time. Wash your base layers after every use. Every few weeks, wash the rest, but not your skates or helmet, and never with chlorine bleach, which can break down the foam and glue. Wipe your helmet and skates by hand. Shower soon after you play, and don't share towels.

PLAYER: Can my neck guard go in the wash with everything else?

COACH: Read the care label before you wash anything, and especially your neck guard. It's meant to be worn unaltered, the way the maker says. One maker's neck guard label says wash it cold, lay it flat to dry, and never tumble dry it.

COACH: Wipe your blades and holders dry after every skate, before the skates go back in the bag. Water sitting on steel is what rusts it. Soakers, the soft blade covers, take up the last of the water, but take them off once the blades are dry. Don't store your skates in them. And replace your laces before they break, because they'll break five minutes before a game. Keep a spare pair in the bag.

COACH: Seventeenth idea. Get dressed, check, and step on in all of it. Pack the same list every time, and put the neck guard on it. Every item on that list has been forgotten by somebody, in a car park, twenty minutes before a game.

PLAYER: Is there a right order to put it all on?

COACH: There's one that works. Each layer goes on before the thing that overlaps it. And you can't bend down to lace skates once your shoulder pads are on. So base layers and jock first. Then shin guards, with socks pulled over them and taped. Then pants, then skates, while you can still reach your feet. Then shoulder pads, elbow pads, neck guard and jersey. Helmet on, strap done up. Mouthguard. Gloves last, so your hands are free for everything else.

COACH: Before you step on, check three things. Chin strap fastened. Shin guards tucked under your pants. Gloves meeting your elbow pads. Those three are what referees and injuries both notice.

PLAYER: Do I need all of it for the warm-up?

COACH: Wear all of it for the warm-up as well as the game. In British hockey that's required. In most British competitions a coach or manager also has to check your helmet, cage, chin strap and neck guard before the warm-up. And take your jewellery off before you dress. Some leagues ban it outright, even under your kit. Elsewhere, a referee can still treat a loose ring or stud as dangerous equipment.

PLAYER: A lot of people cut their gloves or pants to keep cool.

COACH: Don't cut your kit to make it lighter or cooler. A glove with the palm cut out isn't allowed anywhere. In Britain, pants that are cut, torn or changed aren't allowed either. And in Britain your jersey stays outside your pants and covers your elbow pads, so buy one big enough to do that.

COACH: Let me pull it all together.

COACH: Your own helmet, bought new, a goalie mask included, and replaced after a serious hit. Wear it, but never go into a collision head down. Chin strap done up every time, one finger. Pads that fit, strapped so they can't move, and the gaps between them closed.

COACH: In Britain, the neck guard on every time you step on the ice, adults and training included.

COACH: When kit fails in play, let go of it and get off the ice. Drop a broken stick where you stand, never over the boards. If your helmet or neck guard comes off, go to the bench. Hand your goalie a spare. And in a scuffle, keep your helmet on and your hands off the other player's.

COACH: Find out what your league asks for before you buy. A cage for a junior, a cage or full shield for anyone else, and a mouthguard. Skates that lock your heel. Sharp edges on one hollow you know. A stick you can bend. And empty the bag every time you get home.

PLAYER: What should I ask my own coach?

COACH: Three things. What face protection, neck guard and mouthguard does our league need, and which sticker? Can I bring my own helmet, and what can the club lend me? And if our goalie's stick breaks, can they play on, and who carries the spare out? A lot of this depends on where you play. Their answers decide what goes in your bag, and now you know the reason behind each piece.
