PLAYER: Can I make something happen?

COACH: That's the question when you're behind. With a lead, the question is whether they can get the puck back somewhere dangerous. A goal against costs a leading team more than a goal for gains them. And time is on your side. If the clock runs out with the score unchanged, you win. So the trailing team has to force things, and you don't.

PLAYER: So what does that change for me, with the puck?

COACH: It gives you three passes not to make. First, no passes across the middle of your own end, or across the neutral zone. The neutral zone is the middle third of the ice, between the two blue lines. A turnover there becomes an odd-man rush against you. That's a rush where more of them are attacking than you have coming back, like a two-on-one or a three-on-two. Coaches treat those as the most dangerous chances in open play.

COACH: Second, no blind pass from one defenceman to the other across the front of your own goal. In fact, at any score, never pass the puck, or skate it, across the front of your own net. PLAYER: What counts as a chip?

COACH: A chip is a short, deliberate flip, or a play off the boards, that gets the puck past the forechecker and out of your zone. The forechecker is the player chasing you for the puck in your own end. Under pressure, a chip beats forcing a breakout pass.

PLAYER: But I just gave them the puck.

COACH: You did. But you lost it far from your net, with your team facing the right way, and you get your change. A change is when players swap with teammates from the bench. COACH: Good question, because they can look the same from the stands. A panic pass is a puck thrown away without a look, because you felt rushed. Most of the time you had more time than it felt. COACH: Keep the chip in play. Put it along the boards or off the glass. PLAYER: And the player standing in front of our goalie?

COACH: At your net front, hold the inside, next to the player screening your goalie. Don't try to move them. Walking them off the spot can be called interference wherever you play, and an illegal check where checking isn't allowed. No shove, no lean, and never your stick across them. A two-handed shove with your stick is a cross-check, whatever your blade does. Never into their back, and never lift anyone off their feet. If they turn and you can see their numbers, pull up. Stay square, so you keep your balance. Keep your head up and your chin off your chest. The goal post can hurt you the same way the boards can. Even going slowly, a crash there can hurt.

COACH: Then, as the puck arrives, lift their stick. Get your blade under their shaft, low, well below their bottom hand. Lift it up, not sideways, then let go. Never at the hands, and never a chop.

PLAYER: Why does the net front matter that much?

COACH: Coaches say most six-on-five goals come from tips, screens and rebounds. A count of recent NHL games found tips and quick rebounds more common than usual, though well short of most. Either way, the front of your net is where it gets decided.

COACH: Every clear is a shot. Don't just chip it out. When the lane is clean, get your head up and put the puck at the empty net. If it goes in, it's a goal and there's no icing. But if it misses and crosses their goal line, it's icing. So shoot when you can actually hit the net, not as a blind heave. A blind clear straight to their point player is the worst outcome of the three.

COACH: And keep the backswing short. In at least one adult recreational league, a wind-up past about fifteen inches counts as a slap shot. That's a penalty there, and if it hurts someone it gets you thrown out of this game and at least the next. If you play adult rec hockey, ask whether your league bans the slap shot.

PLAYER: Wait, can we really be iced when they've pulled their goalie? We're a player down.

COACH: You're not, actually. The goalie counts, so six against six is equal strength, and icing is called. Under the professional rulebooks and in British hockey, that means a faceoff in your own end, at the dot they pick, with no change and no time-out. Five tired players, against six. So get it out with a purpose, or put it on net.

COACH: Faceoffs in your own end at five against six decide games, so agree the plan before you line up. And take no penalties. Six against four is close to a goal.

PLAYER: Let me give the end of the game back. Pull the goalie earlier than feels natural. With the extra attacker, get set up, move it with one touch, screen without touching the goalie, never let both point players pinch, and change only with the puck. On a delayed penalty the extra attacker is free, as long as we don't put it in our own net.

PLAYER: With our own net empty, no foul helps, and a loose puck in our crease gets knocked straight down. Defending an empty net, don't chase, hold the inside with no shove, no lean and never my stick across them, and remember we can still be iced.

COACH: That's it. Now contact and discipline.

COACH: Thirteenth idea. When a penalty is worth taking, and when it isn't. Rarely, a penalty is worth it. The test is simple. Is the thing you're stopping more likely to become a goal than the power play you're giving them? Which trades are worth it is your coach's call.

COACH: Picture this. Last minute of a close game, and a player is clean through on a breakaway. You're behind them. What would you do?

PLAYER: Pull them down. A power play's better than a goal.

COACH: Not here. If you haul down a player on a breakaway from behind, they get a penalty shot, not a power play. Across recent NHL seasons, penalty shots went in a little more often than power plays did, though not every season. So that foul saves you little or nothing. The time to stop the breakaway was four seconds earlier, with your gap. Your gap is the space between you and the carrier.

PLAYER: So when does a penalty pass the test?

COACH: Stopping an odd-man rush in the last minutes of a close game can pass it. A clean breakaway doesn't. If it really is a goal or a penalty, with your goalie in the net, take the penalty. But keep it a minor, because the trade only works while it's one. A reckless trip or hook can be a major and a game misconduct, which throws you out, with nobody hurt. In at least one league it always is, and there you should plan on a match penalty too, with a suspension of several games likely. A hook that injures ends your game in most leagues, and in some a trip that injures does too. And with your own goalie pulled, take none at all.

COACH: Now when it isn't worth it. Never with a lead in the last five minutes. If you're beaten, be beaten, and let them have the outside shot. Never while you're already killing a penalty. Coaches rank five against three as the most dangerous spot in hockey. Never while you're on a power play. Nothing wastes an advantage faster. Never at six against five, either way. Never in the first period. And never after the whistle, to send a message, or to get even.

PLAYER: What about just firing the puck away after the whistle? That's nothing, right?

COACH: It isn't always just two minutes. Skate away and leave the puck where it is. In some leagues firing it away can get you thrown out of the game. In at least one, doing it in protest of a call does exactly that, and it costs you your team's next game too. And protest is exactly the mood you're in when you do it. Knocking the puck away from an official who is picking it up is a misconduct in every league.

COACH: Fourteenth idea. Contact, and finishing your check. Before anything else, find out whether you may body check in this game at all. It depends on your age group, your league, and sometimes the game itself. In many youth, girls' and women's, and adult recreational leagues, you can't. In American hockey, when a checking team plays a non-checking team, nobody checks for the whole game. So check the game, not just the age group.

PLAYER: And where we can't check?

COACH: Take the ice the player needs, and play the puck. The contact isn't yours. A body check there is a penalty. A violent one can get you thrown out of the game, and in some leagues one that hurts the player always does.

COACH: Where checking is allowed, play the body of the player who has the puck, or take the ice they need. No run-up. Building up speed into contact, even with two hard strides, can be charging. Never your stick across them, never into their back, and never lift anyone off their feet. If you can see their numbers, pull up and play the puck. Keep every hit off the head. Never drive anyone head first into the boards. And even going slowly, a hit into the boards can hurt.

PLAYER: Coaches always shout "finish your check". What does that actually mean?

COACH: It means hitting a player who has already passed or shot. That's a late hit. Picture this. A player passes, and you're one stride from them. What would you do?

PLAYER: I'm that close, so I hit them?

COACH: Ask yourself one thing first. Could you still pull out of the hit? If you could, pull out. Where you may check, the check isn't late the instant the puck leaves. But the window is tiny, about the length of your arm and your stick at the moment they release it. Skating one or two more strides after the puck has gone, and then deciding to hit, is not acceptable. Going looking for that window is how players get thrown out.

COACH: And the window never covers a player who isn't ready for the hit. If they no longer have the puck and aren't ready for a hit, don't hit them. In some leagues that hit counts as reckless, window or not.

PLAYER: How much trouble is a late hit, really?

COACH: Two minutes is the floor, not the ceiling. A violent or reckless one can be a major and a game misconduct, which throws you out, even with nobody hurt. In at least one league, plan on a match penalty for a reckless one, and a suspension of several games. And a hit meant to hurt is a match penalty in most leagues. In some leagues, being thrown out also means sitting out at least your next game.

COACH: If they've turned away from you, leave them. If you can see their numbers, pull up and play the puck. A hit on their back is checking from behind. In almost every league, that throws you out of the game, even with nobody hurt. And never drive anyone into the boards or the goal frame from behind. If they can't protect themselves, some leagues must make that a match penalty.

COACH: Fifteenth idea. After a goal against, and after you score. They've just scored. You're going out for the next shift. What's the first thing in your head?

PLAYER: Get it back. Right now.

COACH: That instinct is how a one-goal deficit becomes a three-goal deficit. Play one deliberately simple shift instead. Win a puck, get it deep, pick up the player you're covering, and change. Nothing clever. No passes across the middle, no pinches, no one-on-one moves. Whoever is first in on the forecheck goes for the puck with the stick, not a hit, and if you can see their numbers, pull up. Reset your risk to where it was.

PLAYER: Doesn't that look like giving up?

COACH: A simple shift isn't a lack of ambition. It's the right play for that moment. Its job is to stop the bleeding and hand the game back to the bench in one piece. And being the player who can deliver one makes you very hard to leave out of the lineup.

COACH: Say one short sentence, from anyone. Reset, next shift. Silence after a goal against does damage. Then play the game in front of you. Replaying the goal in your head costs you decisions you can't afford. Coaches treat the minute after a goal against as the most dangerous stretch in hockey. That's coaching lore, not a measured fact, but the habits are worth having either way.

COACH: And don't go looking for a hit to make yourself feel better. That's how a late hit happens, and it can end your game.

PLAYER: And after we score?

COACH: Play the next shift like any other. The bench is loud, and somebody starts cheating up the ice for the next goal. So take the centre-ice draw like one in your own end. Win it back, get the puck deep, pick up your player. Whether your coach sends the scoring line straight back out, or a checking line to settle things, is a coaching choice. Ask which yours does.

COACH: Sixteenth idea. Frustration, and the reset. Coaches say most penalties in amateur hockey come from frustration.

PLAYER: Is that actually true?

COACH: Part of it is measured, and part isn't. Studies of youth hockey do find that most penalties are the aggressive kind, like roughing and slashing, rather than hooking and holding. But nobody has measured why a penalty was taken. So most being frustration is coaching experience, not data. The advice holds either way.

COACH: Three habits. Keep your feet moving, so your stick doesn't reach. Give up the last word, so a push back doesn't cost you the power play. And have a reset you actually use. A breath at the faceoff dot, a tap of your pads, a look at the clock. Something small and physical.

COACH: Pull discipline and contact together for me.

PLAYER: A penalty is worth it only to stop something more likely to score than their power play, only while it stays a minor, and never with our goalie pulled. Never with a lead late, never on the kill or the power play, never after the whistle. Find out whether I may check at all. Where I may, if I could pull out of a hit, pull out, and if they've turned away, leave them. After a goal against, play one simple shift. And have a reset.

COACH: That's all of it.

COACH: Seventeenth idea. Line matching and last change. After a whistle, the visiting team puts its players out first, then the home team gets to change. That's called last change. The home coach can wait, see who you've sent out, and pick who goes against them.

PLAYER: Who do they usually pick?

COACH: The classic use is a checking line against the other team's best scorers. A checking line is a line picked for defence, forechecking and faceoffs, rather than scoring.

COACH: Last change is the main mechanical part of home advantage, though only part. Travel, rest and knowing the rink are in there too. The road team's answer is to change on the fly, when the other bench can't react cleanly. So change discipline matters even more on the road.

PLAYER: And if I'm on the line being checked?

COACH: Change on the fly, not at whistles, so you don't show your hand first. Don't take the bait and try to beat your player one-on-one into traffic. Move the puck early. If three of them are focused on you, two of your teammates are open. A shift with no goals either way and no penalties against a strong checking line is a win, if your other lines are winning theirs.

PLAYER: And if I'm on the checking line?

COACH: Your first job is not to get scored on. Get the puck deep and change. Long shifts against a top line are how the goal happens. Be first on the faceoff and first back. You were sent out to be reliable, not creative.

PLAYER: Does it matter how I get on and off at a whistle?

COACH: It does. After a whistle, go straight to the faceoff dot, or straight to the bench. Under the professional rulebooks and in British hockey, dawdling on the way is a bench penalty, with no warning first. And plenty of rec leagues don't run a formal change at stoppages at all. The referee just drops the puck. Ask yours. If so, your matching tool is the change on the fly.

COACH: Eighteenth idea. Four against four. When a player from each team goes to the penalty box at the same stoppage, and nothing else is on the clock, many leagues play four against four. In British hockey that usually isn't how it works, so ask.

PLAYER: What changes at four against four?

COACH: There's more ice, so there's more time. Hold the puck a beat longer, and make the second option available. The middle is open, which cuts both ways. More clean entries for you, and more odd-man rushes against you. So the defensive worry is the rush up the far side. If you lose the puck at their blue line, it's two against one with nobody to help.

COACH: Nineteenth idea. Overtime and shootouts. First, find out whether your league plays overtime at all. Formats run from four against four, to three against three, to a straight shootout, to none at all. In British hockey, many games simply end level, so a tied third period ends with the horn. Ask before the game.

COACH: Say it's three against three. You've got the puck in their zone, and you've got a half-chance from the side. What would you do?

PLAYER: Shoot. There's so much space.

COACH: Three against three is a possession game, not a shooting gallery. If you don't have a really good look, don't shoot. If you give up the puck, you face three against two the other way straight away. And a shot that misses the net often turns into a goal against.

PLAYER: So what do I do instead?

COACH: With no good option in their zone, carry or pass the puck back out through the neutral zone, reset, and come again. Retreating without forcing anything is normal and correct. A pass back to your own goalie is a fair reset too. Change only when you have the puck. Never give it up just to get fresh legs.

COACH: Where teams switch ends for overtime, your bench is the far one. The winner often comes from catching a tired defender who can't get off. Where they don't switch ends, a tired player can reach the bench, so that plan is much less reliable.

COACH: Where overtime is full periods of five against five, it's normal hockey played tired, where one mistake ends it. Play simple, get pucks in behind their defence, don't be the one who makes the play that loses it, and keep your shifts short.

PLAYER: And if it goes to a shootout?

COACH: Decide your move before you start skating. Deciding at the hash marks, the short lines outside the faceoff circles, is how you end up doing neither thing well. Have two moves, and know which one you're using. Head up, puck out in front, so both a shot and a deke are possible. Get the goalie moving first, then go where they've just left. And hit the net. A miss is the worst outcome, because a shot on target at least gives the goalie a chance to make a mistake.

PLAYER: And if I'm the goalie?

COACH: Stay square and patient, track the puck, not the hands, and let the shooter decide first. A goalie who moves first hands the shooter a side. How many shooters each team gets, and whether the same player can shoot again, depends on your league. Find out before it happens.

COACH: Last idea. The time-out. Each team usually gets one. Use it for rest and instruction at a moment you choose, like before a big faceoff in your own end, or before going six against five.

PLAYER: Not to change the momentum?

COACH: It isn't a way to change momentum. The study behind this found no real effect on winning. But it's free, and most benches never use it at all. Ask for it early, while the players are still changing after the whistle. In some leagues that's when the window closes, not when the puck drops. And some games with a hard finishing time have no time-outs at all, so find out at the start.

COACH: Let me pull it all together. Keep asking how much risk the score and the clock allow.

COACH: With a lead, manage the puck. Nothing across the middle, never across the front of your own net, chip it out and keep it in play, take away the middle, short shifts, and win your own-end draws.

COACH: Behind, add risk in order, scaled to the clock. Somebody is always the last player back, with the swap called out loud.

COACH: Change when your team has the puck going north. Never when they have it in your end, never as the last player back, and not in the last minute of a period. And never ice it to get a rest.

COACH: Pull the goalie earlier than feels natural, and screen without touching the goalie. On a delayed penalty, get the extra attacker on at once. With your own net empty, no foul helps you, and a loose puck in your crease gets knocked straight down. Defending an empty net, don't chase, and remember you can still be iced.

COACH: After a goal against, play one simple shift. Find out whether you may check at all, and if you could pull out of a hit, pull out. Keep your feet moving, and give up the last word.

PLAYER: What should I ask my own coach?

COACH: Much of this is your team's choice. So here are three questions for your coach. What forecheck do we switch to with a lead? Who covers the point when a defenceman pinches? And what shape do we play at six against five, at both ends? And two for your league. Can we body check in this game? And what happens after an icing, and is there overtime?

COACH: Their answers tell you how your team wants you to manage a game, and now you know why each answer makes sense.
