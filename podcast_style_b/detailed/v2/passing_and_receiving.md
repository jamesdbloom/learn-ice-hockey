If you know the reason, you can still make the right play when the ice looks a bit different from the picture in your head.

COACH: A pass takes two players. One sends it, and one receives it. Most of this episode comes back to one of those two jobs. We'll start with four big ideas, because they matter most. If you only hear the first part, take those four with you. After that we go deeper. First receiving, then the passes themselves, then the rink, one end at a time.

COACH: First idea. Move it, then move yourself. Picture yourself picking up a loose puck in the middle of the ice. There's open ice in front of you, and a teammate is free on the far side. What would you do?

PLAYER: Honestly? Skate. There's room.

COACH: Think about what the other team does then. If you carry it, they just skate alongside you. If you pass it, four or five of them have to look again, and each one has to pick a new player to cover.

PLAYER: Because the puck gets there before they do.

COACH: Right. The puck moves faster than any skater can, and that one fact is the whole argument for passing. It's how a team that moves the puck well can beat a team of better skaters. You can't out-skate five people. But you can out-pass them.

PLAYER: Is that the only reason?

COACH: There's a second one. When you carry, all the risk sits on one player, and that player is you. A completed pass makes the defender's work count for nothing. So when the puck comes to you, your first thought is who is open. Not where can I skate.

PLAYER: So I should basically never carry it?

COACH: No. Carry when nobody is open. Carry when the defender in front of you has misjudged the space between you. Or carry to buy time while a teammate gets to you. Those are choices you make on purpose. The rest of the time, pass first, then skate into space.

COACH: Second idea. Look before the puck gets to you. Picture two players getting the same pass on the half-wall. The first one watches the puck the whole way. It lands, they lift their head, and the checker is right there. They panic and throw it up the boards to nobody. By the checker, I mean any opponent coming at you, after the puck or not. Coaches also say "your check" for the player you're covering, but in this episode the checker is always the one coming at you.

PLAYER: And the second player?

COACH: Looks over both shoulders while the pass is still travelling. When the puck lands, they already know where it's going next.

PLAYER: What's wrong with looking once it's on my stick?

COACH: Two things. By then the checker is already on you. And what you see is already out of date. The best passers have decided where the puck is going before it reaches them. Players who look calm under pressure didn't think faster. They decided earlier.

PLAYER: Does that go for when I'm the one passing?

COACH: The same. Keep your head up. Your eyes need to see the open line to your teammate, the checker and any hit that's coming. If you have to look down at the puck to pass it, your passing is only as good as your stickhandling. So work on handling the puck by feel, and keep your eyes for the ice.

COACH: Third idea. Pass to where your teammate is going, on the side away from the checker. Your winger is flying up the boards, and you've got the puck in the middle. What would you do? Where do you aim?

PLAYER: Just ahead of them, I think. Not right at their stick.

COACH: Good. Think about what happens if you aim at where they are now. By the time the puck gets there, they've moved on, so it arrives behind them. You put the puck ahead, timed so they skate onto it without breaking stride. That's called passing into space. A pass right onto the blade, where it is now, is for a teammate who's standing still or about to shoot.

COACH: Read their feet, not their chest. A player's chest can face anywhere. Their skates tell you where they're really going, and how fast. PLAYER: So where I put the puck decides where their head is.

COACH: Exactly. Put it where they can take it with their head up.

COACH: Given a choice, three things. Their forehand, away from the checker, and slightly in front, so their first touch goes forward. Then your teammate's body sits between the puck and the defender, and their body protects the puck for them.

PLAYER: What if their right is their backhand?

COACH: When those three clash, away from the checker wins, even if that means the backhand. A backhand catch with time beats a forehand catch with a stick in your ribs.

COACH: Fourth idea. Never pass the puck, or skate it, across the front of your own goal. You're in your own end with the puck. A teammate is open on the far side, and there's a clear gap straight across in front of your net. What would you do?

PLAYER: It's tempting. It looks open.

COACH: It does, and it's still the pass not to make. A pass picked off at their blue line costs you the puck. The same pass picked off in front of your own net is a scoring chance against you, and your goalie may not be set for it. The same goes for skating it across. Lose it there, and it's the same chance against you.

PLAYER: So which passes are out in my own end?

COACH: Three. First, no blind pass up the middle. The middle of your own end is right in front of your net, and if you can't see the receiver, the pass doesn't exist. Second, no pass from one defenceman to the other through the slot. The slot is the dangerous ice in front of the net, between the two faceoff circles. That pass goes behind the net instead, because a blocked or deflected pass in the slot is an easy goal against. Some teams also allow one higher up, above the tops of the faceoff circles.

PLAYER: And the third?

COACH: No pass across the ice in your own end. If it's picked off, your whole team is on the wrong side of the puck. Some teams accept one above the tops of the circles, where a pick-off isn't a chance from the slot. Below them, almost nobody does.

PLAYER: Are those rules, then?

COACH: No. They're safe defaults, not laws of hockey. Some breakouts build in a planned pass between the defencemen, behind the net. That's a designed play, with eyes up, support set and a way out. PLAYER: It sounds like my end and theirs are almost different games.

COACH: In a way they are. In your own end, the standard is certainty. In their end, the standard is value. A mistake in front of your net gets punished straight away. COACH: Those four are the core. Give them back to me.

COACH: Fifth idea. Get open for the passer. Your defenceman has the puck, head up, looking for you. You're skating hard and nobody's near you. And still the pass doesn't come.

PLAYER: That happens to me. I end up thinking they just didn't see me.

COACH: Maybe. Or maybe one defender's stick was lying across the only line to you. A passing lane is a clear line along the ice between the puck and a teammate, with no stick or skate in it. PLAYER: So when a pass goes wrong, it's not always the passer.

COACH: Most bad passes are really bad pairs. The receiver makes the pass as much as the passer does. Passing gets practised, and receiving mostly gets assumed. That's backwards, because a pass is only as good as the touch that ends it, and a lot of pucks are lost on the catch.

PLAYER: What is the passer actually looking for?

COACH: Someone who is open, in a clear lane, and ready now. If nobody is all three, there's no pass, and the player holding the puck usually gets the blame. So as the receiver you owe the passer four things. First, move into a lane, don't just move. Skating hard through ice that a defender's stick covers doesn't help. Second, timing. Get to the open space too early and a defender arrives with you. When you arrive matters as much as where.

PLAYER: And the other two?

COACH: Third, a target. Put your stick blade flat on the ice, out in front of your body, angled toward the passer, and hold it still. That blade is what the passer aims at. If it's off the ice or waving about, the passer has to guess, and guesses end up in your feet. A still blade on the ice says I'm open and I want it now. Then add your voice. Call for it. A called name and a still blade leave no doubt. Fourth, a next option. Know where you're going once you have the puck. A catch with nowhere to go next isn't support. It's a trap.

PLAYER: Does the passer owe me anything back?

COACH: Three things. A pass you can take in stride. A pass on the side away from pressure. And the pass at the moment the gap opens, not two seconds later, once you've drifted into coverage. So if your team can't complete passes, look at the players without the puck first. Then look at the passer.

COACH: Sixth idea. Cushion the puck. This is the central receiving skill. Think about catching a raw egg someone has thrown to you.

PLAYER: You'd let your hands go back with it. Otherwise it breaks.

COACH: The puck works the same way. Meet it out in front of you, with your bottom hand pushing the stick away from your body. As the puck touches the blade, draw the blade back toward you. Let it travel with the puck for a few inches. You're soaking up the pass, not stopping it dead. And hold the stick with a loose grip. That's what people mean by soft hands.

PLAYER: What does a tight grip actually do?

COACH: It makes the blade stiff, and a stiff blade is a wall. A puck that hits a wall bounces off it. A blocked pass bounces a foot or two off your blade, and that foot is all a checker needs. A cushioned pass stops on your blade, under control and ready to play.

PLAYER: Forehand or backhand?

COACH: Where you can, take it on your forehand. It gives you a cupped blade, more of the blade under the puck, and a shot or a pass straight away. Usually you can choose, by turning your body a little before the puck arrives. But don't let that make you late. Getting your blade to the puck matters more than which side it's on. And practise backhand catches on purpose, because in traffic you won't get to choose.

COACH: Seventh idea. Your first touch decides the play. A good first touch buys you half a second. A bad one costs you the puck.

PLAYER: Half a second doesn't sound like much.

COACH: On the ice it's the difference between a shot and a blocked shot. Or between a clean exit and a battle in your own corner. The rule is simple. Take the puck where your next play needs it, not where it happens to land. Let's try some. A checker is closing from your left as the pass arrives. Where does your first touch go?

PLAYER: Away from them. To my right.

COACH: Slightly to your right, yes, so the catch and the escape become one movement, not two. Now you're in the slot, and the pass is coming.

PLAYER: Somewhere I can shoot from.

COACH: Beside your back foot, ready to shoot. Not into your feet. And skating up the ice?

PLAYER: Forward, so I keep going.

COACH: Then your next stride is a stride, not a stop. And with nothing on in the middle, take it into open ice. Don't stop it under your feet and then look up. A puck under your feet has to be moved before you can play it. Here's a test for every catch. After your first touch, could you pass or shoot straight away? If not, that touch was wasted, and every wasted touch is time you've handed to the defence.

COACH: Eighth idea. Receive on the move, with your body open to the ice. Almost every real catch happens at speed. So think about how you wait for a pass. Do you glide?

PLAYER: Usually, yes. It feels steadier.

COACH: It costs you a stride every single time, and a player standing still is a target. Keep your feet moving through the catch. And change your speed to meet the pass, rather than asking the pass to meet your speed. A small glide, or a hard extra stride, turns a difficult pass into an easy one, and nobody else even notices you did it.

PLAYER: Where should my blade be?

COACH: Out ahead of your skates, not beside them. If the puck reaches you beside your feet, you've got no room to cushion it. And turn to the puck with your feet first, then your hands. Don't let your stick pull your body off line. Reaching with your arms while your skates go somewhere else puts you off balance.

PLAYER: You said open to the ice. What does that mean exactly?

COACH: Your hips and shoulders are turned so you can see up the ice and across it as the puck arrives. Not square to the passer, with your back to everything you want to do next. Come to the puck on an angle, not straight at the passer. Curl into the catch, so your speed and your eyes already point where you want to go.

PLAYER: What do I get out of curling in?

COACH: Your next option is already in sight when the puck lands. If you have to turn to find it, you've given the checker the time they needed. So decide which way you're going before the puck arrives, and be pointing that way when it does. It matters most in two places. The half-wall in their end, and any breakout pass in your own end. There, a closed body lets the pressure arrive on your back.

PLAYER: And on the boards, pressure on my back means what?

COACH: A hit from behind into the boards. Opening up is your first answer, because you see the checker coming instead of feeling them arrive. But there's a second answer, for the moment of contact, and that one isn't a choice. Never turn your back to anyone coming at you. And never duck. Keep your head up and your chin off your chest.

PLAYER: What's the thinking behind that one?

COACH: Your head and neck. With your back turned, you can't see the hit coming or brace for it. If you duck, your head meets the boards first. That's true even going slowly, so a slow checker doesn't make it safe. So here's the position you aim for. Skates parallel to the boards. Forearm and hip to the boards. Never the point of your shoulder, and not the whole shoulder if you can help it. Head up, chin off your chest.

PLAYER: And if I'm already facing the wall and can't turn?

COACH: Get your stick and gloves up on the glass, spread wide, with your head off it. That's only for when things have gone wrong. It isn't a position to aim for. From here on I'll call all of that the boards position, and say it the same way every time. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to anyone coming at you, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly.

COACH: Ninth idea. Bad passes, and pucks in your feet. The test of a good receiver is what they do with a bad pass. A pass comes in hard, at your skates and wide of your stick. What would you do?

PLAYER: Try to get my stick on it and make the play anyway?

COACH: That's how bad passes become turnovers. First, get your body in front of it. Shin pads, skates and chest are all legal ways to stop a puck, and a puck stopped by your body is still your puck. But this is a pass, not a shot. Stepping in front of a shot is a different skill, with its own technique and its own times not to try it. Nothing here carries over to it.

PLAYER: Then what?

COACH: Second, stop it first, then look. Don't try to catch a difficult puck and make a play in the same motion. Stop it, then play it. Third, accept losing half a second. If a bad pass costs you the play you wanted, take the safe next option. Forcing the first plan through a bad catch is the real mistake.

PLAYER: What about one that's bouncing, or rolling on its edge?

COACH: Cup the blade over it. That means the top edge of the blade tilts forward over the puck. Use the same angle every time, hopping, on edge or flat. The cup comes from keeping your top hand off your hip. With your top hand on your hip, the blade opens, and the puck jumps right over it. A flat puck takes one touch. A bouncing one usually takes two, one to knock it flat and one to play it, so plan for the extra beat.

PLAYER: Does it matter where on the blade?

COACH: Take it on the middle of the blade, not the toe. The toe is the tip, the far end from the shaft, and it has the least surface. And angle the blade so that if the puck squirts off, it goes somewhere safe, not into the slot.

PLAYER: And when it ends up right in my feet?

COACH: That will happen too, and handling it is a skill, not bad luck. Kick it forward to your stick with the inside of your skate. One deliberate, controlled kick to a spot in front of you, not a panicked scuff. Send your skate at the puck, never at a player. Or open your hips, let it run through, and pick it up behind your stride. Turning your body a little is usually faster than reaching. What you don't do is reach back with your stick while you keep skating forward. That's how you end up bent over, off balance, and hit.

PLAYER: What do I have to watch with the kick?

COACH: Kicking the puck is allowed anywhere on the ice. Kicking a player is something else, and a puck in your feet usually has an opponent's feet right beside it. Kicking an opponent, or even trying to, is a match penalty in most leagues, which throws you out of the game. Everywhere else, the referee can still throw you out. And never use your skate to push a player either, even without a kick. In at least one league that alone is a major penalty, and you're thrown out of the game. So keep the kick small, deliberate, and at the puck.

PLAYER: Can I score off a kick?

COACH: A puck you kick in off your own stick counts as a goal in some leagues and not in others. So kick it to your stick, get control, and then shoot it clearly. And never kick your own stick blade into the puck to shoot. That's called a kick shot. It won't count as a goal.

COACH: Tenth idea. Pucks in the air, and your hand. A clearing attempt pops up off a stick and is coming at you about head height. What's your move?

PLAYER: Knock it down with my stick?

COACH: Knock it down at chest height, never above your shoulders. Hitting the puck with your stick above shoulder height isn't allowed in any league. In at least one league, shoulder height is measured with you standing up straight, so crouching doesn't make a higher puck fair. And keep playing until you hear the whistle. In most leagues the whistle waits to see which team gets the puck, so it can come late.

PLAYER: What if I just swing at it and hope?

COACH: Then the part that matters most is where your stick goes. If it reaches an opponent above the shoulders, it isn't a puck rule any more. It's a penalty in every league. Every league has a way to throw you out of the game for it, and for a reckless one, some leagues always do. Some leagues let off an accident on a normal shooting follow-through. None lets off a wild swing at a bouncing puck, and some give no excuse at all. In at least one league there's no small penalty up there. Any contact above the shoulders is a major, and it ends your game unless it was an accident and nobody was hurt.

PLAYER: Even if I don't touch anyone?

COACH: In some leagues, carrying your stick up high near an opponent can be a penalty on its own. So play the puck at chest height, and keep your stick down near other players.

PLAYER: Can I just catch it with my glove instead?

COACH: You can catch it, but put it straight down on the ice. If you close your hand on it and skate, even one stride, in most leagues that's a minor penalty. If you knock it to a teammate with your hand, that's a hand pass. It's only allowed in your own end, inside your own blue line. Anywhere else, play stops. So keep the puck inside your blue line when you touch it and when your teammate gets it, and keep your teammate inside that line too. Then it's fine in every league.

PLAYER: Is it different right by our net?

COACH: In your own crease, yes. That's the painted area in front of your net. Never cover the puck there, hold it or pick it up. Knock it straight down. Covering it there can give the other team a penalty shot, a free shot alone against your goalie. With your goalie pulled for an extra skater, it can give them a goal.

COACH: That's receiving. Let's hear it back.

PLAYER: Get open, in a clear lane, on time. A still blade flat on the ice, and call for it. Cushion the puck with a loose grip. First touch into my next play. COACH: Spot on. Now the passes themselves, one at a time. For each one, how to make it, when to use it, and what it costs when it goes wrong.

COACH: Eleventh idea. The flat pass, also called the sweep pass. Use it any time the lane is clear and your teammate can be reached along the ice.

PLAYER: Why's that the default, and not something fancier?

COACH: It's the easiest pass to catch, and that's the point. Start the puck near the heel of the blade. That's the end nearest the shaft. Your bottom hand goes about halfway down the shaft. Cup the blade over the puck, so it stays flat. An open blade sends the puck up on its edge. Then sweep it, don't slap it. Your bottom hand pushes the puck along the ice in one motion. The puck rolls from heel to toe and leaves off the toe.

PLAYER: My passes go wide a lot.

COACH: Then check first whether you're slapping. A slap touches the puck for an instant, while a sweep guides it. Next, move your weight from your back leg, through your hips, to your front foot. The power comes from your body, not your arms. Passes made with just the arms float, and floaters get picked off. Finish low, about knee height, with the blade pointing at your target. The puck leaves along the line the blade face points, so finishing at the target is how you get that line right. A high finish sends pucks over sticks and into shins.

PLAYER: So it's mostly about technique.

COACH: Actually, the risk with a flat pass is almost never the technique. It's the lane you pick. A flat pass into a stick or a skate is a turnover, so a clear lane comes first.

COACH: Twelfth idea. The other passes, and when each one earns its place. Start with the saucer pass. It goes through the air, over something, and lands flat.

PLAYER: Mine tend to land on their edge.

COACH: Open the face of the blade slightly, like a wedge. Sweep from heel to toe, and roll your wrists up as it leaves. Drawing the blade slightly toward you puts spin on it, and the spin is what makes it land flat. Land it a few feet short of your teammate's stick, so it settles and slides the last bit. A saucer that's still in the air when it reaches the stick is nearly impossible to catch.

PLAYER: When should I use it?

COACH: Only when it's the only option. A stick lying across the lane is one. A pass that has to cross a body is another. Never saucer when a flat pass is on, because air is a difficulty you're adding on purpose. And one that lands on its edge is worse than no pass, because your teammate commits to it and then loses it.

PLAYER: What about a really hard pass?

COACH: The hard pass, or snap pass, is a shorter sweep with a sharp snap of the wrists. You trade a little accuracy for a lot of speed. Use it when a lane is open but closing. Say a backchecker, a forward racing back to defend, is about to fill the gap. Or a teammate is about to be checked and needs the puck early. It's also the pass into the slot for a one-timer, a shot taken straight off the pass.

PLAYER: So harder is better?

COACH: Not at a teammate standing still with nobody near them. A hard pass your teammate can't handle is your turnover, not theirs. Pace is a tool for beating a closing gap, not a show of strength.

PLAYER: And the backhand pass? I mostly avoid it.

COACH: Then defenders will learn that fast. If you can only pass off your forehand, you have to turn to make half your plays, and you rarely have time to turn. So they take your forehand away and force the turn, because the turn kills the play. Your backhand pass doesn't need to look good. It needs to exist, so the checker can't cheat to one side.

PLAYER: How do I make one?

COACH: Start the puck at the middle or heel of the blade on your backhand side, and roll it heel to toe, just as on the forehand. Pull with your bottom hand and sweep across your body. Drive through with your lower shoulder. The power comes from your body turning, not just your arms. A backhand is slower and floats more, so give it a slightly cleaner lane.

COACH: Thirteenth idea. Using the boards, with the bank, the rim and the reverse. The boards can be a teammate. Say there's a stick or a player in your direct lane to a teammate, but the boards run behind it.

PLAYER: So I go round it, off the wall.

COACH: That's a bank pass. Play the angle, not your teammate. The puck comes off the boards at about the mirror of the angle it goes in, like a ball off the cushion on a snooker table. So aim at the spot on the wall that bounces toward them. Keep it flat and firm, and hit the boards low, where the bounce is truest. Boards bounce differently in every rink, so test a bank in the warm-up.

PLAYER: Is it a safe pass?

COACH: Safe, because the puck never goes through the lane the checker is guarding. It goes behind them, off a wall they can't cover. But safe isn't the same as reliable. The bounce isn't fully predictable, and your teammate might misread it too. So call it.

COACH: Now the rim and the reverse. They're opposite plays, and players mix them up. A rim goes onward around the boards, the way the play is already going. It's hard, flat and low, following the curve of the rink. It beats a checker who has you sealed against the wall, because it goes past them. A reverse goes back the way the puck came. It's short and firm, usually on your backhand, into the space behind you. It beats a checker who came in too hard and skated past you, because it goes into the space they just left.

PLAYER: How do I pick one in the moment?

COACH: Let's try it. You're on the wall with the puck. The checker came flying in, and now they're past you, feet pointing the wrong way. What would you do?

PLAYER: Reverse it, back into the space they left.

COACH: If a teammate is there, yes. Watch which way the checker is moving, not where they're standing. If they're still coming and have you pinned, rim it. If they've already gone by you, reverse it.

PLAYER: Why only if a teammate's there?

COACH: Never reverse on hope. A reverse needs a teammate there. A reverse into nobody is a turnover you can't chase, because the puck goes one way and you go the other. And reverses usually happen deep in your own end, where a loose puck is a chance against you. The rim can go wrong too. Call your rim, because a rim your teammate isn't expecting goes to the opponent beside them. And a soft rim dies in the corner.

PLAYER: How should I be standing when I rim it or reverse it?

COACH: That's part of the play, because you're on the wall with a checker on you. The natural way is square to the wall, head down, watching the puck onto your blade. Don't do it. Head down on the wall, digging for the puck, is exactly the picture of a hit from behind.

PLAYER: So what instead?

COACH: Rim it in the boards position. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to anyone coming at you, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly. Find the boards by feel, not by looking. And if you're caught facing the wall, get your stick and gloves up on the glass, spread wide, head off it. That's only for when things have gone wrong.

PLAYER: And if I'm the one arriving on the wall?

COACH: Play the puck, and leave the turned back alone. Never hit or push a player from behind. That hit can end your game in any league.

COACH: Fourteenth idea. The chip, the drop and the stretch. You make these passes on a read, never on a hope. The chip, or area pass, aims at a patch of ice, not at a stick. You're carrying the puck at their blue line, and a defender is standing you up there. Your winger is already skating past on the outside.

PLAYER: Chip it past the defender, into the space behind.

COACH: That's the classic one. Put it flat, hard enough to get past the nearest stick, but not so hard that it runs to the far boards. It can't be picked off like a normal pass, because it isn't aimed at anyone. It turns into a race.

PLAYER: And who wins the race?

COACH: That's the question to ask before you chip. Only make it when your teammate can win, with more speed or a better angle. A chip into a defender's feet with nobody chasing just gives the puck away.

COACH: The drop pass is when you leave the puck behind you for a teammate following you. That teammate is called the trailer. So, you're carrying in, you think someone is behind you, but nobody has said anything. Do you drop it?

PLAYER: Drop it and hope they're there?

COACH: Keep it. If you're not sure the trailer is there, don't drop it. The drop fails a lot, and it's worth knowing why. Two things make it work, and both are usually missing. One, leave the puck flat and dead. Don't push it back. A puck moving back at a trailer skating forward is nearly impossible to catch. Two, keep skating hard, so you take a defender with you. If you slow down, nobody has been taken out of the play.

PLAYER: What else goes wrong with it?

COACH: The trailer isn't really there, or is level with you instead of behind. Nobody said anything, so it surprises you both. And at their blue line, a failed drop sends them straight back at you, with all your forwards going the wrong way. Some teams build a drop pass into how they enter the zone, on purpose. That's a coaching choice, so ask whether yours does.

COACH: Then the stretch pass. It's a long, hard pass, usually from a defenceman in or near their own end, to a forward who has got behind the other team's forwards. It needs a full weight shift and a long follow-through. Most missed stretch passes are too soft, not off target.

PLAYER: When is it on?

COACH: It works best right after your team wins the puck back, while their forwards are still skating the wrong way. If it connects, it's a breakaway or a two-on-one. If it misses, you usually lose the puck with your own forwards caught up the ice. That's how odd-man rushes against you start, where more of them attack than you have back. So ask yourself honestly. Is a teammate actually behind their forwards right now, or am I hoping? Hope isn't a passing lane.

PLAYER: And if I'm the forward waiting for it?

COACH: You've got one job at their blue line. A pass can cross both blue lines and the red line at centre. What limits how far forward you can be is offside. You're offside if both your skates are completely over their blue line before the puck crosses it. So keep the blade of your back skate on the ice, touching the line or behind it, until the puck is over. That's onside in every league.

PLAYER: Let me try the passes. Flat is the default, and the lane comes first. A saucer only when something's in the way. A hard pass to beat a closing gap. Have a backhand. Bank it when a stick's in the lane, and call it. Rim past a checker who has me pinned, and reverse behind one who has skated past. Chip, drop and stretch only on a read. And at their blue line, back skate on the ice.

COACH: That's all of it. Now the rink, one end at a time. These are the common approaches. Where coaches differ, I'll say so, so you can ask which way your team plays.

COACH: Fifteenth idea. Your own end. Breaking out first. Say you're a defenceman with the puck behind your own net. The common first option is a firm, flat pass to the winger on the puck side, up the boards. Put it on their forehand, or off the boards to them.

PLAYER: And if that winger's covered?

COACH: Reverse it behind the net to your partner, or to the centre swinging low. If everything is covered, skate it or rim it hard, but never across the front of your net. And never answer pressure there with a pass through the slot. Teams run different breakout patterns, so ask where your team wants the first pass.

PLAYER: What if I'm the winger taking it on the boards?

COACH: It's the catch we covered earlier. Come to it open to the ice, and decide your way out before it gets to you. Whoever has the puck on the boards, the defenceman rimming it or the winger taking it, if the hit comes, use the boards position. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to anyone coming at you, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly.

COACH: Now pressure. You've got the puck in your own corner, and two of them are closing. Under pressure you have less time to look, the lanes close faster, and a mistake costs more.

PLAYER: So I need the perfect pass.

COACH: The opposite. The pass you can definitely complete beats the pass you'd like to complete. Keep it simple. You've got a handful of safe tools. A rim. A bank. A give-and-go, where you pass to a teammate, skate straight into open space, and get it straight back. A chip into space. Or a reset back to your defence. All of them keep the puck moving without threading a needle. And use the boards and the glass. They're always there, and nobody can intercept them.

PLAYER: Doesn't a reset feel like giving up?

COACH: A reset isn't a failure. Sending the puck back to your defenceman, or off the glass and out of your end, is a completed play. It's only a failure if you had a better option and didn't see it. Two more things. Buy extra time by moving your feet, not by standing still. A player standing still under pressure has one option, and it keeps shrinking. And decide your way out while you're still skating to the puck, not after you're pinned.

PLAYER: Does it change when we're winning late on?

COACH: Protecting a lead late in a period, take no chances with the puck in your own half. No pass across the middle of your own end. No soft pass through the middle at your own blue line. Get it out off the boards, or deep into their end. Make them come the whole length of the ice to score.

COACH: Sixteenth idea. Don't panic pass. You'll know this moment. The puck comes to you, a checker is charging in, and before you've even looked, the puck's gone. Off your stick, to nobody in particular.

PLAYER: Guilty. It feels like there's no time at all.

COACH: That's a panic pass, a pass made without looking, just to get rid of the puck. Panic is the normal state for a player who's still learning, and panic spends time you actually had. Every rushed pass, and every puck flipped to nobody, was a moment with more time than it felt.

PLAYER: How would I know I had more time?

COACH: Ask yourself one question after every shift. Did I have more time than I thought? The honest answer is almost always yes. Keep asking it for ten games and the habit takes.

PLAYER: So the fix is just to be braver?

COACH: Nobody can act on "be braver". The fix is practical. Get one more look before you receive, so you know the answer before the puck arrives. And while the puck is still travelling, name a second choice as well as your first. Panic is what happens when you run out of options in the middle of a play. If your first option disappears, you're already on your second.

COACH: Let's try it. You're out in the middle of the ice, outside your own end. The puck comes to you, a checker is closing fast, and you haven't seen a teammate. What would you do?

PLAYER: Get it out of there? Someone might be open.

COACH: That's the panic pass again. When you don't know, keep it, and turn side-on, away from the pressure, rather than passing into it. Your shoulder goes to the checker, with the puck on the far side of your body. Never turn your back to them, anywhere on the ice. Keeping it costs you a second. A pass into pressure is a turnover. And near the boards, that means the boards position.

PLAYER: Should I keep it in my own end too?

COACH: No. In your own end, holding it is how the turnover happens. One look, one decision, move it, with one of the safe tools, like a rim or back to your defence.

PLAYER: Is there anything that helps in the moment?

COACH: Listen for "time". If a teammate or your goalie shouts it, it means nobody is close to you. Believe them. They can see what you can't. Slow down, lift your head, and make the good play. And if you're the one calling it, be sure. If you're not sure, say nothing. A false "time" sends a teammate into a hit they weren't braced for.

COACH: Seventeenth idea, and it's short. The middle of the ice. The neutral zone is the middle of the rink, between the two blue lines. There, when a direct pass would have to thread through traffic, the chip is often the safe choice. Chip it into space for a teammate who can win the race.

PLAYER: And if their blue line is a wall of players?

COACH: Regroup. Pass it back to your defence and come again with speed. Don't force a pass into a crowd at their blue line. A regroup is a completed play.

COACH: Eighteenth idea. Their end. Start with a two-on-one, two of you against one defender, and you've got the puck. What would you do?

PLAYER: Pass across straight away, so my partner has the open net?

COACH: Not yet. Make the defender choose. Drive at the net under control, as if you're going to shoot. Pass only when the defender's stick leaves the lane. Then pass early and flat, to your partner's stick ahead of them, not at their skates, so they can shoot before the goalie gets across. If the defender takes the pass away, shoot.

PLAYER: And after I shoot?

COACH: Pull up short of the goalie. Near the net, keep your head up and your chin off your chest, because the post can hurt you the same way the boards can. And never go into the goalie. A goalie is watching the puck, and one who is down or turned can't see you coming. Contact can hurt them, it can get you thrown out of the game, and it can cost your team the goal.

COACH: From behind their net, wait for a teammate to arrive in the slot. Pass to the spot they're skating into, flat and hard. A pass out front with nobody arriving is a turnover in the most dangerous place to have one.

PLAYER: What about passing back up to the point?

COACH: The point is the area just inside the blue line where defencemen usually stand. It's an area, not the line itself. From the half-wall up to the point, keep it hard, flat, and on the defenceman's forehand. A soft pass there is the one an opponent jumps on for a breakaway. If the lane isn't clean, cycle it down low instead, along the boards.

COACH: Then one-touch passing. A one-touch pass is one you send straight on, without stopping the puck first. A quick run of them is sometimes called tic-tac-toe.

PLAYER: How does that beat a defence that's already set up?

COACH: Defenders and goalies react to where the puck is. Every touch you take gives them time to reset. A one-touch moves the puck faster than a defence can shift. So it lands in a space that was covered when the pass started, and isn't covered when it arrives. The best power plays are built on one-touch passing.

PLAYER: When is it on?

COACH: When three things are true. The pass to you is flat and catchable. Your next target is already open, and you already know where. And the lane will still be there a moment later. It's off for a bouncing puck, a target you haven't found yet, or a defender close enough that a miss is an instant turnover. A one-touch that fails usually fails badly, because you never had the puck under control.

PLAYER: How do I set it up?

COACH: Get ready before the puck arrives. Pick your target while the puck is still coming. Set the angle of your blade before contact, because the blade face at contact is the pass. Keep a tiny cushion even then, soft on the catch and firm on the release. And set your feet, so you have a stable base.

COACH: Last in their end, fooling the defence. Defenders and goalies read your eyes, your shoulders and your blade.

PLAYER: So I can use that against them.

COACH: Look at one teammate, let the defender shift toward them, then pass to the other. That's the safest fake there is. You've already looked, so you know where both teammates are, and moving your eyes costs nothing. Turning your shoulders and hips one way pulls a defender's weight that way. Once a defender has committed their weight, they can't recover in time to stop a pass the other way. And sometimes holding the puck a beat longer works better than any fake. A defender timing your release freezes, and there's nothing to read.

PLAYER: What about a no-look pass?

COACH: That only makes sense when three things are true. You've already seen a teammate who isn't going to move. The defender is reading your eyes. And the pass is short. Never use one as a habit, and never in your own end. Your teammate may have moved since you looked, and then you've passed to where they used to be. And a fake only helps if the pass after it is accurate. Get the passing right first.

COACH: Nineteenth idea, and it's short. How to practise. Passing improves faster than almost any other skill. Most of it can be practised off the ice, on a smooth surface, with just a stick and a puck.

PLAYER: What do I actually do on my own?

COACH: Pass against a wall or a rebounder, and receive every return properly. Cushion it, control it, reset. Half the value is in the catching. Spend as much time on your backhand as your forehand. The backhand improves fastest, because it starts furthest behind. And mark a small target on the wall and aim at it. Accuracy without a target is just repetition.

PLAYER: And with a friend?

COACH: Skate down the ice side by side, leading each other into stride. That's the best drill there is for learning the lead. Have your partner send you bad passes on purpose, rolling, bouncing and behind you. Hardly anybody does it, and it's the fastest way to get better at the passes you'll really get. Then add a third player who pressures the passer, stick on the puck and no body contact. Clean passing in a quiet drill doesn't carry over to a game on its own.

COACH: Let me pull it all together.

COACH: Move it, then move yourself. Look over both shoulders while the puck is coming. Lead your teammate, on the side away from the checker, because a puck in their feet puts their head down. And never pass the puck, or skate it, across the front of your own goal.

COACH: Get open, give a still target, and call for it. Cushion the puck, and take your first touch into your next play. Keep your feet moving, and receive open to the ice. If the hit comes on the boards anyway, use the boards position. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to anyone coming at you, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly.

COACH: Flat is the default. Have a backhand. Rim or reverse by reading the checker. Chip, drop and stretch only on a read.

COACH: In your own end, make the pass you can complete, decide it before you get there, and never go through the slot. A reset is a completed play.

COACH: Don't panic pass. Get one more look, and have a second choice ready. Outside your own end, when you don't know, keep it, side-on to the checker. In your own end, one look, one decision, move it. And anywhere on the ice, never your back to anyone coming at you.

COACH: In the middle, chip it when the lane is crowded, and regroup when the way in is closed. In their end, make the defender choose on a two-on-one. Drive the net under control, head up, and never into the goalie. Pass hard and flat to the point. One-touch it when the target is already open. And look off a defender before you pass.

COACH: Kick the puck, never a player. Play a high puck down at chest height, and keep your stick down near other players.

PLAYER: What should I ask my own coach?

COACH: Three things. Which passes do we allow in our own end, and do our defencemen ever pass to each other above the circles? On the breakout, where do you want the first pass to go? And do we use a drop pass when we enter their zone? Ask, because these are your team's choices, not rules. Their answers tell you which passes your team wants, and now you know the reason behind each one.
