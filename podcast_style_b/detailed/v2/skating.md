Keep your shoulders level and your hips open to the play, so you can still see the puck and the attacker.

PLAYER: And if I'm playing forward?

COACH: Forwards need backward skating too. You use it reading the play while retreating through the middle of the ice. And you use it when you cover for a defenceman who pinches, which means stepping down from their blue line to keep the puck in.

COACH: Thirteenth idea. Pivot late, and know which way. A pivot is switching between skating forwards and skating backwards without stopping. Say you're skating forwards and need to skate backwards, still facing the play. When would you turn?

PLAYER: Early, so I'm set in time.

COACH: Stay square, going forwards, as long as you can. Every moment you're going forwards is a moment you could still step up, which means moving forward to challenge the carrier. If you step up, it's your stick on the puck, not a hit. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call, so find out. And if you can see the carrier's numbers, the numbers on their back, pull up and play the puck.

PLAYER: And when I do turn?

COACH: Open your hips first, not your shoulders. Your shoulders turning first spins your eyes off the carrier. Open your lead skate, put your weight on it, bring the other skate round, and start your C-cuts at once. And keep your knees bent through it. Players who stand up as they pivot are the ones who get beaten.

COACH: Then the part that holds for everyone. Pivot late. Once you've turned to skate forwards, you can't react to a change of direction any more. So stay backwards as long as you can.

PLAYER: And going from backwards to forwards?

COACH: That's for when the puck turns over and you need to go. Open your hip toward where you're going, plant, and drive out on your inside edge with a short, quick start. Most forwards lose a step here on a rush going the other way, so drill it both directions.

PLAYER: Which way should I pivot?

COACH: The usual coaching default for a defenceman is toward the boards, not toward the middle. The default is about what happens if you fail. If you get beaten in the pivot, you want to be beaten toward the wall. There, the boards act like a second defender, and the shooting angle is poor. You don't want to be beaten into the slot, the ice in front of your net between the two faceoff circles. That's where goals come from. How much the wall helps depends on how wide your rink is. On a wide rink, an attacker forced outside still has room to keep going. But on any rink, being beaten toward the wall is the better failure. And being beaten toward the wall isn't an invitation to ride the attacker into it, push them into it, or trip them into it. Driving a player into the boards is boarding. It's a penalty in every league, and it can get you thrown out of the game.

PLAYER: So toward the boards, always?

COACH: It's a default, not a law. Some coaches want a pivot to the middle in certain situations. One is when turning away would take your eyes off the puck. Another is when you have help on the inside, and the bigger danger is the attacker cutting behind you. So ask your coach which they want. And drill both ways, because the attacker decides the right direction, not which way you happen to be good at.

COACH: Let's pull backwards together. Hold your gap with your feet, C-cuts for cruising and backward crossovers when they speed up. Sit low, weight over the middle of the blade. Open your hips, keep your knees bent, and pivot late. Pivot toward the boards by default, but ask your coach, and drill both ways.

COACH: Fourteenth idea. Using your skating in a game. The same few situations come round every shift, and each one has a skating answer. Start with the loose puck. The race is the first three steps, so drive them. But how you arrive matters just as much.

PLAYER: Arrive how?

COACH: Knees bent, head up, on an angle to the boards, and able to stop. A player who wins the race at full stretch has no balance left to protect the puck. And because it ends at the boards, the boards position again. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to anyone coming at you, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly.

PLAYER: And when we lose the puck?

COACH: Stop and go the other way. Don't loop. Then the backcheck. Come back hard in your lane and pick up your attacker on the inside. That means goal side, between them and your net, not level with them. Get there with your skating. It's your stick on the puck, not a hit. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call. Never hit a player who can't see you coming, and never from behind. If you can see their numbers, pull up and play the puck.

PLAYER: Who takes which lane on the way back?

COACH: That's a team choice. Commonly the centre takes the middle lane and the wingers take the outside. Some teams give the middle to whoever gets back first. Ask which yours plays.

COACH: Next, driving the net. You're skating at the net for a rebound. What would you do when you get there?

PLAYER: Right on top of the crease, so I'm nearest the puck.

COACH: Near it, but stopped. Skate a straight line, stick on the ice, and pull up a skate's width outside the red line round the crease. The crease is the blue paint in front of the net. Rebounds go to the player who has stopped and is ready, not to the one still gliding past the post. Keep your skates off that red line as well as out of the blue, because some leagues count the line as part of the crease. And while the goalie is in the crease, keep your stick out of it too. In some leagues, a goal scored with a stick or skate in there doesn't count. Pull up short of the goalie, and never go into the goalie. A goalie watching the puck can't see you coming or brace for you, and contact can hurt them. It can also be a penalty, and in some leagues it can get you thrown out of the game. And it can cost your team the goal. At the net front, keep your head up and your chin off your chest. A goal post can hurt you the same way the boards can.

PLAYER: What about when I want the puck in their end?

COACH: Find open ice and stop in it. A stopped player gives your passer a target. A player circling through the space is open for an instant, and then gone. Keep your eyes up and your feet ready, because a stopped player is also easy to hit. On a breakout, the play that gets the puck out of your own end, many teams want the opposite. They want you swinging through with speed, to take the pass on the move. Ask your bench which they want, and where.

COACH: Last, the forecheck. Their defenceman has the puck behind their net, and you're the first one in. Where do you skate?

PLAYER: Straight at them.

COACH: Then they just turn away into open ice. Skate at where the carrier is going, not where they are. Come at an angle that takes away the middle, and you steer them toward the wall, where the play gets smaller. You steer with your skating and your stick, not your body. Pick your line early, hold it, and add nothing when you arrive. An arm, a shoulder, a hip or a late swerve turns the angle into a check. Stick on the puck, never on their hands or body. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call, so find out first. Never your stick across them, and never into their back. If you can see their numbers, pull up and play the puck. Even going slowly, a crash into the boards can hurt.

PLAYER: Who goes in first?

COACH: Who goes in first, and how hard, is your team's system. Ask what yours plays.

PLAYER: Let me try the game part. Drive the first three steps to a loose puck, and arrive able to stop. When the puck turns over, stop and go back. Come back goal side, stick on the puck, never a hit on someone who can't see me.

PLAYER: At the net, stop short of the crease, skates off the red line, stick out while the goalie's in, never into the goalie, and head up. Stop in open ice to get the puck, unless my team wants me swinging. And on the forecheck, skate at where the carrier's going, with my skating and my stick, not my body.

COACH: That's it.

COACH: Fifteenth idea. How to get better. You can't see yourself skate. Nearly every fault in this episode is invisible from the inside.

PLAYER: So how would I know if I'm finishing my push?

COACH: You honestly don't, from the inside. Or whether you're bringing your foot back under you. So get a coach to watch you. That's what power skating is, coached skating on its own, away from team practice. No coach? Have someone film you from the side and from behind. The faults are obvious on video.

COACH: And before you decide you've got a technique problem, check you don't have an equipment problem. Boots that are too big, too soft, or not done up properly make a deep knee bend and hard edges impossible, because your foot moves inside the boot. Dull or uneven blades, or a groove you're not used to, change how every edge feels.

PLAYER: Say I get thirty minutes of open ice. What do I do with it?

COACH: One sensible order. It's a coach's judgement, not a tested ranking, and a coach who has watched you may change it. Edge work first. Then weak-side crossovers, forwards and backwards. Then stops and starts both ways. Then pivots, forwards to backwards and back again, down the length of the ice. Then full strides from goal line to goal line, counting them. Then all of that again with a puck.

PLAYER: No sprints?

COACH: Long sprints at top speed aren't on the list. Game skating comes in short bursts, so edges, starts and pivots buy you more.

PLAYER: And why the puck half the time?

COACH: Because a puck changes your skating until you train it. Your arms stop, your head drops, your feet narrow, and you coast.

PLAYER: What if I don't get much ice time?

COACH: Then your position helps you choose. A centre gains most from pivots, turning both ways, and keeping good technique when tired. A winger gains most from the first three steps, straight-line speed, and switching from forechecking to backchecking. A defenceman gains most from backward skating, backward crossovers, pivots both ways, and moving sideways along the blue line. Those priorities assume a common setup, so ask your coach what your team's system asks of your feet. Goalies skate a different way altogether, and should train it with a goalie coach.

PLAYER: How long before I notice a difference?

COACH: Stopping and turning both ways takes a few months of steady practice. Elite edges and fast pivots take years. That's one coaching provider's guidance, not a measurement. You won't get fast this month. But you can get noticeably steadier, lower and better balanced this month, and that alone changes how many battles you win.

COACH: Let me pull it all together.

COACH: Start at the boards. Go in on an angle, head up, chin off your chest. If contact comes, the boards position. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to anyone coming at you, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly.

COACH: Win the first three steps. Change speed on purpose, and keep your feet moving when the puck arrives. When the play turns, stop and go straight back, on either side.

COACH: Stay low, keep your eyes up, and train your outside edges.

COACH: When you fall onto the ice, go to your side, hands in, chin in. Into the boards, head up. Check yourself before you get up. If a concussion is suspected, come off, don't go back that day, and not until a medical professional says you can.

COACH: Push out to the side and bring the foot back fast. Cross over with room, turn tight without it. Stop both ways.

COACH: On defence, hold your gap with your feet, and pivot late.

COACH: In a game, arrive able to stop. Come back goal side. Stop short of the crease, and never into the goalie. And steer with your skating and your stick, not your body.

PLAYER: What should I ask my own coach?

COACH: Three things. Which way do you want me to pivot? When I want the puck, do you want me stopped or swinging? And which is my weak side, for stopping and for crossovers? Ask, because much of this depends on how your coach teaches and what your team plays. Their answers tell you how your team wants you to skate, and now you know why each answer makes sense.
