COACH: The draw in your own end. The draw when you're a player short. The draw protecting a late lead. And the draw after an icing. PLAYER: And on a loss?

COACH: Your defencemen are the whole plan. They close the space on the carrier straight away and defend the rush. Stick on the puck, not a hit. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call. And if you see their numbers, pull up.

COACH: This is the place to try things. A draw you're still learning. Your flipped grip on your weak side. Or a draw you want the other centre to have in their head when you meet them in your own end later. A lost neutral-zone draw costs you almost nothing.

PLAYER: You mentioned something called losing it forward?

COACH: That means pushing the puck forward past the other centre and going after it. It works here because there's space behind them, and little danger if it fails. In their end it can sometimes beat a centre who's set on pulling back. In your own end, never. Go round the other centre, never through them. They're now between you and the puck, so chase the puck, not the ice they're standing on. Driving through them to reach it is interference, a penalty in any game, and where checking isn't allowed it's an illegal check as well. In women's hockey, stepping or gliding into a player who's standing still is a penalty too, and a major there gets you thrown out of the game.

COACH: Twelfth idea. Draws in their end. An offensive-zone draw is the one place in hockey where you get to run a real set play, from a known start, against defenders who are standing still. Here's a common default. Your team's may differ, so ask.

COACH: The centre tries to win it back, not forward. The defenceman on the draw side sits at the point, feet already set, stick already loaded. That's the shooter. The other defenceman holds the far point, as the safety against a counterattack. The inside winger is the usual target for a quick pull-back shot, and drives the net under control if the draw is lost, staying off the goalie. The boards-side winger is ready to attack the wall, stick on the puck, or spin off it.

PLAYER: Why back to the defenceman? The winger's closer to the net.

COACH: Because it's one of very few shots in hockey where you don't have to take a pass, settle it and then set your feet. The feet are already planted. That's worth more than a slightly better angle. One thing to watch. In at least one adult recreational league, a big backswing counts as a slap shot, which is a penalty there, and if it hurts someone it can end your game. If that's your league, keep the backswing short, and keep your follow-through below everyone's shoulders.

PLAYER: Say I'm the one driving the net. How close can I get?

COACH: Get to the front of the net, but outside the crease, the goalie's painted area. Keep your skates off its red edge too. Arrive under control. Building up speed into contact, even with two hard strides, can be charging. And stay off the goalie and out of their way. At the net, hold your spot with your feet. No shove, no lean, never your stick across anyone, and never into anyone's back. If you can see a player's numbers, pull up. Never lift anyone off their feet. Know where the post is, keep your head up and your chin off your chest, and never duck. Even going slowly, a crash there can hurt.

PLAYER: Why so careful with the goalie?

COACH: A goalie who's down or turned can't protect themselves, so contact can hurt them. It can also be a penalty, and it can cost you the goal. Even standing so the goalie can't move freely in the crease can cost the goal.

PLAYER: And if we lose it clean?

COACH: The job flips at once to not giving up a counterattack. The far defenceman holds. The near one gets back to the line. And the wingers pressure the puck rather than drop back.

PLAYER: Why pressure, and not get back?

COACH: You've got four players above the puck, and their ways out are limited. Your forecheck is never better placed than it is right now. Pressure means stick on the puck, not a hit. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call. And if you see their numbers, pull up.

COACH: On a tie-up, attack the puck with the two nearest players, sticks first, and keep both defencemen at the line. A tie-up in their end is a free loose puck in their zone. That's a good result, not a failure. And after the whistle, stay out of shoving matches. In their end there's a cost on top. If you join one inside the edge of the circle, the draw moves out to the neutral zone, and you've thrown away the draw in their end.

COACH: Thirteenth idea. Set plays, and a small playbook. Three set plays are common, and all of them are coaching choices. The first is the D one-timer. The centre pulls it straight back to the draw-side defenceman, who shoots it first time, without stopping it. That's what a one-timer means.

PLAYER: Doesn't everyone expect that one?

COACH: They do, and that's its weakness. And your own inside winger is standing in the shot's line. So on the drop they step out of it, usually to the net front to screen the goalie, staying off them. Never a push.

PLAYER: What's the second play?

COACH: The winger walk-out. The centre pulls it to the inside winger, who takes a stride toward the middle and shoots, or passes into the slot. Use it against a team that rushes straight out at your point, because you're attacking the space they just left.

COACH: The third is the tip play. The pull goes to the point. Someone screens the goalie, standing in their line of sight, outside the crease. A screen is your body, nothing else, so keep your hands and your blade down. Someone else tips the shot. Each shot is worth a bit less, but it makes rebounds and chaos. And it's the safest of the three, because a point shot into traffic rarely turns into a rush the other way. Some teams also loop the boards-side winger round to the inside, or swap a winger and a defenceman before the drop. Those tend to work about once a game against a team that's paying attention.

PLAYER: How many plays should we have?

COACH: Two or three per zone, not six. Teams that put in six plays run them all badly, and the players spend the drop trying to remember which one is on. One play run very well beats six run fairly well. The centre calls the play in one word at the dot, and everyone acknowledges. A play nobody heard isn't a play.

COACH: And give every play a failure branch. If we lose it, this player does this. Most plays are drawn up only for the win. That's why teams that lose an offensive-zone draw so often look lost. When there's confusion, no call, or a replacement taking the draw, use the simple default. Tie it up in your own end. Point shot in theirs.

COACH: Let's pull the middle and their end together.

PLAYER: In the neutral zone, carry or pass it in on a win. On a loss, close the gap with my stick on the puck. Experiment there, and go round the other centre, never through. In their end, win it back to a set shooter. At the net, outside the crease, off the goalie, no shove and no lean. On a loss, pressure with my stick, not a hit. Keep two or three plays, one-word calls, and a plan for the loss. And after the whistle, stay out of shoving matches.

COACH: That's it. Now the special situations. Fourteenth idea. Draws on special teams. On the power play your team has an extra player, because the other team took a penalty. In some leagues you get to choose which of the two dots in their end the draw is at. If so, pick your centre's strong side, or the side your one-timer shoots from. Ask for it early, not at the dot. In other leagues the official picks, so be ready for either side.

PLAYER: And then go for a clean win?

COACH: Win it, or at least don't lose it cleanly. A tie-up that keeps the puck in their zone is fine. A clever set play that ends with them clearing it is not. If they clear it, you have to break out, regroup, and carry it back in against defenders already set on their blue line. That can cost a third of your two minutes. The entry you avoid is worth more than the draw you win.

PLAYER: What about before the power play starts? When the referee's arm is up for a penalty on them?

COACH: That's a delayed penalty. Play goes on until they get control of the puck. Keep the puck, and don't hand the referee a whistle of your own. In some leagues, if you ice it then, the power-play draw leaves their end and goes to the neutral zone. So you've thrown away the draw you were about to get.

COACH: On the penalty kill, when your team is a player short, everything flips. You aren't trying to create anything. You're trying to end their possession. A draw in your own end a player short is where the disadvantages pile up. You're a player down, in your own end, against a set unit with a rehearsed play and a shooter whose feet are planted. So a tie-up is a win here too.

PLAYER: Who should take that one?

COACH: The common choice is to put your best draw-taker out, even if they're not a natural penalty killer. The draw is worth more than twenty seconds of coverage. Ask whether your bench does. And a clean win can be time off the clock. In most leagues, while you have fewer players on the ice than they do, you can shoot the puck the length of the ice without icing being called. Some youth levels don't allow that, so check yours. And it ends when your penalty time runs out. In at least one league that's so even before your player is back on the ice.

COACH: Fifteenth idea. The draws that come with a situation. Start with this. The other team has just iced it. What would you do?

PLAYER: Take a breath and set up?

COACH: The opposite. Win the draw and attack at once. They iced it because they were pinned in their end, so they're tired. That tiredness is their weakness, and it disappears the moment they get to breathe. In some leagues, the team that iced it can't change players or take a time-out, and the other team chooses the dot. In others they can change, so check yours before you build a plan on it. Where that freeze applies, your coach can send out whoever they like against their tired line. That edge only lasts one shift, so use it.

PLAYER: And when we're the ones who iced it?

COACH: It's the other way round. You're tired, stuck in your end, and can't rest. Get the puck off the glass and out of your zone at the first chance. Nothing clever.

PLAYER: What about late on, protecting a lead?

COACH: A one-goal lead in your own end, late, makes that draw worth more than all the draws before it. There's no time to recover from losing it. So this is where you accept the tie-up. A scramble that burns time beats a fifty-fifty try at a clean win. The other common approach is to put your best taker out with a plan for the win and for the loss. Many coaches do both, so ask which yours stresses. And get the alignment right before the play. Late in a game, more draws are lost because players are unsure of their places than because the other centre was better.

PLAYER: And chasing a goal, seconds left?

COACH: Win it straight back to a shooter whose feet are already set. Put a forward at the net for the screen and the rebound, outside the crease and off the goalie. There's no time for a second pass. Another option is for the centre to shoot straight at the net off the draw. That gives the goalie no time to set, but it's a weak shot. Ask your bench which, and settle it at the bench, not at the dot.

PLAYER: And if we're the ones defending that draw?

COACH: All you need is time. A tie-up is a win. The wingers get into the shooting lanes rather than chasing the puck. Get close to the shooter. Stay on your feet, square to the shot, hands behind your body, and head out of the lane. Keep your stick flat on the ice beside you, never out in front. Commit fully, still on your feet, or get out of the lane. And a won puck goes off the glass and out. Every second of a scramble is a second off their last attack.

COACH: After a time-out, expect the other team to have a plan too, and expect it to be different from their usual one. With your goalie pulled for an extra skater, the extra player usually goes to the front of the net or to the high slot. At the net, they stay outside the crease and off the goalie. Both defencemen go to the points. The centre has to win it back cleanly. There's no safe outlet, and a lost draw lets them clear it and run down the clock.

PLAYER: And against a pulled goalie, can we just fire it at the empty net?

COACH: Win the draw and get the puck out of your zone under control. Shoot at the empty net only with a clean lane. Six against six isn't a player short. So a miss from your own half is icing, and the draw comes straight back to your end.

COACH: At four on four, your coach might send out two forwards and two defencemen, or three forwards and one. Either way the ice is huge, and a lost draw can quickly become two of them against one of you. So keep both of your back players back. At three on three in overtime, a lost draw is often a scoring chance within ten seconds, and the game ends the moment anyone scores. Get that one exactly right.

COACH: Sixteenth idea. How to practise. A draw is the nearest thing hockey has to a fixed skill. Same start, same cue, same distance, dozens of times a game. That's exactly the kind of thing that gets better with reps.

PLAYER: Does practice actually show up, though?

COACH: In one recent NHL season, among centres who took a lot of draws, the best won about six in ten, and the worst about four in ten. Coaches read that gap as a skill that rewards work. Practise in pairs with a real drop, from the right height, with unpredictable timing, because you're training a reaction. End practice with ten minutes of draws, both circles, both grips. Spend the reps on your weak side and the draw you're worst at, because that's where the gains are.

PLAYER: Anything off the ice?

COACH: Coaches recommend grip and wrist strength and quick hands, because a draw is about hand speed before it's about strength. That's coaching craft. Nobody has published a measurement of faceoff reaction times. And rehearse with all five players, and run the win, the loss and the tie-up. Have every winger take draws, because every winger will take one in a game when the centre is thrown out. And teach your wingers the rule about the hash marks. It takes thirty seconds.

COACH: Let's pull it all together. Prepare the draws that matter. Your own end, the penalty kill, a late lead, and after an icing. Watch the linesperson's hand, not the other centre. Get low from your knees and hips, never your head. Own three or four draws, and change them. When a centre beats you, tie them up, stick on stick, never a hand on it, never their hands.

COACH: The four off the dot move on the drop, to places you agreed. In your own end, win it to the boards, never the middle, and rim it all the way or keep it tight. Whoever fetches it, or takes it on the wall, remember. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to anyone coming at you, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly. And if you're chasing it, stick on the puck, pull up if you see their numbers, and know whether your game allows checking.

COACH: Tell your linemates. Skates and stick behind the hash mark, skates off the line. Whole blade in the white. Your skate goes at the puck, never the player. Your hands, your stick and your head stay to yourself at the dot. In their end, win it back to a set shooter, and at the net stay off the goalie. Keep the playbook small, and always plan for the loss.

PLAYER: And the questions I take to my coach?

COACH: Three, and one for your league. Do we play zone or man-to-man off our own-end draws? Who takes the draw if our centre is thrown out, and who's second? What are our two or three set plays, and what happens if we lose the draw? And for your league. Does our game allow checking? Ask, because these are your team's choices and your league's call. Their answers tell you how your team wants you to play its faceoffs, and now you know why each answer makes sense.
