Faking someone out, which needs someone to fake. Protecting the puck when someone is really trying to take it. Looking around, because the picture keeps changing. And your compete level, because there's something to win.

PLAYER: Is that proven?

COACH: To be honest about the evidence, the research is supportive but thin, and mostly not about hockey. That small-area games build decisions is very likely, and nearly every coach believes it. But it hasn't been well tested. Use them anyway.

PLAYER: If I'm the one setting one up, how should I run it?

COACH: Add rules on purpose. One touch only. Three passes before anyone can score. The puck has to go below the goal line first. Each rule forces a habit without a coach having to shout it. Keep the numbers low, because two-on-two and three-on-three give far more touches than four-on-four. And keep the shifts short, thirty to forty-five seconds. Longer than that, and the quality falls apart.

PLAYER: My team doesn't actually have a practice. We just get a game slot.

COACH: That's true of most rec teams. Ask whether your rink runs stick-and-puck or drop-in small-area sessions. A drop-in is a session you just turn up to. They're usually the cheapest and best-value ice you can buy.

COACH: Now the bit nobody thinks about, standing in line. Coaches reckon about half of a typical team practice is spent queuing. That time is either wasted or used. What would you do with it?

PLAYER: Get my breath back, mostly. Lean on my stick.

COACH: Don't lean on your stick. That habit follows you into games, where your stick needs to be on the ice. Instead, watch the rep in front of you, and say to yourself what you'd do differently. That's free practice. Keep your feet moving, because standing still in cold air is how you pull something on your next hard rep. Keep your head up, because practices have loose pucks flying around, and a line standing still against the boards is exactly where they end up. Then picture your next rep, exactly as you want to do it.

PLAYER: And after practice?

COACH: Stay on for five minutes and do the thing that went badly, slowly, with no pressure. Then write one line about what you learned. By the next morning you'll have forgotten the detail.

COACH: Twelfth idea. Train your decisions, not just your hands. A skill is something you can do. Hockey is a string of choices between the things you can do, under time pressure. And almost nobody trains the choosing. Drills take the read out so the skill can be practised. That's fine, but drills alone build a player with good hands and no idea when to use them.

COACH: Picture one rep twice. First, nobody is defending, and you make the pass. Then add one passive defender. Nobody else moves. But the defender's arrival takes the passing lane away. What would you do now?

PLAYER: Find something else. Another pass, or carry it.

COACH: And that choosing is the whole difference between a skill drill and a decision drill. There are cheap ways to train it. Always have an opponent, even a token one, playing with their stick, not a hit. Add a rule that forces you to look around. For example, shoulder check before every pass you receive. A shoulder check is a quick look over your shoulder to see what's around you. Or never pass back to the player who passed to you. And shrink the space and shorten the clock. Time pressure is what separates a decision you can make from one you can make in a game.

COACH: Then practise doing nothing. Amateur players tend to always do something. Dump it, force it, shoot it into someone's shins. So practise holding the puck one more beat, or making the simple, boring pass sideways. Just never across the front of your own net.

PLAYER: That's me. I get the puck and it's gone before I've looked.

COACH: That's a panic pass, a pass made without looking, just to get rid of the puck. Panic is normal for a player still learning, and it spends time you actually had. After a shift, ask yourself, did I have more time than I thought? The honest answer is almost always yes. So get one more look before the puck arrives. Outside your own end, practise that extra beat. When you hold it, stay side-on to anyone coming at you, with the puck on the far side of your body, never your back to them. In your own end, don't hold it at all. There, holding it is how the turnover happens. One look, one decision, move it.

PLAYER: And the deciding in advance thing?

COACH: Work out off the ice what you'll do in the situations that keep coming up. The puck on your backhand on the boards, with a forechecker closing. A forechecker is an opponent pressuring you in your own end, trying to win the puck back. Or taking a pass at the blue line, with a defender stepping up at you. A choice you've already made comes far faster than one you invent on the spot.

PLAYER: How do I practise that off the ice?

COACH: Here's a cheap drill. Play a game clip, and pause it the moment a player gets the puck. Say out loud what you'd do, and why. Then play on. Do it twenty times. You're training the same reading of the play the game asks for, at a speed you can think at.

COACH: Thirteenth idea. Film yourself, and count things rather than points. Video is the cheapest way to get the outside eye most amateur players are missing. You can't see your own stride, where your head is, or the four passing options you never looked at.

PLAYER: How do I film it, though? My phone footage is useless.

COACH: Film one period from a fixed spot, high up. A phone on a tripod in the stands is enough. You need to see the ice, not just the play. Then watch it with one question, and only one. How many times did I shoulder check before I got the puck? Did I stop, or did I curl? A general watch gets you a general shrug.

COACH: Count something, so you have a number to beat next month. And watch your shifts without the puck. Measured at five-on-five, that's nearly all of your ice time, and it's where most of your improvement is.

PLAYER: I hate watching myself.

COACH: Expect it to feel bad. Everyone looks slower on video than they feel. That gap is exactly what you need to see. And if you can't film yourself, watch one good player at your position for a full shift. Follow them, not the puck.

PLAYER: Why not just count my points?

COACH: Goals, assists and plus-minus depend a lot on your linemates, the other team, your ice time and luck. Over a short amateur season, that noise drowns out the signal. You can improve a lot and score less. So count the things you control. Shoulder checks before you get the puck, per shift. First passes completed out of your own end. Passes you move on in one touch. Times you got beaten wide in a game. Battles won along the boards. Whether you can stop both ways at full speed. That one is a yes or no, and most players can't honestly answer it. And how long a set edge-work pattern takes you, timed once a month.

PLAYER: That's a lot to track.

COACH: So pick one or two, check them monthly, and write them down. If you pick more than two, you'll end up measuring none. And there are other signs you're improving when the scoresheet doesn't show it. The game feels slower, because you're reading it faster. You're less tired doing the same work, usually because you skate more efficiently. You get the puck more, because teammates pass to players who take it cleanly. And your mistakes get more advanced. A pinch is a defenceman stepping in from their blue line to keep the puck in. Getting caught on a pinch beats not knowing the pinch was there.

COACH: Let's pull team practice together. Get on early, and know your one thing. Ask what the drill is for, and do it at game pace. Compete in the battle drills, with your stick on the puck, not a hit, and never push or hit anyone from behind. If a battle goes into the boards, skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to anyone coming at you, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly. In the line, watch, keep your feet moving and your head up. Train decisions with an opponent and a clock. Don't panic pass, and in your own end, one look, one decision, move it. And film yourself, and count one or two things you control.

COACH: Fourteenth idea. Build a plan you'll actually keep. If you skate once or twice a week, like most adult players, your ice time is probably a game. And a game gives you about a minute with the puck.

PLAYER: So my real practice for the week is basically nothing.

COACH: Roughly nothing, unless you make it off the ice. Here's one example of a workable week, about three to four hours in total. It's a coaching example, not a prescription, so change it to suit you. Head-up stickhandling, fifteen minutes on three weekday evenings. That's the most carry-over you can get per minute off the ice. Wall passing, ten minutes twice, half of it on your backhand. That trains receiving, the half of passing most players neglect. One twenty-minute shooting session off a pad. Few shots, done well, changing where you release from. That's for mechanics only. The decisions come on the ice.

PLAYER: What about the gym?

COACH: Two strength and mobility sessions of thirty to forty minutes. Work on one leg at a time, your hips, and your inner thigh muscles. That supports your stride, and it may lower your risk of groin and hip injuries, though no exercise guarantees that. Then ten minutes of edge work at the start of every practice or stick-and-puck, before the puck comes out. For most adults, that's the only skating practice they get. On game day, one thing to work on, chosen in advance. That turns the game into a rep. And twenty minutes a week of video, with one question. That's the feedback that makes everything else deliberate.

COACH: The off-ice parts happen wherever you have floor space. Remember, this episode has only judged whether they carry over, not whether that room is safe. If you can, add one stick-and-puck or drop-in session a month spent only on skating. And one power-skating session a season, which is a class just for skating technique. Neither is exciting, and both do more for you than anything else on the list.

PLAYER: What if I get a lot more ice than that?

COACH: If you get three to five sessions a week, you have enough ice to give each session its own job. One skills session, low volume, full attention, ideally with someone who tells you the truth. One small-area game session, for decisions, competing and touches. One team practice. One or two games. And two strength sessions, kept away from your hardest days on the ice. But only one thing at a time should be a real technique rebuild. Rebuilds make you worse for a while. If you rebuild three things in one month, all three get worse at once, and you quit.

PLAYER: I always start these plans and drop them after a fortnight.

COACH: Then here's the reality check. If your plan needs more than about four hours a week, and you have a job and a family, it's a fantasy. You'll drop it, and it'll feel like a failure of character, when the real problem was the maths. So build the plan you'll keep in your worst week, not your best one. Fifteen minutes of head-up stickhandling four times a week, kept up for a year, beats a big programme kept up for a fortnight.

COACH: Fifteenth idea. Don't get hurt while you're trying to get better, because getting better stops when you're hurt. What do you think is the most common way a developing amateur player gets hurt?

PLAYER: A hit, I'd guess.

COACH: Coaches see something else. It's a sudden jump in how much they train. That's what coaches see, not a measured ranking, and it's no reason to take contact less seriously. The classic pattern is four months of nothing, then five sessions in the first week. So build up slowly. Raise how much you do, or how hard you do it, but never both at once. And not by much in any one week.

PLAYER: When's the riskiest time?

COACH: Coming back from a break, like a summer off or an injury. Your body has lost fitness, but your competitiveness hasn't. You'll feel ready before you are. That's when groin and hip problems tend to show up, the classic hockey overuse complaint. So come back at low volume. Warm up before hard skating. Do it especially in a cold rink, and before a game where your first shift is flat out. Standing in a line and then sprinting is a real risk. That's one more reason to keep your feet moving while you queue. And treat a technique change as extra load too. A new movement uses your body in ways it isn't used to. So start it at low volume.

PLAYER: How do I tell normal soreness from an injury?

COACH: You'll hear a rule of thumb. Soreness that eases as you warm up is usually fine. Pain that gets worse is not. Treat that as a rough guide, nothing more. It doesn't come from any medical guideline, and a real injury can feel better once you warm up. So see a physiotherapist for anything sharp, swollen, on one side only, or lasting more than a couple of weeks. A week off now costs less than a season. And sleep and protein aren't optional extras. They're how practice turns into skill, and training turns into fitness.

COACH: Let's pull the week and your body together. Build the plan you'll keep in your worst week. Rebuild only one thing at a time. Build up slowly, never more and harder at once, and come back from a break at low volume. Warm up before you skate hard. And see a physiotherapist for anything sharp, swollen, on one side only, or lasting more than a couple of weeks.

COACH: Sixteenth idea, for anyone starting as an adult. If you haven't found a session to go to yet, start with the getting started episode. This part assumes you have. You can learn this as an adult. Plenty of people start in their thirties, forties and fifties, and become properly competent. But be honest about both sides.

PLAYER: Start with the hard side, then.

COACH: Skating is the big one. Balancing on a thin blade comes easily to children and slowly to adults. Expect it to hold you back for a couple of years. Fear of falling is the second. Adults fall from higher, weigh more, and have work on Monday. That fear makes you stand tall and lock your legs. And standing tall is the root of most skating faults. So the fear isn't just uncomfortable. It's the actual technical problem. You also get fewer hours than a child. You need more recovery and get less of it. And being the worst player on the ice is harder to take at forty than at ten. It makes adults avoid the very practice they need.

PLAYER: And what's easier?

COACH: You can read about a defensive system once and understand it. Good positioning makes you useful before your skills arrive. A well-positioned poor skater beats a badly positioned good one more often than anyone admits. You can choose what to work on, book ice, and hire a coach. You can put up with a dip that lasts weeks, because you know why it's happening. You can spend money on power skating, a few coaching sessions and gear that fits. And you can learn far more from watching hockey on television than a child watching the puck.

PLAYER: People say you had to start at four to be any good.

COACH: The research on sport doesn't support that. On average, the more skilled athletes did not start their sport earlier.

COACH: First steps. Learn to fall, and to get up, on purpose, in full gear. Losing the fear is what lets you bend your knees. But don't assume your gear makes a fall harmless. It doesn't protect a wrist you put out to catch yourself. And a fall is one of the ways a concussion happens. That's why you practise the technique, not just falling over.

PLAYER: So what's the technique?

COACH: Get low first. Go down to the side, onto your hip and thigh. Keep your hands in, rather than out on a locked, straight arm. That's widely taught coaching craft, not a written rule. Then the chin, and it changes with where you're falling. Falling backwards onto the ice, tuck your chin in. That keeps the back of your head off the ice. Going forwards into the boards, the opposite. Head up, and never duck. A chin on your chest puts your neck first in line. So, falling flat, chin in. Hitting a wall, head up. And after you fall, take two or three seconds to check you aren't hurt. If you are hurt, stay down.

PLAYER: What if I hit my head?

COACH: A concussion is an injury to the brain, from a knock to the head or a jolt that snaps it. You don't have to be knocked out to have one. If a concussion is suspected, come off straight away, and tell someone. You're off for the rest of that day. And you don't play again until a medical professional says you can, even if you feel fine. It isn't the player's call, because a concussed player will often say they're fine. Straight after the knock, someone stays with you, for at least the first three hours, and the UK guidance says twenty-four. You don't drive, you don't drink alcohol, and you don't go home on your own. And if anything gets worse in that time, call an ambulance. In the UK there's also a minimum wait before you play again. No games for at least twenty-one days from the injury. And no training where you could take a knock to the head until you've been free of symptoms for two weeks.

PLAYER: And if it's a teammate who's down?

COACH: Then think about their neck as well. After any knock to the head, treat it as a possible neck injury too. If a player is still down, don't help them up or move them, unless you have to, to keep their airway clear so they can breathe. Leave their helmet on. And call an ambulance straight away if they have any one of these red flags, even if it seems mild. Being knocked out. A fit, twitching, or going rigid. Any neck pain or tenderness. Weakness, numbness or tingling in their arms or legs. Double vision, or losing their sight. Being sick more than once. A bad headache, or one getting worse. Getting more confused, drowsy, restless, agitated or aggressive. Or a dent in their skull. And if any symptom is getting worse, that's an ambulance too. In the UK, the official list is longer. It includes a player on blood-thinning medicine who takes a knock to the head, even if they seem completely fine. The conditioning and recovery episode goes through the full list. If either list says call, call.

COACH: Then the rest of the first steps. Bend your knees more than feels natural, because almost every adult skating fault is a straight-legs fault. And get skates that fit. Badly fitting skates make skating harder, in a way a fitter can see and you can't.

PLAYER: How often should I be going out?

COACH: Coaches advise two shorter sessions a week rather than one long one. That hasn't been tested, and the research on spacing out practice doesn't all point one way. And work on one skill a month, not five.

PLAYER: How long until I'm decent?

COACH: You'll see confident figures for how long adult beginners take. Every one of them comes from coaching companies and shops, not from research, and people vary hugely. So don't measure yourself against them. Think of it like this. Months for the basics, years for the rest, and the curve is a staircase.

COACH: Seventeenth idea, and it's about learning things you read, not things you skate. Say you've just read about your team's breakout system. You've got ten minutes. Do you read it again, or test yourself on it?

PLAYER: Read it again. That feels like it sticks better.

COACH: It feels that way, and that's the trap. Trying to remember an answer teaches you more than reading it again, but only if you then check the answer. Straight after studying, reading it again looks better. A week later, the people who tested themselves remember more. In one study, students who read a passage four times remembered about forty percent of it a week later. Students who read it once and tested themselves three times remembered about sixty. So the method looks worse at exactly the moment you'd judge it.

PLAYER: And the checking part matters?

COACH: The check is not optional. A wrong answer you never correct is worth close to nothing. A wrong answer you actually thought about helps. A wild guess doesn't. All of this was tested on things people read, not on physical skills. So use it for systems, reads and rules, not for your shot. When you're learning how your team plays, try to answer questions about it before you look. Be willing to be wrong. Then go and check. The checking is the part that does the work.

COACH: Let's pull it all together. Give every rep a goal, and find out whether it worked. Get an outside eye, because you can't see your own stride. And audit your hours, don't count them. Do your reps at game pace, at the edge of what you can do, on the hard side, and don't cheat them. Keep your head up on every puck-handling rep, at home most of all.

COACH: Put an opponent in the drill, and ask for small-area games. Compete for the puck with your stick, not a hit, and never push or hit anyone from behind. At the boards, skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to anyone coming at you, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly. Don't panic pass. Get one more look. In your own end, one look, one decision, move it.

COACH: Aim at your goalie's pads and glove, never the head, from where the drill puts you. Don't count on the whistle. Skating first, with edge work at the start of the session. Change one thing at a time, expect it to get worse first, and judge it on a cold retest a week later. Off the ice, do what copies the real thing, and ask what habit a drill will give you.

COACH: Build up slowly, never more and harder at once, and come back from a break at low volume. When you fall, get low, go onto your hip and thigh, hands in. Falling flat, chin in. Going into the boards, head up, never duck. If a concussion is suspected, off for the day, and not back until a medical professional says so. Someone stays with you afterwards, and you don't drive. After any knock to the head, don't move a player who is down unless you must to keep their airway clear, and leave their helmet on. Any one red flag, even a mild one, means an ambulance, and so does any symptom getting worse in the hours after the knock. Later on in the UK, getting worse, or not better by two weeks, is a call to one-one-one.

PLAYER: There's a lot here that depends on my coach and my team, though.

COACH: There is. So here are three questions to ask your coach. What should I be working on first, and why? What is this drill for? And can someone watch me, or film me, and tell me the truth? Then go and practise the thing that makes you look bad.
