It builds crossover confidence. You can do it very slowly, so you learn to put the crossing foot down and push under before you need to do it at speed.

PLAYER: Slow feels like cheating. Isn't it fine to rush it once I know it?

COACH: Rushing it is the mistake on this one. The drill is worth most done slowly, with each crossing foot placed on purpose.

COACH: Next, crossing-under lemons forwards. A crossover push gets your feet right under your body, rolling from the inside edge to the outside edge. This drill does that on both feet. Start with heels together and toes turned out, knees bent, astride a line. Push both feet out on the inside edges. As they come back in, roll the feet onto the outside edges, so both edges get used, and let the feet cross completely under your body. Push out again from the crossed position. Small lemons first, then wide ones.

PLAYER: So it's a lemon that doesn't stop in the middle. Stopping there would be the mistake.

COACH: That's it. If the feet stop at the middle on the inside edges, they never roll over and never cross.

COACH: Then crossing-under lemons backwards, for the feel behind backward crossovers, rolling between the inside and outside edges skating backwards. Start with your toes together and heels apart, knees bent, astride a line, with clear ice behind you. Push both heels out on the inside edges. As they come back in, roll onto the outside edges so both edges get used, and let the feet cross completely under your body. Push out again from the crossed position. Small lemons first, then wide ones.

PLAYER: And the mistake is the backward one again. Leaning over my toes.

COACH: Right. It stops the heels pushing out, and it stops the feet crossing.

COACH: Last of the two-foot edges. Backward crossovers, two or three steps and stop. Picture a defenceman following a rush, moving backwards and sideways with speed, and then holding their ground. Check behind you. Then skate backwards down the ice, knees bent. You'll zig-zag, going slightly backwards and sideways each way. Move diagonally backwards and to your right. Cross the left foot over in front of the right, then step the right foot out from behind.

PLAYER: How many times?

COACH: Two or three. Then push both heels out, toes turned in, and stop on both feet. Now go diagonally backwards and to your left. Cross the right foot over in front of the left, two or three times, and stop again. Keep zig-zagging down the ice, slowly at first.

PLAYER: And I look over my shoulder as I go, not at my feet.

COACH: Right, because looking down at your feet is the mistake. It tips you forward onto your toes and kills the crossovers.

COACH: Give me the two-foot edges.

PLAYER: Lemons forwards, out on the inside edges and back until the boots touch. Lemons backwards, heels out and in, with clear ice behind me. Forward crossovers, two steps and stop, slowly, zig-zagging up the ice. Crossing-under lemons, forwards and backwards, rolling onto the outside edges and crossing right under me. And backward crossovers, two or three steps and stop, checking behind me first and not looking at my feet.

COACH: Spot on. Now the biggest group, turns. We'll start with turning from forwards to backwards.

COACH: First, the stepping turn, forwards to backwards. It's for turning from forwards to backwards without stopping, the way a defenceman turns to face the play. Glide forwards on both feet, knees bent, with clear ice ahead. Turn your shoulders and hips the way you want to go. Put your weight on one foot, and lift the other with its toe turned out.

PLAYER: Then where does it go?

COACH: Put that foot down on its own, pointing backwards, its heel almost touching the heel of the gliding foot. Then lift the other foot and turn it round. Put it down, and glide backwards on both feet. Next time, turn the other way.

PLAYER: So shoulders and hips first, then the feet. If the feet go first, I'd guess the step goes wide.

COACH: Wide and wobbly, instead of heel close to heel. That's the mistake.

COACH: Next, the stepping three-sixty, alternating directions. Turning all the way round while still moving lets you look behind you and keep your speed. Glide forwards on both feet, with clear ice ahead. Turn to your right. Step your right foot out sideways, toe pointing to your right. It's the only foot on the ice. Put your left foot down on its own, pointing backwards, and lift the right. Bring the right foot down beside it, so you're gliding backwards on both feet.

PLAYER: That's halfway. Then what?

COACH: Keep turning right. Right foot sideways, then the left foot down on its own, pointing forwards, then the right beside it. That's a full circle. Do the next circle turning to your left, leading with the left foot each time.

PLAYER: I'd probably stop halfway and start again.

COACH: That's the mistake. Stopping halfway round to reset makes it two separate half turns instead of one smooth circle.

COACH: Now two turns that use the inside foot to make a turn tighter, so you can change direction in a small space. The forward soft drag first. Skate in forwards, knees bent. Put the inside foot's toe on the ice, heel up, and drag the toe round through the turn. Carve the outside foot round it on its inside edge. Come out of the turn and skate away. Repeat turning the other way.

PLAYER: I'd guess pressing the toe too hard is a problem.

COACH: It is. Press it down too hard and it acts as a brake, and you stall in the turn.

COACH: Then the forward anchor, for a much tighter turn. You drag the inside heel as an anchor. Skate in forwards with a wide stance. Plant the inside foot with the ankle collapsed, meaning rolled in, the pressure on the heel and the toe up. Drag that heel through the turn while the outside foot carves round it on its inside edge. Come out of the turn and skate away. Repeat turning the other way.

PLAYER: What if I put the whole blade down?

COACH: That's the mistake. The toe catches and you trip. Keep the toe up and the pressure on the heel.

COACH: Quick test, then. What's the difference between the forward anchor and the forward soft drag?

PLAYER: The soft drag drags the toe, with the heel up. The anchor drags the heel, with the toe up and the ankle rolled in. And the anchor turns tighter.

COACH: That's it. Same idea, opposite end of the blade.

COACH: Next, tight turns. You need them to turn sharply without losing speed, to follow the puck or your check. Your check is the opponent you're covering. Skate in on two feet, knees bent, and open into a scissor stance. That's your inside leg well forward and your outside leg back.

PLAYER: Then round the marker?

COACH: Turn round a marker holding the scissor, inside foot well ahead. Lean both blades in. The inside foot is on its outside edge, the outside foot on its inside edge. Your head points where you're going, and at stick-and-puck, so does your stick. Stay low as you come round, then stride away the way you came. Repeat turning the other way.

PLAYER: So standing up in the turn would be the mistake.

COACH: It is. The edges let go and the turn goes wide.

COACH: Next, continuous crossovers forwards. In the flow of a game, you build speed through turns and change direction without stopping. This is how. Skating forwards, cross the outside foot over in front. Then think cross-under. The real push is the inside foot driving out under your body on its outside edge. Step onto the inside edge of that foot, and that starts the curve the other way. Cross over back the other way and push under again. Keep going. Crossover, inside edge, crossover, inside edge, without stopping.

PLAYER: That's the cross-under you said most players miss. If I skip it, I'm just walking round.

COACH: Exactly. Step the outside foot over without the push underneath, and the crossover just walks round the curve and adds no speed.

COACH: Next, small-circle pivots. Defencemen switch between forwards and backwards every shift while always facing the play, and so do forwards skating back to defend. Face one way, say one end of the rink, and keep facing it all the way round a small circle. Skate forwards round the first side. Pivot from forwards to backwards on the front of both skates, heels swinging round. Ideally with a small hop, so the heels don't cut the ice.

PLAYER: Then backwards round the far side?

COACH: Still facing the same way, and glancing over your shoulder. Then step from backwards to forwards. With the outside leg going backwards, step straight onto the other leg going forwards, without stopping. Then go round the other way.

PLAYER: So my chest never turns away from that end.

COACH: Right. Turning your chest with your feet is the mistake. You end up facing round the circle instead of facing one way.

COACH: Next, the three turn, forwards to backwards. From a slow glide, with clear ice behind you, stand on your right foot on its outside edge, curving to your right. Turn your shoulders and hips into the curve, and rock onto the front of the blade, so the skate spins half a turn. Come out gliding backwards on the inside edge of the same foot, still curving the same way. Repeat on the left foot. It gives you balance turning on one foot, and a quick turn from forwards to backwards.

PLAYER: Can I hop it round?

COACH: That's the mistake. Hopping, or putting the free foot down to get round. Let the blade turn on the ice.

COACH: Next, the backward soft drag. When the play changes direction, a defenceman has to turn sharply while skating backwards. Check behind you. Then skate backwards, knees bent. Put the inside foot's toe on the ice, heel up, and drag the toe round through the turn. Going backwards, you use the toe, not the heel. Carve the outside foot round it on its inside edge, in a sharp, tight C. Come out of the turn and skate away backwards. Repeat turning the other way.

PLAYER: Let me guess. Looking down at my feet.

COACH: That's it. The shoulders drop and the turn wobbles. Keep your head up.

COACH: Then continuous crossovers backwards. It's how a defenceman stays in front of a rush that cuts across, with backward power and changes of direction without stopping. Check behind you. Then, skating backwards, cross the outside foot over in front, and glance over your shoulder as you go.

PLAYER: And the cross-under again?

COACH: The real push is the inside foot driving out under your body on its outside edge. Step onto the inside edge of that foot, which starts the curve the other way. Cross over back the other way, and push under again. Keep going without stopping.

PLAYER: Same mistake as forwards, then. No push underneath.

COACH: Same mistake. You walk round the curve and add no speed.

COACH: Now turns for when the play reverses. Picture it. You're skating hard one way, the puck turns over, and you need to be going the other way right now. What would you do?

PLAYER: Stop, and start again the other way?

COACH: That's two moves. These two drills make it one. First, jam turns, for changing direction in the shortest space. On a clear patch, skate in with some speed. Sink, and swing both skates hard into the turn, so the blades bite and spray snow. You nearly stop, still on two feet. Then push hard out of the turn, and stride away the way you came. Repeat turning the other way.

PLAYER: I'd want to lean back as they bite.

COACH: That's the mistake. Lean back, and you stall or fall instead of driving out.

COACH: Then punch jams. They get you turned back on the play and up to full speed in the new direction in one or two steps. On a clear patch, skate in with some speed. Jam the turn. Both skates swing round and bite, spraying snow. Without pausing, punch out. The outside foot crosses over in front of the inside foot, and the inside foot pushes hard underneath. Keep driving with short, quick strides. Repeat turning the other way.

PLAYER: And the mistake is doing what I said. Stopping and starting.

COACH: That's it. Stopping dead after the jam and then starting again, instead of turning straight into the crossover.

COACH: Last of the turns, and it comes in four steps. The punch turn. It drops you onto the outside edge of your inside foot and gets you out of the turn fast. Learn it in this order, and start each step slowly.

COACH: Step one. A T-stop onto the outside edge. A T-stop means stopping by dragging the back foot behind the front heel, so the two skates make a T. Skate forward slowly, knees bent, with clear ice ahead of you. Turn your left foot out so it points across your path, and lean back onto its outside edge.

PLAYER: Where does the other foot go?

COACH: Put your right foot down behind it in a T, as in a T-stop. That foot is your safety, there to catch you. Glide to a stop. Then do it with the right foot. Start slow, and add speed as you trust the edge. This step is about committing to the outside edge of your inside foot, the first move of a punch turn, with that safety foot behind you.

PLAYER: So easing onto it bit by bit would be the mistake.

COACH: It is. The safety foot is there so you can commit.

COACH: Step two. Jump onto the outside edge. Now you trust the edge without the safety foot, so you can drop onto it fast in a game. Skate forward slowly, knees bent, with clear ice ahead of you. Jump onto your left foot, turned out across your path. Land on its outside edge with a bent knee, and dig in. Keep your right foot just off the ice behind you, ready to touch down if you need it. Do it both ways, and add speed only when it feels solid.

PLAYER: I'd probably put the back foot straight down out of habit.

COACH: That's the mistake. Do that and you never learn to trust the edge.

COACH: Step three. Dive onto the edge going forward. What makes a punch turn quick is turning out of straight-line speed without losing it, and that's this step. Skate straight forward with some speed, and look along your path first. Without slowing, open your left foot and dive onto its outside edge, letting it carry you round to the left. Keep your weight moving forward through the turn, not sliding off to the side. Your right foot stays just off the ice. Do it both ways.

PLAYER: I bet I'll want to slow down first.

COACH: Most people do, and that's the mistake. Kill your forward speed to make the turn, and you end up sliding sideways.

COACH: Step four. A figure eight round two markers. This is the full punch turn, for getting away from pressure with a quick, efficient change of direction, then exploding out. Pick two markers a few metres apart on clear ice, and skate a figure eight round them. One way round the first, the other way round the second.

PLAYER: What do my feet do in the turn?

COACH: As you go into each turn, dive onto the outside edge of your inside foot, and bite the ice with the inside edge of your outside foot. Dig it in and pull yourself round the marker.

PLAYER: When do I cross over?

COACH: At the far point of the turn, which is called the apex. Cross the outside foot over in front of the inside foot, then explode out towards the other marker. When that flows both ways, do it carrying a puck at stick-and-puck.

PLAYER: And I guess gliding round and pushing late is the mistake.

COACH: It is. Gliding round the turn and only starting to push once you're out of it.

COACH: Let's pull the turns together. Your turn.

PLAYER: Forwards to backwards with the stepping turn, shoulders and hips first. The stepping three-sixty, one smooth circle, both ways. Soft drag on the toe, anchor on the heel with the toe up. Tight turns in a scissor stance, staying low. Continuous crossovers, forwards and backwards, with the push underneath. Pivots round a small circle, always facing one way. The three turn on one foot. The backward soft drag, head up. Jam turns and punch jams on a clear patch. And the punch turn in four steps, the T-stop, the jump, the dive, then the figure eight round two markers.

COACH: Spot on. Last group on the ice, stops. Hard stops go on a clear patch.

COACH: First, the one-foot stop in front. It's a controlled first stop, and it gives you the feel of scraping the ice with an inside edge, which every harder stop builds on. Glide forward on both feet, knees bent, feet about hip-width apart. Turn your right toe in and your heel out, and slide that foot a little in front of you. Press down onto the inside edge, so it scrapes the ice and sprays a little snow.

PLAYER: And the other foot?

COACH: Keep the left foot gliding straight, and sink your knees until you stop. Then the other foot. Work both feet equally.

PLAYER: I'd guess leaning over my toes is bad here too.

COACH: It is. The scraping foot catches and tips you, instead of slowing you.

COACH: Next, the outside-edge T-stop. Glide forward on your right foot, with the left foot lifted. Bring the left foot behind your right heel at a right angle, making a T, toe pointing out. Lower it, and drag it on its outside edge, pressing a little harder as you go. Keep your shoulders and hips facing forward and your knees bent until you stop. Then the other foot, both feet equally.

PLAYER: What's that one for?

COACH: Slowing down while staying square to the play, and control of the outside edge of the trailing foot.

PLAYER: And if I end up sideways?

COACH: Then you've twisted your hips round towards the back foot. That's the common mistake. It turns you sideways instead of stopping you.

COACH: Now the hockey stops. They're the quick, full stops players use to change direction, stay with a check, or arrive at the puck under control. First, the two-foot hockey stop with the feet parallel. On a clear patch, glide forward on both feet with your knees bent. Turn your hips and both blades together across the line you're travelling along, keeping the blades parallel.

PLAYER: Then lean back?

COACH: Sink your knees and lean back against the slide until you stop. The outside skate, the one that ends up leading, is on its inside edge. The inside skate is on its outside edge. Practise turning both ways.

PLAYER: I tend to skid round in a half circle.

COACH: That's the usual mistake. Turning only the shoulders, or only one foot. The blades end up at different angles and you skid round instead of stopping.

COACH: Then the two-foot hockey stop, side by side, with your feet split, one ahead of the other. It's a stable, strong stop that keeps you balanced when you're stopping hard. On a clear patch, glide forward on both feet with your knees bent. Turn both blades across the line of travel together, and split the feet into a scissor stance, the inside skate further forward, towards where you're facing.

PLAYER: Which edges this time?

COACH: Sink your knees and lean back against the slide until you stop. The outside skate, on the side you're sliding towards, is on its inside edge, and the inside skate is on its outside edge. Practise turning both ways.

PLAYER: So if my feet come level, it's just the parallel stop again.

COACH: Exactly. That's the mistake on this one.

COACH: Now stopping on one foot. Balance and edge control here let you stop and push off again in one movement. The one-foot glide to a one-foot stop, forwards, on the inside edge. On a clear patch, glide forward on your right foot, with the left foot lifted beside it. Turn your hips and the right blade across the line of travel, the toe turning towards the lifted foot. Sink the knee and press the inside edge until you stop, keeping the left foot off the ice. Then the other foot, both feet equally.

PLAYER: And I don't put the lifted foot down.

COACH: Right. Put it down to save your balance, and it's back to being a two-foot stop.

COACH: Then the same on the outside edge. One-foot glide to a one-foot stop, forwards, outside edge. That's the edge you need to stop and go the other way in one movement. On a clear patch, glide forward on your right foot, with the left foot lifted. Turn the right toe out to the right, so the right heel comes round in front of the left foot, and the blade is across the line of travel.

PLAYER: Then press?

COACH: Sink the knee and press the outside edge until you stop, keeping the left foot off the ice. Then the other foot, both feet equally.

PLAYER: I'd probably lean my upper body over the stopping foot.

COACH: That's the mistake. The outside edge slips out.

COACH: Last drill of all. The one-foot glide to a one-foot stop, backwards. Defencemen need it, and so does anyone covering an attacker, before they can push forward again. Check behind you. Then skate backwards, and glide on your right foot with the left foot lifted. Turn the right toe out and the heel in. Bend the knee and press the blade outwards on its inside edge, so it scrapes the ice until you stop. Then the other foot, both feet equally.

PLAYER: Leaning back over my heels would be the mistake, I suppose.

COACH: It is. The blade slides out from under you.

COACH: Give me the stops.

PLAYER: The one-foot stop in front, scraping with the inside edge. The outside-edge T-stop, hips facing forward. The two hockey stops, blades parallel, or feet split with the inside skate ahead, both ways. Then the one-foot stops, inside edge, outside edge and backwards, without putting the lifted foot down, and checking behind me for the backward one.

COACH: That's every drill. Now, off the ice, starting with stick work. By coaching consensus, stickhandling is the off-ice skill that carries over best, along with passing against a wall. Why it carries over is a story for another episode, on practice and development. This part is the how. And a scene first. You've got a stick, a ball and a kitchen. Name two things you'd sort out before you start.

PLAYER: Somewhere I won't break anything. And something better than carpet to play on?

COACH: Both of those. Check the space yourself. Room from people, from windows and from glass. Safe footing. And for a child, an adult watching. Then use a smooth surface. A shooting pad, tiles or a smooth floor, not carpet. A ball builds soft hands. A puck sliding on a smooth surface is closer to the real thing. So use both.

PLAYER: How long should I go for?

COACH: Little and often. Short, focused sessions most days beat one long one a week. Leave the pad and a ball somewhere you walk past them, so starting takes no effort. And keep your head up on every rep. Watch the television while you do it, or call out numbers someone has stuck on the wall. Head-down reps at home become the habit that shows up in games.

PLAYER: What about passing on my own?

COACH: Pass against a wall or a rebounder, with half your passes on the backhand. Ten minutes gives you hundreds of receptions. And if you shoot, shoot off a pad, not concrete. Concrete wrecks the blade, and it teaches you to chop at the puck.

COACH: Then off-ice exercises. Off-ice training doesn't teach you skating. What it does is remove the limits that stop you skating well. Single-leg strength, ankle stability and hip mobility. For an actual programme and a realistic week, follow the guidance on conditioning and recovery.

PLAYER: Anything to watch out for?

COACH: Four things. Warm up first. Ten to fifteen minutes is enough. Get coached on the lifts before you add weight. Build up gradually. In coaches' experience, going from nothing to a full programme in a week is a common way for adults to get hurt. And for young players, get qualified coaching before a young player lifts at all.

COACH: Let me pull it all together.

COACH: At a public session, skip the easy laps. Work on edges, turns and stops. If you go round, use crossovers, more in your weaker direction. Thirty minutes of ice? Edges first, while you're fresh, then crossovers, stops and starts, and turns. At stick-and-puck, do it all again with a puck, head up.

COACH: One side weaker? Do every one-foot drill on both feet and every turn both ways, and give the weaker one more. New drill? Learn it slowly, then do it hard where you have room. A timid edge on a flat blade teaches nothing. Pick two or three drills and do them well, rather than a dozen once. Reaching down to touch the ice or your skate? Keep your head up, because looking down drops your weight forward and flattens the blade.

COACH: Session busy? Do the slow drills. Save jumps, backward work and hard starts and stops for a clear patch of a quiet session, if the posted rules allow them, and look before you move. Wear a helmet for all of this work, edges included. In Britain, a neck guard every time you skate. Fall on your side, hands in. Chin tucked for the ice, head up if you're sliding into the boards.

COACH: Fell, and might be concussed? Stop for the day, and tell someone. Don't go home alone, and don't drive yourself. Don't skate again until a medical professional clears you, even if you feel fine.

COACH: At home, stickhandle with your head up, little and often. And for strength work, build up gradually, and follow a proper conditioning programme.

PLAYER: What should I ask before next time?

COACH: Three things. Ask the rink which public session is quietest, and what its posted rules allow. Ask what gear stick-and-puck needs. And to find your weaker side, film yourself, or ask a coach who's watched you skate, and ask them whether they'd change the order you work in. Then pick two or three drills, and go and skate them on both feet.
