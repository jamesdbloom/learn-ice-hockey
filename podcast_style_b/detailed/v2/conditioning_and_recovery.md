As for strength training, expert statements support it for young players, on one condition. It has to be properly planned and supervised. So get qualified coaching before a young player lifts at all. If you coach a young player, work with qualified coaches and, where it's needed, medical professionals.

PLAYER: So for the week. Train the shift, hard efforts with long easy rests. Get strong one leg at a time, and get coached on the lifts. Strengthen my groin before it hurts, and take pain that keeps going to a physio. Regular beats hard. Build in the off-season, keep it in-season, nothing hard the day before a game. And young players need broad skills, more than one sport, and qualified coaching before lifting.

COACH: Spot on. Now game day, from your last meal to your last shift.

COACH: Eleventh idea. Fuel before you play. Hockey burns through the carbohydrate stored in your muscles far more than its short playing time suggests. Single muscle fibres can run empty, even while the muscle as a whole still has fuel. And an empty fibre can't contract hard.

PLAYER: So that's my third-period legs again.

COACH: Running low on fuel and fluid is one of the likeliest reasons for it. In elite players, how well someone plays in a game has been linked to how much fuel was stored in their muscles before it. So eat your main meal three to four hours before the game. Build it round carbohydrate, like rice, pasta, potatoes or bread, with some protein. Keep fat and fibre low, because they slow digestion and leave your stomach heavy. If you need it, have a small top-up one to two hours before, like a banana, toast or a cereal bar. Keep it small and familiar.

COACH: Those times are standard sports-nutrition advice, and they're approximate. What you've tested and can digest matters more than the clock. If you have a dietitian, use their numbers instead. Never try a new food or drink on game day. Test it at practice first. And arrive with enough to drink already in you. In one small study of para hockey players, four in ten turned up a little short of fluid before they'd even started. A glass of water an hour before is worth more than a litre at the rink.

COACH: Twelfth idea. The warm-up itself. Most of the evidence is from other sports, so let's be straight about what it shows. In one review of teenage team athletes, warm-up programmes with several parts cut total injuries by about a third. Most of those athletes played other sports, not hockey, and the review calls its own evidence low quality. But it's the strongest reason to bother.

PLAYER: And the doing-it-every-time part?

COACH: In studies of hamstring injuries, players who did their prevention routine in at least three sessions out of four had roughly two-thirds less risk of injury. Players who rarely did it got almost no benefit. Most of that is from football, meaning soccer, and other team sports, and the review rates its own evidence as low certainty. So take the pattern, not the exact numbers. A plain routine you never skip beats a clever one you do now and then.

COACH: So here's an off-ice routine. Ten to fifteen minutes, before you get dressed, in four parts. First, raise your temperature. Three or four minutes of light jogging on the spot, skipping, or a fast walk around the building. Get warm enough to notice it. Second, loosen up by moving. Leg swings front to back and side to side, and the side-to-side ones are for your groin. Then walking lunges with a twist, hip openers, deep squat holds, arm circles and shoulder rolls.

COACH: Third, wake up the muscles your stride depends on. Glute bridges, side steps with a band round your legs, and a few squats with no weight. Fourth, a few short sprints, jumps or hops, building up to nearly full effort. That gets your nervous system ready to produce force fast, and it's the part people leave out. That four-part shape is a standard coaching framework applied to hockey. The research backs structured warm-ups in general, not this exact order.

PLAYER: I always do a few long stretches before I go out.

COACH: Warm up by moving instead. Long, still stretches just before you play can leave you a little weaker and slower for a while. And stretching before you play doesn't prevent injury. The loss comes mainly from long holds, so a short hold of ten or fifteen seconds inside a fuller warm-up is unlikely to ruin your game. Stretch for range of movement at a separate time.

COACH: Then the few minutes you get on the ice. Most rec games give you three to five. What would you do first?

PLAYER: Grab a puck and start shooting.

COACH: Skate first, shoot later. Two or three laps, building from easy to brisk. Include skating backwards, crossovers both ways, and a few hard starts and stops. Your edges need waking up more than your wrist shot does. Then handle the puck a little. A few touches and some short passes get your hands going. Then shoot, starting easy. Don't open with a full slap shot from the blue line. That asks a cold body for an all-out twist.

COACH: And give your goalie proper warm-up shots. Not the top corner from the slot on your first shot. The top corner is head height, and a cold goalie is least ready to track that first one. Start with easy shots from distance and work in. Your goalie can't ease into the game the way you can. That's a coaching caution, not a law of hockey. Finish with one or two full-speed bursts of about five seconds, so your first shift isn't your warm-up.

COACH: Thirteenth idea. Drink while you play, and keep your shifts short. You sweat more than a cold rink suggests. Elite players have been measured losing around two litres an hour. So drink on the bench every shift or two, in small amounts, rather than one big drink between periods. That little-and-often habit is the usual advice from practitioners, not something those studies tested.

PLAYER: Water, or a sports drink?

COACH: Water is fine for most rec games. For a long, hot or very hard game, a standard sports drink with carbohydrate and salt makes sense, because it replaces fluid, adds fuel and replaces salt. Between periods is your best chance for a slightly bigger drink. On a long tournament day, add a small carbohydrate snack. And keep drinking after the game. In one study of college players, some of the fluid they lost was still missing the next morning. And keep your shifts short. Change before you need to. A forty-second shift played well beats a long one where you were a passenger.

COACH: Fourteenth idea. Meeting the boards. Picture yourself chasing a puck into the corner, and you can hear someone coming in behind you. No amount of gym work protects your shoulder the way meeting the boards correctly does. Shoulder pads that fit, and a strong upper back and shoulder blades, help too. But most of the protection is skill and awareness. And this is your job in every league, checking or not, because a wall doesn't need an opponent to hurt you.

PLAYER: Why the shoulder in particular?

COACH: The shoulder is the most common place for an upper-body injury in young hockey players. The classic hockey shoulder injury comes from landing on the point of your shoulder, or being driven into the boards. So go into the wall on an angle, not straight at it. If contact is coming and you still have time to turn, skates parallel to the boards, feet apart, knees bent. Forearm and hip to the boards. Never the point of your shoulder, on any approach, and no part of your shoulder if you can help it. Head up, chin off your chest. Never turn your back to anyone coming at you. And never duck.

PLAYER: Why are those last bits so strict?

COACH: They protect your head and neck. If you turn your back, the contact becomes a hit from behind, and you can't see it coming. If you duck, or tuck your chin to your chest, your neck straightens and your head meets the boards first. That can injure your neck even at walking speed. So keep it up even going slowly. Taking a hit in open ice has its own technique, and what it says about your shoulder is the opposite of what you do at the wall. So don't bring it to the wall.

PLAYER: And if I'm already facing the wall and can't turn?

COACH: Then things have gone wrong, and this is damage limitation. Spread the hit over as much of you as you can, with your stick and gloves up on the glass. Head up, chin off your chest, and keep your head off the glass. It isn't the position you were aiming for. From here on I'll use one short version, the same words every time. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to anyone coming at you, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly.

COACH: Two more injuries you can make less likely. Your wrist first. About three in ten upper-body hockey injuries land on a wrist or a finger, and the wrist is one of the most often broken places in the game. So wear gloves that cover the gap between the glove and your elbow pad, because that's where slashes land. Wear elbow pads that fit. And don't put a bare hand out to break a fall.

COACH: Then your knee. The ligament on the inside of the knee is the classic hockey knee injury. It usually comes from a blow to the outside of the knee, or a leg trapped in a collision or against the boards. General leg strength, one-leg strength, balance, and landing and slowing down well all help. Some knee injuries are just collisions, and no training prevents those.

COACH: Fifteenth idea. Know whether body checking is allowed in your game. A body check is using your body to knock an opponent off the puck. One review of youth hockey reports that body checking is the biggest injury risk that can be changed. It links checking with about three times as many concussions, and it reports that banning checking has cut injury rates by fifty to seventy percent. Those are figures the review gathered from other studies, not ones it measured. But the direction is clear. No amount of fitness does what a ban does.

PLAYER: So how do I know if I'm in a checking game?

COACH: Find out before you play, because your age doesn't settle it. In North America, checking is banned in the younger age groups. That's twelve and under in the United States, and under-thirteen and below in Canada. But that's a floor, not an answer, because local bodies can ban it at older ages too. Adult hockey is split into checking and non-checking leagues. And in girls' and women's hockey in the United States and Canada, checking is banned at every age. Playing the puck doesn't make a check legal there.

PLAYER: And in the UK?

COACH: Your competition's own rules set the age. In the twenty twenty-six to twenty-seven season, in the junior leagues IHUK runs, under-tens and under-twelves are non-checking, and under-fourteens and up are checking. NIHL Divisions One and Two are checking. The women's national league, the WNIHL, is non-checking, so don't body check there at all. For any other league, ask.

COACH: UK women's hockey has extra limits, at any age, even where a league allows checking at all. If your league says non-checking, that wins. Otherwise a check is only allowed when you're clearly playing the puck. Even then, stepping or gliding into someone is a penalty. So is pushing someone into the boards or pinning them there, or hitting a player who is standing still. That holds however clearly you were playing the puck.

COACH: Wherever checking is banned, a check is a penalty, and a deliberate bump, shove or push can be one too. If it's reckless or violent, or it injures someone, plan on being thrown out of the game. In some leagues a ban for several more games is likely as well. So find out which kind of game you're in, before your first shift.

PLAYER: Game day, then. Carbohydrate meal hours before, nothing new on game day, and turn up with enough to drink in me. Warm up off the ice, by moving, every time. On the ice, skate first, shoot later, and give the goalie easy shots to start. Drink a little every shift or two, and change before I need to.

COACH: And at the boards?

PLAYER: The short version. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of my shoulder, head up, chin off my chest, never my back to anyone coming at me, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly. And find out whether checking is allowed in my game before I play.

COACH: That's it. Now after the game. Recovering, so your next game, or your next morning, goes better.

COACH: Sixteenth idea. Protect your sleep before you buy any recovery product. Sleep, fluid, carbohydrate and protein are where the recovery evidence is. Of the recovery tools, sleep is the best supported, the cheapest and the most ignored. It's also the first thing most players give up when life gets busy. That's why it sits at the top, not because anyone has measured it as the biggest effect in hockey.

PLAYER: Does sleep affect injuries too?

COACH: There's an early link between short sleep and a higher injury risk. That comes from a study that hadn't finished being checked by other scientists, so treat it as a hint, not a fact. Aim for a regular seven to nine hours, which is the general advice for adults. A regular time to sleep and wake beats a big lie-in now and then. If you have to choose, protect the night before a game more than the night after.

COACH: Before a heavy stretch, like a tournament or a run of late games, get extra sleep beforehand rather than trying to catch up after. Extra sleep banked in advance helps you stay alert when your sleep is cut short later. And a twenty to thirty minute nap is fine on a day with an evening game.

COACH: Seventeenth idea. The late game. A puck drop at half past ten at night is what most adult players face, and it causes three problems at once. First, you haven't eaten for hours. Dinner at half past six and a game at half past ten means playing on an empty tank. So eat a proper meal with carbohydrate in it three to four hours before. Then have a small, easy top-up an hour or so before, if you feel empty.

PLAYER: And the second?

COACH: You're already tired from a day's work. Accept that your ceiling is lower, and keep your shifts shorter to suit. Third, and this is the real cost, you can't sleep afterwards. You finish near midnight, wound up, thirsty and hungry. So drink and eat something during the game and straight after it, not at one in the morning. Keep screens and bright light down afterwards.

PLAYER: What about a drink after?

COACH: Keep the post-game beer modest, because alcohol dries you out further and makes your sleep measurably worse. And give yourself a wind-down routine, rather than going straight from the rink to bed. If you play at half past ten on a work night, lost sleep, not training, is your main recovery problem. No product fixes it. Plan the next morning around it. So for a late game, eat early, keep your shifts short, and refuel at the rink, so you can get to sleep.

COACH: Eighteenth idea. Refuel, move easy the next day, and be honest about the products. After the game, refuel. Carbohydrate comes first, with some protein for repair. Replace your fluid, using how you feel and the colour of your urine as a rough guide.

PLAYER: Do I have to eat within half an hour?

COACH: For most amateur players, how much you eat matters more than exactly when. The idea of a thirty-minute window after exercise is far weaker than it was once made out to be. If your next game or practice is more than a day away, a good meal within a couple of hours is plenty. Fast refuelling matters when you play again the same day or the next morning, like on a tournament weekend. The day after, easy movement helps. A walk, an easy bike ride, or easy skating. Just don't make it hard enough to count as training.

PLAYER: What about ice baths? Everyone online swears by them.

COACH: Ice baths help you feel less sore. But straight after a strength session, the cold may blunt what the session was for, because cooling cuts the blood flow to the muscle and slows how it takes in the protein it uses to rebuild. So if you lift to get stronger, skip the ice bath straight afterwards. Use one after a game or a tournament, when you want to feel better and play again soon. Compression clothing is genuinely unresolved. One broad review was favourable, and a recent trial found nothing. It's harmless and comfortable, but don't count on it.

COACH: Foam rolling and massage can make you feel less sore and move more freely for a while. But the evidence that they help you play better, or prevent injury, is weak. Feeling less sore can help you get back to training, but it isn't repair. It doesn't break anything up inside the muscle. And stretching after a game does little for soreness. Stretch because you want range of movement.

COACH: Most of this research is small and short. A lot of it measures how sore people feel, not whether they played better the next week. Where two reviews of the same method disagree, which happens a lot, the honest reading is that the effect is probably small. So spend your effort on sleep, fluid and food first.

COACH: Nineteenth idea. Going back after a concussion. This is about the days after a knock to the head, not the moment itself. Picture this. It's three days after a knock. You feel completely fine, and there's a game on Saturday. What would you do?

PLAYER: If I feel fine, I'd want to play.

COACH: Going back to play is a medical decision, and it happens in stages. Everyday activity first, then light aerobic exercise, then exercise for your sport, then drills with no contact. Only after a medical professional clears the player does full-contact practice come, and then games. Each stage depends on coping with the one before. If symptoms come back, you step back.

PLAYER: Why so careful, if I feel fine?

COACH: A brain that's still recovering is more easily hurt again. And a second injury during recovery is linked to worse problems that last longer. That's the reason for the same-day rule, and the reason going back happens in stages. A qualified medical professional who has assessed the actual player manages those stages. Children and teenagers are managed more carefully than adults. So be suspicious of any hockey resource that gives you a schedule.

COACH: So come off. Tell someone. Don't drive yourself home. See a doctor. And don't go back on the ice until a medical professional says you can, even if you feel fine by then. If you're a coach or a captain, taking a player off is yours to enforce, not the player's to overrule.

PLAYER: Is it different in the UK?

COACH: The UK guidance adds some minimums underneath that medical decision. There's no return to competition before day twenty-one, and the day of the injury counts as day zero. That's a minimum, not a date. You aren't ready on day twenty-one because it's day twenty-one, and it doesn't replace the medical assessment or the stages. So a clearance on day ten doesn't put you in a UK game on day ten. Training with a risk of a knock to the head, including contact practice, waits until you've had no symptoms at rest for fourteen days, and it can't start before day fifteen.

COACH: The UK guidance no longer expects a doctor to sign you through each stage. But it still needs the first step. No more physical sport or work activity, even if the symptoms go, until you've been assessed by a healthcare professional, or by calling one one one for the NHS. Get that done within twenty-four hours. After that first assessment, call one one one if your symptoms get worse, or haven't improved fourteen days after the injury, and see your GP if you still have symptoms after twenty-eight days. A red flag at any point, like a headache that's getting worse, growing confusion or drowsiness, or being sick more than once, is still nine nine nine.

COACH: At grassroots level, those minimums may be the only checks anyone makes after the first assessment. They apply even if nobody has cleared you, and you should still get cleared. The only exception is a player in elite or professional sport whose concussion is managed by specialist sports-concussion professionals. Grassroots hockey isn't that group.

COACH: Let's pull it all together. The big four first. Build the easy aerobic base that rebuilds you between shifts, and do it off the ice. Change before you need to. If you catch yourself gliding, you needed to change fifteen seconds ago. And warm up off the ice before every game, every time.

COACH: If a concussion is suspected, the player comes off and doesn't play again that day. Treat every knock to the head as a possible neck injury. Phone for help first, don't move them except to keep their airway clear, and the helmet stays on. Phone for an ambulance straight away for any one red flag, at any point, even if it seems mild, and for any symptom getting worse in that first day, while someone stays with them. Any neck pain is a red flag. Where CRT six and the UK list differ, act on whichever fires first. In the UK, a knock with no red flag means one one one within twenty-four hours, and one one one again if symptoms get worse later or haven't improved by day fourteen. Someone stays with them, and they don't drive or go home alone. And a medical professional decides when they play again.

PLAYER: In the week, train the shift, hard efforts with long rests. Get strong one leg at a time, and get coached on the lifts. Strengthen my groin before it hurts. Build in the off-season, keep it in-season, and never go hard the day before a game.

COACH: On game day, eat hours before and drink on the bench. At the boards, the short version. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to anyone coming at you, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly. And know whether checking is allowed in your game. After the game, protect your sleep and refuel. Don't spend money on recovery before you've sorted sleep, fluid and food.

COACH: Some of this depends on your team and your league. So here are three questions to ask your coach. Is body checking allowed in our category? Who at our games is trained to look after a hurt player, and who phones for help? And how long do you want our shifts to be? If you play in the UK, add one more. Who on our team takes blood thinners?

PLAYER: And one thing to do before my next game?

COACH: Put CRT six on your phone, and know where the red flag list is on it. Then warm up, and keep your shifts short.
