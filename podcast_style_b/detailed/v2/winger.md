Offside means both your skates have completely crossed their blue line before the puck has. So one skate still touching the line keeps you onside. That gives you a technique. Drag your back skate along the line while your body, stick and speed get ahead of it. Done right, you're a full stride into the zone, at speed, and still onside. But keep your head up as you cross. Your body, hands and stick are in the zone before your skate is, in the ice a defenceman standing up at their own line is defending. You can't brace for a hit you can't see.

PLAYER: Can I lift the back skate just over the line?

COACH: Keep the blade touching the ice. Don't lift it. A dragged, touching skate keeps you onside wherever you play, and a lifted one doesn't, in some leagues.

PLAYER: Wouldn't it be safer to slow down at the line so I'm never offside?

COACH: Don't coast at the line to be safe. Arriving late and slow costs your line more than a whistle would. And if you do go in too early, there's often a second chance, called a delayed offside. The linesperson's arm goes up, but play carries on. Your team fixes it by tagging up, clearing the zone and touching the blue line. That means all five of you, not just you. Nobody goes back in until every teammate has cleared. As the winger who's usually first to the line, you're the one who goes back in too early. So tag up, then look before you go. Not every level plays delayed offside this way, so ask how it works in your games.

COACH: That's the middle of the ice. Your turn.

PLAYER: Hold my lane and stay wide, because a flat line can't cover three wide forwards. On a two-on-one, drive the net and make the defender choose. When they have the puck, know whether we contain or press. Dump it in on a read, pick the right kind, and chase it on an angle, and pull up if the goalie comes out. Carry it past the red line before I dump it. And at their blue line, drag my back skate with the blade on the ice, and keep my head up.

COACH: That's it. Now their end.

COACH: Twelfth idea. In their end, shoot when the look is yours, and never stand still. Your spots in their end are the half-wall, your own faceoff circle, and the side of the net. From the half-wall, you've got three things to look for. A shot. A pass to your centre in the slot. Or a pass back to your defenceman at the point. If you have space to drive the net, do it, under control, and never into the goalie. A winger standing still is easy to cover, so keep moving.

PLAYER: I usually look for one more pass. It feels like the team play.

COACH: Most wingers pass up good shots rather than take bad ones. That's coaching know-how, not a counted finding. And the defence learns it. If you always look for one more pass, they can sit in the seam and wait for the pass instead of coming at you. So when a teammate is already at the net front, take the shot from your circle or the half-wall. The extra pass only gives the defence time to close.

PLAYER: So I should always shoot?

COACH: Shoot first is a habit to lean toward, not a rule. A teammate with a clear look at an open side of the net is always worth the pass. What you're breaking is the habit of always looking for one more.

COACH: From your faceoff circle, be ready for a one-timer, a shot straight off a pass without stopping the puck. Set your feet before the pass arrives, with your body open toward the passer and your weight already on your back foot. Start your backswing as the pass is released, not when it arrives. Hit the ice just behind the puck. Aim low to the far side, or for a tip. And if the pass is bad, don't force it. A missed one-timer usually ends up in the neutral zone going the other way. Take the extra touch.

PLAYER: What about the high slot? It's right there.

COACH: It usually isn't yours, unless your team's setup sends you there. Don't crowd it when someone is already there, and find out who that should be rather than assume it's the centre. The front of the net, right in close, is a different patch of ice, and it's very often yours.

COACH: Now say you're in the corner, under pressure. It's the boards again. The boards position. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to anyone coming at you, never duck. Protect the puck and wait for your centre to come and help, rather than forcing it out through heavy traffic. A rushed pass out of the corner gives the puck away. And before you pass back to the point, look up. Your defenceman may have stepped down toward the top of the circle to help.

PLAYER: And when nothing's open down there?

COACH: Cycle it. Protect the puck down the boards and leave it on the boards behind you for the teammate coming in low, usually your centre. Then skate up and away from the wall, to get open for the return pass or a shot.

PLAYER: What does that do to the defence?

COACH: The defender who followed you down the boards is now out of position. The one who stayed is facing two of you. And if the boards are sealed, reverse it behind the net to the far side instead. That low cycle is the common approach. Some teams go back up to the point early and get bodies to the net. Ask which yours runs.

COACH: Thirteenth idea. The forecheck. Forechecking means pressuring the other team in their own end, to win the puck back rather than skate back and wait. The forecheck has three roles, called F one, F two and F three. You dumped it in, you beat everyone to the puck, and your centre is still on the ice behind you. Do you wait for the centre?

PLAYER: Isn't the centre usually first in?

COACH: The roles are set by who arrives first, not by position. Whoever gets to the puck first is F one. The second player in is F two. The highest is F three. So if you got there first, you're F one, even with your centre on the ice. Don't wait for the centre.

COACH: As F two, come in behind F one on the puck side. Read where F one has pushed the carrier, and take the carrier's next way out, usually the boards or behind the net. Pin the puck against the boards, not the player. Never wrap the player up against the boards with your arms. That's holding.

COACH: As F three, the highest forward, stay up in the zone and read the play. In the usual setup you cover the middle and the far side. That stops an opponent slipping up the middle, and it covers a pass across the ice to the far side. And you're ready to go the other way the moment your team wins the puck.

PLAYER: Is that how every team does it?

COACH: No. In the stacked version, F three shifts to the puck side instead. All three forwards overload one corner, and the far-side pass out is given up on purpose. Teams also differ on how many go deep. A two-one-two forecheck sends two forwards in. A one-two-two sends one. Ask which your team runs. And on the forecheck, the angle you take matters more than how fast you go. If everyone chases the puck, the other team just passes around you. So stay in your role, and trust your linemates to be in theirs.

COACH: Fourteenth idea. The front of their net, in more detail. Screening from outside the crease, without contact, is one of the most useful things you do. Body out of the blue paint, skates off the red line around it. Staying off that line is the answer that holds wherever you play. Keep your stick out of the crease too, even with both your skates outside, or it can cost you the goal.

PLAYER: Can I stand right on top of the goalie, as long as I'm outside?

COACH: Take the goalie's eyes, never the goalie's movement. Standing so the goalie can't move in the crease can cost your team the goal, even from outside it. And leave the goalie a path home. If the goalie has come out to play the puck and is heading back, don't stand in the way. Blocking that route on purpose can be a penalty with no contact at all. In some leagues it always is, and the goal comes off as well.

PLAYER: What if a defender shoves me into the crease?

COACH: Get out, and go around the goalie, not through. Being pushed in doesn't excuse running into the goalie when you made no effort to avoid them. You can still be penalised, the goal can still be taken away, and if the goalie is hurt you can be thrown out of the game, and in some leagues that's automatic. What counts is that you tried to avoid them, and a last-second try may not be enough. So crash the net under control.

COACH: Never go into the goalie, in or out of the crease. Deliberate contact with a goalie is a penalty everywhere, whether or not the puck goes in. Charging into one, which means hitting hard after building up speed, can get you thrown out of the game in every league, and in some that's automatic. A charge meant to hurt the goalie is a match penalty in most leagues, and in at least one league plan on a match penalty for a reckless one too, with a likely suspension of several games. So stand outside the crease to block the view, and never go into the goalie at all, slow or fast.

PLAYER: It's a battle in front, though. People are pushing me with their sticks.

COACH: Make room with your feet, never with your stick across someone. A cross-check means using the shaft of your stick, held in both hands, to hit or shove a player. It's a penalty whoever you put it on. If it rides up toward the head, injures them, or is reckless, you can be thrown out of the game. In several leagues that's automatic, whether you meant it or not. And in at least one league, plan on a match penalty for a reckless one, which goes past being thrown out, with a likely suspension of several games.

COACH: And never put your stick or your body into anyone's back at the net front. The player boxing you out usually has their back to you, close to the post, and a goalie's back is no different. Hold your ground, or work that player from the side. Where body checking isn't allowed, keep your body off the defender and tie up the defender's stick with yours instead, no chops and never a hand on it. Hit a player from behind and in most leagues you're thrown out of the game. Drive them into the goal frame when they can't protect themselves and you'll almost certainly be thrown out, and in some leagues it's automatically a match penalty.

PLAYER: And in a league with no checking, what's allowed in front?

COACH: Holding the spot you already have is fine anywhere. Shoving someone off their spot isn't allowed where body checking is barred, with your body as well as your stick. There, hold your spot without leaning on anyone to keep it, and make them move you. In some leagues that's true at every level, checking game or not, because leaning on a player who doesn't have the puck is interference. In a lot of youth, girls' and women's, and adult hockey, a no-checking game is the game you're playing. So find out whether your league, your age group and your game allow body checking before you play.

COACH: And even where hitting is allowed, don't pile a hit onto a player who's already battling someone else for the puck. In some leagues that's a penalty in every game, and a reckless one gets you thrown out.

COACH: That's the front of their net. Give it to me short.

PLAYER: Take the goalie's eyes, never the goalie's movement or path. Outside the crease, off the red line, stick out of it too. Shoved in, get out and go around the goalie. Never into the goalie, never a stick across anyone, never into anyone's back. And where checking isn't allowed, hold my spot rather than shove for one.

COACH: Fifteenth idea. Faceoffs. Your centre takes the draw. You line up at the hash marks. One winger stands on the inside hash, facing the slot. The other stands on the outside hash, facing the boards. Usually the winger whose natural side matches the faceoff spot takes the outside, but teams vary, so ask. Your job on every draw is the same. Read who wins it and react straight away. Jump on a loose puck, support your centre, or get to your defensive spot.

PLAYER: I sometimes creep in a bit to get a jump on the loose puck.

COACH: Don't creep inside the circle or over your hash marks before the puck drops. If you do, usually your centre gets thrown out of the faceoff, not you, and your team takes the draw with its second choice. Do it twice on the same draw and it's a penalty, and in some leagues the penalty is yours. Stay off the circle line too, because some leagues count a skate on it. And keep your stick blade back.

COACH: Keep your hands to yourself as well, and your stick off theirs. In most leagues a glove or a shoulder on the player beside you before the drop costs your centre the draw, exactly like a skate over the line. In a non-checking game it can put you in the penalty box too. So stand still until the puck lands. And give the official room to get out of the circle.

PLAYER: What if our centre gets thrown out?

COACH: Be ready to take the draw yourself. Centres get thrown out several times a game. Know before the draw whether you're the backup. Go for a clean tie-up of the other player's stick with yours, never a hand on it. And if you caused the violation, some leagues won't let you step in, so have a second name ready, in case it can't be you. Once you step in to take the draw, contact with the other player before the drop can be a penalty as well as a violation, and in some leagues that's automatic. Get low from your knees and hips, not by pushing your head out over the dot. If the linesperson tells you to move back, move back the first time. And even with a safe stance, two heads end up a few inches apart on every draw. No rule makes that space safe. Keep your head back.

COACH: Sixteenth idea. The power play, when your team has an extra player. On the power play you're almost always in one of two spots, the half-wall or the front of the net. From the half-wall you're the main passer on your side, and you have four options. The point. The bumper, the player in the slot between the point and the front of the net. The net front. And the far half-wall, through the seam.

PLAYER: That's a lot to choose from.

COACH: That's what makes it such a good job. The penalty-killing team can take away any one of those four options, but not all four. So walk down toward the goal line to drag a defender with you, then use the space you just made.

COACH: From the net front, your job is screening, tips and rebounds. Get in the goalie's eyes. Body out of the crease, skates off the red line, and don't start contact or crowd the goalie. Deliberate contact with the goalie is a penalty whether or not you score, so it can cost you the power play you're on. From either spot, move the puck quickly. A penalty kill can cover any one option, but not three in two seconds. And ask whether the bumper is yours. It's usually the centre, but plenty of teams put a winger there.

COACH: Seventeenth idea. The penalty kill, when your team is a player short. Your job is to shrink the ice, not to chase the puck. Your team plays in a shape, and the shape only works if you hold your place in it. In a box, the standard shape, you're one of the two high players, each covering one point. In a diamond, the centre usually takes the top, and you take a middle spot, covering a half-wall.

PLAYER: And the wedge? I've heard of a wedge plus one.

COACH: In a wedge plus one, you're often the plus one, the single player working outside the triangle. That job is pressure, not pursuit. Get at whoever has the puck. When the puck moves, you stop and drop into the triangle, and the nearest player steps out as the new plus one. If you follow the puck around the outside, the power play pulls the shape apart and beats it. The route you take out to the puck is your coach's choice, so ask which one you run.

COACH: Force the players at their points into poor shots. How much your team blocks shots at all is a coaching choice, so ask your coach and your goalie what they want.

PLAYER: And if we do block?

COACH: Then here's the whole of it, and it goes together. Do it from close and on your feet. Square to the shooter. Head out of the shooting lane. Hands behind your body, with the backs of your gloves out. Stick flat on the ice beside you, never out in front, because a puck off a blade held out in front tends to ride up toward your face. That last part is coaching know-how, not measured. Tucking in behind your shin pads is for when you go down, not for standing. Standing, it folds you forward and puts your head down into the lane. Going down to block is a different technique, and this episode doesn't teach it. And never block from in front of your own goalie's eyes. A puck deflected off a body your goalie couldn't see is one they can't save. Block properly, or get out of the lane and let them see it.

COACH: Clear the puck whenever you get it. Don't be fancy. On a penalty kill, shooting it the length of the ice is usually allowed without icing being called, but not at every level, so ask about yours. And don't chase the puck around the outside and pull the shape apart. That's exactly what the power play wants.

COACH: Eighteenth idea, and it's short. Line changes. Some games, especially for younger players, change lines in a set way, and then your coach and the officials tell you how. In games where your line chooses when to change, change as a group, with your centre. When the centre calls it, go with them, rather than staying out for one more look.

PLAYER: What's the harm in one more look?

COACH: A line that drifts off one player at a time spends part of every shift a player short in the middle of the ice. And in those games, never change while the puck is in your own end and your team doesn't have it. In the collapse, you're the point coverage. Leave, and their defenceman is alone at the blue line with the puck coming to them.

COACH: Let me pull it all together.

COACH: Start at their net. Crash it on every shot. Driving in, arrive with the shot. On the net-front job, be planted before it. Just outside the crease, off the red line, stick on the ice, and never into the goalie. Know where the near post is, take a defender's shove braced, and keep your head up.

COACH: On the boards, puck on the far side and feet moving, with the decision made before you get there. The boards position. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to anyone coming at you, never duck. Free hand on your own stick.

COACH: In your own end, stay high, level with their defenceman, until your team wins the puck. Then come down the boards for the first pass. Take your look while the pass is coming, then move it. Rim it or reverse it rather than force it through the middle, and never across the front of your own net.

COACH: When the puck turns over, sprint back through your lane, find the late forward, and if you see their numbers, pull up.

COACH: Through the middle, hold your lane and stay wide. Dump it in on a read, and chase it on an angle. Drag your back skate at the blue line, with your head up. And in their end, shoot when the look is yours, and never stand still.

PLAYER: What should I ask my own coach?

COACH: Much of this depends on what your team plays. So here are five questions for your coach, and two for your league. Which system do we play in our own end, and if it's a hybrid, where's the line? Which breakout do we run, and where on the boards do you want me? On the backcheck, who takes the middle? In the neutral zone, do we contain or press? Who takes the draw if our centre gets thrown out? And for the league. Which rules do we play under? And is body checking allowed?

COACH: Their answers tell you how your team wants you to play, and now you know why each answer makes sense.
