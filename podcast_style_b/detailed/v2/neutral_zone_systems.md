A forward who takes a regroup pass standing still has wasted the regroup. The other is leaving too early. That means turning up the ice before your defenceman has the puck under control. Then the pass has to be perfect. If it isn't, you're offside, or the puck is behind you.

PLAYER: So what's the timing?

COACH: As your defenceman gets the puck, curl back in the same direction the puck is going. Stay below it, nearer your own end than the puck. Then, as the pass goes across, turn and drive up the ice into your lane. You want the puck when you're already moving forward, close to top speed, facing the play. It's like a relay runner taking the baton already running, not from a standing start. And don't all curl to the same place. One forward stays close for a short pass. One swings wide for the outside lane. One stays high for the long pass.

COACH: Seventeenth idea, and it's short. When not to regroup. Three of you are coming over the red line against two of them. Do you regroup?

PLAYER: No way.

COACH: Right. Take the three-on-two. Throwing away an odd-man rush isn't clever. And don't regroup when your line change is due. A regroup on tired legs means coming into the zone with nothing left. Then a hard dump past the red line and a change is a fair play, not a failure.

PLAYER: Anything to watch on the dump?

COACH: Two things, in some adult leagues. They count any wind-up past about fifteen inches behind the puck as a slap shot, which is a penalty there. If it hurts someone, it ends your game and costs you at least the next one. So keep the backswing short. They also make it a five-minute major to catch someone above the shoulders with your stick, even by accident, and if it hurts them, you're out of the game. So keep your follow-through low too. And last, don't regroup when the pass back isn't clean. A regroup into pressure is just a turnover in a worse place.

COACH: Let me pull the regroup together. When there's no way forward, go back and come again. It keeps the puck, makes their forwards turn, and gives yours speed. Make the pass across flat and hard, never blind through the middle. Defencemen, drop back for the hinge. Forwards, curl with the puck and take it moving. In your own end, never pass the puck, or skate it, across the front of your net, and one look, one decision, move it. And skip the regroup when you have numbers, when you need to change, or when the pass back isn't clean.

COACH: Now let's attack through the middle. Eighteenth idea. One forward in each lane. Remember the three lanes. Left boards, middle, right boards. Put one forward in each.

PLAYER: Shouldn't I get close to the carrier, to help?

COACH: Count the options. Three forwards spread out are three different passing options. A flat line of defenders can't cover three lanes without spreading thin. If two forwards share one lane, one defender covers both, and your team has one fewer option than it could have. It goes wrong because everyone follows the puck. The puck goes to the left boards, and your centre goes there too. Your right winger drifts over to help. Now the whole right side is empty, the defence has slid over, and there's nowhere to go.

PLAYER: So I hold my lane even when the puck isn't in it.

COACH: Hold your lane, and stay level with the puck or ahead of it. Out here, helping the carrier means being open in a different place, not being close. A lane belongs to whoever is in it, not to one player. You'll swap all the time, so call it. "I've got middle." And if you're carrying the puck and both your other forwards are in your lane, there's no pass. Slow down, hold the puck, and let them spread out.

COACH: Nineteenth idea. The middle lane. A puck touched in the middle makes every defender reset.

PLAYER: How does one touch do that?

COACH: A defence covers one side at a time, sliding toward the puck. One quick pass into the middle, and back out to the far boards, makes them slide twice, and a defence that's sliding has gaps. Boards, middle, far boards. It takes about a second, and it's the most reliable way to get in with the puck against an organised defence.

PLAYER: What's the catch?

COACH: Losing the puck in the middle costs the most, in coaches' judgement. So play the middle pass with one touch. Pass it straight on, without stopping it. If you stop, turn and look around, you'll get hit, and the puck will be going the other way. And expect to get hit there. Coaches say the middle is where open-ice hits happen, though nobody has counted it. A one-touch pass doesn't keep your head up for you. You have to do that yourself. So take it with your head up, because you can't brace for a hit you never saw. Do your looking before the puck arrives. The same goes when you skate home through the middle, and when you skate onto a long pass.

COACH: Twentieth idea. Taking a pass on the boards, with their defenceman stepping up. The puck comes to you on the boards, and their defenceman is stepping up to meet you. You've got about a second. What would you do?

PLAYER: Get it past them somehow.

COACH: You've got three good answers. Bump it into the middle with one touch, if your centre is there and moving. The defender who stepped up is now behind the play. Or chip it off the boards past the defender's outside, and skate around them, but only if a second forward is coming to support you. Or turn it back to a defenceman and regroup.

PLAYER: What about beating them one-on-one?

COACH: That's the one you mustn't do. Don't stop on the boards and try to beat the defender with nobody behind you. A turnover there is a rush the other way, with you on the wrong side of it. Some teams chip every puck on the boards, and some play it to the middle. That's a coaching choice, so ask your bench which comes first.

COACH: Now take that pass the safe way, because a defender stepping up on you is exactly when it matters. Get your skates parallel to the boards. Forearm and hip to the boards. Never the point of your shoulder, and not the whole shoulder if you can help it. Head up, chin off your chest. Never turn your back to anyone coming at you. And never duck.

PLAYER: Why those two especially?

COACH: Both protect your head and neck. If you turn your back, you can't see the hit coming or brace for it. If you duck, your head meets the boards first. That holds even going slowly. And it holds where checking isn't allowed, too. The boards don't need an opponent to hurt you. A legal angle, where a defender skates you toward the boards and takes away your room, can still put you into them.

PLAYER: What if I'm already facing the boards and can't turn?

COACH: That's the last resort, not a choice. Get your stick and gloves up and spread wide on the glass, with your head off it. From here on we'll call all of that the boards position, and say it the same way every time. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to anyone coming at you, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly.

PLAYER: So, one forward in each lane, and hold mine. Use the middle with one touch, and my head up. And on the boards, with a defender stepping up, bump it, chip it with support, or go back, and take it in the boards position.

COACH: That's the lot. Now, reading the play while you've got it.

COACH: Twenty-first idea. Reading the middle while you carry the puck. You've already read F one's route. Now two more reads. First, count the bodies. Lift your head at your own blue line, and count the opponents between you and their blue line. You see two. What would you do?

PLAYER: Attack it. There's space.

COACH: Yes. Three across means a one-three-one, so look behind the line before you look through it. Four in a line means there's nothing there. Start again, or chip it in and change.

PLAYER: And the second read?

COACH: Watch their defencemen's feet. Make this read early, around the red line, not at their blue line. If their feet are moving backwards, the defenceman is backing off. Carry it in, because you have the line. If their feet have stopped, or they're squared up at the line, they're standing up. Chip it past them.

COACH: Twenty-second idea. Chip and chase. When their defenceman is standing up with a tight gap, don't try to carry through them. Chip the puck past them into space, and skate onto it. Off the boards, off the glass, or softly into a spot behind them.

PLAYER: Why does that beat them?

COACH: A defenceman whose feet have stopped can't win a race to a puck behind them. But the chip is a placement, not a clearance. Put it in a spot you can get to first. Soft off the boards, so it dies in the corner you're already skating to. Or flat past their hip on their stick side, so you can go round them on your forehand. And the second and third forwards have to chase it. A chip nobody chases is just giving the puck away. Chasing still means this. Stick on the puck, not a hit. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call. And if you see their numbers, pull up.

COACH: Get it over the red line first. Icing is shooting the puck from your own half across their goal line, with nobody touching it. Play stops, and the faceoff comes back to your end. In some leagues your tired line can't change before that faceoff, either. So get it across the red line, or chip it soft enough that it stays in. Some adult leagues use the blue lines for icing instead, so ask your league where its line is.

COACH: Twenty-third idea. The stretch pass. That's a long pass to a forward who has stayed high, near or past their blue line. It skips the middle entirely.

PLAYER: When does it work?

COACH: Against a line of defenders that's still standing out in the middle. A trap at the red line, or the three across a one-three-one. There's real ice behind that line. Against a one-three-one, a forward behind the line is in a race with one defender. So get behind the line, not level with it. Level, a defender can cover you. Behind, you're racing them for the puck. And the passer passes to space, not to feet, flat and hard. A long pass to a player standing still is a turnover.

PLAYER: How do I stay onside out there?

COACH: Offside means both your skates are completely over their blue line before the puck has completely crossed it. So keep one skate touching the line, or on your side of it, until the puck crosses. The safe way is to drag your blade along the line. That works in every league. A skate lifted in the air doesn't count everywhere, so don't rely on it. And time your run, rather than waiting out there.

COACH: Leaving a forward high has a cost, because you defend four against five while you do it. Some coaches want a stretch option every time. Others forbid it in some score situations. So ask which yours wants.

COACH: Twenty-fourth idea. The late player. Picture a rush. Two of your forwards are driving in, and you're the third. What would you do?

PLAYER: Catch up, so there are three of us going in?

COACH: Try holding back. Stay ten to fifteen feet behind the first two, on purpose. Then arrive into the space they've emptied. That space is usually the high slot, the ice in front of the net between the faceoff dots and the tops of the circles. This player is called the late man, or the trailer.

PLAYER: Why does being late help?

COACH: Defenders watch the puck and the attackers nearest it. So the player arriving second is the one nobody picks up. Coaches rate it as one of the best scoring chances in hockey, though nobody has counted that. The discipline is to be really late. That means a full gap back, not five feet. Then the defence has to choose between you and the puck. And if the puck turns over, you're first back. That's the deal.

PLAYER: And when they do it to us?

COACH: In the usual setup, the forwards coming back take that player, not the defencemen, who are busy with the first attackers. But some coaches give the trailer to the far-side defenceman. Ask which your team plays.

COACH: Twenty-fifth idea. Beating a trap. A trap hands you the puck and defends the ice instead. So having the puck tells you nothing on its own. What follows works against any shape that won't come and get the puck. A trap, a one-three-one, or a one-four.

PLAYER: Where do I start?

COACH: First, move the puck faster than they can slide. A line of defenders covers one side at a time. When the puck goes across, they all have to skate across with it, and a hard pass gets there first. A trap beats teams that carry the puck. It doesn't beat teams that pass it. Second, use the boards on purpose. A hard rim, with a short backswing, goes along the boards around the curve of the rink and past a defender who is guarding the ice. So does a chip off the boards to a forward arriving behind the pressure.

PLAYER: That forward's going to get met, though.

COACH: Expect it. Stepping up hard on a pass to the boards is a defenceman's job in most of these shapes. So take it in the boards position. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to anyone coming at you, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly.

COACH: Third, stretch a forward behind the line, while it's still out in the middle. But not against a one-four. A one-four holds its line on its own blue line, so a forward already behind it is offside the moment the puck crosses. Against a one-four, chip the puck hard past the flat line, and race for it. Nobody in a flat line is deeper than anybody else, so it's a real race. Race for the puck, not the player. Stick on the puck, not a hit. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call. And if you see their numbers, pull up. And if their goalie comes out for it, pull up. Take away the goalie's pass, and never hit them. A goalie racing for the puck is watching the puck, not you, and can't protect themselves. A hit there can hurt them. Unnecessary contact with a goalie is a penalty in every league. And no league makes a goalie outside the crease fair game.

PLAYER: What if I can see a gap in the trap?

COACH: That's the fourth way, the seam. The seam is the gap between two defenders. A skilled carrier cutting back through the middle, behind a forechecker who has stepped up, can break the whole trap. It's the biggest reward and the biggest risk. Amateur players often try it first. Try it last. If you can't clearly see the seam, it isn't there.

COACH: Fifth, if the way in isn't on, dump it deep past the red line on purpose. Then hunt it with three forwards. Stick on the puck, not a hit. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call. And if you see their numbers, pull up. A team with five players back in the middle is built to stop rushes, not forechecks.

PLAYER: Isn't dumping it the dull answer?

COACH: Usually carrying it is better. Tracked games in top professional hockey found that carrying the puck in produces about twice the shots of dumping it in. That same work found players give the puck up at the line too easily, not too rarely. But against a trap, a dump-in you chase beats a turnover at the blue line. That's a read on the trap in front of you, not a reason to dump every time.

COACH: Sixth, be patient. A trap lets you have the puck and waits for you to make a mistake with it. So if nothing is open, regroup, and make them come out and get it. But keep the puck moving, not standing still.

COACH: Let me pull the attack together. Watch their defencemen's feet. Backing off, carry it in. Standing up, chip it past and chase it, over the red line first. Use the stretch pass only behind a line still out in the middle, and drag your blade to stay onside. Against a one-four, chip and race. If the goalie comes out for it, pull up. Never hit a goalie. Against a trap, move the puck, and try the seam last.

COACH: Twenty-sixth idea, and a short one. Faceoffs in the middle of the ice. A faceoff out there turns straight into a rush, because everybody is already facing the right way. So before the puck drops, know two things.

PLAYER: Which way we're trying to win it?

COACH: That's one. Are we winning it forward or back? Your centre should tell you. The other is who your player is if we lose it. Then don't creep. Everyone else stays about fifteen feet away from the two centres. If a teammate creeps in early, your centre gets thrown out of the faceoff. If your team does it a second time on the same faceoff, it can be a penalty. In most leagues that's automatic, so plan on it. Where you line up is a coaching choice, and it varies a lot. Ask your coach for yours, and hold your line until the puck drops.

COACH: Let me pull it all together.

COACH: The middle of the ice gives you about three seconds, so decide before the puck reaches you. Know which shape your team is playing this shift, and play the same one as the other four.

COACH: When the puck is lost, get between your player and your net, and come home through the middle with your head up. Don't hang at the red line unless your coach has made you the stretch option. Your backpressure is what lets your defencemen play tight.

COACH: At your own blue line, stand up when you have help behind you, back off when you don't, and call it. Stand up with your feet, never with a hit. Stick on the puck, not a hit. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call. And if you see their numbers, pull up. A head-down carrier tells you they can't change direction, and nothing more.

COACH: With the puck, read F one's route. Keep one forward in each lane. When the way in isn't there, regroup, and take the puck moving. Against a trap, move the puck, and try the seam last.

COACH: Don't panic pass. Get one more look, and out in the middle, when you don't know, keep it, side-on to the checker, with the puck on the far side of your body. In your own end, one look, one decision, move it, and never pass the puck, or skate it, across the front of your own net. And anywhere on the ice, never your back to anyone coming at you.

COACH: And on the boards, the boards position every time. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to anyone coming at you, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly.

PLAYER: What should I ask my own coach?

COACH: Three things. Which shape do we play, and what's the call to switch it? How hard do you want the blue line held? And do we leave a forward up for the stretch pass, and who is it? Ask, because these are your team's choices, not rules. Their answers tell you how your team wants you to play the middle of the ice, and now you know the reason behind each one.
