COACH: It's giving up on purpose. It stops odd-man rushes and dangerous chances, and that's all. An odd-man rush is when the attackers outnumber the defenders coming back. The price is any real chance of winning the puck in their end, and so any offence. So it's a tool for a moment, not a system for a game. A one-goal lead in the last two minutes. The last seconds of a period. The tail of a long shift with no legs left. Or a far better opponent. Nobody serious plays it all game.

PLAYER: And if we're the ones facing it?

COACH: A hard chip past the flat line, and a race for it. Nobody in that line is deeper than anyone else, so it's a straight footrace. And if their goalie comes out for it, pull up. A long pass to a player already behind the wall doesn't work, because that player would be offside. Offside means an attacker crossing the blue line into the other team's end ahead of the puck.

COACH: Thirteenth idea. Choosing a forecheck by the game. This is your coach's decision. But you should understand the logic, because you'll be told to switch in the middle of a game, often with just a shout of "back off". So, you're one goal up with three minutes left. Where would you set the dial?

PLAYER: Down. Sit back.

COACH: Right, down to a containing shape. One more goal is worth little, and giving one up costs a lot. Trailing late, turn it up, to a two-one-two and then the press. Now giving one up costs almost nothing. Tied early, play your team's normal system. There's no reason to change it. Against a defence that panics, two-player pressure makes turnovers straight away. When your line is tired, go passive. A passive shape is far cheaper to skate, so survive to a whistle or a change. And straight after a long shift in your own end, don't chase. Get the puck deep and get off the ice.

PLAYER: Is there anything coaches argue about?

COACH: One case, and you'll hear both answers. That's a defenceman who can really pass the puck. The case for pressure is that a good passer needs time. If you take the time away, the skill never gets used. That suits fast, fit forwards, or a team chasing the game. The case for containing is that pressure gives a really good passer what they want, a committed forechecker with space behind. One pass past your F one, and you're worse off than if you'd never gone. That suits a team that can't be sure of getting there first, or is tired, or is protecting a lead.

PLAYER: So who's right?

COACH: The tiebreaker isn't the opponent. It's you. Pressure only beats a good passer if you actually arrive before the pass. Ask which your bench wants, because the two send F one on opposite routes.

PLAYER: Let me try the systems. A system is a shape plus answers, so I ask what we do when they wheel it. F one makes the play predictable, F two closes the escape route with the stick, and F three stays high. The press and the two-one-two hunt the puck and risk what's behind them. The lock keeps three back. The one-two-two and the one-three-one build a wall, and the one-four retreats.

COACH: And the two things that go with every one of them. Stick on the puck, not a hit, and if you see their numbers, pull up. And know which system your team plays, and the one it switches to with a lead.

COACH: Fourteenth idea. A dump-in, a turnover and a set play behind their net are three different starts. Most players forecheck all three the same way. They shouldn't. Start with the dump-in. You control the clock. You chose when the puck went in, and you know where it's going. So you can get there early, at full speed on the way in. Then the same rule as always. Feet under you for the last two or three strides, able to stop, because the player you're arriving on has their back to you.

PLAYER: When should I start skating?

COACH: Before the puck does. Whoever will be F one should be speeding up as the dump is released, not after it. Players throw that head start away all the time by stopping to watch the puck.

COACH: And aim the dump at a person, not at an area. Put it where their defenceman has to fetch it facing their own end boards. Then remember what you've made. That player's back is to you. Take the puck or the ice, never their back. Stick on the puck, not a hit. Whether you may hit at all depends on your league, your age group and the game, so find out first. And if you see their numbers, pull up.

COACH: Because you know where the puck is going, F one can take the puck, and F two can go early to the passing lane instead of following into the corner. F three and the defencemen can be more aggressive too, because your team is organised and coming forward together. There are several kinds of dump-in, and the timing of the chase changes with each one. So your line should agree which one is coming.

COACH: One more limit on a dump-in. On an obvious icing or offside, when the linesperson already has an arm up, take the puck or the ice and let them have the wall. In almost every league, contact that puts them into the boards there gets called as boarding.

COACH: Fifteenth idea. Where to aim the dump-in. In top professional men's hockey and international hockey, there's a shape painted behind each net. It's called the trapezoid. It's narrow at the goal line and wider at the end boards. Their goalie may play the puck behind the goal line only inside it. So, there's a trapezoid. Where would you dump it?

PLAYER: Straight behind the net, so it comes round?

COACH: That's the one place not to. Behind the net is inside the trapezoid, so their goalie is allowed to play it there and will be waiting. A dump straight down the middle is a gift. The goalie stops it, sets it up for a defenceman, and your forecheck has done nothing. So dump into the corners. A rim that dies in a corner is the best dump of all where there's a trapezoid. Their goalie may not play it there, so a defenceman has to skate over and fetch it, facing the boards. Their goalie may still touch it, and you still keep off them. When you get there, go in on an angle, and take the puck or the ice, never their back.

COACH: A dump into the far corner, away from where their defencemen are set up, adds a second or two of travel for them. And watch the goalie's stick side. Many goalies are much better on their forehand, so a dump to their backhand side buys you time.

PLAYER: And if there's no trapezoid?

COACH: Many leagues have none. Look for the lines behind the net, and ask. In Britain, in any competition, plan for their goalie to come out for the puck anyway. Without a trapezoid, a goalie who handles the puck well becomes a third defenceman, and dump-and-chase loses most of its value. So change the plan. Carry the puck in. Or dump it where the goalie has to leave the net completely. Or forecheck the pass, not the corner, by putting F one on the defenceman waiting for the goalie's pass.

COACH: And don't forecheck expecting a whistle when their goalie falls on the puck out there. The harder you close on them, the more likely their freeze is allowed. And a freeze you think was illegal still stops play. If you skate on into that goalie, you're the one who gets penalised.

COACH: Sixteenth idea. Their goalie out of the net is someone to chase down, never someone to hit. Picture it. No trapezoid. You've rimmed the puck, and their goalie has come all the way out to the corner and turned to the boards to stop it. You're arriving at speed. What would you do?

PLAYER: Do I go and win it off them? They're out of their net.

COACH: Chase the puck, not the goalie. Close the goalie down so they have to move it quickly. Put your stick in the lane they want to pass into. Then pull up before you arrive. The crease is the goalie's painted area in front of the net, and a goalie outside it is never fair game, in any league. A goalie who's turned to the boards for a rimmed puck can't see you, and can't protect themselves. A hit there can hurt them. It's a hit from behind like any other, and it can get you thrown out of the game. Unnecessary contact with a goalie is a penalty in every league. Charging into a goalie can get you thrown out of the game, and in at least one league that's automatic, wherever on the ice it happens. In some leagues any deliberate contact with a goalie at all, in the crease or out of it, is a penalty, and the big version throws you out. So the habit is simple, and it's the same everywhere. Take the pass away, and keep off the goalie.

COACH: Seventeenth idea. Off a turnover, you're recovering, not hunting. When you lose the puck in their end, you're out of shape and they're not. Somebody is out of position, and at least one of you is skating the wrong way. For about two seconds, they have the numbers.

PLAYER: So everyone goes after it to win it straight back?

COACH: The closest player does, whoever that is. Stick on the puck, not a hit, and if you see their numbers, pull up. This is the clearest case of F one being a job, not a person. Two seconds of pressure buys the rest of your team time to sort themselves out.

COACH: Everyone else's first job is to find their place in the shape, not the puck. The instinct after a turnover is to chase and win it straight back. The right instinct is to look where your teammates are, and take the empty job. A second and third chaser after a turnover is how a lost puck becomes a goal against. Don't pinch off a turnover, unless the puck is really dying on the wall. You don't have the shape behind you that a dump-in gives you. And accept the smaller win. Slow them down, and collect the puck in the neutral zone.

COACH: Eighteenth idea. When they set it up behind their net, your team chooses. This is the third start, and the one nobody practises. Their defenceman simply has the puck stopped behind their own net, head up, with their forwards starting to move. It happens all the time, off line changes and whenever they win a race you thought you had. Here your team picks one of three answers.

COACH: The first is the deep trap. All five of you back off and meet them at the blue line. You give them time, space and speed through the build-up, and take it all away in one place instead. The turnovers come at the line.

COACH: The second is to swing with them. A team breaking out with control usually sends one or two players swinging deep behind the net or through the corner, to build speed. So F one stays in to put off the pass, while F two and F three skate with the players swinging deep. Shadow them. Never hit them. None of them has the puck. If their defenceman keeps the puck and comes out, F one angles in, stick on the puck, and forces them to one side. Whether you may hit at all depends on your league, your age group and the game, so find out first.

PLAYER: What's the cost of swinging with them?

COACH: It kills their speed, but it opens the long pass, so your defencemen have to watch their high forward the whole time.

COACH: The third is the quick flush. Attack before they're organised, because a team that has just stopped behind its net hasn't decided who goes where yet. F one forces the carrier to one side, ideally onto their backhand. Once F one has committed, F two comes in on an angle and doubles it. F three and both defencemen drop off to take away the longer passes. This is the only one of the three aimed at winning the puck outright, and it commits the most players before the puck is won. On a flush, you're both arriving on a player facing their own end boards. So this is angling, not hitting. Stick on the puck, not a hit. Whether you may hit at all depends on your league, your age group and the game, so find out first. Never finish into their back, and if you see their numbers, pull up.

PLAYER: So which of the three is right?

COACH: That's a coaching choice. It tends to follow the rest of your system. A team that runs a one-two-two is the likelier one to trap, and a team that runs a two-one-two the likelier one to flush, though nobody has counted that. Ask which yours does, because all three start with F one doing something different.

PLAYER: Let me try the three starts. Off a dump-in, I'm hunting, so I leave before the puck does and aim it at a person. Off a turnover, I'm recovering, so the closest player pressures and everyone else finds their place. Off a set play behind their net, we're choosing. Trap, swing or flush.

COACH: Spot on. And where there's a trapezoid, dump into the corners. Where there isn't, plan for the goalie to come out. And chase their goalie down, but never hit them. Keep off the goalie.

COACH: Nineteenth idea. Defencemen, you decide whether a failed forecheck costs anything. Forwards get most of the attention on a forecheck. But if you hold the zone, a failed forecheck just becomes a second forecheck. If you get it wrong, it becomes two against one going the other way.

PLAYER: Where should I be standing?

COACH: At or just inside their blue line, with your feet moving, on your toes. Standing still is the mistake. If you're standing still when a rim comes round, you can't reach it, and you can't get back either. Small steps forward and back keep you ready for both. And if you keep one skate touching the line, blade on the ice, you're onside in every league. That's why a defenceman can keep one heel on the line and still reach a long way into the zone.

COACH: Now when to pinch. Picture a rim coming round the boards toward you. Their winger is sliding down the wall to meet it. Do you go?

PLAYER: If I can get there?

COACH: That's nearly it. Pinch when you can arrive on contact. That means you and the puck reach the wall at the same moment, so you can play it. The coaching test is simple. If you can't arrive on contact, don't go. Play the puck, not a hit. Whether you may hit at all depends on your league, your age group and the game, so find out first. And if you see their numbers, pull up. If nobody is contesting the puck, you need to get there clearly first, not probably first. Coming second there just means you turned your back on the play.

COACH: Pinch when the puck is on the wall and slowing down, not zipping past you. And pinch only when F three is high and ready to come back and cover the point you're leaving. That swap is what makes pinching safe. You go down, F three comes back. If F three is in the corner, the swap isn't there.

PLAYER: And when shouldn't I?

COACH: Don't pinch when you'd be reaching for it, or when you'd have to turn your back to the play. Don't pinch when they already have speed coming through the middle, or when you're protecting a lead late. And don't pinch when you've already been beaten once on that shift. The second gamble is how one mistake becomes two.

COACH: Twentieth idea. Only one defenceman pinches at a time. At five on five, in every forecheck we've talked about, the usual approach is that only one of you goes. It's a very widely taught coaching choice, not a law of hockey. If nobody has told you otherwise, play on it. The weak-side defenceman, the one away from the puck, holds the middle of the ice and doesn't join in. So look for your partner before you go. If your partner is already down the wall, you're the safety.

PLAYER: Why does it matter so much?

COACH: The defenceman who stays home is the reason a failed pinch is two against one, and not a breakaway. With both of you down, there's nobody in the middle at all. Both of you go only when the bench calls it. That means a full-ice press, a delayed penalty against them, or with your own goalie pulled. Every one of those is a decision to accept a breakaway as the price.

COACH: There is a realistic alternative. Some teams run a five-player rotation, or give both defencemen a standing green light in their end. Then whichever forward is highest covers the line. That's a real system, not a mistake. So ask which yours is, before you decide your partner is out of position.

COACH: And ask who covers the point you leave when you pinch. Usually F three comes back to it. If nobody does, a beaten pinch turns into a rush against, with you below the puck and the line empty. Some teams slide the other defenceman across instead, and accept a hole on the far side. Some only pinch once F three has called that they're high. Ask which of the three your team runs.

COACH: Twenty-first idea. What you do when you get to the wall. First, find out whether you may body check in this game at all, because it depends on your league, your age group and the game. Where checking is barred, a pinch is a play on the puck, not a check.

PLAYER: And where it's allowed, what do I do with the winger?

COACH: Second, take the spot on the wall, not the player. Get to the spot first, in front of the winger and moving their way, and play the puck. Hold a spot you reached first with your position, never with your body driven into them. No shove, no lean, no hip, no arm round them, and never your stick across them. A hip into them makes it a check. Never use the boards to push, pin or lean them out of the play. Never into anyone's back, and if you can see their numbers, pull up. Never lift anyone off their feet. Even going slowly, a crash into the boards can hurt. In some leagues even holding a spot means giving way rather than taking contact you could avoid, so ask yours.

COACH: An arm wrapped round a player to pin them on the boards is holding, whether your league allows checking or not. And keep your skates out of it. In at least one league, pushing off a player with your skate gets you thrown out of the game.

PLAYER: What if the winger gets there just before me?

COACH: Third, never cut across into the winger. Stepping down off the line, you're moving sideways, and the winger often doesn't have the puck yet. Cutting across into them can be a penalty in every league, and it can climb to getting you thrown out. So get to the spot first, and play the puck. Fourth, if you end up behind them, play the puck. Never into their back. Even a light push from behind into the boards can be a penalty.

COACH: Fifth, don't be late. Once the puck has gone, the window is narrow, and in at least one league there's no window at all. The practical test is the gap, not the clock. If the puck was already more than an arm and a stick length from you when it left, you were late. Pull up and take the lane instead. And deciding to hit after the puck has gone is late, whatever the gap. No league caps a late hit at two minutes.

COACH: Sixth, never pinch in to check a player who's already battling your teammate. Take the puck or the ice instead. In at least one league that hit is a penalty on its own, wherever it happens.

COACH: A pinch takes you to the wall, so the boards position is yours too, in every league, checking or not. Contact still happens in non-check hockey, and the boards are just as hard. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to anyone coming at you, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly.

PLAYER: Let me try the defencemen. Feet moving on the line, one skate touching it. Pinch only when I can arrive on contact, with the puck slowing on the wall and F three high. One of us at a time, unless the bench calls it, and know who covers my point.

COACH: And at the wall?

PLAYER: Take the spot, not the player. No arms, no hip, no skates, never cut across, never late, and never onto a teammate's battle. And the boards position. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of my shoulder, head up, chin off my chest, never my back to anyone coming at me, never duck.

COACH: Twenty-second idea. Reading the forecheck that's coming at you. You'll spend as much time breaking a forecheck as running one, so learn to spot which one you face. When your defenceman touches the puck behind your net, look at three things. First, count their forwards below the top of the circles. Two or more means an aggressive system, so expect contact now. One means containment, so expect space now and a wall later.

COACH: Second, watch their F one. Straight at the puck at speed means they want it. They've committed, so a reverse or a quick D-to-D pass beats them. Curving to one side and slowing down means they're steering you. They want you to go one way. Going that way walks into their trap, so change sides before you commit.

COACH: Third, watch their far-side defenceman. Standing inside their blue line with feet moving means they plan to pinch. A rim past them is your best play, with lots of ice behind. Backing off toward the red line means they're giving up the zone. You have time to make a proper play.

PLAYER: Anything else to read?

COACH: One more tell, one pass later. After a D-to-D pass, watch what happens. If a new forechecker goes straight at the defenceman who just got it, they're running an aggressive system that rotates, and D-to-D won't save you. If their whole shape just shifts across and keeps its distance, it's containment, and D-to-D is a free reset you can use again and again.

COACH: Now what to do about each. Against an aggressive forecheck, move the puck early, before the pressure arrives, not after. A reverse beats a forechecker who has over-committed. A rim beats one who has closed you in along the boards. A rim or chip that gets past their point is worth more than a pretty pass. And wingers, come underneath the pressure to take the pass, rather than standing still on the wall.

PLAYER: Early's fine when I see it coming. But sometimes it's on me before I've looked, and I just fire it anywhere.

COACH: That's a panic pass, a pass made without looking, just to get rid of the puck. For a player still learning, panic is normal, and it spends time you actually had. Every rushed pass and every puck flipped to nobody had more time than it felt. "Be braver" isn't something you can act on. Get one more look before the puck reaches you, so you know the answer before it arrives.

COACH: Breaking a forecheck happens in your own end, and in your own end you never hold it. Holding it there is how the turnover happens. One look, one decision, move it. Whenever you do have to protect it for a moment, anywhere on the ice, stay side-on, with your shoulder to the forechecker and the puck on the far side of your body. Never your back to anyone coming at you. And never pass the puck, or skate it, across the front of your own net. A puck lost there is a shot against. If a teammate calls "time", it means nobody is close, so believe them, because they can see what you can't. And when you're the one calling it, be sure. If you're not sure, say nothing, because a false "time" sends a teammate into a hit.

COACH: Against a containing forecheck, stop trying to force it. Shift their wall with D-to-D passes and calm regroups. A regroup is passing it back to reset and come again. Then chip the puck past their line and race for it, and if their goalie comes out for it, pull up. Or bank a pass off the boards to a teammate already behind it. The one thing that never works is skating into the middle of three players.

COACH: Breaking a forecheck takes you down the wall too. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to anyone coming at you, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly. Protecting the puck never justifies breaking any of those.

COACH: Twenty-third idea, and it's short. At rec level, simpler is better. If you play adult rec hockey, run the forecheck everybody can remember late on a weeknight. A one-two-two is usually the right default there. That's a suggestion for your league, not a claim about what most teams play. It's forgiving, nobody has to sprint, and when it fails you just don't get the puck back. You don't give up a breakaway. If your team can keep up a two-one-two, run it.

PLAYER: And if we can't even agree on a system?

COACH: If your line agrees only that one goes, one supports and one stays high, you have most of what a real system gives you. The habit that matters most isn't a shape. It's that only one defenceman goes, unless the bench has called an all-out press. Coaches say both defencemen going at once is where most rec-league odd-man rushes come from. Nobody counts that. Take it as the habit worth drilling.

COACH: And be honest about fitness. An aggressive forecheck is a fitness decision as much as a tactical one. Run the shape your legs can hold all game, keep your shifts short, and save two-player pressure for when it pays. A two-one-two for ten minutes and nothing after is worse than a one-two-two for the whole game.

COACH: Let's pull it all together. Pressure and risk are the same dial. Know where your team sets it, and what it switches to with a lead. F one, F two and F three go by who gets there first, and you say which one you are. When you're beaten, take the empty job instead of chasing.

COACH: Skate at the space, not the puck, on a curve, with your stick in the lane. Steer, don't finish. Get there early, then arrive under control. Never finish into the back of a player facing the boards. Your own dump-in put them there. Take the puck or the ice, and if you see their numbers, pull up. Find out whether you may check in this game at all, because it depends on your league, your age group and the game.

COACH: Know your system's answers, not just its shape. Off a dump-in, hunt. Off a turnover, recover. Off a set play, choose. Dump into the corners where there's a trapezoid, plan for the goalie to come out where there isn't, and keep off the goalie, every time.

COACH: Defencemen, pinch only when you can arrive on contact, with F three high, one of you at a time. Take the spot on the wall, not the player. And at the wall, every time. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to anyone coming at you, never duck.

PLAYER: A lot of this depends on what my team plays, doesn't it?

COACH: It does. So here are three questions for your coach, and one for your league. Which forecheck do we run, and which one do we switch to with a lead? Which way does F one angle, and what do we do when they wheel it? Who covers the point when a defenceman pinches? And for the league. Which rules do we play under, and can we body check? Their answers tell you how your team wants you to forecheck, and now you know why each answer makes sense.

### 3. The panic-passing passage (twenty-second idea)

Placed where the player is breaking a forecheck, which is where a panic pass happens. Sources: panic pass defined as in the passing v2 model and Defender v2; "panic is normal… spends time you actually had", "every rushed pass and puck flipped to nobody had more time than it felt", "be braver isn't something you can act on", "one more look before you receive" (time_and_space :613–:615, :634–:638); in your own end never hold it, one look, one decision, move it (T&S :615, :639); side-on, shoulder to the checker, puck on the far side, never your back (puck_handling :34; the passing v2 F1 wording); never pass or skate it across the front of your own net (core_principles :25); trust "time"; be sure, say nothing if not sure, a false "time" sends a teammate into a hit (T&S :640, Comm :641).

⚠️ **Scope check:** the time_and_space "when you don't know, turn away from pressure" advice is not voiced, because breaking a forecheck is in your own end and its scope word ("except in your own end") would sit next to "never your back". The first draft did put "Except in your own end" straight after "Never your back to anyone coming at you"; it was rewritten before the dry run so the own-end rule comes first and the side-on line says "anywhere on the ice".
