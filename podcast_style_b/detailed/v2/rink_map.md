COACH: One more for the middle. Know your bench side. Both teams' benches are on the same side of the ice, opposite the penalty boxes. So one half of the ice is the bench side, and the other is the far side.

PLAYER: Does it really matter which half I'm in?

COACH: It's a real tactical fact, not trivia. Changing is safe on the bench side. On the far side, lines get caught. Picture this. You're on the far side, your legs are gone, and the puck's still in the middle. What would you do?

PLAYER: Sprint for the bench?

COACH: Stay in position and finish the shift. A bad change is worse than a long shift.

COACH: When you do change, the player coming off has to be at the bench and out of the play before the next one steps on. If not, it's a bench penalty for too many players on the ice. Nothing is painted to show how close counts, and the distance varies. So get all the way to the bench before your teammate jumps on.

PLAYER: Does bench side come up anywhere else?

COACH: At faceoffs. There are nine faceoff spots. One at centre ice, four in the neutral zone with no circles, and two with circles in each end. Players name the end-zone ones by end and side, like D-zone draw, bench side, or O-zone draw, far side. Your bench side decides who can change.

COACH: The hash marks keep players apart on a draw, and this is the part that catches people out. If a winger creeps over the hash marks before the puck drops, it's usually the centre who gets thrown out of the faceoff, not the winger.

COACH: Let me pull the middle together. Keep somebody above the puck, unless your team has chosen to send everyone. Puck over the blue line before both your skates, and drag your back skate, don't lift it. Get it over the red line on your stick before you dump it in. Gain ice north-south, and break their shape east-west. Where checking is barred, steer with your skating and your stick, never a bump. Change on the bench side, and get all the way to the bench before your teammate jumps on.

COACH: Now their end. Hold the point, and don't mix it up with the half-wall. Once you're set up in their end, the blue line is where the defencemen live. The point is the area just inside the blue line, near each set of boards. It's an area, not the line itself. There are two, the strong-side point on the puck side, and the weak-side point.

PLAYER: What's the point player actually doing up there?

COACH: From the point, a defenceman can shoot for tips and rebounds, slide along the line to open a shooting lane, or pass down to either half-wall. A pass from the corner or the half-wall out to the point is called low-to-high. It sets up most point shots.

PLAYER: And the half-wall?

COACH: The half-wall is the boards about level with the faceoff dot, between the corner and the point. You'll also hear it called the half-boards. Wingers live there when their team has the puck. From there you can see the point, the slot, the corner and the front of the net, and every one of those passes is open to you. Coaches call it the most important spot in their end that you don't score from. That's coaching observation, not a measured ranking.

COACH: Don't mix up the point and the half-wall. They're a long way apart. On an NHL-sized rink the half-wall is a little under forty-four feet from the point. A defenceman told to hold the point stays just inside the blue line. One who drifts down to the half-wall has left the line.

PLAYER: What does holding the line mean, exactly?

COACH: Keeping pucks in at the blue line. A puck that slides back out into the neutral zone means the attack has to start again. But don't get caught inside. If you can't hold the line, get out and reset. Holding the line is stick on the puck, not a hit. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call, and if you can see their numbers, pull up.

COACH: Next. Defencemen, step down only as far as you can get back from. Picture three depths in their end. Just inside the blue line is normal. The top of the circle, the edge of the faceoff circle nearest the blue line, is more active, and you can still get back. Below the circles is a commitment. Down there you're a fourth forward, and you can't get back in time.

PLAYER: So when can I go below the circles?

COACH: Only when a forward has taken the blue line for you before you went.

PLAYER: Why do coaches always say the top of the circle, specifically?

COACH: Because it's a clear, limited instruction. You know exactly how far to go, and you know you can recover from there.

COACH: Next, behind their net, and the far side of their net. Behind their net is a different place from behind yours. Commentators call it the office. The name comes from Wayne Gretzky, who set up there.

PLAYER: What's so good about it?

COACH: The defence can't easily pressure a player behind the net without opening up the slot. And a carrier back there can come out on either side, so the defence has to commit. Then there's the backdoor. That's the front of the net on the weak side, away from the puck. A player arriving there is behind the goalie's view, which is what makes it dangerous. How it ranks against every other open space, nobody has counted.

COACH: At the net front, live just outside the paint. Picture yourself heading to their net to set a screen. Where exactly do you stand?

PLAYER: As close as I can get.

COACH: Close, but not in the crease. Stay out of the paint, but get as close to it as you can, because the ice just outside it is where tips, screens and rebounds happen. And keep your skates off the red line round the crease, not just out of the blue.

PLAYER: The red line too?

COACH: Most rulebooks count that red line as part of the crease. The rest don't say it clearly. Off the line is the only ice that's safe wherever you play. It matters because a skate in the crease can cost your team the goal. In some leagues no goal counts while an attacker is in the crease, whoever scored it. In others the goal only counts if the player in the crease isn't blocking the goalie's view. Blocking the view is exactly what a screen does. So either way, a skate on the line can wipe out your teammate's goal.

COACH: So plant yourself just outside the line, not on it. A clean screen is fine in every league. That means clear of the crease and its red line, in the goalie's line of sight, with no contact.

COACH: Keep your head up there, chin off your chest. A defender can move you while your eyes are on the puck, and a goal post can hurt you the same way the boards can. If you're driving to the net, come in under control, and stop short of the goalie.

PLAYER: What if I bump the goalie by accident?

COACH: Any contact with a goalie can be a penalty, and it can get you thrown out of the game. A goalie who is down or turned can't protect themselves, so contact can hurt them. And contact can cost your team the goal as well. Deliberate or unnecessary contact with a goalie is a penalty everywhere, in the crease or out of it, goal or no goal. Some leagues let a touch go if it happened while the goalie was playing the puck, it was truly accidental, and you tried hard to avoid it. Others call even an accidental touch. So do everything you can not to touch the goalie at all.

PLAYER: And once the goalie's out of the crease? Fair game then?

COACH: No. A goalie outside the crease is not fair game. Play the puck, not the goalie, wherever the goalie is. And building up speed into contact, even with two hard strides, can be charging. A charge on a goalie can get you thrown out of the game in every league, and in some it always does. It can mean a ban for games after this one, too.

PLAYER: What about when the goalie comes out behind the net for a puck?

COACH: The same holds behind the net and out in the corners. You can still go for a puck the goalie is playing out there, with your stick, never with your body. So play the puck.

COACH: One more thing at the net front. When their goalie has come out to play the puck, clear the way back to the net. Do it even if you never touch them. Skating into the path of a goalie who doesn't have the puck can be interference, even with no contact at all. Usually that's two minutes. A violent or reckless one can cost a major and get you thrown out, and trying to hurt someone gets you thrown out of the game everywhere.

COACH: Next, forechecking. F one, F two and F three are jobs, not people. A forecheck is when your forwards pressure the other team in its own end, to win the puck back rather than skate back and wait. F one is the first forward to the puck, and pressures it. F two is the second forward in. F two supports F one and closes off the way out that F one has taken away. F three is the third forward, who stays higher, as the safety, and covers the rush back.

PLAYER: So our centre's always F one?

COACH: No. The order of arrival decides the job, not your position. Say your centre is usually first in, but tonight a winger gets there first. Then the winger is F one, and the centre takes the support job. Nobody swaps positions. You swap jobs. And it can be somebody different every time down the ice.

PLAYER: What goes wrong if we mix it up?

COACH: Teams that treat F one as the centre's job end up with two players chasing and nobody high. And if two players both think they're F two, nobody pressures the puck. So take the job your order of arrival gives you. You'll also hear third forward high. That's the same F three job, said from the other direction, the forward who stays above the puck. How high F three sits is your coach's choice, so ask.

COACH: Pressure means stick on the puck, not a hit. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call. Never hit a player who can't see you coming, and never from behind, because they can't protect themselves. If you can see their numbers, pull up and play the puck. And building up speed into contact, even with two hard strides, can be charging.

COACH: Next. Where there's a trapezoid, dump the puck into the corners outside it. The trapezoid is a shape painted behind each net in some leagues. It's narrow at the goal line and wider at the end boards. On an NHL rink it's twenty-two feet wide at the goal line and twenty-eight at the boards. Where it's used, a goalie who plays the puck behind the goal line outside it takes a two-minute penalty. The one exception is a goalie who keeps a skate touching the crease while playing it.

PLAYER: Does every league have one?

COACH: No, and this difference changes how you play. The NHL and international hockey have one. The main amateur rulebooks in the USA and Canada don't. In the PWHL, play as if it counts. So find out which rulebook your league plays under. Where there's no trapezoid, expect the other goalie to come out and play your dump-in.

PLAYER: And in Britain?

COACH: Britain plays the international rulebook, so keep your own goalie inside it. But England Ice Hockey's rules say it isn't currently enforced, and it's unclear how far that reaches. So in Britain, keep your own goalie inside it, and outside the Elite League, expect theirs to come out. Ask your league.

PLAYER: Why does it matter to a skater, though?

COACH: It's why dump-and-chase works. That means shooting the puck deep into their end and chasing it, instead of carrying it in. Where the trapezoid applies, their goalie can't calmly collect a puck rimmed into the corner. A defenceman has to go and get it, with your forechecker arriving. So aim your dump-ins away from the trapezoid, into the corners.

COACH: Chase the puck, not the player. Stick on the puck, not a hit, and whether you may hit at all is your league's call. If you can see their numbers, pull up. And if their goalie comes out for it, play the puck, never the goalie. Any contact with a goalie can be a penalty and can get you thrown out of the game.

PLAYER: And when they're the ones dumping it into our end?

COACH: Then the trapezoid turns round. It's your defencemen's problem, not your goalie's. Go into the corner expecting no help from your goalie. Play the puck, not the player. Whether anyone may hit at all is your league's call, and if you can see their numbers, pull up. And the boards position. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to anyone coming at you, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly.

PLAYER: Anything else for goalies?

COACH: Two more things. No trapezoid doesn't mean a goalie can do anything behind the net. Playing the puck there may be free. Sitting on it is not. Freezing the puck behind the goal line, outside the crease, is a two-minute penalty everywhere, trapezoid or not, even after a save. Play it with your stick. Out in front, coming out to cut down the angle, making the save and then covering the puck is legal in most rulebooks, so ask about yours.

COACH: And keep your goalie on their own side of the centre red line. A goalie who joins the play beyond it takes a two-minute penalty. Some rulebooks judge that by the goalie's skates, not by where the puck is. So keep the skates on your own side, even when reaching for a puck that's still on your side.

COACH: Let me pull their end together. Hold the point just inside the blue line, and don't drift down to the half-wall. If you can't hold the line, get out. Step down to the top of the circle, and below it only when a forward has your line.

PLAYER: And at the net front, live outside the paint and off its red line, head up, chin off my chest, and stop short of the goalie. Any contact with a goalie can be a penalty and can get me thrown out. When their goalie comes out, clear the way back.

COACH: Right. Whoever gets to the puck first is F one, and pressure means stick on the puck, not a hit. Where there's a trapezoid, dump it into the corners outside it. And if you do go into the boards after it, the boards position. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to anyone coming at you, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly. Stick on the puck, not a hit, and never push or hit anyone from behind.

COACH: Last one. Look at the rink you're actually on. The measurements in rulebooks describe rinks built to those rulebooks. A lot of community, recreational and older rinks weren't. That's general observation, not a counted figure, but it's enough to tell you not to assume.

PLAYER: Is that just a problem at small local rinks?

COACH: In Britain it isn't just a rec-rink problem. The governing body's own rules allow rinks shorter and narrower than the international minimum, at every level. So if you play in Britain, assume your rink is smaller than every diagram you've seen. Playing international rules tells you which penalties get called. It tells you nothing about the size of the ice.

PLAYER: How does the size change the way I play?

COACH: Start with wide international ice. The corners are bigger and further from the net. A rimmed puck is a longer chase and a longer pass back to the middle, so dump-and-chase pays less. The half-wall is further from the slot too. A pass from the wall to the middle takes longer, and that gives defenders time to close. So play in their end gets more patient and stays more round the outside.

COACH: Steering an attacker to the boards no longer finishes the play, because they still have room to keep skating. So your gap and your stick matter more. And getting back across the ice takes longer, so late attackers arrive more freely. The neutral zone stays about the same length. What changes is the width. So a neutral-zone trap that squeezes the width is much harder to run.

PLAYER: And coming back to a narrow rink?

COACH: It all reverses. You have far less time on the wall, and quick simple plays beat clever slow ones. Smaller rinks change things too. On a short sheet the neutral zone shrinks, so long passes and regroups mostly stop working. On a narrow sheet everything happens faster and closer.

COACH: So picture your first shift at a rink you've never played in. What would you check?

PLAYER: How the boards bounce, maybe?

COACH: That's one of them. Check three things on purpose. How far it is from the goal line to the end boards. Whether the trapezoid is painted. And how the puck comes off the corner boards on a rim. Those three answers change your breakout. If the trapezoid isn't painted, that's a strong hint your league isn't enforcing it, but only a hint, because the rule turns on where the puck is, not on the paint. And learn your home rink's dead spots, the places in the boards that kill a rim. They're a real advantage.

COACH: Let me pull it all together.

COACH: Say our end and their end. Read high, low, strong side and weak side from your team and the puck, not from the paint.

COACH: Win the slot. In your own end, know who covers it and which layer is yours. In theirs, get a puck and a body there together, under control, short of the goalie, head up, chin off your chest. Shoot from inside home plate, and make them shoot from outside it.

COACH: Be inside, force them outside, with your feet and your position. Stick on the puck, not a hit. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call. Never hit anyone who can't see you coming, and if you see their numbers, pull up.

COACH: In your own end, go behind the net, never through the slot, and never pass the puck or skate it across the front of your own net. Under pressure there, one look, one decision, move it. Clear it off the glass, not over it. Keep somebody above the puck. Puck over the blue line before your skates, and drag your back skate, don't lift it.

COACH: At the net front, live outside the paint and off its red line. Stay off the goalie, wherever the goalie is, because any contact can get you thrown out. Whoever gets to the puck first is F one, and pressure means stick on the puck, not a hit. Where there's a trapezoid, dump it into the corners outside it, and chase the puck, not the player. And look at the rink before you trust the diagram.

PLAYER: What should I ask my own coach?

COACH: Three things. What system do we play in our own end, and who has the slot and the goalmouth? Do we pass D-to-D behind our net, or high in the zone? And when do we give up having somebody above the puck?

PLAYER: And my league?

COACH: Three more. Which rulebook do we play under? Can we check? And is the trapezoid enforced here? Ask, because these are your team's and your league's choices, not things the map can tell you. The answers tell you where to stand, and now you know why each place matters.
