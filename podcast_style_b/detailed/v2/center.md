And you're the passer when you come out from behind the net, because a pass from there up to the slot crosses the road almost every time. So the pass to refuse is the extra one along your own side.

COACH: If defenders close in, find a winger on the half-wall or in the circle, or a defenceman at the point. The half-wall is the stretch of boards roughly level with the faceoff dot, between the corner and the point. It's your wingers' ice, not yours. Keep moving, and keep the puck moving.

COACH: Now a moment that catches centres out. Your defenceman pinches, which means stepping down the boards to keep the puck in. What would you do?

PLAYER: Stay in the slot, in case it comes to me.

COACH: Then if they lose it, you've left an odd-man rush going the other way. Somebody has to fill the point they left, and in many systems that's you. Slide up to the blue line on their side and play defence until they're back. Some teams give that job to a winger, so ask who covers the pinch.

COACH: Sixteenth idea. Under pressure down low, and the cycle. Picture yourself in the corner of their end with the puck, and a defender on you.

PLAYER: Protect it.

COACH: With your body. Side-on to them, with the puck on the far side of your body, never your back to them. Look for a quick pass to a winger on the half-wall, or to the defenceman at the point. Or go behind their net and use it as a pivot. Come out the other side, or find a teammate arriving late.

PLAYER: Why behind the net?

COACH: From there you can see the whole ice, and a turnover there isn't a shot against you. And down here, the boards position. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to anyone coming at you, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly. If you end up facing the boards anyway, stick and gloves up and spread on the glass, head off it. Behind the net it matters twice over, because the goal post is right there.

COACH: Cycling is how you keep the puck low in their end until the slot opens. Your line rotates around the corner, the half-wall and behind the net, passing short and protecting the puck. The usual version is to leave the puck on the boards behind you for a teammate coming in low, then rotate up into the space they just left.

PLAYER: What's the point of going round and round?

COACH: The defenders are skating without the puck. They can't change, and as they tire they shrink in toward it. That opens the slot you want to arrive in. Four reads make it work.

COACH: First, rim or reverse, and the checker tells you which. If the checker is still in front of you, rim it on past them. If they've gone past you, reverse it into the space they left. Second, bump it off the boards rather than onto a stick. Fire the puck at a spot on the boards so it bounces to your teammate. A stick in the passing lane can't block that. But call it, because a bump nobody expects is a turnover.

PLAYER: And when I run out of boards?

COACH: That's the third read. Cut back. Skate back up the way you came. A defender whose weight is going toward the corner can't turn as fast as you can. Make the turn while they're still too far away to reach you. Once they can reach you, it's too late. Go side-on to the boards instead. Never turn so you're facing the wall with them behind you.

COACH: Fourth, the scissor. You and a teammate skate at each other and cross, with the support player going on the outside. Drop it to them or keep it. Defenders in the middle of swapping who they cover are briefly covering nobody. And the cycle has to end in something. A shot from in front, a pass across the middle, or a drive to the net. Three tidy laps and no shot is a win for the defence.

COACH: Seventeenth idea. The front of their net, in more detail. It's three separate skills, not one place to stand. Screening, tips and rebounds.

PLAYER: Screening's just standing in front of the goalie, isn't it?

COACH: Not quite. Stand between the goalie's eyes and the puck, not between the goalie and the net. Those are two different lines. When your defenceman slides along the blue line, that line moves, so slide with them. Chest up and square to the shooter, hips low and knees bent. Stand inside the faceoff dots, about six to eight feet out from the goal line. The crease reaches about six, so live at the far end of that. And before you plant, know where the near post is.

PLAYER: Should I keep moving so they can't tie me up?

COACH: Between shots, yes. But be still at the moment the shot is taken. If you keep shuffling, the goalie gets flashes of the puck through the gaps. Think of a curtain. One that keeps flapping lets the light through. And track the puck, not the shot. Your eyes move, but your chest doesn't. Turning your body to follow the shot un-screens the goalie, and takes you out of the tip and the rebound.

PLAYER: What about tips?

COACH: Keep your blade on the ice. Most pucks you can tip come in low, and a stick off the ice at the net front is doing nothing. You're only changing the puck's direction a little, not shooting it. Angle the blade and give with the puck a touch. A big swing usually misses. Hold your blade out where you want the puck, and shout for it.

PLAYER: And rebounds. When do I go?

COACH: Decide before the shot, not after. Time it by your job. If the net front is your job, be planted there before the shot. If you're driving in from further out, arrive as the shot is released, not well before it. Go early, and a defender has time to tie you up and turn you away from the puck. That split is coaching know-how, not measured. When the rebound comes out, shoot it straight back. Don't settle it, and don't look for a pass. The window is a second or two, while the goalie is still down. If you get tied up, go to the far post. Nobody is covering it.

COACH: Now keeping yourself safe there. You're standing still with your eyes on the puck. So a defender pushing you sideways is someone you feel, not someone you see. And that path has a steel post at one edge and the goalie at the other.

PLAYER: So what do I do about that?

COACH: Know where the near post is before you plant. Keep your head up and your chin off your chest, and never duck. If you meet a post, meet it with an arm or a leg, never your head first. Even going slowly, a post met head first can break your neck. A box-out, where a defender holds you away from the net with their body, walks you along the same path, whatever your league allows. When a defender pushes you, keep your spot, but don't push back or lean on them, and never put your stick across someone. A shove with your stick held in both hands is a cross-check, whatever the blade is doing. And if the goalie moves into you, give ground, even when it costs you the screen.

COACH: Eighteenth idea. The crease, and the goalie. Work just outside the blue paint, and off the red line around it too. In most leagues that line counts as part of the crease. Keep your stick out of the paint as well. In some leagues your stick in the crease, with their goalie in it, takes the goal off, whoever scored.

PLAYER: How close can I get to the goalie?

COACH: Never so tight they can't move. Take the goalie's eyes, never the goalie's movement. And when their goalie comes out to play a puck, leave them their path back. Blocking it can be a penalty with no contact at all, and it can take the goal off. Screen them once they're home.

PLAYER: What if a defender shoves me into the crease?

COACH: Get out at once, around the goalie, not through. Being pushed in only helps you if you make a real effort to avoid the goalie. Every league penalises a player who doesn't try. Inside the blue paint, assume any contact with the goalie costs the goal.

PLAYER: And if I'm going in hard for a loose puck?

COACH: Never go into the goalie, in or out of the crease, slow or fast. On a rebound the goalie is often down or turned, and can't protect themselves. Going into one can hurt them. It's a penalty whether or not the puck goes in, and it can cost your team the goal. Arrive into a goalie with speed, which is charging, and you can be thrown out of the game in every league. In some leagues, if it's reckless, plan on a match penalty and a likely suspension of several games.

PLAYER: Let me try the rest of their end. In the slot, shoot, or pass across the middle. Cover the point when our defenceman pinches. Cycle to open the slot, and make it end in a shot.

COACH: And at the net?

PLAYER: Screen from outside the paint and off the red line, be still at the release, keep my blade on the ice, and decide on the rebound early. Head up, never duck, and never into the goalie.

COACH: That's all of it. Now the parts of the job only a centre does.

COACH: Nineteenth idea. Faceoffs. You're your team's main faceoff taker. Quick guess first. Does winning draws decide games?

PLAYER: Pretty much, I'd have said.

COACH: Most draws barely matter. In top-level hockey, a team's faceoff record explains only a small part of whether it wins. The ones that do matter are in your own end, on special teams, in the last minute, and after an icing. That's when a team fires the puck the length of the ice, untouched. Coaches put those at ten or fifteen a game. Prepare for those, and agree with your linemates beforehand where you're trying to win each one.

PLAYER: Where do I win them to?

COACH: In your own end, back to a defenceman, ideally the one on the boards side, for a quick rim out. A clean loss there puts a set team running a prepared play into your slot, while your five are standing still and facing the wrong way. In their end, win it to a defenceman for a one-timer, a shot taken straight off the pass, or back to a winger in the circle for a quick chance. In the neutral zone, win it clean if you can. If not, a tie-up your wingers can chase down is fine.

PLAYER: And if I can't win it clean?

COACH: Lift or press their blade with yours, over or under it, for an instant. Then play the puck so your wingers can get to it. Stick on stick only. Never on their hands, never a chop, never hook it or hold it down, and never grab it. And keep your hand off the puck altogether until a third player has touched it.

PLAYER: What if I lose it in my own end?

COACH: Stay with their centre. Tie up their stick if you can, stick on stick, for an instant. Then go with them toward the slot, rather than chasing the puck. Take the ice, not the body. That's the usual job, but some teams give the centre a different player, so ask. And study the other centre. Most favour one hand or one way of taking the draw. Vary your own.

COACH: Now staying in the dot, which is your problem more than anyone's. Get low by bending your knees and dropping your hips, not by pushing your head out over the dot. Two centres getting low the wrong way end up helmet to helmet. That can get you thrown off the draw and given a penalty. If the linesperson tells you to move back, move back the first time.

PLAYER: My wingers creep in sometimes.

COACH: And you pay for it. If a linemate creeps into the circle or over the hash marks before the drop, you're the one removed, not them. In some leagues a winger's stick over the line counts too. So warn them before every draw that matters. Be set when the linesperson is ready, and never time your arrival to beat the clock. And agree two back-up takers, not one. In some leagues the player who caused the violation can't take the draw. So if your only back-up is the winger who just crept in, you have no plan.

COACH: Twentieth idea. A puck in the air near your own net. Picture a puck dropping out of the air in front of your net, and you catch it. Now what?

PLAYER: Drop it somewhere safe?

COACH: Knock it straight down where you caught it, and play it with your stick or your skate. Catching it is fine, except at your own faceoff, where your hand stays off the puck until a third player has touched it. Holding it, carrying it, or dropping it somewhere better can be a penalty.

PLAYER: And if it's over the crease?

COACH: It's worse. The crease goes up into the air, as high as the top of the net. So a puck caught above your paint counts as in the crease. Close your hand on it there and it's usually a penalty shot, a free shot alone against your goalie. And with your net empty, it can be a goal given to them. So never cover a puck in your own crease with your hand or your body.

COACH: Twenty-first idea. The power play, when your team has an extra player. The setup most often described as the modern default is the one-three-one, and there you're usually the bumper.

PLAYER: The bumper?

COACH: The middle of the three players across the slot, between the player at the point and the player in front of the net. Be the passing target for both half-walls, shoot quickly, screen the goalie and tip point shots. A penalty kill playing a box has nobody in the middle. So the bumper is the hardest player for them to cover, and that's exactly why a kill often switches to a diamond against you.

PLAYER: And in other setups?

COACH: In an umbrella or an overload you'll usually be low, or on the boards on the puck side. There's no bumper in a true umbrella, so don't go looking for the spot. Ask which your team runs.

COACH: Getting into their zone, you often drop back for the breakout pass and carry it through the neutral zone. Don't skate straight into their defence, and don't dump it into traffic. Both just hand them the puck. Come in with control, carrying it into space or passing it in across the line. Passing in is not better than carrying in. Pick whichever lane is open.

COACH: Twenty-second idea. The penalty kill, when your team is a player short. A kill is four skaters, usually two forwards and two defencemen. Know your shape before the puck drops, and hold it. A box and a diamond ask very different things of you.

PLAYER: Where am I in a box?

COACH: In a box, the standard shape, you're one of the two high players, each covering one point. There's no single top spot, so don't drift into the middle looking for one. In a diamond, used against a one-three-one, there is a single top spot, and it's usually you. Pressure the point, while the middle two take the half-walls and someone accounts for the bumper.

PLAYER: I've heard of a wedge plus one.

COACH: There you're often the plus one, the player working outside the triangle. That job is pressure, not chasing. Get at whoever has the puck. When the puck moves, you stop and drop into the triangle, and the nearest player steps out as the new plus one. Following the puck around the outside is how that system gets beaten. The route you take out to the puck is your coach's choice, so ask.

COACH: Whatever the shape, don't get pulled out of position chasing the puck around the outside. Clear it whenever you get it. In most leagues a team a player short may ice it. But not at every age, and not once the numbers on the ice are even again. So check before you build your kill on it.

PLAYER: What about blocking shots?

COACH: When their defenceman winds up from the point, you may have to get in the shooting lane. How much your team blocks shots at all is a coaching choice. So ask your coach, and ask your goalie what they want.

COACH: If you block, here's the whole of it, and it goes together. Get into the lane before the shot, not sliding in as it comes. Block from close and on your feet, square to the shooter. Head out of the lane. Hands behind your body, with the backs of your gloves out. Stick flat on the ice beside you, never out in front, because a puck off a blade held out in front tends to ride up toward your face. That last part is coaching know-how, not measured. If you do go down, go down toward the shooter, not sideways, with your hands behind you or tucked in behind your shin pads.

PLAYER: Is there anywhere I shouldn't block?

COACH: Two places. Never in front of your own goalie's eyes, where all you do is deflect a puck they never saw. And never on a two-on-one, when you're the only defender back. Your job there is the pass, and your goalie already has the shooter.

PLAYER: What if a blocked shot ends up under me?

COACH: That's fine. But never reach for it with your hand to hold it there. That's a penalty.

COACH: Twenty-third idea. Icing, and line changes. Icing is shooting the puck from your own side of the ice across their goal line without anyone touching it. In most leagues your side means your half. Play stops, and the faceoff comes back to your end.

PLAYER: How do centres give it away?

COACH: With the lazy dump from just behind the centre red line. The fix is cheap. Carry one more stride, so the puck is past the red line on your stick before you let it go. In most leagues the dump is then fine however far it goes. A few leagues split the ice at the blue lines instead, so ask where your league's line is. Or make the dump stop short of their goal line. That works everywhere.

PLAYER: Why does it matter so much to me?

COACH: After your own icing, the faceoff is in your end, and in some leagues your line can't change for it. So a lazy dump means a tired centre taking a draw in their own end that they badly need to win.

PLAYER: And if my legs are gone and I just need it out?

COACH: Never put it over the glass. Put the puck off the glass and out of your zone, not down the ice. From your own zone, a puck that flies straight out over the glass is a penalty in some leagues even when you didn't mean it. Put it over on purpose and it's a penalty everywhere.

COACH: When the other team ices it, it can be one of your best draws of the night. In some leagues they can't change. Then you're taking a draw in their end against five tired players. Have a set play ready.

COACH: Now line changes. Some games, especially for younger players, change lines in a set way, and then your coach and the officials tell you how. In games where your line chooses when to change, and it changes as a group, you go off first. Call it, go, and the wingers follow you off.

PLAYER: Why me first?

COACH: Your route covers more of the ice than either winger's, and the centre is reckoned to tire first. That's coaching know-how, not a measured finding. So the line changes on your legs. Change when your legs go, not when the play happens to stop. Don't change when there's a faceoff in their end that you're needed to take. And after your own icing, where your line can't change, don't start drifting toward the bench.

COACH: Let me pull it all together.

COACH: In your own end, get below the puck, inside the dots and below the hash marks, unless your team plays a centre fly. Never leave the slot empty. Go to the corner or hold the high slot, and call the weak-side winger in when you go. Under pressure, get the puck to the outside. Never pass it, or skate it, across the front of your own net. One look, one decision, move it. At the boards, the boards position. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to anyone coming at you, never duck.

COACH: Own the middle. Head up going forward. On the backcheck, get inside the late forward, take the ice, not the body, and never finish a check on a player's back.

COACH: On the forecheck, take the angle and steer, and arrive under control. In their slot, shoot, or pass across the middle. At their net, screen from outside the paint and off the red line, head up, and go around the goalie, never into them.

COACH: At the dot, get low with your knees, warn your wingers, and agree two back-up takers. On the kill, know your shape and hold it. And change on your legs.

PLAYER: So what should I ask my own coach?

COACH: Three things, because these are your team's choices, not rules. Do we play a swing breakout or a centre fly? In our own end, is it a low zone collapse, man-on-man, or a hybrid? And on the backcheck, who takes the middle lane? And one for your league. Does my division allow body checking? Their answers tell you how your team wants you to play, and now you know why each answer makes sense.
