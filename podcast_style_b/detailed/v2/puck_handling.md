COACH: The inside shoulder means driving at the middle of the ice. The defender has to believe that threat, because the middle is what they're taught to protect first. The cost is that you're skating into help. The other defender and the forward racing back are in the middle.

PLAYER: How do I choose in the moment?

COACH: Take the outside when you have more speed than the defender, and want to beat them wide or cut back underneath. Take the inside when you don't have the speed to win wide. Take it too when support is coming through the middle, or when the defender is already pushing you wide. Either way, the point is to make them turn, and then take the way past that their turn opens, not the one you planned before the rush. Neither choice is settled coaching doctrine, so ask your coach which they want from your position.

COACH: Next, change your speed, not just your direction. Defenders are good at defending direction. They're coached on it, and their backward skating is built for it. What they defend badly is timing.

PLAYER: Because they have to match my speed?

COACH: Exactly. A defender keeping their distance has to match your speed. So slow down to bait them, then explode. Once they've slowed down, they can't speed up backwards again quickly. Go the instant they settle. Or attack the moment they turn from skating backwards to forwards, or move sideways. For that moment they can't change direction again. Come at the same speed in a straight line every time, and the defender has only one problem to solve, and that's easy.

COACH: Use the defender's weight too. A defender who has committed their weight one way can't take it back. If they lunge or reach with their stick, go round them, not through them. If they're skating backwards hard, stop suddenly, and they keep going back into space you've left.

PLAYER: And if I've just got more speed than them?

COACH: Then just skate. Coaches see this all the time. A player with a clear lane skates right at the defender to try a move, and gets stood up. If you have speed, use it. And remember what the move is for. Beating the defender isn't the goal. Making a scoring chance is. A move that beats them but leaves you no speed and no angle has done nothing. A simple drive wide that beats nobody, but pulls two defenders and opens a pass to the middle, has done a lot. So win one-on-ones with a change of speed and body position more than with your hands.

COACH: Thirteenth idea. Fakes, and how they really work. A defender can't wait to see what you actually do. By then it's too late for them. So they read your signals instead. Your head, your eyes, your shoulders, your hips, your weight, and the angle of your blade. They commit on what those tell them.

PLAYER: So a deke is really just lying to them.

COACH: That's a good way to put it. A deke, a move to get past someone, is a false signal. You show one thing, they commit to it, and you do the other. A defender who commits the wrong way is already beaten.

PLAYER: Then why do my dekes not work?

COACH: Usually one of three things. First, commit fully. A lazy head turn fools nobody. The fake has to look exactly like the real thing until the last moment. If you wouldn't have believed it, neither will they. Second, a fake with only your hands isn't a fake. If you move the puck while your body stays still, you've told the defender nothing, because they're reading your body. Third, speed up out of it. The fake makes the gap, and your legs use it. If you don't burst away, the defender has time to recover.

PLAYER: If I only learn one move?

COACH: Learn the head and shoulder fake. A sharp head turn one way, your shoulder dips with it, and your weight shifts. Then push off and go the other way. It costs nothing, it can't be poke-checked, and it works at every level.

COACH: Two more moves, and when each one fits. The fake shot. Load up exactly as for a real shot, weight shifting and blade drawn back, then don't shoot. Defenders drop to block or turn their hips, and goalies commit to a save. Then pull the puck round them, or pass. It only works if your real shot is a threat. In a CARHA league, fake a wrist shot, never a slap shot, because a big slap-shot backswing is a penalty there.

COACH: Then the toe drag. Push the puck out on your forehand, offering it. Roll the blade over the puck so the toe is on the far side of it, and drag it back toward your feet. Then roll the blade back to catch it. That last part is the hard part, and it's where the move is usually lost. It works best against a defender who reaches early with their stick.

PLAYER: What if I mess it up?

COACH: That's what makes it a fairly safe move. Even a failed toe drag usually ends with the puck in your skates, where you can kick it back to your stick. That's a much better way to fail than most moves. But don't use it in a game until you can do it cleanly several times in a row in practice.

COACH: That's open ice. Try it.

PLAYER: When the puck arrives in stride, keep striding. Push it ahead and to the side, and carry it rather than stickhandle. Let the puck and me take separate routes when a stick is in the way, but slip forward, never out of trouble. I can beat one player, not two. One move, not four. If I take a defender on, make them turn, change my speed, and sell one fake with my whole body. And the goal is a scoring chance, not beating the defender.

COACH: That's it. Now down low in their end.

COACH: Fourteenth idea. You've got the puck deep in their end, behind their net or in their corner. Here, holding it has a different purpose. In your own end, you protect the puck to get it out. Down here, you protect it to make their defence move. Every second you keep it is a second they spend chasing instead of covering.

PLAYER: So I can take my time.

COACH: Some, but keep your feet moving and the puck on your far side. A player who stops behind the net or in the corner gives the defender a still target to pin. Moving, you can turn either way. And before anything else, look at the front of their net. A teammate there who has got free of their check is a better option than anything you can do on the boards.

PLAYER: And if nobody's free there?

COACH: The common approach is the cycle. Cut back up the boards you came down. That works because a defender who has committed toward the corner can't change direction as fast as you can. Make the turn while they're still too far away to reach you. Once they can reach you, it's too late. Go side-on to the boards instead. Never turn so you're facing the wall with them behind you. Or pass the puck back and down the boards to the teammate arriving behind you, and then get away to a new spot rather than stand and watch.

PLAYER: Is there a way to go to the net myself?

COACH: Walk out. Say the defender turns their back to the play, or commits too far toward the boards. Then turn off the boards into the space they gave up, and take it to the net. That's the moment a cycle is meant to create, and it doesn't last. Play the puck there, never the goalie. A goalie watching the puck can't see you coming or brace for you, and contact can hurt them. It can also be a penalty in every league, and cost you far more than two minutes.

COACH: It's still a battle on the boards, so the position doesn't change. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to anyone coming at you, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly. How long your team keeps the puck low before it goes to the net or the point, the area just inside their blue line where your defencemen stand, is a team choice. Some teams want it at the net at the first chance. Others want to wear the defence down first. Ask your bench which yours plays.

COACH: Fifteenth idea. Bad pucks. Most pucks you get in a game aren't perfect, and handling them well does more for keeping the puck than any deke.

PLAYER: What do I do with one that's bouncing?

COACH: A bad puck still needs the blade tilted over the top of it, and a soft give as it arrives, whether it's bouncing, on its edge or flat. An open blade is what a puck jumps over. Let a bouncing or rolling puck settle before you do anything with it. One extra moment to flatten it is quicker than chasing your own bad touch.

PLAYER: And one that lands on my backhand when I'm under pressure? I hate those.

COACH: Under pressure, the backhand is often the right puck. If the checker is on your forehand side, your backhand is the far side, and the far side is where the puck belongs. So tilt the blade over the puck on your backhand as hard as on your forehand.

COACH: Sixteenth idea. How to practise, and the hands to build. Puck handling is the one hockey skill you can really improve without ice. Take that chance.

PLAYER: What do I need at home?

COACH: A smooth surface, like a shooting pad or plastic tiles, and something to handle. Get the surface first, or the puck won't slide. A golf ball or a tennis ball costs nothing and builds fast hands. Never train only with a weighted puck or stick, or you build a stroke that only works with weight.

PLAYER: How much should I do?

COACH: A little and often beats a lot once in a while. One coaching publisher's advice is ten to fifteen focused minutes every day, rather than two hours once a week. That's one source's guidance, not a measured comparison, but it fits how skills are generally thought to settle in, so it's worth following. Leave the pad and a ball where you walk past them. The practice you actually do beats the practice you planned.

PLAYER: And what do I work on?

COACH: Start with your grip. Hold the stick firmly enough that it can't escape, and softly enough that you aren't crushing it. A squeezed hand can't feel, and if you can't feel the puck, you can't play with your head up. If your hands feel dead, check your bottom hand first, the one lower down the shaft. That's one coach's tip, not a count. Let your top hand do the steering. It opens and closes the blade over the puck. Your bottom hand guides and steadies, and stays loose so the shaft slides through it.

COACH: Tilt the blade over the top of the puck, on your forehand and your backhand, so the puck sits in the pocket instead of rolling up the blade and over. Rolling your top wrist is what changes the tilt from forehand to backhand, and that wrist roll is the heart of stickhandling. And let the blade give as the puck arrives, so it deadens instead of bouncing off. Think of catching an egg. If your hands don't go back with it, it breaks.

PLAYER: Should I be swinging my arms to move it?

COACH: Use your wrists, not big arm swings. Arm swings are slow, a defender can see them coming, and they pull you off balance. And you can stop a wrist move halfway, but you can't stop an arm swing halfway.

COACH: Practise both wide and tight. Wide means big sweeping pulls outside your feet, for open ice and for setting up a move. Tight means small, quick touches near your skates, for traffic, the boards and the slot. Players who only handle wide get poke-checked in traffic. Players who only handle tight can never move a defender.

PLAYER: I mostly just go side to side.

COACH: Then go front to back as well. Side to side alone is a rhythm, and a defender who has watched it for three seconds knows exactly when the puck will be on your backhand. So work the whole circle round you, in front, both sides, and behind your heels. Front to back is also what makes the toe drag possible. And keep the puck moving. A puck sitting still on a still blade is the easiest thing in hockey to take.

COACH: Then do all of it with your eyes up. Stickhandle facing a wall with words or numbers on it, and read them out. If you can't read them, your head is down. Scatter a dozen pucks or balls and handle through them without touching any. And play keep-away against another person. Nothing shows up a player who watches the puck faster than someone trying to take it.

PLAYER: Does it carry over to the ice straight away?

COACH: Not straight away. Off-ice hands aren't on-ice hands until you've used them under pressure. The first time you take them onto the ice, expect a small step backwards while you get used to skates and a puck that slides much more freely. That's normal. In every practice, do the same movements on skates, and get into small-area games, like keep-away or cross-ice, anything in a tight space. Small games force the tight handling and protection that matter, at game speed, against real sticks.

COACH: Let me pull it all together.

COACH: Play with your head up, every time you have the puck. Build the feel, so your eyes can come up on their own.

COACH: Keep your body between the checker and the puck. Puck on the far side, top hand away from your body, low and wide, feet moving. Take contact, don't give it. In a game without body checking, hold your spot and never lean, bump or drive into anyone. In a CARHA league, stay out of the contact altogether.

COACH: On the boards, the boards position. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to anyone coming at you, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly.

COACH: Know your out before you get to the puck, because protecting it buys a second, not a minute. Don't panic pass. Get one more look before it arrives. Outside your own end, when you don't know, keep it, side-on to the checker. In your own end, one look, one decision, move it. And anywhere on the ice, never your back to anyone coming at you.

COACH: Look for the pass first. In your own end, through the middle, or as the last player back, don't stickhandle. Off the glass, never over it.

COACH: Free hand high on your own stick, braced, never holding, never on the cage. Trap a loose puck and play it. Kick the puck, never a player. And a puck that pops up at your own net, knock it straight down.

COACH: In open ice, keep your speed and carry the puck. If you take a defender on, make them turn, change your speed, and sell one fake with your whole body. Down low in their end, keep moving, look to the net first, and play the puck, never the goalie. And remember the goal is a scoring chance, not beating the defender.

PLAYER: What should I ask my own coach?

COACH: Three things. Off our own boards, which out do you want from me first? How much do you want me carrying the puck, and how much passing? And on a one-on-one, which shoulder do you want me to attack? And one for your league. Is body checking allowed in our games? Ask, because these are your team's choices and your league's rules. Their answers tell you how your team wants you to handle the puck, and now you know the reason behind each one.
