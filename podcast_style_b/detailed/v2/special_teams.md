COACH: Spread out wide, in the spread shape. Move the puck side to side across the middle, not around the outside. Every pass across makes the goalie push all the way across, and three or four in a row means they won't be set. Don't shoot from the outside into three shot-blockers. Wait for the goalie to be moving. The goal usually comes from a one-timer at the far post or a second rebound, so have someone at the net for both. They stand just outside the crease, hands off the goalie, holding their spot without pushing back, head up, chin off their chest.

COACH: But time on the outside isn't safety in the slot. Three killers will still jump a bobbled pass, close hard on anyone walking into the slot, and get a stick or a body in front of a shooter. So move the puck before they close. Protect it on every touch, side-on to them, puck on the far side of your body, never your back. And walk into the slot expecting to be hit, head up, chin off your chest, never duck. Be patient, but keep looking for the shot. Two minutes of passing with no shot is a failed five-on-three.

PLAYER: And if we score?

COACH: Usually only one penalty ends. A goal frees one player from a minor penalty, the ordinary two-minute kind, so five-on-three becomes five-on-four. Go again straight away, while the kill is still sorting itself out. A major penalty is served in full, however many you score, and a fight is a major. So never fight, and never start one. It can cost your bench a player for the rest of the game.

PLAYER: Let me try the power play. Be ready to shoot or pass the instant the puck arrives. Look across the middle before I shoot. On a walk-down, head for the middle, not the corner. On the boards, the boards position. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of my shoulder, head up, chin off my chest, never my back to anyone coming at me, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly. Get in with control, and regroup rather than force it. When they clear it, one player chases and the other four re-form. At five-on-three, spread out, move it across, and shoot when the goalie's moving.

COACH: Spot on. Now the penalty kill.

COACH: Start with the shapes on the kill. Again, which shape you run is your coach's choice, and the same four players can run all three in one game. So know which one is called before the faceoff.

COACH: The first is the box. It's the one almost everyone learns first. Two forwards high, one on each point. Two defencemen low, covering the corners and the front of the net. A box has no single top player. Two forwards, side by side, is what makes it a box.

PLAYER: What do the high forwards do?

COACH: Stay in the shooting lanes, and never both drift to the same side. Turn your inside shoulder so you can see your point player and the middle at once. Low down, the defenceman on the puck side pressures the puck in the corner or the low circle. Pressure with your stick on the puck, not a hit. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call, and if you can see their numbers, pull up. The far defenceman covers the front of the net and the far post. When the puck moves across, all four slide together, and the shape stays a box.

PLAYER: How should I cover the front of the net? There's always a big forward parked there.

COACH: Whoever covers it, in any shape, does it the same way. Stick in the lane and body in position. Head up, chin off your chest, because a goal post can hurt you the same way the boards can. Hold your spot without leaning or pushing. Never put your stick across anyone, never into anyone's back, and pull up if you can see their numbers. Never lift anyone off their feet. Even going slowly, a crash there can hurt. Don't try to walk a screener out. And don't back into your own goalie. A goalie who is down and can't see you can be hurt.

COACH: Why do teams play the box? It's simple, easy to teach, and very good at protecting the front of the net. It beats an overload, because it can wait it out, and a two-three, which attacks the areas a box defends best. The weakness is that nobody in a box is assigned to the middle of the slot. So against a one-three-one, the bumper stands in that hole, uncovered by design.

PLAYER: So what fixes that?

COACH: The second shape, the diamond. One player at the top. Two in the middle, one on each side. One low. It exists because a box leaves the bumper unmarked. In a diamond, the two middle players each take a half-wall, and both squeeze onto the bumper when the puck goes to the middle. The top player pressures the point and takes away the middle shooting lane. Pressure with your stick on the puck, not a hit. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call, and if you can see their numbers, pull up. Don't get walked around up there, because you're the only one. The low player covers the front of the net, alone, the same way, with no lean, no push, and head up.

PLAYER: What's the catch?

COACH: The low player is alone against a net-front player and a backdoor arriver at once. That's a two-on-one you're choosing to give up. And a confused diamond is worse than a good box.

COACH: The third is the wedge plus one. Three killers hold a tight triangle around the net, two defencemen just off the edges of the crease and one forward higher up in the slot. The fourth player, the plus one, pressures whoever has the puck. The triangle stays tight and protects the slot. It doesn't chase. The two near the crease hold their spots with no lean and no push, head up, chin off their chest.

PLAYER: And the plus one tries to win the puck?

COACH: No. Their job is to make every possession uncomfortable, not to win the puck. Pressure with your stick on the puck, not a hit. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call, and if you can see their numbers, pull up. When the puck moves, the nearest triangle player steps out as the new plus one, and the old one drops back in. That handover is the hard part, so everybody has to know who's out and who's in, every second.

COACH: Teams play it because it protects the most dangerous ice and still puts pressure on the puck, and it makes turnovers. The cost is that everyone has to keep track of a job that keeps moving, so it's a poor choice for a young or thrown-together unit. And a plus one who just chases the puck around the outside leaves you killing with three. Coaches teach the plus one's route in different ways, so ask how yours moves.

COACH: Next, the pressure setting. Every shape also has a pressure setting, passive or aggressive, and that's your coach's choice too. It's a separate decision from the shape.

PLAYER: Passive sounds a bit lazy.

COACH: It isn't. Passive means holding your shape, giving up the outside, blocking lanes and waiting for a clear. It's the lowest-risk way to play. It's the usual choice when you're protecting a lead, when you're tired, late in a kill, or when their power play is much better than you. Aggressive means going at the puck carrier to force mistakes. It can create chances for your team going the other way. But it also creates chaos, and chaos with four against five isn't usually your friend. Pressure spends a killer to buy a mistake, and the mistake doesn't always come.

COACH: Pressure means your stick on the puck, not a hit. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call. And if you can see their numbers, pull up and play the puck. Whichever setting it is, pressure as a unit or not at all. One player pressuring while three sit back is how the passing lanes open.

COACH: Now, kill it before it gets in. The best place to kill a penalty is before the puck enters your zone. A power play that never gets set up never gets to use what?

PLAYER: Its extra player.

COACH: Right. In the middle of the ice you can make them play your way. Once they're set up in your end, you're only limiting damage. So whatever the plan, take away the middle and push them to one side. A power play forced to come in along one side of the ice has lost half its options. Angle them with your skating, not your stick.

COACH: Stand up at your blue line as a pair. Both defencemen step up, or both back off. One of each leaves a hole. Stepping up means your stick on the puck, not a hit. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call, and if you can see their numbers, pull up.

PLAYER: Who do I go after? The carrier?

COACH: The receiver, not just the carrier. The dangerous player on the entry is usually the one arriving with speed off a drop pass or a swing. Taking away the target is easier than taking away the puck. So put your stick in that player's passing lane. When the puck reaches them, go for the puck, not the player. Stick on the puck, not a hit. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call. Where hitting isn't allowed, even a step or a glide into them can be a penalty. And contact before the puck arrives is interference, a penalty in every league.

COACH: Make them dump it. A dump-in with your defencemen already turned is a win. Make them fail to get in three times, and the penalty can be over without you ever defending your own end. And don't lunge or dive at your blue line. A missed poke at the puck there is a clean entry, with you out of the play.

PLAYER: How do I know when to pressure and when to back off?

COACH: Go through it in this order. Can you actually get there? Pressure that arrives a stride late is worse than none, because you've spent a player for nothing. Where's the puck? Pressure the puck when the carrier is looking down, receiving it badly, or facing the boards. Never go at one with their head up and help on both sides. Now a safety point that goes with that. A carrier who's looking down, facing the boards, or has their back turned is telling you the puck can be won. That's all it tells you. It isn't a reason to arrive harder. A player facing the boards is exactly the picture the checking-from-behind rule is written about. So angle to the puck, never through their back. If you can see the numbers on their jersey, pull up and play the puck. If the only way to the puck is through their back, you were never winning it, so leave it. Never hit from behind.

PLAYER: What does it cost if I get that wrong?

COACH: It's never just two minutes. In most leagues any call for it throws you out of the game. Where it doesn't, you get a ten-minute misconduct on top of your penalty, and a hard one along the boards throws you out there too. If you drive a player who can't protect themselves into the boards or the net from behind, plan on a match penalty. In some leagues the referee has no choice. And you don't need the boards for the worst case. In most leagues a hit from behind meant to hurt, or a reckless one, can be a match penalty even in open ice. In some leagues a reckless one is a match penalty, and a ban of more than one game is likely. And in some, being thrown out also costs you your next game.

PLAYER: You said there was an order. What comes after where the puck is?

COACH: What's the clock? Pressure early, while you're fresh. In the last thirty seconds, or when you're out of gas, back off and make them beat your shape. And what's the score? Protecting a one-goal lead late isn't the time to gamble.

COACH: Next, once they're set up in your end. Your stick is the most useful thing you have when you're a player short. Blade flat on the ice, out in front of your body, in the passing lane, not on the puck carrier. Sticks in lanes stop far more passes by putting players off than by picking them off.

PLAYER: Which lane, though? There are loads of them.

COACH: Take away the seam first. Point your stick at the most dangerous pass, not at the puck. The puck is already being handled by a teammate of yours. The pass around the outside is the one you're happy to give up. And stay low with your knees bent, because you can't move sideways quickly from standing up.

COACH: The power play wants you to chase. Them having the puck on the outside isn't dangerous. You leaving your spot to chase it is. If you chase and miss, you've made the seam yourself, because four players can't cover five with one of them out of place.

PLAYER: So I never chase?

COACH: Chase only a puck you can actually win. A bobbled pass. A player with their back turned. A puck on the boards with no support. And a turned back still tells you the puck is winnable and nothing else. Stick on the puck, not a hit. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call. If you can see their numbers, pull up and play the puck.

COACH: Talk all the time. Call switches, call the bumper, call the backdoor. Most breakdowns on a kill are two players working to different ideas, not two players being lazy. And tell your goalie what you're giving up. If the plan is to give up shots from the point, your goalie will set up for a shot through traffic, instead of waiting for a pass across.

COACH: Change when you can, not when you must. Clear first, then change, in that order. And when your unit changes, count before you go. Too many players on the ice is a penalty, and on a kill that's two players short.

COACH: Now, blocking shots. Blocking shots is a skill you learn, not just bravery. And how much your team blocks at all is a coaching choice. Some kills want players collapsing in front of the goalie and blocking a lot. Others want the lane taken away on your feet, with the goalie seeing everything. A kill that blocks less isn't a soft kill. So ask your coach which they want, and ask your goalie.

PLAYER: Say we're a blocking team. How do I do it without getting hurt?

COACH: Here's the whole of it, and it goes together. Be in the shooting lane before the shot, not sliding into it. Sliding gets you a puck off the ankle, and a shooter who has already changed their angle. Block from close, square to the shooter, with your head out of the lane. Hands behind your body, with the backs of your gloves facing out. Never lift a leg. Never turn your back to the shot or turn sideways to it, and never lunge. Stay on your feet until the puck has actually left the stick. Tucking your hands behind your shin pads is for a block when you're already down on the ice, not a standing one.

PLAYER: Should I get my stick out in front?

COACH: Block with your body, not your stick. Keep the stick flat on the ice beside you, or out of the lane. A puck off a stick held out in front tends to go upward, often into your own face. That's widely taught coaching know-how, not something anyone has measured. And never block from in front of your own goalie's eyes. Half-deflecting a shot your goalie couldn't see is worse than letting it through cleanly. If you can't get properly into the lane, get out of it.

COACH: Next, clearing the puck. A skater short, where your league allows it, fire the long clear the moment you have it. Aim it to make their fetch awkward, into the corner away from their goalie, or hard around the boards. Keep it hard and flat, and keep the backswing short. In at least one adult league a big wind-up counts as a slap shot, a penalty there, and if it hurts someone you're thrown out.

PLAYER: How long can we do that for?

COACH: Only while a penalty leaves you with fewer skaters than them. Once you're level again, even at four-on-four, icing is called. But if you're killing two penalties and the first one ends, you're still a skater short, so you can still fire it. And it counts from when the puck leaves your stick. So say there are two seconds left on the kill and the puck comes to you. What would you do?

PLAYER: Fire it, rather than hang on to it.

COACH: Right.

PLAYER: What about when I win it with a forward right on top of me, and I haven't seen anything?

COACH: That's when players panic pass. A panic pass is a pass made without looking, just to get rid of the puck. On a kill, in your own end, it usually goes straight back to them, in the worst place to lose it. But the answer isn't to hold on to it, because in your own end, holding it is how the turnover happens. The answer is to have looked already. Get one more look before the puck reaches you, so you know the answer before it arrives. Then one look, one decision, move it. And if you've seen nothing, don't throw it into the middle. Get it hard off the boards and out, under the glass. And never pass the puck, or skate it, across the front of your own net.

COACH: Now the two penalties a tired kill talks itself into. Both cost two minutes instead of a faceoff, and both happen when you're desperate to get the puck out. The first is putting it over the glass. In many leagues, shooting the puck straight out of the rink from your own end is a two-minute penalty, even when you're short, whether you meant it or not. And flipping it out on purpose is a penalty from anywhere on the ice, in every league. So never flip it out to buy a whistle.

PLAYER: But off the glass is fine?

COACH: A clear played hard off the glass that stays in the rink is exactly what you want. But where that rule applies, a clean flip over the glass from your end is a second penalty, and that makes it five-on-three. So aim your clears hard and flat, not lofted. Goalies too, because in some leagues a goalie's clear out of the rink can cost two minutes even by accident. Keep it under the glass.

COACH: The second penalty costs more. It's smothering the puck to get a whistle. A skater, not the goalie, who deliberately falls on the puck or gathers it into their body takes a two-minute penalty. If the puck is inside your own crease, or you are, plan on a penalty shot instead, a free shot alone on your goalie. If your own goalie has been pulled, plan on it being a goal for them. And in at least one league they can choose to take a second player off you instead of the shot, which makes it five-on-three.

PLAYER: So what am I allowed to do with a puck in the air?

COACH: Knock it down with your hand, or catch it and drop it straight back to the ice. That costs nothing. If you hold on to it, it's the same penalty. A shot that goes under you while you're down blocking isn't a penalty, but using your hand to cover it is. And don't leave it for your goalie to freeze. A goalie who freezes the puck when nobody is checking them can take two minutes too. Get a stick on the puck and clear it.

COACH: Next, the shorthanded rush. Picture it. You've just picked off a pass at your blue line, and there's open ice ahead. What would you do?

PLAYER: Go. That's a chance.

COACH: Maybe. When you win the puck, the default is always to get it out, then change. A shorthanded rush is the exception, and you take it only when it's free. Take it when all three of these hold. The turnover has handed you a real two-on-one or a breakaway, not one you'd have to make with speed. Their players are behind you. And you can get there before their point player gets back. If it's a race you'll lose, it isn't a rush.

PLAYER: And when should I leave it?

COACH: If any one of these is true. It's a two-on-two or worse. Your partner hasn't seen it. You're protecting a lead late. Or you're the last player back. Lose it, and you're four against five again, with two of you out of the play and them coming back at speed. If you have to think about whether it's on, it's not on.

COACH: Let me pull the kill together. Know your shape, and hold your own job. Pressure as a unit, with your stick on the puck, or not at all. Go for the puck, never through their back, and pull up when you see their numbers. Sticks in the lanes, seam first. Block from the lane, on your feet, head out of it. Never lift a leg, turn your back or lunge, and never block from in front of your goalie's eyes. Clear, then change. Look before the puck reaches you, so you're never panic passing. Keep every clear in the rink, and never smother the puck.

COACH: Now the moments that belong to both sides. Start with when a penalty ends. On the power play, know the clock. The last ten seconds are your best chance.

PLAYER: Why the end, when we're nearly out of time?

COACH: The kill is watching the clock too, and their concentration dips. On the kill, watch for the player coming out of the box. That player is a fresh attacker, arriving from the side at full speed, with nobody assigned to them. So whoever is nearest calls it out, loud, and picks that player up. And if you're the one in the box, watch the play, not the clock. Come out facing the ice, and sprint. For about three seconds, you're the most dangerous player on the ice.

COACH: Next, faceoffs on special teams. They matter more here than anywhere else. A player up, winning the draw skips the zone entry completely, and the entry is the hardest part. A player short, losing it means defending a set power play from the first second.

PLAYER: So what should we sort out before the draw?

COACH: Agree the play before you line up. On the power play, are you winning it back to the point, to the half-wall, or forward? On the kill, are you winning it clean for a clear, or tying up the other centre so a winger can sweep it? And where is that clear going? A tie-up is stick on stick only. Lift or press their stick with yours, over or under the blade, for an instant, then play the puck. Never a hand on their stick, and never your stick on their hands. And on the kill, never aim the draw out of the rink. A draw steered out on purpose can be a penalty, and on a kill that's five-on-three.

COACH: In some leagues, the team given the power play picks which faceoff dot in their end the first draw is on. Pick the side that suits your one-timer. Win the draw with your stick, not by leaning on theirs. Contact with the other centre before the puck drops can be a penalty on its own, the very first time, and in some leagues the referee has no choice.

PLAYER: And the wingers?

COACH: Stay off the hash marks, the short lines on the outside of the circle, until the puck drops. If a winger creeps in early, it's your centre who gets thrown out of the circle, not the winger. If your team does it twice on the same draw, in most leagues it's a penalty. On a kill, a penalty there makes it five-on-three.

COACH: Now four against four, and three against three. At four-on-four, each player has about a quarter more ice, mostly in the middle. So skill and skating matter more. But you have one fewer layer of help behind you, and a two-on-one comes at you faster.

PLAYER: So as a defenceman I should join the rush more? There's room.

COACH: Less, not more. Get caught up the ice and it's an odd-man rush the other way. Change with real care. A change with the puck loose hands them a rush against three. So change when you have the puck in their zone, or after a clear. And don't spread out just because there's space. Support the puck with short passes.

COACH: Three-on-three overtime is about keeping the puck. They can't score while you have it. So if the attack isn't there, carry it back out to the middle of the ice and start again. That's standard practice at the top level, and it's allowed there. Attack only when you have the extra player. A two-on-one at three-on-three is nearly a goal, and a one-on-one is nothing. Try to get one defender alone in space and go at them. And don't all chase the puck together.

PLAYER: When do we change, then?

COACH: Never with the puck loose, because a bad change at three-on-three is a breakaway. Change while you hold the puck, often by passing it back to your goalie. The goalie settles it and plays it back out once the change is made. They don't freeze it. Freezing it with nobody checking them can be a two-minute penalty, and the stoppage hands the other team the faceoff anyway. Not every league plays overtime this way, and some don't play it at all. So check how yours works.

COACH: Next, pulling the goalie on a power play. Late in a game, a team on the power play can pull its goalie for an extra skater and make it six-on-four. That's the biggest advantage in hockey.

PLAYER: Where does the extra player go?

COACH: Where they force a decision, to the bumper spot or the backdoor, never out on the outside. Put two at the net front, both feet off the red line, and neither one touching the goalie. Hold your spot, don't push back. Head up, chin off your chest, because a goal post can hurt you the same way the boards can. Move the puck quickly, and accept that the shooting lanes will be crowded.

PLAYER: And if we're the ones killing it?

COACH: Their empty net is your weapon. Win the puck and you can fire it the whole length of the ice into that net, and it counts. So clear with your head up, looking for the empty net. Keep the clear low and under the glass. Keep the backswing short too, because in at least one adult league a big wind-up is a slap shot and a penalty. A puck over the glass from your own end is still a penalty in many leagues.

COACH: Blocking shots and sticks in lanes matter more than ever. Block the same way as always. Be in the lane before the shot, square to the shooter, head out of the lane, hands behind you, stick flat beside you. Stay on your feet until the puck has left the stick. Never lift a leg, never turn your back or go sideways, never lunge, and never block from in front of your goalie's eyes.

COACH: There's a related moment. When the referee raises an arm for a penalty against the other team, play goes on until they get control of the puck. Your team will often pull its goalie for an extra skater then. In that window, what's the danger, do you think?

PLAYER: A shot against us?

COACH: Not really. It's one of your own players sending the puck back into your own empty net. So be careful how you move it backwards.

COACH: Last, and it's short. Your goalie, on both. On the kill, agree who fetches the puck behind your net before it starts. In some leagues your goalie can't play the puck in the corners, but they can still stop a rim behind the net and leave it for you. Either way, your goalie moves a puck on, never sits on it. Freezing it out there is a penalty.

COACH: Let me pull it all together.

COACH: On the power play, make one of them cover two of you. Move the puck, look for the second pass, and cash it in close to the net. Be ready the instant it reaches you. Know your shape and your spot in it. Get in with control, and when they clear it, one player chases.

COACH: At their net, screen from just outside the crease, both feet off the red line. Know where the post is, head up, chin off your chest, never duck. Never touch the goalie, and arrive at the far post late.

COACH: On the boards, the boards position. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to anyone coming at you, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly.

COACH: On the kill, choose what you give up, and hold your own job. Pressure as a unit, with your stick on the puck. Kill it at the blue line if you can. Go for the puck, never through their back, and pull up when you see their numbers. Sticks in the lanes. Block from the lane, on your feet, head out of it. Never lift a leg or turn your back, and never block from in front of your goalie's eyes.

COACH: When you win it on the kill, clear it, then change. In your own end, one look, one decision, move it, and never across the front of your own net. Fire the long clear where your league allows it, keep it in the rink, and never smother it. A player up, never ice it.

COACH: And when the numbers change again, at four-on-four, in overtime, or with a goalie pulled, keep the puck. Change only when it's safe, and attack with the extra player.

PLAYER: What should I ask my own coach?

COACH: Three things. Which shape do we run on the power play and on the kill, and where do I stand in each? Do you want us shooting a lot, or waiting for the kill to break, and how much do you want us blocking shots? And at our level, can a team that's a skater short ice the puck, and is body checking allowed? Their answers tell you how your team wants to play special teams, and now you know the reason behind each one.

### 5. Wording that follows the source, not the old script

The old script was written against ST blob `faa98c3` (`69b0686`). The word diff `69b0686..HEAD` was read in full. Changes that touch this script:

- **"Never your back to the checker" → "never your back to anyone coming at you"** in the walk-down posture and every short form (ST :329, :340, :402, :413, CM :1199; owner's 21 Oct ruling, and the brief's first safety rule). The checker definition is now "any opponent coming at you along the boards, whether they're after the puck or just coming at you".
- **Checking-from-behind cost** (ST :651–:654, :668, CM :1199). The old cost sentences are kept: never two minutes; most leagues throw you out (ST: "ends your game in every book but USA Hockey"); otherwise a ten-minute misconduct; into the boards or net defenceless, plan on a match, mandatory in some; open ice can be a match in most leagues. The old script's "In most leagues a hit from behind in open ice can also be a match penalty" is kept as a "can" rather than narrowed, so nothing reads cheaper; **a rules reviewer should confirm it still holds.**
