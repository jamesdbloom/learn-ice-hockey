COACH: Chip it back deep and forecheck again. Don't force it out through their blue line. Losing the puck in their corner is free. Losing it at their blue line, with your defence pinched in, is a rush against.

COACH: Twelfth idea. Before you take a chance, ask whether you're anticipating or gambling. There are a few chances a player takes on purpose. Jumping a passing lane, which means stepping into a pass to pick it off. A pinch, which is a defenceman stepping down from their blue line to keep the puck in their zone. Stepping up, which is moving up to challenge an attacker, in the neutral zone or at your own blue line. And activating, which is a defenceman joining the attack low in their zone.

PLAYER: Aren't those all good things?

COACH: You need all of them, because hockey with no risk never scores. The question is never whether to take risks. It's whether this one is anticipation or a gamble. Anticipating means acting on information. Gambling means acting on hope. When they work, they look exactly the same from the bench. But they're completely different decisions.

PLAYER: How do I tell them apart in the moment?

COACH: You have information if you can say why before you move. The carrier's hips and shoulders have turned toward a teammate. The carrier has looked at that teammate twice. That teammate's stick is out as a target. You've counted the carrier's other options, and there aren't any. Or you've seen this player make this exact pass already tonight. If the honest answer is that it felt like a pass was coming, you're gambling.

COACH: Then the second question. Not only, am I right? But, what happens if I'm right and I still miss it? Even a well-read risk is a bad one if nobody covers the space you left. Picture two pinches. In the first, your centre is high in the zone behind you. What happens if you lose the puck?

PLAYER: The centre's back there. They can cover.

COACH: Right. Your centre becomes the third defender, and the worst case is two against two. So that's a good pinch, even if you lose the puck. Now the same pinch with your centre down below their goal line. That's a bad pinch, even if you win the puck. You were one bounce from a two-on-one, and you didn't know it.

COACH: So here's the full test, three questions you can run in half a second. Do I have information, not just a feeling? If I miss, is someone behind me? And what does the scoreboard say I can afford? Two yeses, and a green light from the scoreboard, then go. Otherwise hold your position. Holding is a decision too, and often the right one.

PLAYER: And when I do pinch?

COACH: Pinch for the puck, with your stick, not a hit. Whether you can hit at all depends on your league, your age group and your game, so find out first. Even where hitting is allowed, it's only while they still have the puck. If you can see their numbers, pull up. A pinch often meets the boards, so use the boards position. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to anyone coming at you, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly. If you end up facing the boards, stick and gloves up on the glass, spread wide, head off it.

COACH: Thirteenth idea. Stay on your skates. When you close down a player with the puck, skate at them. Stay on your feet, and keep your stick in their passing lane. Take away their options rather than the puck. A defender on their feet with a good stick beats a defender sliding with a great one.

PLAYER: Sometimes diving in feels like the only way to get there.

COACH: Try this. You're closing on a carrier, and you leave your feet to dive at them. What can you do once you're in the air?

PLAYER: Nothing, I guess. I'm committed.

COACH: Exactly. You can't change direction in the air. A carrier who sees you leave your feet makes you miss with one move. Then you're on the ice behind the play for four or five seconds, just when your team needs all five players. And the upside is small. At best you break up one play. That's true even of a leap no referee would call.

PLAYER: What about when closing down turns into a hit?

COACH: Whether it can end in a body check depends on your league, your age group and your game. Where checking isn't allowed, this is still how you close a carrier down, with your feet on the ice, your stick in the lane, and no hit. Where it is allowed, the check is made on your skates, with the same limits every time. Never a run-up. Building up speed into contact, even with two hard strides, can be charging. Never your stick across them. Never into their back. If you can see their numbers, pull up. Never lift anyone off their feet. Keep it off the head. Never drive or push anyone into the boards. Even going slowly, a hit into the boards can hurt. And never finish a check once the puck has gone.

PLAYER: Is leaving my feet a rule thing or a coaching thing?

COACH: It's a rule, not just a habit. Jumping into a check is charging, in every league. A charge can get you thrown out of the game in every league, and if someone is hurt, some leagues make that automatic. A charge meant to hurt someone is a match penalty in most leagues, which goes past being thrown out.

PLAYER: What about sliding to block a pass on a two-on-one?

COACH: That depends on your team's system. Where your job is to hold the lane, sliding early gives it away. Some teams coach a timed slide across the lane as the plan itself. So ask which yours plays. Either way, slide at the pass, never sprawl at the shot. And never slide into a player's legs. In most leagues that's a penalty even if you touched the puck first, and it can take them off their feet with nothing to break the fall.

COACH: Let me pull the attack together. In their end, shoot when there's a lane and a body in front. A middle pass, when it's really there, beats everything. Cycle under pressure, and never force it out through their blue line. Before a pinch, a step up or jumping a passing lane, ask the three questions. Information or a feeling? Who is behind me? What does the score allow? Go for the puck with your stick, not a hit. And stay on your skates.

COACH: Fourteenth idea. Let the scoreboard set your risk, not your personality.

PLAYER: I'm just a careful player, though. That's how I play.

COACH: The right amount of risk isn't part of who you are. It moves with the score and the clock. Say you're leading by one in the third period. Do you take more risk or less?

PLAYER: Less.

COACH: Less, and reach for the safe options earlier than usual. A goal against gives them the game back. A goal for only adds to a lead you already have. Leading by three, take it right down. Nothing a creative play could win is worth what a comeback would cost. Manage the puck, forecheck, and get your changes. Tied and early, play it straight. Run the checklists as they are.

PLAYER: And when we're losing?

COACH: Trailing late, take risk up. Pinch, activate, force your way into their zone, and try the riskier pass. When you're two down late, a goal against costs you almost nothing, and a goal for is worth everything. So a player who doesn't raise their risk when trailing isn't being safe. They're being unhelpful. When you pinch, pinch for the puck, with your stick, not a hit. Whether you can hit at all depends on your league, your age group and your game, so find out first. If you can see their numbers, pull up.

COACH: Shorthanded, when you have fewer skaters because of a penalty, take risk down. A turnover in your own end is worse than usual. Clear it, and clear it hard. The one exception is a real two-on-one the other way, one you've actually read and not just hoped for. That's one of the best chances in hockey.

COACH: On the power play, when you have the extra player, be creative inside their end and careful at their blue line. A turnover there sends their penalty killers the other way, and a shorthanded rush against you is the worst thing that can happen on a power play.

COACH: If your league plays three-on-three overtime, be patient. In all that space, every turnover is an odd-man rush, and there's no next shift to fix it. The usual approach is to attack only with numbers, and to regroup rather than force your way in. In the last minute of a period, take risk down. Give them nothing to walk off with.

COACH: When you're trailing and your goalie is pulled for an extra attacker, take risk all the way up. The only bad decision then is a slow one. When they've pulled theirs and you're leading, take risk all the way down, but shoot at the empty net rather than hunting for a better play.

PLAYER: Does any of this change at my level?

COACH: It does. In a team that doesn't practise much, the give-and-go you can see is one your teammate can't. In rec hockey, a turnover often meets nobody coming back at all. And the goaltending varies more, both ways. So your own turnovers cost more, not less. The blunt version is this. Most amateur teams lose far more goals to unforced turnovers than they win with creative plays. Tighten up nothing but your own end, and you'll win more games. Nobody is asking you to stop trying things. Try them in their end.

COACH: So risk is a dial, not a switch. A big lead, a tired bench, or a backup goalie in your net all turn it toward the simple play. A team that's being outplayed turns it the other way.

COACH: Fifteenth idea. A penalty is a decision too, and it has a price. In the NHL's last regular season, the twenty twenty-five to twenty-six season, about one power play in five ended in a goal. So taking a minor penalty, the usual two minutes in the penalty box, hands the other team roughly a one-in-five chance of scoring. Against about three goals a game, that's a real piece of a game, given away.

PLAYER: So when is a penalty worth it?

COACH: Less often than players think, and only if the foul really does cost just two minutes. The test is unforgiving. Is the thing you're stopping more likely to become a goal than that one-in-five chance? Two cases pass. The first is stopping a certain goal. A one-in-five chance beats a sure thing every time, in the first minute as much as the last. But it has to be a foul that costs a minor. Never haul a player down, from behind or from the side. And never take their legs. A player who falls at full speed can't protect themselves. And a hook that drags someone down is never sure to stay a minor. If it hurts them, most leagues make it a major and throw you out of the game. And in some leagues a reckless hook or trip gets you thrown out even if nobody is hurt. One meant to hurt someone is a match penalty in most.

PLAYER: What does a breakaway change?

COACH: Picture a player clear on a breakaway, alone with only your goalie to beat. If you foul them from behind, that isn't two minutes. In every league it gives them a penalty shot, a free shot alone on your goalie. Over eleven seasons in the NHL, penalty shots went in a bit more often than power plays do. And if you're the goalie, coming out to foul a breakaway from the front doesn't save you either. In some leagues that's a penalty shot too. With your own goalie pulled, it's worse. A foul from behind then gives them the goal in every league, and in some leagues a foul from the front does too. There's no shot left to save.

COACH: The second case is late in a game, to break up a rush that would tie it or win it. With thirty seconds left and a one-goal lead, they only get part of a power play. In both, the same limits hold. It has to be a foul that costs two minutes, never one that can hurt someone. A trip can cost you your game in several leagues. In some, a reckless hook or trip gets you thrown out even if nobody is hurt. And one meant to hurt someone is a match penalty in most leagues.

PLAYER: What doesn't pass?

COACH: Retaliation. Sending a message. Sticking up for a teammate after the whistle. And in the first period, nothing at all except stopping a certain goal. There are forty minutes left to pay for it. And never pin the puck on the boards for a whistle because you're tired and can't change. That's a delay-of-game penalty, and a power play against you is worse than a tired shift.

PLAYER: So if I'm chasing a breakaway from behind?

COACH: If you're chasing from behind, and the only way to stop them is a foul, you're probably saving nothing. With your net empty, you're saving nothing at all. The time to stop the breakaway is four seconds earlier, with your positioning.

COACH: Never slew-foot. That means using your leg or foot to knock a player's feet out from under them, sometimes while pushing their upper body back. It puts them down on their back with nothing to break the fall. Once it's called, it ends your game in most leagues, and it can in every league. Touching the puck first doesn't excuse it.

COACH: One more in front of your own net, and this one has a free option. Picture a puck in the air in your crease, and you're not the goalie. What would you do?

PLAYER: Catch it and get rid of it?

COACH: You may catch it, but put it straight down to the ice, or just knock it down. That costs nothing, in any league. If you hold on to it, skate with it, turn with it or throw it, that's what costs you. And if you're not the goalie, never fall on the puck, hold it, or cover it with your hand. Anywhere on the ice, that's a penalty. If you do it in your own crease, plan on a penalty shot. With your own goalie pulled, treat it as a goal against.

COACH: Most penalties in amateur hockey aren't trades at all. Nobody weighed the odds. They look more like habits, which is what coaches see rather than anything measured. Two habits cover most of it. A stick foul that's really a skating foul, like a hook because you stopped skating twenty feet ago. And retaliation, like a slash after losing a battle, or a cross-check after the whistle. Fix the first with your skating, and the second with your composure. Use that to fix your own game, not as a fact about everyone else's.

PLAYER: What about selling a call? Going down to get a power play?

COACH: That's one penalty that's a decision, and a bad one. In most leagues, diving to draw a call is a penalty of its own, and it can hand them the power play you hoped to get.

COACH: Let me pull the scoreboard and penalties together. Take risk down when you lead, right down by three, and up when you trail late. Shorthanded, clear it unless you've really read a two-on-one. A minor gives them about a one-in-five chance, so take one only to stop a certain goal, and only a foul that costs two minutes, never one that can hurt someone or get you thrown out. Never from behind on a breakaway. Stop the breakaway earlier, with your positioning. Never slew-foot. And catch a puck in the air only to put it straight down.

COACH: Sixteenth idea. The four seconds after you give the puck away. Everybody makes bad decisions, at every level. What separates good players is almost entirely what happens in the next four seconds. So picture it. You've just lost the puck at their blue line. What's your first move?

PLAYER: Chase it. Get it back.

COACH: That's the natural one, and it's usually wrong. One. Turn and skate. Not toward the puck. Skate to the middle of the ice, between the puck and your net, and become the extra defender. Chasing the carrier from behind gets you nothing. If they're clear on a breakaway, fouling them gives them a penalty shot.

PLAYER: My old coach wanted the first forward back to chase the carrier.

COACH: Some teams coach exactly that, on purpose. That's fine. The first forward back pressures the carrier, while the defence holds the inside. If that's your job, pressure with your feet and your stick, not a hit, and if you can see their numbers, pull up. The usual approach is getting to the middle. Find out which yours plays, rather than making it up on the ice.

COACH: Two. Get to the inside. Your job now is to take away the middle, not to win the puck back. Stop the pass into the slot, and a scoring chance becomes a shot from the outside. Three. Talk. One word does it. Turnover. Back. I'm high. That one word reorganises four teammates who are still reading the play. Saying nothing is the second mistake. Four. Finish the shift. Don't change while the play is live because you're embarrassed. That turns a giveaway into a giveaway plus five against four.

COACH: And never solve the chase with your body. Never hit a player who can't see you coming, and never from behind. If you can see their numbers, the numbers on the back of their jersey, pull up and play the puck. Even where your team pressures the carrier, that pressure is your feet and your stick. A hit on a player's back ends your game in most leagues once it's called, and it can in every league. If you meant to hurt them, it's a match penalty in most leagues. Driving them from behind into the boards when they can't protect themselves is a match penalty in several.

PLAYER: And after that? I usually want to make up for it.

COACH: That's the trap. The worst part of a bad decision is often the next one, the one meant to fix it. You give the puck away, you feel it, and thirty seconds later you force something. A pinch you haven't read. A cross-ice pass. A hit you leave your feet for. That second mistake is nearly always worse. The first was a play that didn't come off. The second is a bad choice made on purpose. The next play is a new play. If the simple option was right before your turnover, it's still right after it.

PLAYER: What do I do after the game?

COACH: Watch the video if you have it. Ask yourself, what did I see, and what was actually there? The gap between those two answers is what to work on. Bad decisions are the most coachable thing in hockey. Unlike a shot or a stride, they can be fixed in a conversation.

COACH: Let me pull it all together.

COACH: Before you decide, think about where you are, and move to better ice. Your own slot is the worst place to lose the puck. The middle of the neutral zone is high risk too, and their corners are nearly free. Losing it while it travels toward your net is worse than losing it going away.

COACH: Get the puck out of your own slot first, and never pass it or skate it across the front of your own goal. D-to-D goes behind the net. One defenceman to the puck, one at home, and say which. Then the boards position. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to anyone coming at you, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly. If you end up facing the boards, stick and gloves up on the glass, spread wide, head off it.

COACH: Simple in your end, creative in theirs. No panic passes. Off the glass, never over it. Under pressure in your own end, never hold the puck. Take the first option on the checklist that's really there. With time, keep the puck.

COACH: Let the scoreboard set your risk. Before a chance, ask three things. Information or a feeling? Who is behind me? What does the score allow? Stay on your skates, and go for the puck with your stick, not a hit. Take a penalty only to stop a certain goal, only one that costs two minutes, and stop the breakaway four seconds earlier instead. After a turnover, get to the inside, talk, finish the shift, and never solve it with your body. If you can see their numbers, pull up.

PLAYER: What should I ask my own coach?

COACH: Much of this is your team's choice, so three questions. Where do you draw the line on a D-to-D or a cross-ice pass high in our end? After a turnover, do we get to the inside, or pressure the carrier? And on a two-on-one, do you want us holding the lane, or sliding? And one for your league. Is body checking allowed, at our age group and in our games? Their answers tell you where your team wants you to take your risks, and now you know why each answer makes sense.
