COACH: First idea. Where you shoot from matters more than how you shoot. Start with the slot. That's the ice in front of the net, between the two faceoff circles, from the edge of the crease out to the tops of the circles. The crease is the goalie's area, the blue paint in front of the net. The top part of the slot is called the high slot. Everything outside the middle, along the boards and up by the blue line, is the perimeter.

PLAYER: And a slot shot is better. I know that much.

COACH: It's not slightly better. A shot from the slot goes in several times as often as a shot from the perimeter. Coaches agree on that gap. The exact numbers you'll hear come from a source that doesn't say what data it used, so trust the gap, not the numbers.

PLAYER: So what do I actually do with that?

COACH: Here's the habit. If a teammate is in the slot and you're not, pass it to them. If two strides toward the middle would turn your shot into a slot shot, take the two strides. One pass or two strides like that are worth more than any shooting technique. The most useful part of shooting has nothing to do with your hands.

PLAYER: Surely a really good shooter can score from further out.

COACH: One analysis looked at two of the best shooters in the professional game. One of them took over a fifth of their shots from above the faceoff circles. Less than one goal in fifty of theirs came from up there. If shooters that good can't score from there, you can't either. So get to the middle, or get the puck to someone who's already there. That is what the whole attack is built to do.

COACH: Second idea. Get the shot away before the goalie is set. Two words first. Release speed is how long your shooting motion takes, from your first movement until the puck leaves your stick. Shot speed is how fast the puck travels after it leaves. Which one do you think matters more?

PLAYER: Shot speed. A harder shot is harder to stop.

COACH: Release speed, by a long way. A goalie doesn't stop the puck by reacting to it. A goalie reads you. PLAYER: So how do I get it off quicker?

PLAYER: But I shoot harder when I plant.

COACH: That's why it feels weaker at first, shooting in stride, while you're still moving. You can't plant and load. Your momentum gives you the power back, and the goalie gets less warning.

COACH: Good. Now the shots themselves. There are six things you can do to send a puck at the net, and you'll use three of them all the time.

COACH: Eighth idea. The wrist shot, the one everything else is built on. Start the puck behind your back foot, the foot furthest from the net.

PLAYER: Why back there and not beside me?

COACH: It gives you a runway. If you start it beside your foot, there's nothing to speed up through. Hold the stick firmly, but not tightly. PLAYER: Where does the snap come from, then?

COACH: Your hands, working against each other. Push down with your bottom hand and pull back with your top hand. That bends the shaft, and the bend stores power, like a bow. As the puck leaves, roll your wrists over so it spins. A spinning puck flies flat. A wobbling one goes wherever it likes.

PLAYER: And how do I keep it low, or get it up?

COACH: With your follow-through. Finish low and the puck stays low. Finish high and it rises. You aim the height with where your follow-through ends up, not with your eyes. And you can aim a finish. You can't measure a blade angle in the middle of a shot. Use the wrist shot almost anywhere you have a moment of time. Off the rush, in the slot, off a rebound, from the half-wall. It's your most accurate shot, and it needs the least room.

COACH: Ninth idea. The snap shot, which is probably your shot inside the faceoff dots. It's quicker. The puck sits beside or just behind your skate, about where it is when you're stickhandling. PLAYER: So what does skipping that give me?

COACH: You're already in an attacking stance, so nothing about your body tells the goalie a shot is coming. Move your bottom hand lower than for a wrist shot, to about the middle of the shaft. Drop the blade just behind the puck. Push down with your bottom hand and pull back with your top hand to load the shaft, like cocking a spring. Then release almost straight away. You get most of the power of a slap shot, with most of the quick release of a wrist shot. That's why, at higher levels, it has become the shot players use most.

PLAYER: Which foot should my weight be on?

COACH: Coaches disagree on that part. Most teach moving your weight from back foot to front foot, as on the wrist shot. Some teach shooting onto the leg nearest the puck instead, giving up a little power to gain time. So with a moment to spare, go back to front. Without one, don't waste a stride getting onto the right foot. Shoot off whichever foot your weight is already on. Every coach agrees on the thing to avoid. Never shoot flat-footed with your arms alone. The snap shot won't beat a goalie from the blue line. From twenty feet, it will, again and again.

COACH: Tenth idea. Practise the backhand, the shot off the back of your blade. Be honest. How often do you practise yours?

PLAYER: Almost never.

COACH: Almost nobody does, and goalies find it harder to stop. Goalies face far more forehands, so their timing is tuned to the forehand. A backhand comes off a different point, at a different moment, with less warning. Coaches see this, though nobody has counted it.

PLAYER: How do I shoot one?

COACH: Keep the puck close to your body. Hands a little wider apart than on the forehand. Sweep it across in an arc, not straight at the net, and follow through at the target. Use it in tight around the net, on breakaways, and on rebounds that land on your backhand side. Anywhere turning to your forehand would cost you the chance.

COACH: Eleventh idea. The slap shot, and why it's usually the wrong choice. It's the hardest shot in hockey, and the one players use too often. It takes the longest to set up. It's the least accurate. And it's the easiest to read and block.

PLAYER: But when I do take one, how should it work?

COACH: Lift the stick back for the wind-up. Hands wide, with your bottom hand at the middle of the shaft or below. Then hit the ice an inch or two behind the puck, not the puck.

PLAYER: The ice? That sounds wrong.

COACH: It does. Hitting the ice bends the shaft, and the shaft springs the blade into the puck. If you hit the puck directly, you get an arm-powered slap with none of that stored power. COACH: Thirteenth idea. The one-timer. That's shooting a pass straight away, without stopping it first. It's the quickest way to move a shot from one side of the ice to the other, and that is exactly what makes a goalie move sideways.

COACH: Fifteenth idea. What goalie contact costs. Never start contact with a goalie, anywhere on the ice. A goalie outside the crease is not fair game either.

PLAYER: Why are the rules so strict about goalies?

COACH: Any contact with a goalie can be a penalty, it can get you thrown out of the game, and it can cost your team the goal. A goalie who is turned away, or down, can't protect themselves, so contact can hurt them badly. If you charge a goalie, plan on being thrown out of the game. That can happen in every league, and in some it's automatic, even if nobody is hurt. Building up speed into contact, even with two hard strides, can be charging. So pull up early.

PLAYER: And if the goalie's turned away from me?

COACH: Then contact on their back is checking from behind. Never go into a player's back, goalie or not. No league prices that at a bare two minutes, and in almost every league it puts you out of the game. If you drive a goalie who can't protect themselves into the net, some leagues make it an automatic match penalty. That goes past being thrown out. If the contact reaches the head, that can put you out of the game in every league, and in some it's automatic. In some leagues even a careless hit does it. And being thrown out often isn't the end of it. In some leagues it also costs you the games after.

PLAYER: Let me try the front of the net. Screen from outside the paint and off the red line, and leave the goalie room to move. Never start contact with a goalie, in the crease or out of it. If I'm shoved in, feet out first, and don't play the puck or touch the goalie on the way. Stay out of the road home.

PLAYER: Head up, chin off my chest, and know where the post is. And pull up short of the goalie every time, because even two hard strides into contact can be charging.

COACH: Spot on. Now the big moments. The rush, the two-on-one and the breakaway.

COACH: Sixteenth idea. Shooting off the rush. A rush is when you're attacking with speed, before the defence is set. These are some of the best chances in the game, and most are wasted by shooting a beat too late. Picture it. You're carrying the puck wide toward the net. The defender is skating backwards, with their stick out toward the puck. When do you shoot?

PLAYER: When I get to a good spot, closer in.

COACH: That's the beat too late. There's a window, and it closes when that stick arrives. Shoot as the stick is closing, not after. If you wait for a good spot, you're shooting into a blocked lane from a bad angle. And shoot off the foot you don't normally shoot off. It gets the puck away earlier in your stride, at a moment the goalie doesn't expect a shot. It costs a little speed, and it's worth it.

PLAYER: Which foot is that, though?

COACH: Check, because it depends on your wing. Coaches often say the outside foot, nearest the boards. That's right on your strong wing, say a left-handed shot on the left wing. On your off wing, your normal shooting foot is already the outside one. So there, the unusual foot is the inside one. If you get the label wrong, you just practise the habit you already have.

COACH: Don't stop your feet. The rush shot is the purest case for shooting in stride. On a wide rush, the goalie hugs the near post, so the far side, low, is open. If you cut to the middle, the five-hole opens as the goalie pushes across. And know what's behind you. A trailer is a teammate arriving late, behind the play. If one is coming into the high slot, hold the puck a moment and pass back to them. That often beats your own shot from the wing, because it turns an outside shot into a slot shot.

COACH: Now the two-on-one, two of you against one defender, with the puck on your stick. What would you do?

PLAYER: Pass across early, to my partner.

COACH: Not straight away. Drive at the net as if you'll shoot, head up, so the defender has to choose. If the defender takes the pass away, shoot. The common choice is low at the far pad, because the rebound goes to your partner. If the defender comes at you, pass across early and flat, before the goalie can set. That's the common approach. Some coaches want a shot every time instead. Others want the pass whenever the defender commits. Ask which yours wants. Either way, follow it in and pull up short of the goalie.

COACH: Seventeenth idea. Breakaways. A breakaway is you, the puck and the goalie, with no defender close enough to stop you. One thing decides it. A well-coached goalie's whole plan is to make you move first. They come out to meet you, back in with you to keep the angle, stay on their feet, and watch the puck. Your plan is the mirror image. Make the goalie move first.

PLAYER: So I just wait them out?

COACH: You can't just wait. If nobody commits, you run out of ice and lose. So you need ways to force it. The first is your speed. Come in slower than you feel like. Skate hard to the blue line, ease off through the tops of the circles, then speed up again into your move.

PLAYER: Slower? On a breakaway?

COACH: At full speed you get about one option, and the goalie gets an easy read on where you'll be. The slow-down pulls the goalie out of their rhythm, and the speed-up arrives before they've reset. A move also needs room, and you can't pull the puck across your body once you're past the post. That change of pace beating raw speed is coaching know-how, not a measured finding.

COACH: The second way is fooling the goalie with the puck, not just your head. A good goalie ignores head and shoulder fakes first. So change where the puck is, which forces a real re-read. Look at one corner and put the puck in the other. Sell a shot, and if the goalie gets big and stops moving, that's the moment to deke, which means a fake with the puck. And don't decide your move at the blue line. Decide once you can see what the goalie is giving you.

PLAYER: How do I know what they're giving me?

COACH: Match the move to the goalie. Out of the crease and coming at you, deke. Go forehand to backhand, round them, because they can't get back across from out there. Go round them, never through them. If the room closes, pull up short of the goalie and the post, head up. Running into a goalie is a penalty in every league, and charging one can end your game.

COACH: Deep in the net and square, shoot. A deke against a deep goalie just walks you into the post. Backing in and not yet square, shoot early. Pushing across, go five-hole, because it opens during the push. Stabbing at the puck with their stick, pull it back inside the stick, then shoot. And if you're being caught from behind, shoot now. A backchecker, a forward racing back to defend, gets a stick to you sooner than you think.

PLAYER: That's a lot of moves.

COACH: You need two. Have one move you can do without thinking, then add a second that goes the other way. On a breakaway, blocker side high is the classic target. It's the hardest place for a goalie to get a hand to while backing in. Never the middle of the chest. That's where the puck goes when you shoot without looking. So in the last two strides, look at the goalie, not the puck. You can't aim at a hole you haven't seen. And at rec level, shoot more than you deke. Rec goalies are more often deep and square, and a deke against a deep goalie has nowhere to go. That's what coaches see, not a count.

COACH: Eighteenth idea. Penalty shots and shootouts. A penalty shot is a breakaway the referee gives you, from centre ice. A shootout is a series of them, to settle a tied game.

PLAYER: Is it just a breakaway, then?

COACH: Mostly, with a few differences. Plan on one shot. Keep the puck moving toward the goal the whole way. You can't stop, or circle back to set yourself again. And a rebound almost never counts, so the first shot has to be the one. The main rulebooks set no time limit on the way in, so take your time. Just keep the puck moving.

PLAYER: Can I try something fancy?

COACH: Leave out the spin-o-rama, a full turn on the way in, unless you know your league allows it. In some leagues it stops the shot and there's no goal. The lacrosse-style move means lifting the puck onto your blade and whipping it in. If you try it, keep the puck below your shoulders, because that limit applies in every league. And release it below the crossbar.

COACH: Shootout formats differ between competitions. Some let the same player shoot again, and some don't. Rec and youth leagues often run their own. So ask before the game. If the run-up is shorter, lean toward shooting, because you have less room to change pace.

PLAYER: Let me give back the big moments. Off the rush, shoot as the defender's stick closes, in stride, off the foot I don't normally use. Know where the trailer is. On a two-on-one, drive the net and make the defender choose. On a breakaway, come in slower, make the goalie move first, and look at the goalie in the last two strides. On a penalty shot, keep the puck moving and plan on one shot.

COACH: That's it. Last part. Practice, and your stick.

COACH: Nineteenth idea. How to practise. You can practise most of shooting alone, off the ice, with a shooting pad, a net and a bucket of pucks.

PLAYER: I do that already. I just fire a hundred pucks.

COACH: That's one kind of practice. Lots of repetitions build the motion. Focused practice builds the shot you'll actually use. That means a named target and a reason for every shot. So pick a target, call the shot out loud, take it, and keep count. Practise the release, not the power. Make a rule, no gathering. The puck arrives, you shoot. Move the puck sideways before you shoot, because you won't get a still puck in a game. Practise off both feet, including the one you don't like. Practise the backhand every session. And practise the shots you don't like, the off-balance ones and the pucks that arrive behind you.

PLAYER: What can't I do on my own?

COACH: Tips and rebounds can't be practised on a garage pad, so do those on the ice. Take breakaways at the end of practice against a real goalie, and keep score. Start from your own blue line, so you practise the change of pace too. And fifteen to twenty minutes a day of focused work beats two hours on a Sunday.

COACH: When you practise against a real goalie, protect them. Don't shoot at your goalie's head in the warm-up, and don't fire pucks at them from ten feet in a drill.

PLAYER: It's only the warm-up, though.

COACH: Warm-up or not, the care is yours. No rule covers it, so it's a coaching caution. And don't count on a whistle to protect them. In most leagues, play doesn't stop straight away for an injured player unless it looks serious, and a cage hides how hurt a goalie is. And never shoot on the other team's goalie in the warm-up. Each team warms up on its own half of the ice. Breaking that can get you a penalty, or reported to your league.

COACH: Twentieth idea, and it's short. Your stick. If your stick is too stiff to bend on a wrist shot, you lose power and your release gets slower. The flex number printed on the shaft tells you how stiff it is. A common starting point is about half your body weight in pounds.

PLAYER: I cut mine down. Does that matter?

COACH: Cutting a stick down makes it stiffer than that number. So if you'll cut more than about three inches off, buy a lower flex. Then stand normally in your skates and check that the whole bottom edge of the blade touches the ice. Then stop thinking about it. A stick that fits won't make you a better shooter. It just stops the stick holding you back.

COACH: Let me pull it all together.

COACH: Where you shoot from matters most. Get to the slot, or pass to a teammate who's there. Shoot before the goalie is set, in stride, without gathering. Get the puck across the middle first, or put a body in the goalie's eyes.

COACH: At the net, screen from outside the paint and off the red line. Never touch the goalie, and stay out of their road home. Know where the post is, take a shove braced, head up, chin off your chest. Tip with soft hands and the blade below everyone's shoulders, follow every shot, and pull up short of the goalie.

COACH: Find a lane before you shoot, and aim where the goalie isn't. Inside the dots, the snap shot. Look before you wind up, keep the blade below everyone's shoulders, and in a CARHA league, no slap shot at all. Off the rush, shoot early and in stride. On a breakaway, make the goalie move first.

PLAYER: What should I ask my own coach?

COACH: Three things, and two for your league. Do you want us aiming for the corners, or shooting low to make rebounds? Am I a one-timer option on our power play? On a two-on-one, do you want the shot or the pass? And for the league, which rules do we play under, and is the slap shot allowed? Ask, because these are your team's and your league's choices. Their answers tell you how your team wants you to shoot, and now you know the reason behind each one.
