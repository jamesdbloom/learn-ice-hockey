COACH: Picture this. It's your first night at a rink. You've paid at the desk, you've got a bag of gear you bought last week, and you're about to join a game. You don't know whether anyone in it is allowed to hit you. Nobody has told you, and you didn't know to ask.

PLAYER: And the gear's probably not what the session wanted anyway.

COACH: Quite possibly. This is the detailed episode on getting started. It's for anyone starting ice hockey, or coming back to it after a long time away, and there's a part on how children start too. I'm the coach, and with me is a player who's been playing a while and still remembers what starting felt like. We'll go one piece at a time, and for each piece you'll hear what to do and the reason for it. If you know the reason, you can still do the right thing when your rink does things a little differently.

COACH: Most of starting hockey isn't about skill. It's about asking the right person the right question, before you need the answer. We'll start with four big ideas, because they matter most. If you only hear the first part, take those four with you. After that we go through the rest in the order you'll meet it. Getting in, your kit and your paperwork, hockey in Britain, your first session, and your first few weeks.

COACH: First idea. Find out whether your league allows body checking. Do it before your first game, not after your first hit. Body checking means using your body, on purpose, to knock an opponent off the puck. Some leagues allow it and some don't.

PLAYER: Isn't every game a bit physical anyway?

COACH: There's bumping in every game, when two players go for the same puck. That's body contact, and it's a separate thing from body checking. So ask about both. Now, say you ring the rink desk and ask whether your league allows checking. What do you think you'll get?

PLAYER: An answer, I'd hope. They run the place.

COACH: They run the ice, and they sell it. They can't answer this one. Whether your league allows checking is set by your age group, your league and your country. It's written down, in a rulebook or in a competition's own rules, and nobody at the rink can change it. So ask your coach, or your league secretary. That's the person who runs the league's admin.

PLAYER: Why does this come before kit and everything else?

COACH: Almost everything else about starting is local, and you can sort it out as you go. This one isn't local, and you need the answer before your first game, not after it.

PLAYER: What about a drop-in that calls itself non-contact?

COACH: That's a different question, and people mix the two up. A drop-in is a casual game you pay for at the door. If it calls itself non-contact, that's a house rule, a rule the rink sets for that session. The desk can tell you what it means that night. But it tells you nothing about your league.

PLAYER: And an adult beginner ends up in a non-contact group anyway, surely?

COACH: Not necessarily. Adult hockey isn't all non-contact. Some adult leagues allow checking, in North America and in Britain, and being a beginner doesn't put you in a non-checking group. So when you ring a league about joining, ask in the same call whether it's a checking league. And for a child, ask before you choose where they play, because whether checking is allowed depends on their age and their division.

COACH: Second idea. Before you turn up to any session, ask three things. What gear do I need? What standard is it? And is there contact? Picture three posters on a rink wall. Stick-and-puck. Drop-in. Shinny.

PLAYER: I'd guess stick-and-puck is practice and drop-in is a game. Shinny, I'm not sure.

COACH: That's the usual meaning. Stick-and-puck is usually open ice for practising on your own. There are lots of pucks, and no game. Drop-in is usually a pick-up game. You pay at the door, you get a jersey colour, and you play. Shinny is informal pick-up hockey, and its house rules vary enormously. But notice the word usually. Those names are loose labels, and rinks use them differently. So a session's name tells you almost nothing. The rink's own description of the session is what counts. If it doesn't say, ring and ask.

PLAYER: Why those three questions in particular?

COACH: Each one saves you a different bad night. Gear first. Some stick-and-puck sessions need only a helmet and gloves. Most drop-ins need full equipment, and they'll turn you away without it. It's also the easiest to avoid, because rinks run separate sessions so you can choose. An advanced drop-in is no place to learn to stop.

PLAYER: And contact?

COACH: Turning up to a full-contact session expecting a skills session is how people get hurt. And while you're asking, ask whether lifting the puck off the ice is allowed. Some sessions ban it. That one's a house rule too.

COACH: Third idea. Learn to skate first, or alongside. You don't need to be good.

PLAYER: I'd have thought shooting mattered more.

COACH: Skating is what sorts players into levels. Not shooting, and not how well you read the game. That comes from what coaches see, not from a study. And skating decides how fast you get into real games. So work hardest on two things. Stopping on both sides, and skating backwards. Until you can do both, you belong in a beginner group, however well you handle a puck.

COACH: Now, choosing your level. Say there are two groups, and you think you're somewhere between them. Which one would you pick?

PLAYER: The higher one. I'd improve faster against better players.

COACH: Most people think that. Start one level below where your pride puts you. That's judgement, not a rule, but there's a real reason behind it. Moving up part way through a season is easy, and people welcome it. Moving down is awkward, and it usually comes after a bad injury or a bad month.

PLAYER: So the two mistakes don't cost the same.

COACH: Not at all. Put yourself a level too high, and you spend the season hanging on, with a real chance of getting hurt. Put yourself a level too low, and you spend it improving. So lean low.

COACH: Fourth idea. On the ice, two habits carry a new player further than any skill. Short shifts, and eyes up. A shift is one turn on the ice, from the moment you go on until you come off and a teammate replaces you. How long do you think a good one lasts?

PLAYER: A couple of minutes?

COACH: Well inside one. Go hard, and come off. A well-managed shift lasts thirty to forty-five seconds. Your first shifts will run long, because you don't yet know when to leave. That's the biggest reason your first sessions feel so exhausting.

PLAYER: Wouldn't it be smarter to pace myself and stay out longer?

COACH: Hockey is short bursts of flat-out effort. A tired player is slower and sloppier. And at a drop-in, a long shift isn't saving your energy. Nobody is managing the ice time, so a long shift is you taking someone else's turn.

COACH: Now eyes up. You'll barely touch the puck. That's true at every level, from beginner to professional, and it isn't a sign you played badly. Most of a shift happens away from the puck.

PLAYER: So what do I do with all that time?

COACH: Look around. Where are your teammates? Where's the space? Who's coming? A glance while a pass is on its way to you costs nothing. A glance after it lands does cost you, because by then someone is on you. That's how new players end up panic passing, which means getting rid of the puck without looking, usually to nobody. Get your look in early, so you already know where it's going when it lands.

COACH: Two safety points belong here, because they matter most. The first is the goalie. You've got a puck on your stick, and the goalie at the far end is having a drink of water. Do you shoot?

PLAYER: It's only a warm-up shot.

COACH: Never shoot at a goalie who isn't ready. A goalie is ready when they're facing you, set, with their stick down. A goalie who is drinking, fixing a strap or talking to someone is not ready. Shoot at a net, not at a person. This one carries real injury risk, and it's also the one that makes people angriest.

COACH: The second is the boards. That's the wall around the rink, with the glass on top of it. Sooner or later you'll race someone for a loose puck and end up there. On the way to that puck, go for the puck, not the player. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call, or your session's. Never hit a player who can't see you coming, and never from behind. If you can see their numbers, pull up.

PLAYER: And when I get to the wall myself?

COACH: Decide how you'll meet it on the way in. There's no time to decide when you arrive, but you usually have several strides to choose. Best of all, go in at an angle rather than straight at the wall. Then a side, an arm or a leg takes the knock, and your head stays clear. If you can't avoid meeting it square on, turn your skates parallel to the boards, so you're side-on to them. Forearm and hip to the boards. Never the point of your shoulder, and not the whole shoulder if you can help it. Head up. Chin off your chest. And two things you never do. Never turn your back to anyone coming at you, and never duck. With your back turned, you can't see the hit coming or brace for it. And a tucked chin can break your neck, even at walking speed.

COACH: That's when things have gone wrong. Get your stick and gloves up on the glass, spread wide, with your head off it. And all of this holds whether or not your session allows contact. COACH: Everyone else there is new too. One example ends every session with a guided game of half an hour. Blocks vary a lot in length and price, even inside one league, and they change every season. So don't budget from a price you find online. Ask the club for this season's figure.

COACH: Second, stick-and-puck, drop-in or shinny. There's no coach, and no registration beyond a waiver, a form you sign before you skate. You pay at the door. That matters most once you're on a team that gets one slot a week.

COACH: Third, an adult recreational league. That's a season of scheduled games, usually split into divisions by standard. Some leagues take single players and put them on a team. Others only take whole teams.

PLAYER: So I might not need a whole team of friends?

COACH: You might not. Ask which, before you assume you do. And ask in the same call whether it's a checking league.

COACH: Fourth, a club with an adult section. In England, lots of clubs give adults a way to stay in the sport, even if they don't want to play at league level. Asking to try it first is normal. England Ice Hockey's rules even include a short trial registration for it, which we'll come back to.

PLAYER: How do I choose a level for any of these?

COACH: Three things help. Watch a game at the level you're thinking about. Twenty minutes in the stands beats any description. Watch whether players can stop on both sides, take a hard pass, and change on the fly without crashing into each other. Changing on the fly means swapping players while the game keeps going. You skate to your bench, and a teammate jumps on in your place.

COACH: Then ask the league or the club to place you. They do it all the time. They'd rather place you right than lose you, and they usually judge better than you do. And ask for a trial or a skills session before you commit. That's standard practice at most clubs, and free at many.

PLAYER: The level names never make much sense to me.

COACH: They won't help you much. Local adult leagues use letters, numbers or words, like A to D, or division one to six, or beginner up to elite. They mean different things in different places, so ask what each level means at that league. And think about time. For an adult, a recreational season is usually one game a week, plus any extra ice you buy.

COACH: Now a word on playing goal. Ever thought about it?

PLAYER: I've wondered.

COACH: Then say so early, and loudly. Goalies are scarce at every level of amateur hockey, so a goalie place is often free or cheap, though not always. Ask rather than assume. Clubs often own goal equipment and lend it out. Borrow the pads, the blocker, the catching glove and the chest protector. But buy your own mask. A goalie mask counts as a helmet, and a shared one is where borrowing stops. You can't see a crushed liner or a hairline crack, and you can't know what a shared mask has been hit by.

COACH: Seventh idea. How children start. The stages are the same, and there are named programmes for each one. For a first taste, USA Hockey runs Try Hockey For Free. PLAYER: Does a child need to learn to skate first, like an adult?

COACH: Then a learn-to-play block. In the United States that's Learn to Play, run by the NHL and its players' association. It's for first-time players aged four to nine. In Canada it's The First Shift, a six-week programme for ages six to ten, with kit included. Find it through Hockey Canada's own website. PLAYER: And is everyone in an age group at the same level?

COACH: Usually there are levels. In Canada, house league is the recreational level. Players almost always get equal ice time, whatever their skill, and it takes a few hours a week. COACH: The youngest age groups play on part of the rink, across it or on half of it, rather than the whole sheet. The small surface is the point, not a compromise. PLAYER: What about a child starting in England?

In university hockey in England and Scotland, and in Scottish recreational hockey, you need at least a half visor. And in England, wear your full kit, helmet included, in the warm-up before a game.

COACH: One rule worth knowing early, because North American material may tell you otherwise. Never hit anyone from behind. If you can see their numbers, play the puck instead. In the rulebook British hockey plays, there's no two-minute penalty for it at all. Plan on a major penalty and being thrown out of the game. And at a club in England or Wales outside the Elite League, expect a ban on top.

PLAYER: And the two minutes I've read about?

COACH: North American coaching material may mention two minutes for it. That's never the whole penalty. Extra penalties come on top, in some leagues it always ends your game, and a hit from behind into the boards can end your game in any league, and in some it costs you more games after that.

COACH: And look at the rink you're actually standing on. Nearly every diagram and distance in hockey coaching comes from the NHL's rink, two hundred feet by eighty-five. The international rulebook allows rinks of different widths, and some British rinks are smaller still.

PLAYER: Does a few feet really change anything?

COACH: On a wide rink the corners are further from the net. So a distance you learned from a diagram may not be yours. Learn the names of the areas from coaching material, but not the distances.

COACH: Give me Britain back.

PLAYER: My rules are the international ones, with British changes, not the NHL's. Wear my neck guard every time I go on the ice, adults included, and expect no warning. Put a junior in a cage. Never hit anyone from behind. And learn the names of the areas, not the distances.

COACH: Eleventh idea. Your first session, before you get there. Before you leave the house, confirm the session, the time and the gear. Do it by phone or from the rink's own page, not from a friend's memory. And pack the night before.

PLAYER: What do people usually forget?

COACH: A mouthguard and tape. Bring water and a towel. Bring something to wear on the walk back to the car, because you'll be soaked. And bring your paperwork if you haven't registered online.

COACH: Now the time. Your session starts at eight. When would you get there?

PLAYER: Quarter to?

COACH: On time in hockey means dressed and ready before the stated time. That means arriving forty-five to sixty minutes before a game, and twenty to thirty before a practice. For a genuine first session, arrive a full hour early. Dressing takes twice as long the first three times as it will afterwards. Learn to Play asks children to arrive at least an hour before their first session, to check in and get dressed.

COACH: Twelfth idea. The dressing room, and getting on the ice. In the dressing room, take a free spot near the door and ask if it's somebody's. Keep your bag under the bench, not spread across your neighbour. Say hello. Listen more than you talk. And leave the goalie alone in the last few minutes before you go on.

PLAYER: What I'd worry about is people judging my skating.

COACH: Nobody will comment on your gear or your skating. What people do notice is someone who takes up three spaces, or who's still lacing skates when the ice is ready. When you get on the ice, step on and skate. A couple of easy laps, the same way as everyone else. Don't stand at the gate. And don't skate through the crease. That's the painted blue area in front of each net. Goalies clear it of snow, and they treat it as theirs. Skate round it.

COACH: Thirteenth idea. How a drill line works. If you've never been to a coached practice, this is what confuses people most. What follows is the usual way, but it varies by coach and by rink, so watch what others do. The coach explains the drill, and usually shows it once. Get close enough to hear, or you'll go the wrong way. Players form lines, usually in the corners. Join the shortest line, not the one with the good players.

PLAYER: How do I know when to go?

COACH: On the whistle, or when the player in front is about halfway through. Watch the first two groups go before your turn. That's what the first two groups are for. Go at full speed, and get out of the way at the end. The most common beginner mistake is finishing a drill and then standing exactly where the next player needs to be.

COACH: Say you've gone the wrong way halfway through a drill. What would you do?

PLAYER: Stop and say sorry, probably.

COACH: Keep skating and rejoin the line. Nobody cares. Stopping in the middle of the ice to apologise is what causes the collision. And when the whistle goes, stop what you're doing and look at the coach. Shooting pucks while someone is talking gets you noticed for the wrong reason.

COACH: Fourteenth idea. Drop-in habits. No governing body runs your local drop-in. So these are habits, not rules, and they vary by rink. But they're close to universal. Change quickly. At a drop-in, nobody manages the ice time. So head to the bench when the puck is going the other way, and call for your replacement. The player coming on waits until you're within a stick's length of the boards.

PLAYER: What else?

COACH: Let faster groups play through. Look before you cross the ice, and don't skate through someone's drill. Collect your own pucks. Don't shoot into a group. And never shoot at the far net if someone is near it collecting pucks.

PLAYER: How hard should I play?

COACH: Match how hard you play to the session, and to the least willing player in the battle. Adult drop-in is usually non-contact. But that's a house rule, so check at the desk. Even where contact is allowed, nobody at a Tuesday drop-in is being scouted. The skills that matter there need no hit at all, and they annoy nobody.

COACH: Pass to the new player. The person who does that on your first night is the reason you come back. Next year, you can do the same for someone else. Get off promptly at the buzzer. Ice time is rented by the minute, and the next group is waiting at the gate. And thank the goalie, with two taps on the pads at the end. They paid to be there too, and they spent the session stopping your shots.

COACH: That's your first session. Run it back for me.

PLAYER: Confirm it, pack the night before, and arrive a full hour early. In the dressing room, take a spot near the door and listen more than I talk. Step on and skate, and stay out of the crease. In a drill, go full speed and get out of the way at the end. When the whistle goes, look at the coach. At a drop-in, change quickly, collect my own pucks, and play at the level of the least willing player in the battle.

COACH: Fifteenth idea. Your first few weeks, and what your body will tell you. You'll be more tired than you expect. Hockey is short bursts of flat-out effort, not steady exercise.

PLAYER: So it's my lungs that go first?

COACH: Your legs go before your lungs. The hockey stance, knees bent and low, works the front of your thighs and your inner thighs like nothing else. Groin and inner-thigh strains are the classic hockey overuse injury. Expect cold hands and feet for the first ten minutes, then heat. Expect sore feet in new skates. Expect bruises you don't remember getting, even in non-contact hockey.

PLAYER: How do I tell normal soreness from an injury?

COACH: A day-two ache in your hip flexors and lower back is usually the stance, not an injury. But pain that's sharp, on one side, or still there after a few days is different. That's a question for a qualified medical professional.

COACH: And build up gradually. Picture someone who's done nothing for four months, then signs up for five sessions in the first week.

PLAYER: Keen, at least.

COACH: Keen, and at risk. Coaches say the classic way amateurs get hurt is a sudden jump in how much they do, exactly like that. That's a coaching observation, not a measured ranking of injuries. So don't take it to mean collisions are the smaller risk.

COACH: Sixteenth idea. Knocks to the head. This matters in your first weeks, and in every week after. A concussion is an injury to the brain. It can come from a knock to the head, from a hit to the body that snaps the head, or from a fall. You don't have to be knocked out to have one. Most concussions don't involve being knocked out at all.

COACH: Picture a teammate who takes a knock, sits on the bench for a minute, and says they're fine to go back out. What would you do?

PLAYER: If they say they're fine, I'd probably let them.

COACH: If a concussion is suspected, yours or a teammate's, the player comes off the ice straight away. They don't go back on that day. Not that shift, not that period, not that game. And they don't play again until a medical professional says they can. Suspected is enough. Nobody at a recreational rink can rule a concussion out, and a concussed player will often tell you they're fine. So it's never their call.

PLAYER: And if they're still lying on the ice?

COACH: That's a different first job. After any knock to the head, treat it as a possible neck injury too. If a player is still down, phone for help first. Don't help them up and don't move them, unless you need to keep their airway clear so they can breathe. Leave their helmet on.

PLAYER: When is it an ambulance?

COACH: Phone for one straight away for any one of these, even if it seems mild. Being knocked out, a fit, neck pain, double or lost vision, weakness, numbness or tingling, or being sick more than once. It includes a severe headache, growing confusion, getting drowsy or less responsive, getting more and more agitated, or a skull that looks out of shape. Any one of those is enough. And any sign that is getting worse means an ambulance too.

COACH: If you play in Britain, the UK guidance adds more signs. Things like memory loss, a change in behaviour, one weak arm or leg, twitching or going rigid, or trouble speaking, understanding or balancing. Any sign of a broken skull, even just a cut, a bruise, swelling or bad pain where they were hit. A player who has been drinking or taking drugs. And a player who has had brain surgery, has a bleeding disorder, or takes blood-thinning medicine. Any of these means phoning nine nine nine for an ambulance, even for a player who gets up and says they're fine. If there's a first aider or medic at the rink, get them too, but still make the call.

PLAYER: And if none of those signs show up?

COACH: Someone still stays with them. The UK guidance says for the first twenty-four hours, and anywhere it's at least the first three. In Britain, ring one-one-one, the NHS line, so they're checked within those twenty-four hours. They don't drive, and they don't go home on their own. No alcohol, and no drugs that weren't prescribed for them. They don't do anything with a risk of a knock to the head, a fall or a collision until a medical professional clears them. Symptoms clearing up is not the all-clear. In British grassroots hockey there's also a minimum wait, on top of the clearance. No return to competition before day twenty-one, counting the day of the injury as day zero. And no training with a risk of a knock to the head until fourteen days free of symptoms, at rest.

COACH: Seventeenth idea. How long until you're really playing.

PLAYER: That's the one I'd most want to know.

COACH: The timeframes you'll see come from coaching businesses and shops, not from research. There's no measured evidence behind them, so treat them as experienced guesses. One of them says six to ten hours of ice to get from clinging to the boards to skating forwards with confidence. Then three to six months to playing in a beginner league. Then one to two seasons to feeling comfortable. People vary hugely.

COACH: The useful way to think about it is months for the basics, years for the rest. And progress comes in steps, not a smooth line. You'll have flat spells where you feel stuck, then a sudden jump, more than once. That too is what coaches see, not a measurement.

PLAYER: So a flat spell doesn't mean I've stopped getting better.

COACH: Exactly that.

COACH: Let me pull it all together. Before your first game, ask your coach or league secretary whether your league allows body checking. Not the rink desk. Before any session, ask what gear you need, what the standard is, and whether there's contact. Learn to skate, and work on stopping both ways and skating backwards, because skating sorts you into a level. Start a level lower than your pride wants.

COACH: On the ice, short shifts and eyes up. Never shoot at a goalie who isn't ready. Racing for a loose puck, play the puck, not the player. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call, or your session's. Never hit a player who can't see you coming, and if you can see their numbers, pull up. At the boards, go in at an angle if you can, and use the boards position. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to anyone coming at you, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly. And never hit anyone from behind.

COACH: If a concussion is suspected, the player comes off and doesn't come back that day. They don't play again until a medical professional clears them. Someone stays with them, and they don't drive or go home alone. If a player is still down after a knock to the head, phone for help, don't move them, and leave the helmet on. If anything looks serious, phone for an ambulance.

COACH: Borrow kit, but never a second-hand helmet. Register through your club, and check the league is sanctioned. And in Britain, your rules are the international ones, not the NHL's, and you wear your neck guard every time you go on the ice.

PLAYER: That sounds like a lot of phone calls.

COACH: Fewer than you'd think. You can ask almost everything else in one call to the club, the association or the rink. What do you run for someone at my level, and when does the next one start? What does it cost, and what isn't included? What does registration involve, and what dates does the year run? What equipment do I need on day one, and do you lend any? What level is this, and is there a trial? How much time is it a week, with travel? Is there contact, and what are the house rules? In Britain, add two more. Is this sanctioned, and am I insured in it? And which body am I registering with?

PLAYER: And what should I ask my coach?

COACH: Three things. Is our league a checking league? Which level would you put me in? And who should I talk to, now that I'm completely new? Amateur hockey runs on volunteers. At most clubs, somebody has the job of answering exactly that last question. And any teammate could explain most things in thirty seconds. They want to hear from you.
