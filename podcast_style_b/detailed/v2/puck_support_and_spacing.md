COACH: Picture this. Your defenceman has the puck behind your own net, and two of their forwards are coming. Your defenceman looks up, and there's nobody to pass to. Three of your teammates are bunched together near the puck, and the fourth is watching from the far side.

PLAYER: So they fire it down the ice, or they lose it.

COACH: Right. And nobody did anything wrong with the puck. The problem was everyone without it. This episode is the detailed one on puck support and spacing. That means where you go when you don't have the puck, so the teammate who does have it has somewhere to put it. I'm the coach, and with me is a player who already has the basics and wants to get properly good at playing without the puck.

PLAYER: Which is most of a game, really.

COACH: It is. We'll take it slowly, and for each piece you'll hear the reason behind it. If you know the reason, you can still do the right thing when the play looks different from the picture in your head.

COACH: Start with the big idea behind all of it. Your right spot on the ice isn't a fixed place. It's a distance and an angle from two things that keep moving. One is the puck. The other is your teammates. So every time the puck moves, your right spot moves too.

PLAYER: So there's no such thing as my spot.

COACH: Not one that stays put. We'll start with four big ideas, because they matter most. If you only hear the first part, take those four with you. After that come the skills that make you a good option anywhere, and then the rink, one end at a time.

COACH: First idea. Give the carrier two options at different angles. A triangle, not a line. The carrier is the player with the puck. An option is a teammate they could pass to right now. Picture your teammate on the boards with the puck, and you're their only option. What does the nearest defender do?

PLAYER: Cover me. Or at least cover the pass to me.

COACH: Right. The straight line a pass would travel along is the passing lane. One option means one lane, and a good defender will sit in it. With no option at all, the carrier has to beat their checker one-on-one or lose the puck. Your checker is the opponent marking you. Near the boards it also means anyone coming at you, after the puck or not. Even very good players lose that one-on-one a lot of the time.

PLAYER: And with two of us?

COACH: With two options at different angles, the nearest defender can't cover both. Someone else has to come across to help. And the moment a second defender commits, a third option opens up somewhere else.

PLAYER: So it isn't about being better than them.

COACH: No. Average players who keep giving each other two options can beat pressure that would strip better players who give none. Over a whole game, the team that offers two options has the puck more. That's reasoning, not a measured result. But it's why spacing is a big part of keeping the puck, not a footnote to skill.

PLAYER: Why do the angles have to be different?

COACH: Because two teammates in the same lane are really one option. One defender's stick covers both. Picture three of you strung out in a line up the boards. One defender standing in that line is between the carrier and both of the others. One stick blade kills two options at once. A line is the cheapest shape in hockey to defend.

PLAYER: So one of us steps off the line.

COACH: Into the middle. Now you have a triangle, and three things become true. First, no single defender can be in both lanes. Covering one means turning away from the other. Second, each of you has two teammates at different angles, so whoever ends up with the puck already has support. Third, and this matters most, the triangle survives the pass.

PLAYER: Survives it how?

COACH: When the puck moves from one corner to another, it's still a triangle, and nobody has to sprint to rebuild it. A line has to be built again after every pass. So the triangle isn't a formation you set up once. It's a shape you keep while everything moves.

COACH: Second idea. Be the angle that's missing. There are three kinds of support. Behind the puck, level with it, and ahead of it. Each does a different job.

PLAYER: Start with behind.

COACH: Behind the puck is the outlet. You're between the puck and your own net, or at least further back than the carrier. A pass back to you goes into space the other team has already left, so it's the least contested pass on the ice. And it's the option that's still there when the other two are covered.

PLAYER: What happens if nobody gives it?

COACH: Then the only answer to good pressure is dumping the puck away, and that's a turnover you chose. Whoever is lowest gives the outlet. In your own end, that's usually a defenceman or the centre. The usual call for it is "back".

COACH: Level with the puck is the side switch. You're about the same distance from your own net as the carrier, but across the ice from them. The strong side is the side of the ice the puck is on, and the weak side is the other side. Most teams defend by packing onto the strong side. So a pass across to you moves the puck to where they're thinnest, and every defender has to reset. In your own end, though, the switch goes behind your net, never across the front of it.

COACH: Ahead of the puck is the attacking option. You're further up the ice than the carrier. It's the only one of the three that gains ground. It's also the easiest pass to intercept, and if it fails, you're behind the play.

PLAYER: So which one should I be?

COACH: That depends on the other two. You need all three at once, and that's what good spacing actually means. All behind, and you're safe but going nowhere. All ahead, and there's no outlet, so pressure takes the puck off you. All level, and you go side to side and never up the ice. So when you skate to support, don't ask whether you're open. Ask which of the three is missing. Look at where your teammates are, and go and be that one.

PLAYER: Does the side switch work the same everywhere?

COACH: There's one place it changes. Your teammate has the puck on one side of your own end. You're on the other side, and the ice straight across in front of your net looks open. Do you want that pass?

PLAYER: It's tempting. It looks open.

COACH: It's the pass not to make. Never pass the puck, or skate it, across the front of your own net. That pass goes through the slot, the ice right in front of your net, between the two faceoff circles. An interception there isn't a turnover you can recover from. It's a shot from point-blank range, by a player already facing your net. So in your own end, the side switch goes behind your own net, never through the slot. Or the puck goes up the wall.

COACH: Third idea. Skate to the space, not to the puck. Coaches name this as the habit that most often separates newer players from better ones. That comes from coaching material, not a measured ranking.

PLAYER: I've seen it in kids' games. Everyone in one big pile.

COACH: It goes on well past kids' games. On a young or new team, the puck seems to pull everyone in. Ten players end up in a small circle. The puck squirts out to whoever has a teammate outside the pile. Then it all happens again twenty metres away.

PLAYER: Why does everyone do it, though?

COACH: Your eyes follow the puck, and your legs follow your eyes. Skating hard at the puck feels like effort, and skating away from it feels like slacking. And nobody wants to be the player who wasn't there.

PLAYER: But it's wrong.

COACH: Most of the time, for four reasons. First, you don't arrive alone. Your checker comes with you, so the space gets more crowded, not less. Second, you remove an option. A teammate five metres away is an option. One a metre away, inside the same checker's reach, isn't. Third, pucks come out of piles into empty ice, and if everyone's in the pile, that empty ice belongs to the other team. Fourth, nobody's left back. Then a normal turnover becomes an odd-man rush the other way, with more of them attacking than you have defending.

PLAYER: So how do I decide in the moment?

COACH: Every time the puck moves, ask yourself one question. Who is closest? If it isn't you, and your system hasn't sent you in, your job is the space, not the puck. If it is you, go for the puck, not the player. If you can see their numbers, pull up.

PLAYER: And where is the space?

COACH: Where the puck is going to come out, not where it is. From a corner battle, it comes out either up the wall or behind the net. Go and stand in one of those two places, with your stick on the ice. How many of you go to the puck is decided by your team's system, not made up on the spot. The habit to break is everybody going. So learn your role before you move.

COACH: Fourth idea. Before you go below the puck, know who is above it. Two new words first. Above the puck means between the puck and your own net. Below the puck means the puck is between you and your own net.

PLAYER: So above is the side nearer our net.

COACH: Yes, and it has nothing to do with which end of the ice you're in. A forward deep in the other team's corner is below the puck if the puck is up at their point. The point is the area just inside the blue line where defencemen usually stand. It's an area, not the line itself. And above and below flip the moment the puck changes hands. Players who were usefully below the puck while attacking are suddenly stuck below it, defending.

PLAYER: So somebody has to stay up.

COACH: Somebody stays above the puck, in every zone. That player isn't part of the attack. They're the insurance in case the attack fails. If the puck turns over and nobody is above it, the other team is off on a rush with nobody in the way.

PLAYER: Who is that, usually?

COACH: It depends on your system, so find out. Know who, not which position, because under some systems the player up there is a winger. And if nobody is above the puck, it's you.

COACH: Those four are the core. Give them back to me.

PLAYER: Two options at different angles. A triangle, not a line, because a triangle survives the pass. Be the angle that's missing, behind, level or ahead. In my own end, never pass the puck, or skate it, across the front of my net, so the switch goes behind the net. Skate to the space, not to the puck, unless I'm closest or my system sends me in. And before I go below the puck, know who's above it.

COACH: That's them. Everything else adds detail to those four. Next, the skills that make you a good option anywhere.

COACH: Fifth idea. Support from a distance the pass can beat. The test is time, not a tape measure. Ask yourself this. If my teammate let the pass go right now, would it reach me before that defender does?

PLAYER: And if it wouldn't, I'm not really an option.

COACH: Right. That's why the same distance can be right in one place and wrong in another. Twelve feet is safe in open ice. Under a forecheck, when the other team is pressuring you in your end to win the puck back, it's useless, because the defender gets there before the pass does.

PLAYER: What's wrong with being really close, though? It feels safer.

COACH: Three things. If you're within a stick's reach of your teammate's checker, that one checker is guarding two of you, and somebody else is free. You're also standing in the space your teammate needed to turn into. And if they pass to you from two metres away, their checker is on you before the puck settles. The pass hasn't helped. So back off until one defender can't reach both of you.

PLAYER: And too far?

COACH: That's a problem too. A long pass spends longer on the ice, so every defender has time to read it and get a stick in the lane. It arrives faster, often bouncing, with a defender already closing on you. And after the pass you're too far away to help the teammate who passed it. So close in until a pass let go now would reach you before the defender does.

PLAYER: Is there a number to start from?

COACH: About two to three stick lengths. That's about ten to fifteen feet, or three to five metres. It's close enough for a hard, flat pass that takes about a second, and far enough that one defender can't mark both of you. Then adjust. When the carrier is under heavy pressure on the boards, tighten to eight to ten feet. The pass has to be immediate, and short and safe beats good and late.

PLAYER: Anywhere else I go shorter?

COACH: In your own end, where a turnover is a chance against. And below their goal line, behind their net and in the low corners, because it's tight and fast down there. Go longer in open ice, where you can afford the extra half-second, and it stretches the defence. And go longer above the faceoff dots in their zone. Players at the point are far from the puck on purpose, and that's why they're open.

PLAYER: Is that an official distance?

COACH: No. It's a coaching rule of thumb, not a rule of hockey, and coaches differ. One coach describes possession play as short passes of eight to ten feet or less, which is the tight end of the same range. So let your coach adjust it.

COACH: The size of the rink matters a little. On a bigger rink the distances stretch, but the angles stay the same. Only the very widest rinks call for slightly longer support. In Britain the rules allow rinks smaller than the international size, so there your distances get shorter if anything, not longer. Measure your rink rather than guessing.

COACH: And if you play defence, don't mix this up with your gap. Support distance is teammate to teammate. A gap is how much room a defender leaves the attacker they're defending. Both get measured in stick lengths, but between different people. Never carry a number from one to the other.

COACH: Sixth idea. Make yourself available, not just unmarked.

PLAYER: I'll be honest. I'm one of those players who thinks nobody passes to me.

COACH: Then you're probably mixing those two up, like most players who say that. Unmarked means no defender is standing next to you. Available means the pass can actually be completed. That needs three things at once. First, a clear lane. No stick, skate or body between the puck and you. A defender ten metres away who happens to be standing on that line takes you out of the play just as much as one marking you.

PLAYER: Even from that far away?

COACH: Even from that far. Second, a target. Stick on the ice, blade flat, angled so the puck can be taken. A raised stick is an invisible one, and the passer has nothing to aim at. Third, a carrier who knows you're there. Their head is often down. So use your voice. Here. Middle. Back.

PLAYER: So that part's on me, not the passer.

COACH: Making yourself available is the receiver's job. Most players wait to be found. Good support players go and make themselves easy to find. If the lane is blocked, move, even a metre. One stride sideways can put you on the other side of the stick in the way. You don't need more space around you. You need a clear line from the puck to your stick.

PLAYER: So not the biggest bit of open ice.

COACH: No. Move to the passing angle. The biggest patch is often one nobody can get a pass into. A smaller gap with a clean line to the puck is worth more. When an opponent pins the carrier against the boards, the lane up the wall is gone. Get below them or behind them, where that seal doesn't reach. And keep adjusting every time the puck moves. Your lane was clean two seconds ago. Most failed support is a player who found a good spot once, and then stopped.

COACH: Seventh idea, and it's short. Pass and move. The moment the puck leaves your stick, your job isn't finished. What does the teammate you just passed to need now?

PLAYER: Two options. And I'm the nearest one.

COACH: Exactly. So go again straight away. Stop after your pass, and they have one option fewer. Pass and move is how the triangle survives the pass. The pass moves one corner of the triangle, and your next stride rebuilds it.

COACH: Let's hear those skills back.

PLAYER: Judge my distance by time. Would the pass beat the defender to me? Start at two to three stick lengths, and tighter under pressure. Be available, not just unmarked. Stick down, voice on, and move to the angle. Keep adjusting as the puck moves. And after every pass, go again.

COACH: Good. Now the rink, one end at a time, starting in your own end.

COACH: Eighth idea. Support in your own end. The breakout triangle. A breakout is your team getting the puck out of its own end. Say your defenceman picks up the puck behind your net. Before they turn, they need three options. Where would you put them?

PLAYER: Someone on the boards, someone in the middle, and their partner?

COACH: That's the common setup. The strong-side winger stands on the wall, roughly level with the faceoff dot. That stretch of boards, between the corner and the point, is called the half-wall. It's the short option up the boards. The centre swings low through the middle and curls toward the same side. That's the option that turns a breakout into an attack. And the defence partner is on the far side, reached behind the net. Never through the slot.

PLAYER: And the other winger?

COACH: The weak-side winger sits higher and wider than everyone else. That width stops the other team loading up on one side.

PLAYER: Earlier you said the attacking option is the one that gains ground. Is it still first here?

COACH: Here it flips. In your own end, support short and keep your angles safe. Everywhere else on the ice, the attacking option comes first. Here, the safe outlet does, because a turnover here is a chance against. Getting the puck out tidily is a win. Something clever that fails is a goal against. And further up the ice, arriving a little late with speed can be a good habit. In your own end, never delay. The carrier needs the outlet now. A late arrival here is an absent one.

PLAYER: What if there's no short option at all?

COACH: Then the carrier's only play is to fire it the length of the ice, and that's usually icing, a puck shot down past their goal line with nobody touching it. Play stops, and the faceoff comes back to your own end. Under the pro and international rules, and some others, your tired line also has to stay on for that faceoff, with no change. A hurt player can still come off. A tired one can't. That's the price of a breakout with no support.

COACH: Ninth idea. Your goalie is part of your support. Your goalie has the best view on the ice, and no puck to watch. So their calls tell the carrier which option is really there.

PLAYER: Which calls?

COACH: "Man on" means an opponent is closing on you. "Time" means nobody is near, so lift your head and make the good play. "Reverse" means send the puck back the way it came. A player who fears pressure that isn't there panics and throws the puck away. So when you hear "time", believe it.

PLAYER: And if I'm the one calling it?

COACH: Only say "time" when you're sure. If you're not sure, say nothing. A false "time" sends a teammate into a hit they weren't ready for.

PLAYER: Can the goalie play the puck as well, behind the net?

COACH: That depends on your league. In some leagues there's a trapezoid, a shape painted behind the net. The goalie may only play the puck back there inside it, and playing it anywhere else behind the net is a two-minute penalty. Other leagues have no trapezoid, and there your goalie can be a real fourth option behind the net. Even then, a goalie who has left the crease, the blue ice in front of the net, can't freeze the puck back there. Freezing means holding it still for a whistle, and that's a penalty. So expect the puck back moving, not left sitting for you.

PLAYER: How do I know which kind of league I'm in?

COACH: Ask your league. If you aren't sure, or you play in Britain, keep your goalie inside the trapezoid. It costs nothing, and the alternative is two minutes.

COACH: Let me pull your own end together. Three short options for the player behind the net. The wall, the centre swinging low, and the partner behind the net, never through the slot. The weak-side winger high and wide. Support short and safe, and never late. And use your goalie's eyes.

COACH: Now the middle of the ice. Tenth idea. The neutral zone, and its three lanes. The neutral zone is the middle of the rink, between the two blue lines. Think of it as three lanes. The boards on the strong side, the middle, and the boards on the weak side.

PLAYER: One of us in each?

COACH: That's the usual default. Two players in the same lane can be covered by one defender. Coaches put it as same lane, same checker. But that default is a coaching choice, not a law. The realistic alternative is a one-three-one regroup. A regroup is turning the puck back to reset and come again, and the one-three-one puts players in the middle lane on purpose.

PLAYER: Why stack the middle, if it's same lane, same checker?

COACH: To overload the gap between their defenders, and pull the other team's defence inward. So find out whether your team spreads out or stacks the middle. The two look the same for the first stride. Then they need opposite decisions. Either way, two habits help. Use different lanes at different speeds, so the defence can't set one gap that handles everyone. And skate curved routes into open ice, not straight lines. A curve lets you take the pass already facing forward.

COACH: Eleventh idea. Stay wide, even when it feels useless. You're the winger out on the boards in the neutral zone. The puck's in the middle, nowhere near you, and you haven't touched it all shift. What do you want to do?

PLAYER: Drift in. Get closer to the play.

COACH: Everyone feels that, and that feeling is the point. Two defenders can't cover both the middle and both sets of boards. A winger standing wide keeps a defender out there with them, and that defender isn't in the middle. The middle is the most valuable ice there is, and it's open only because someone is standing out wide. It's a bit like holding a door open. Nobody thanks you for it, but let go and it shuts.

PLAYER: So if I drift in, it shuts.

COACH: Your checker drifts in too, and the middle closes. You haven't gained an option. You've taken one away from a teammate, and you're now in your centre's lane. So stay wide, unless your team's rotation sends you inside, or you're driving the net on a shot.

PLAYER: And if I am driving the net?

COACH: Arrive under control, and pull up short of the goalie. Head up, chin off your chest. The goal post can hurt you the same way the boards can. Go round the goalie, never into them. Any contact with a goalie can be a penalty and can get you thrown out of the game. Contact can hurt a goalie, and one who is down or turned can't protect themselves.

COACH: Twelfth idea. Arrive late with speed, not early and still. Arriving at the right place at the right moment is worth far more than standing in it.

PLAYER: But if I get there early, I'm open.

COACH: For a moment. Get to the good spot early and stop, and the defence has as long as it likes to come over and stand next to you. By being early, you've turned yourself from an open teammate into a marked one. A moving player also takes the pass better. You already have speed, so you can attack straight away. A player standing still in traffic is a turnover waiting to happen.

PLAYER: So when do I go?

COACH: The cue in the neutral zone is puck first, feet second. Let the carrier draw a defender before you go. A half-second wait before you speed up does three things. It keeps you onside at the blue line. It means the defender's head has already turned toward the puck. And it means you cross the space at full speed while they're standing still.

PLAYER: I've heard coaches say a player arrives late as if it's a good thing.

COACH: It's praise, not criticism. Arriving late with speed beats a defender who has set a gap. By the time they react, you're already past the point where they could have set one. The trailer on a rush works this way. The trailer is a teammate arriving late behind the play. So does the defenceman jumping into the slot, and the weak-side winger cutting to the far post. If that's you at the far post, head up there too, chin off your chest, and go round the goalie, never into them.

PLAYER: And at their blue line?

COACH: Be exact about offside. You're offside if both your skates are completely over their blue line before the puck has completely crossed it. So time your route so the puck crosses first, or at the same moment as your second skate. Keep one skate on the line or behind it until then. And keep that back blade on the ice. In some leagues a skate held up in the air over the line still counts as onside. In others, only a skate touching the ice counts. Keep the blade on the ice, and you're onside wherever you play.

PLAYER: Let me try the middle. One per lane is the usual default, but ask if we spread or stack. Different lanes, different speeds, curved routes. Wingers stay wide, because the middle only exists while someone holds the outside. Arrive late with speed, puck first, feet second. And back blade on the ice at the line.

COACH: That's all of it. Now their end.

COACH: Thirteenth idea. Spread the zone deep as well as high. Three is the minimum for a triangle, not the maximum. With five skaters, three of you make the triangle around the puck. A fourth gives width on the far side. A fifth sits back above the play, as the safety.

PLAYER: Deep as well as high, meaning what?

COACH: Depth is spread up and down the ice. Width is spread across it. Depth mostly happens by accident, as players string out along the play. Width has to be deliberate, and in their zone, depth has to be deliberate too. So have one player below their goal line and one at their blue line. That stretch forces the defence to choose which end of the zone to defend. If all five of you are between the faceoff dots and the goal line, it isn't an attack. It's a crowd in front of their net.

COACH: Fourteenth idea. In their zone, support is rotation. The puck moves round the outside faster than anyone can skate, so no single spot stays right for long. What has to stay right is the layers.

PLAYER: Layers?

COACH: At all times, have someone below the puck, someone level with it, and someone above it. They're the same three angles we covered earlier, in their zone. Below is behind or beside the net, the reset when the play on the wall dies. Level is the half-wall or the slot. Above is the point, often with a third forward high, as the safety and the shot. When you leave one of those layers, someone fills it. That's the rotation, and it's the part newer teams skip. The layer has to stay filled, not your spot.

PLAYER: So who stays above?

COACH: That depends on your system. In a common setup, both defencemen hold the points unless one of them deliberately joins the attack, and the third forward supports high. A forecheck is your team trying to win the puck back in the other team's end. Under a one-two-two forecheck, the deepest forward is alone below the puck, and two forwards sit above as a layered wall. In a single-high setup, one defenceman may walk the line, and the weak-side winger becomes the safety. Other teams insist both defencemen hold. So find out which your team plays.

PLAYER: Is there ever a time nobody stays up?

COACH: A bench will give it up on purpose in three situations. A called full-ice press, where your team pressures all over the ice. A delayed penalty, when a penalty against the other team is waiting to be called. Or six skaters against five, with your own goalie pulled. In each of those, a counter-attack has stopped costing you anything. Unless one of them has been called, assume somebody stays above the puck.

COACH: How your team rotates is a coaching choice, and it changes who is allowed to go below the puck. Guess wrong, and you end up as the extra player deep, with nobody home. So ask your coach who may go below the puck, and when.

PLAYER: Their end, then. One player below the goal line and one at the blue line. Someone below the puck, someone level and someone above it, all the time. If I leave a layer, someone fills it. And know who stays above the puck in our system, and who may go below it.

COACH: Spot on. Now the moments that decide most of it. Battles on the boards, the second after the puck changes hands, and defending.

COACH: Fifteenth idea. To help a teammate in a battle on the boards, usually move away from it. Your teammate is pinned on the boards, fighting for the puck. What would you do?

PLAYER: Go in and help. Two against one.

COACH: Every instinct says that. Usually, don't. At least, not into the battle. Your teammate needs to get the puck out of the pile. Skate in, and you add a body where there's no room. COACH: There's a real exception. Go in to win the puck when it's a genuine fifty-fifty loose puck that nobody has. And if you do go in, how you arrive matters more than winning the puck.

PLAYER: So how do I arrive?

COACH: Loose pucks at the wall are where hits from behind and boarding injuries happen. So come to the wall on an angle, with your feet apart and your knees bent. Get your skates parallel to the boards before the contact. Forearm and hip to the boards. Never the point of your shoulder, and not the whole shoulder if you can help it. Head up, chin off your chest. Never turn your back to anyone coming at you. And never duck.

PLAYER: Why those last two especially?

COACH: Your head and neck. If you turn your back, you can't see the hit coming or brace for it. If you duck, your head meets the boards first. Neither is only for fast arrivals. Keep it up even going slowly.

PLAYER: And once I'm there?

COACH: Stick on the puck, not a hit. Whether you may hit at all depends on your league, your age group and your game, so find out first. If you can see a player's numbers, pull up and play the puck. And never push or hit the opponent on the wall from behind. If their back is to you, go for the puck at their feet, with your stick on the puck, not on them.

PLAYER: Even a little push?

COACH: Even a light one. A push from behind into the boards is checking from behind, even where body checking isn't allowed. Even a light push from behind can cause a serious head or neck injury. In most leagues, plan on being thrown out of the game for it. If it sends them into the boards unable to protect themselves, plan on more than being thrown out. In many leagues it's a match penalty.

PLAYER: What if I'm already facing the wall and can't turn in time?

COACH: That's the fallback, not the target. Get your stick and gloves up and spread wide on the glass, and keep your head off it. That's only for when things have already gone wrong. From here on I'll call the whole arrival the boards position, and say it the same way every time. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to anyone coming at you, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly.

COACH: Sixteenth idea. The second or two after the puck changes hands. This is when support is hardest. All five of you are still standing where the old possession wanted you.

PLAYER: So everyone's in the wrong place at once.

COACH: Which is why everyone needs a job straight away. When your team wins the puck, the nearest player away from the pressure becomes the outlet. Behind or level with the winner, blade on the ice, calling for it. Everyone else gets wide and into their lanes, rather than turning to watch the puck. A player who wins a battle with nowhere to put the puck often loses it again a second later.

PLAYER: And when we lose it?

COACH: Let's try it. You just lost the puck in their corner, and you're the nearest player to the one who took it. What would you do?

PLAYER: Get on them, before they can look up.

COACH: Yes, with your stick on the puck, not a hit. The nearest player pressures the new carrier at once, before they can look up and find a pass. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call. If you can see their numbers, pull up and play the puck. Never hit a player who can't see you coming, and never from behind. The next player layers behind, rather than joining in. Everybody else gets back above the puck, and that's a race for the ice, not for a player.

PLAYER: How many of us go after it?

COACH: In their end, that first pressure is the forecheck, and how many of you go is set by your system. In a one-two-two forecheck, one forward pressures and the others hold their places. In a two-one-two, two forwards go deep on purpose, and the third reads the play behind them. Whoever goes, it's the same. Stick on the puck, not a hit, and never from behind.

COACH: Sending that nearest player straight in is the common version. Some teams want everyone back above the puck first, and only pressure once they have numbers back. That's a coaching choice, so ask your bench which yours plays.

COACH: Seventeenth idea. Support when you're defending. Layer, don't converge. Attacking, the second player's job is to give the carrier an option. Defending, the second player's job is to back up the first defender, not to copy them. Most defensive breakdowns come from getting this wrong.

COACH: Say your teammate is pressuring the puck carrier, with their stick on the puck, not a hit. You're the second defender. What would you do?

PLAYER: Go and help. Two of us on the puck.

COACH: That's converging, skating at the same carrier. Now two of you are on one puck, and one pass beats you both. The other choice is to layer. That means sitting behind and to the side of your teammate, covering the space and the passing lane they can't. You're the answer if they get beaten, a bit like the safety net under someone on a high wire. And you're the reason the carrier can't simply pass out of the pressure.

PLAYER: Is converging really that bad?

COACH: It's not a small mistake. With two of you on one carrier, the other three of you are covering four of them, and it's the free player who scores.

PLAYER: So how do I make the layer actually work?

COACH: Three things. First, read which way your teammate is steering the carrier, and stand where the carrier goes if your teammate is beaten. Second, get the distance right. Too close, and one move beats you both. Too far, and the carrier skates through the gap between you. Aim to be able to get there in about a second. That's about the same two to three stick lengths, offset diagonally, not straight behind. Third, talk. "I've got your back." "Man on." "You have time." The layer only works if your teammate knows it's there. And only say "time" when you're sure. A false "time" sends a teammate into a hit they weren't ready for.

PLAYER: Does layering fit any system?

COACH: Layering is almost always right. It sits inside every named defensive system. But whether you may hold the layer at all depends on how your team covers in its own end. Under man-on-man coverage, where each player follows one opponent, your job can take you away from the layer. So ask how your coverage changes it.

PLAYER: And if the coach wants two of us on the puck?

COACH: That's the exception, a deliberate double-team, two players sent at one carrier. It's a coaching decision, not a default. Many teams double a puck on the boards below their own goal line, because a turnover there is contained by the net and the boards. Ask your coach where and when you may double. Doubling on your own is how the other team scores at the back door, the far post. And going in for a double-team doesn't change how you arrive. The boards position. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to anyone coming at you, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly. Stick on the puck, not a hit. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call, and if you can see their numbers, pull up. Never push or hit the opponent from behind. Even a light push from behind can cause a serious head or neck injury. In most leagues, it gets you thrown out. Go for the puck at their feet.

COACH: Let me pull that part together. To help a battle, usually move away and give an exit, with your stick down. If you do go in, arrive safe before you arrive first. The boards position. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to anyone coming at you, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly. Stick on the puck, not a hit, and never push or hit anyone from behind. When the puck changes hands, the nearest player becomes the outlet if you won it, and the pressure if you lost it, stick on the puck. And defending, layer behind your teammate instead of joining them on the same carrier.

COACH: Eighteenth idea, and the last. Learning to see it. Spacing is hard to learn because it's invisible. Everyone sees the great pass. Nobody claps the teammate whose positioning made it possible three seconds earlier.

PLAYER: So nobody ever tells you your spacing was off.

COACH: Mostly not, so most players never fix it. You have to give yourself that feedback. When you watch a game, stop watching the puck. For one full shift, watch one player who doesn't have it. It feels odd, and it's the fastest way to learn the game.

PLAYER: What am I looking for?

COACH: Count the options. Every time a player gets the puck, count the teammates who could take a pass right now. Good teams average two or more. Ask whether you could draw a triangle around the puck. Watch the far side. The weak-side winger is the best single sign of a team's spacing. Are they wide and high, or have they drifted in to watch? When the puck changes hands, count how many are below it. That tells you whether the next half-minute is a rush against. And watch the trailer on a rush, not the two who carried it in. The dangerous chance often comes from that third player arriving late.

PLAYER: And for my own game?

COACH: After a shift of your own, ask where you were each time the puck moved, and whether the carrier could have used you. If you can, ask to be filmed, because you can't see your own spacing from inside the play. And small games in practice, across half the ice or less, punish crowding straight away, so the habit breaks itself.

COACH: On the ice, ask yourself one question, over and over. Where are my two options? When you don't have the puck, ask whether you're one of somebody's two options right now. If not, you're watching, not supporting. Move.

PLAYER: And when I've got the puck?

COACH: Ideally before it arrives, name the two. If you can only name one, move the puck to someone who has two, or, outside your own end, buy time for a second to appear. And if you can't name any, that's the moment players panic pass. A panic pass is a pass made without looking, just to get rid of the puck.

PLAYER: Guilty. A checker's coming, and the puck's gone before I've looked.

COACH: Most players who are still learning do it. Panic spends time you actually had. Every rushed pass, and every puck flipped to nobody, was a moment with more time than it felt. After every shift, ask yourself whether you had more time than you thought. The honest answer is almost always yes.

PLAYER: So what do I do instead?

COACH: Get one more look before the puck reaches you, so you know the answer before it arrives. And outside your own end, when you don't know, keep it, and turn side-on, away from the pressure, rather than passing into it. Your shoulder goes to the checker, with the puck on the far side of your body. Never turn your back to anyone coming at you, anywhere on the ice. Near the boards, that means the boards position.

PLAYER: Should I keep it in my own end too?

COACH: No. In your own end, holding it is how the turnover happens. One look, one decision, move it. And that's where support comes back in. A teammate who's already given you two options is the best cure for a panic pass there is.

COACH: Let me pull it all together.

COACH: Give the carrier two options at different angles, a triangle, not a line, because a triangle survives the pass. Be the angle that's missing, behind, level or ahead. In your own end, never pass the puck, or skate it, across the front of your net. The side switch goes behind your net, never across the slot.

COACH: Skate to the space, not to the puck. And before you go below the puck, know who is above it. Judge your distance by time. Make yourself available, stick down, voice on. Pass and move.

COACH: In your own end, support short and safe, and never late. Through the middle, stay wide, and arrive late with speed, back blade on the ice at the line. In their end, keep the layers filled.

COACH: To help a battle, usually move away. If you do go in, arrive safe. The boards position. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to anyone coming at you, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly. Stick on the puck, not a hit, and never push or hit anyone from behind. And defending, layer, don't converge.

COACH: With the puck, name your two options, and don't panic pass. Get one more look. Outside your own end, when you don't know, keep it, side-on to the checker. In your own end, one look, one decision, move it. And anywhere on the ice, never your back to anyone coming at you.

PLAYER: What should I ask my own coach?

COACH: Three things. Who is our safety above the puck, and who may go below it? In the neutral zone, do we spread out or stack the middle? And when do we double-team? Ask, because these are your team's choices, not rules. Their answers tell you where your team wants you when you don't have the puck, and now you know the reason behind each one.
