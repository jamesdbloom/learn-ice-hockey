===== SCRIPT START =====

This episode is about defending in your own end. That means where you stand, and what you do, while the other team has the puck in your zone. It takes its time. For each piece I will tell you what to do, and also why it works. If you know why something works, you can still do the right thing when the play looks different from the picture in your head.

Before any instructions, a few words you will hear all the way through. I will explain them now, so they don't get in the way later.

Your zone, or your end. This is the part of the rink between your blue line and your end boards. It is the end with your own net in it.

Coverage. This is the agreement your five skaters have about who protects what, while the other team has the puck in your end.

Strong side and weak side. The strong side is the side of the ice the puck is on. The weak side is the other side. When the puck moves across, they swap.

High and low. Low means nearer your own goal line, the line your net sits on. High means nearer the blue line.

The point. This is the area just inside the blue line where the other team's defencemen stand. It is an area, not the line itself.

The half-wall. This is the stretch of boards roughly level with the faceoff dot, between the corner and the point.

The slot. This is the lane in front of your net, between the two faceoff circles. Its top part, from the faceoff dots up to the tops of the circles, is called the high slot.

The crease. This is the blue paint in front of the net, where your goalie works.

The doorstep, also called the goalmouth. This is the very front of the net, at the edge of the blue paint. It is not the whole slot. It is where tap-ins and tips happen.

And the house. Picture your two goalposts. Widen out from them to the two faceoff dots. Then go up to the tops of the two faceoff circles. That shape looks like a baseball home plate, so it is called home plate, or the house. It isn't painted on the ice, and analysts each draw it a little differently. But they all agree on one thing. A shot from inside it is worth several times the same shot from outside it.

That is the vocabulary. Now the job.

Here is the problem every defensive system is trying to solve. There are five of you, five of them, and one net. But the ice is not equally dangerous. The house is where the goals come from. So the job is to keep the house closed, and still be ready to take the puck and leave.

You can't stop every chance. There are only five of you. If four of you stand in the house, somebody at the point is free. So defending your end is really about choosing which chances to give up. Twenty shots from the point through clear ice, and no tap-ins at the back post, is a good night.

And remember you are trying to leave, too. If all five of you pack in around the net, the house is safe. But when you win the puck, nobody is free to take a pass, so you give it straight back. Every system trades safety at the net against being ready to break out. Keep that in mind. It explains most of what follows.

I will start with four big ideas, because they matter most. If you only listen to the first part of this episode, take those four with you. After that we will go through the systems, the front of your net, the plays you will face, and getting the puck out.

First idea. Find out which system your team plays.

A system is your team's plan for who covers what. Coverage changes more from team to team than any other part of hockey. Two good teams can play completely different systems, and both be right. So the same instruction, get to the corner, or stay high, can be right on one bench and a big mistake on the next.

Here is why this comes first. When a goal goes in from the slot, someone on the bench often says a player didn't work hard enough. Usually, both players worked hard. They just worked to different plans. The centre thought the winger was dropping down. The winger thought the centre was staying up. Both skated hard, in the wrong directions, at the same moment. That is coaching agreement, not a counted figure.

One question gets you most of the way. When the puck is in our corner, does the winger stay up at the point, or come down to help? The wingers are the two forwards on the sides. If the winger stays up, that points to a zone system, where each player covers an area. If the winger goes down with the player they are covering, that points to man-on-man, where each player covers one opponent. Or it points to a hybrid, which mixes the two.

That answer tells you the family, not the exact system. But it is the fastest question you can ask a new coach.

Second idea. The middle is the dangerous ice. Defend it first, and never give it away with the puck.

Start with defending. Say you have a choice between taking away a shot from far out and taking away a pass into the slot. Take away the pass. Here is why. A shot from the point, through clear ice, is a save your goalie expects to make. A pass into the slot is not. So you are steering the puck to where it is least dangerous. You are not trying to touch it everywhere.

This tells you which chance to give up. It does not tell you where to stand. Your system does that. In one system you stand in the middle. In another you follow your player to the boards, and the middle stays safe because the player who would take that pass has someone on them. So ask yourself which is the more dangerous thing to leave open. Don't just drift to the slot in a system that told you to go with your player.

Next, be goal side. That means staying between the player you are covering, or your area, and your own net. If an opponent is between you and your goalie, you are defending nothing. You are just skating behind the play.

That is also why you angle an attacker toward the boards, instead of reaching across your body for the puck. Reaching turns you. And half a second later, a defender who has turned is on the wrong side.

Now the other half of this idea. When your team wins the puck, never move it across the front of your own goal. Not with a pass, and not by skating it.

Here is why. The moment you win the puck, your whole team turns to leave. If you lose it in the slot, an opponent has it on the most dangerous ice there is. They are in front of a goalie who has just moved across. That is a point-blank chance. If you lose it behind your net, their player has no angle at all.

So if the puck has to change sides, take it behind your net. Some teams switch sides with a pass high in the zone instead, above the tops of the circles. Ask which route yours uses. But neither route goes through the slot. That part never changes.

Third idea. Somebody always has the doorstep, and you both know who.

The player who scores from close in is standing at the edge of the blue paint, with their stick on the ice. So somebody on your team has to be there. Who it is changes with your system. That it is somebody never changes.

The classic breakdown is both defencemen going to the same puck in the corner. Then nobody is on the doorstep, and one pass beats your whole team. Coaches at every level call this one of the commonest ways to give up a goal. That is coaching agreement, not a counted figure.

In the system most teams are taught first, one defenceman goes to the puck. The weak-side defenceman stays home on the doorstep. In other systems it can be someone else. In man-on-man, it is whoever's player went there. And some coaches send both defencemen low on purpose, and give the slot to a forward. So the rule is not never both of you. It is never both of you without knowing who has the doorstep.

The way you both know is by talking. Say it early. And say what you are about to do, not what you can see.

Here is the difference. They're coming down your side describes the problem. I'm going to the corner, you've got the net, solves it. Your partner now knows exactly where you will be.

Coverage is a shared picture of who has whom. The only way to keep that picture shared is out loud. I've got the corner. You have the net. Switch. Back door.

Fourth idea. Don't chase the puck. Take away the next pass.

The other team will move the puck around your boards and behind your net. They want you to chase it, because a chaser leaves a hole. So hold your area, or your player if you play man-on-man. Defend where the puck is going next, not where it is now.

Watch the player skating in to take the next pass. In a scramble, the most dangerous player is almost never the one holding the puck. It is the one about to get it.

Keep your stick flat on the ice in the passing lane, the path the next pass needs. Here is why. A stick lying in the lane takes away a pass without you committing your body. So you can still get to the next thing. A stick held off the ice, or waving at hip height, does nothing. When you can't reach the puck, you can almost always reach the lane.

One habit goes with this idea. The moment you realise you don't know who is yours, go to the middle. Get in front of your net, with your stick in a lane. Lost there, you cost your team less than lost anywhere else.

Let me pull those four ideas together, because they are the core of the job.

One. Find out which system your team plays. Ask the corner question. Does the winger stay up, or come down?

Two. Defend the middle first. Take away the pass into the slot before the shot from far out. Be goal side. And when you win the puck, never take it across the front of your own goal.

Three. Somebody always has the doorstep, and you both know who. Say what you are about to do, and say it early.

Four. Don't chase. Take away the next pass, with your stick flat in the lane. And when you are lost, go to the middle.

Everything else in this episode adds detail to one of those four. So now the systems themselves, one at a time.

Fifth idea. The system most teams are taught first.

It is called the low zone collapse. You may also hear sagging zone, zone coverage, or just the collapse. Coaching material says it is the one most often taught first in North American minor, junior and recreational hockey. Nobody has counted that. If your coach has never named your system, this is probably it.

What it is. In the collapse you defend space, not people. Each of you owns an area. Whoever skates into your area is yours until they leave it.

How it works. Picture the puck in one corner of your zone. Here is where the five of you are.

The strong-side defenceman is on the puck, in the corner or behind the net. Their job is to pressure the carrier and pin the puck, or angle the carrier away from the net.

The weak-side defenceman owns the doorstep and boxes out. They don't get drawn to the puck.

The centre comes down as the second player into the corner battle. Two on one wins it.

The strong-side winger stays high, at the point on the puck side. They stand between that defenceman and your net, blocking the lane and the shot.

The weak-side winger covers the other point. When the centre goes down to the corner, that winger drops into the high slot.

So three of you live low, both defencemen and the centre. That matches their three forwards down there. The two wingers hold the points, so their defencemen never get a clean pass into the middle.

Now the corner itself. Pressure there means stick on the puck, not a hit. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call. And if you see their numbers, pull up.

Pin the puck, never the player. Pinning the puck means trapping it against the boards with your skate or your stick, so it can't go the wrong way. Hold it only a moment, until your second player arrives to dig it out. In some leagues, holding it there more than about three seconds is a penalty even with someone on you, so keep it moving.

Never hold it there for a whistle. And never fall on it or gather it into your body. Freezing the puck to stop play can be a penalty, and falling on it is a penalty in every league.

Now the second player into that corner, usually the centre. You arrive late, and the player you are arriving on can't see you. They are facing your end boards, with their head down over the puck, digging it out.

So arrive on the puck, or on the ice they want. Never on their back. If you can see their numbers, pull up. Put your stick on the puck and angle them off it. That is the whole instruction. Take the puck, take the ice, or angle them off it.

Arrive under control, too. Building up speed into contact, even with two hard strides, can be charging. That counts whether your league allows checking or not.

A hit in the back is never just two minutes, in any league. If you hit a player from behind while they stand along the boards, plan on being thrown out of the game. In some leagues a reckless one is a match penalty, which goes past being thrown out. And if you drive a player into the boards when they can't protect themselves, that is boarding as well. No league caps boarding at two minutes.

Now turn it round. Sometimes it is you on the boards, digging the puck out, with someone coming at you. From here on, the checker means that player, the one coming at you, whether they are after the puck or just coming at you.

Never turn your back to the checker. And never duck.

Both protect your head and neck. If you turn your back, you can't see the hit coming or brace for it. If you duck, your head meets the boards first. That is true even going slowly. And it is true in every league, checking or not, because accidental contact happens there too, and the boards are just as hard.

So here is the position you aim for. Skates parallel to the boards, side-on. Forearm and hip to the boards. Never the point of your shoulder, and not the whole shoulder if you can help it. Head up, chin off your chest.

If you are facing the boards, turn side-on if there is any time at all. If you can't, that is the last resort, not a choice. Get your stick and gloves up on the glass, spread wide, with your head off it.

That position matters more than anything else in this idea. From here on I will use one short version of it, the same words every time, so you can learn it like a line. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the checker, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly.

So why does a team play the collapse? The reasons come from how it works.

First, it protects the middle just by people standing in the right place. You don't need to make perfect reads for the house to be full.

Second, it is the easiest system to learn. The areas are fixed, so even when the play is chaos, you know roughly where to be.

Third, it is very hard to beat by passing the puck around the boards. They are working on the least valuable ice, while you stand on the most valuable.

Fourth, both wingers are already high. So the moment you win the puck, you have two players ready for a pass.

Every system gives something up, and here is what the collapse gives up. It gives up shots from the point, on purpose. The deal is that a shot from far out, with a stick in the lane, is one your goalie should stop. That deal gets worse against a defenceman with a really good shot. It also means long spells in your own end, waiting for a mistake. And it has a seam. That is the gap between two players' areas, where each thinks the other has the attacker. Good attackers go and stand right there.

Sixth idea. In the collapse, the jobs move with the puck.

Coverage in the collapse is a set of positions that swap as the puck travels. It is not a set of jobs fixed to people. Here are the swaps.

If the puck goes from the corner to behind your net, the defenceman on it follows it to the post. They hand it off at the middle of the net. Their partner takes the carrier coming out the other side, and the first one slides back to the doorstep. Neither of you ever leaves the net empty. And say it. I've got them, you've got the net.

If the puck goes from the corner up the boards toward your strong-side winger, that winger goes and gets it. That is not a corner battle. That is your breakout starting, the clean exit from your end.

If the puck goes back up to the point, everyone climbs a level. The wingers close on their points. The centre steps up into the high slot. The defencemen get back to the front of the net.

Now the moment that decides whether the whole system works. It belongs to the weak-side winger.

When the puck is in the corner and the centre goes down to help, the high slot is suddenly empty. It can't stay empty. So the weak-side winger drops off their point into the high slot. As soon as the puck comes out of the corner, that winger climbs straight back up to their point.

Why does this matter so much? The high slot isn't the most dangerous ice. The ice nearer the crease is. But the high slot empties by design when the centre goes down, and the dangerous passes run through it. The pass across to the back post crosses it. And a pass back up from low can be shot from there.

Two things to get right. First, anyone with a clear view calls it. The centre, the weak-side defenceman, or the winger. Don't wait to be told. By the time someone notices it is missing, it is late.

Second, only the weak-side winger does this. The strong-side winger holds their point. If both wingers drop, one pass across the top gives their defenceman a free walk in on your net.

There is one time it doesn't work. Say the weak-side winger has already followed their defenceman down off the point. Then that winger is busy. The defencemen have to watch for players arriving, or the centre doesn't go all the way into the corner. Read it, and call it.

Seventh idea. Man-on-man.

What it is. Every player is given one opponent, and covers them everywhere in the zone. If the defenceman you are covering goes to the corner, you go to the corner. Nobody is ever left free, because free doesn't exist in this system.

How it works. The two defencemen usually take the two most dangerous forwards, whoever is low and around your net. The centre takes the third forward. And the wingers take the two opposing defencemen, and follow them, even down into the corner. That is the biggest difference from the collapse, and it confuses teammates more than anything else.

When two attackers cross, two of you can swap players. Switch only when it is clean, and both of you call it out loud. Otherwise, stay with your player. When to switch is your team's rule, not a law of hockey, so ask.

Staying with your player means goal side of them, within a stick's length. It does not mean holding them, hooking them, or bumping them. Each of those can be a penalty wherever you play.

Why does a team play man-on-man? Nobody is ever left free, so the seam the collapse has just doesn't exist. Passing the puck around the boards achieves very little, because every attacker already has someone on them. Pressure everywhere means bad passes everywhere, so man-on-man wins the puck back. The collapse waits. Man-on-man takes. And a coach can put their best defender on the other team's best player, all shift.

What it gives up. Teams beat it by running players into your defenders on purpose, so the player you are covering gets free. One beaten player leaves an attacker with the puck and nobody near. It is exhausting, because you skate their pace for a whole shift. And when you win the puck, your five are wherever their five chose to stand. That is rarely a good shape to break out from.

The classic mistake is following your player into harmless ice, while their teammate walks into the slot you just left. Good man-on-man teams have a rule about letting a player go in that case. Ask what yours is.

Eighth idea. The hybrid.

What it is. Man-on-man below the faceoff dots, zone above them. Some teams put the line at the hash marks instead, or at the tops of the circles. The hash marks are the short lines on the sides of each faceoff circle, about level with the dot. So learn exactly where your team's line is.

How it works. Down low, the two defencemen and the low forward, usually the centre, each take one of their three forwards. One takes the carrier. One takes the player at the front of your net. One takes the player supporting.

Up high, the wingers cover the points by area. But if the defenceman at a winger's point comes down past the line, that player becomes the winger's, and the winger goes with them.

When the puck goes below the goal line, the low three stay locked on their players. When it comes back up to the point, everyone lets go and goes back to areas.

Why play it? It is a deliberate compromise. Man-on-man where a free attacker would be deadly, and zone where it isn't. It fixes the collapse's two big holes, the defenceman walking down from the point and a free attacker at your net. And it keeps the wingers high enough to break out. If the low part falls apart in a scramble, you can go back to protecting the house without changing anything up high.

What it gives up. It is the hardest system to learn. All five of you have to agree where the line is, and switch at the same moment. Picture two players who disagree about whether an attacker is below the dots yet. That disagreement is exactly how goals go in.

Ninth idea. Three more shapes you may meet.

The first is box plus one. Four players make a box around the slot. Two are low, near the posts. Two are higher, near the tops of the circles. The fifth player is the plus one, and is the only one who chases the puck. When the puck moves, whoever is nearest becomes the new plus one, and the box turns to fill the gap.

If you are the plus one, chasing means stick on the puck, not a hit. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call. And if you see their numbers, pull up.

Why play it? Coaching material calls it the usual first system for young players and beginner adults. It is calm, it forgives mistakes, and everyone has a simple picture of where to stand. It is meant as a stepping stone to the collapse or the hybrid. In recreational hockey, it stops all five people chasing the same puck. Nobody has counted who plays it.

What it gives up is almost everything around the outside. One chaser can't pressure a good passing team, so they keep the puck as long as they like.

The second shape is the collapsing box. It isn't a full system. It is a state. As the puck goes behind your goal line or into the corner, the box shrinks toward the crease. In the end all five of you are in the house, sticks in lanes, and you give up the whole outside on purpose.

When does a team do that? Protecting a lead late in a game, when a point shot costs nothing and a pass into the slot costs the game. When the other team has pulled its goalie for an extra attacker. When your structure has already gone. Or against a team that is dangerous low but gets nothing from its defencemen.

The third shape is the strong-side overload, and it is the opposite. Instead of shrinking toward the net, all five of you shift to the puck side. That cuts the ice in half and squeezes the carrier. Why do it? It is aggressive, and it wins the puck back. The price is that the whole far side is empty, so one hard pass across beats it.

Squeezing the carrier means stick on the puck, not a hit. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call. And if you see their numbers, pull up.

Some teams play the overload all the time. Others use it only when they need the puck back. Which of these shapes your team uses, and when, is your coach's call. So ask.

Let me pull the systems together. In the collapse you defend areas. One defenceman on the puck, one on the doorstep, the centre as the second player in, and both wingers high. Only the weak-side winger drops into the high slot, and climbs back as the puck leaves the corner. In man-on-man you stay goal side of your player, and switch only when it is clean and called. In the hybrid it is man below the line and zone above it, so know where the line is. And in every one of them, somebody has the doorstep, and if you see their numbers, you pull up.

Now the front of your own net.

Tenth idea. Own the front of your net with your position, never with a push.

Step one. Get there first. Your inside shoulder ahead of theirs, and your body between them and the puck. That is called boxing out.

Here is why you race to get there. Holding a spot you already have is fine in most leagues. Driving someone off a spot they already have is not. So getting there first is the real battle.

Then hold it. Hold your spot. No shove and no lean. Never your stick across anyone, and never hit or push into a back. If you see their numbers, pull up. Never lift anyone off their feet. Even going slowly, a crash there can hurt, with the post and your goalie right beside you.

Hold only ice you got to first. If someone is already standing on it, go around them.

Step two. As the puck arrives, lift their stick. Up, not sideways. Low on the shaft, well below their bottom hand. Then let go.

Why the lift? A forward at your net with a free stick is a tip waiting to happen. With their stick lifted, they are just a person standing there. It is the most valuable thing a defender does at the front of the net.

Why low? Low keeps you off their hands, and still takes the stick off the ice. It also keeps your blade further from their head. Stop at their stick, because the stick you lift ends up near their face. Never carry on up into their head.

Never go at their hands. A lift at the hands can be called hooking. And never chop at the stick or the hands. A chop is slashing in every league, and it can get you thrown out even with nobody hurt.

And never swap the lift for a shove with your stick held in both hands. That is a cross-check, whatever your blade is doing. A cross-check in the back is never just two minutes. It can get you thrown out of the game with nobody hurt. And in some leagues, one that lands above the shoulders gets you thrown out for certain.

Now the screen. A screen is an attacker standing in your goalie's line of sight. If they are outside the crease and not touching your goalie, that is normally legal. The referee will not move them for you.

So beat it with your body. Get between the screener and your goalie's line of sight, and hold that spot. Deal with the screen. Don't just watch it.

Never walk a screener off their spot. That can be a penalty in any league, whether your game allows checking or not. And whether checking is allowed can change from game to game, not just by age group. So check the game, not the age group.

And never shove an attacker into your own goalie, hoping the goal gets called off. Here is why. Your goalie is usually down, square to the shooter, and can't see the attacker coming. The attacker lands backwards on your goalie, with the post right beside them. That can hurt your goalie, and it can be a penalty on you in every league. And the goal usually still counts, because you pushed them there. The injury part is coaching caution, not a counted figure.

If the shot is already on its way and you are in your goalie's line of sight, don't try to block it from there. A half block on a shot your goalie can't see is worse than no block. You change the direction of a puck they never saw. Get out of the line of sight. Or take the inside on the player you are covering, without pushing them, and let the puck through.

If you block at all, block from close, about ten feet from the shooter. And the whole method goes together. Close with your feet, don't lunge. Get square, with your shin pads to the puck. Head up, and out of the shot's path. Hands behind your body, stick down beside you. Never lift a leg. Never turn sideways or turn your back on it. And stay on your feet until the puck has left the shooter's stick.

And talk to your goalie. Screen left. I've got the tip. Your goalie is trying to find the puck through a crowd of legs.

Eleventh idea. The scramble at your own net. A rebound, a loose puck at the top of the crease, four bodies, two seconds.

Body first, puck second. Before you look for the puck, take away the most dangerous stick. Here is why. A player with a free stick at your doorstep will score before you get to the puck.

As the puck arrives, lift that stick. Up, not sideways, low on the shaft, well below the bottom hand. Stop at the stick, and let it go. Or press their blade down to the ice with yours as the shot comes, and let go once the shot is through.

Never at the hands, and never a chop. Never hook the stick, keep it pinned, or grab it with your hand. Holding a stick is a penalty, and if it hurts someone, it can get you thrown out. And no shove with your stick in both hands. That is a cross-check, whatever your blade is doing.

The bodies there follow the same rule as always. Hold your spot. No shove and no lean. Never your stick across anyone, and never hit or push into a back. If you see their numbers, pull up. Never lift anyone off their feet. Even going slowly, a crash there can hurt, with the post and your goalie right beside you.

Don't lunge at the puck through your goalie. You will knock the puck loose, or knock your goalie off their post. Trust your goalie to cover it, and protect them while they do.

Move the puck with a low jab of your stick, not a swing. A wild swing at a bouncing puck can catch someone high, and that can be a penalty even by accident. It can get you thrown out. And a hard chop that lands on someone's hand can be slashing, even if you were going for the puck.

Stay on your feet as long as you can. A defender lying on the ice is out of a scramble that only lasts two seconds. And going down hands and head first at the doorstep puts your hands and head under four sets of sticks and skates. That is coaching caution, not a rule. It is the reason to use your stick.

Your hand can do one thing. Knock a loose puck down, or push it along the ice, with an open hand. That is never a penalty. Catching a puck out of the air is fine too, as long as you put it straight down.

But never hold it, skate with it, turn with it or throw it. Never close your hand on a loose puck, and never smother it under your body. That can be a penalty, or at least stop play.

Outside your crease, if you block a shot and the puck ends up under you without you trying, that is fine. Reaching for it with your hands to keep it there is not.

Inside your own crease it gets much worse. If you hold or cover the puck while it is inside your crease, the other team gets a penalty shot. That is a free shot, alone against your goalie. With your goalie pulled for an extra attacker, in most leagues they are simply given the goal. And in at least one league, standing in your crease when you cover it can cost the same, even with the puck outside.

The crease goes all the way up to the top of the net. So don't reach up to hold a puck in the air either. Knock it straight down and play it with your stick. Covering the puck in the crease is your goalie's job, and nobody else's.

Let me pull the front of your net together. Get there first, and hold your spot with no shove, no lean, and never hit or push into a back. As the puck arrives, lift the stick low, up not sideways, and let go. Never at the hands, never a chop, never a cross-check. Never walk a screener out, and never shove anyone into your goalie. In a scramble, body first, and jab, don't swing. And never hold the puck in your crease.

Now the plays you will face most, and what to do about each.

Twelfth idea. The cycle.

The cycle is the attackers passing the puck around the corner, the half-wall and behind your net. Each pass goes to a teammate arriving in the space somebody just left. The aim is to make you chase until somebody is out of position.

Don't chase it around the boards. When you chase, your five get strung out, and that is how the cycle wins. Let it come to you, and defend the ways out, not where the puck is now. Take away the next pass, because the player arriving is the threat.

The best place to break a cycle is at the goal line. The dangerous carrier is the one trying to come out from below the goal line to the front of your net. Meet them at the post, not in the corner. Stick on the puck, not a hit. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call. And if you see their numbers, pull up.

In the collapse, be patient. The house is full, and they aren't threatening you. Time is on your side, so let them grind.

In man-on-man or the hybrid, stay on bodies. That means goal side, within a stick's length, with no holding, no hooking and no bumping. Man coverage low is the strongest answer to a cycle.

Cycles end when somebody wins a puck battle. So send two to the carrier. In the collapse that is the strong-side defenceman and the centre. Stick on the puck, not a hit. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call. And if you see their numbers, pull up. The second one arrives late and unseen. So arrive on the puck, or on the ice they want, never on their back. And arrive under control.

And whoever ends up on the boards with the puck, the same words as always. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the checker, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly. If you are already facing the boards, get your stick and gloves up on the glass, spread wide, head off it, and only when things have gone wrong.

Thirteenth idea. The low-to-high play.

They put the puck low, into the corner or behind your net, to drag your coverage down. Then they pass it back up to the point or the high slot, for a shot with your backs turned and traffic in front. It exists to beat the collapse. Coaches call it one of the most common ways teams score now. That is coaching agreement, not a count.

The answer is the strong-side winger's discipline. Stay high, in the lane, between their defenceman and your net. A winger who gets pulled low has handed them the play for free.

Know the trigger. When the puck goes behind your net, expect the pass back up. Wingers start closing on their points as the puck disappears behind the goal line, not when it comes out.

Then get out to the shot. Don't sit under it. A winger standing back near the faceoff dot blocks nothing. One closing hard at the top of the circle does. You are closing to block the shot, never to hit the shooter. And the whole method goes together. Close with your feet, don't lunge. Get square, with your shin pads to the puck. Head up, and out of the shot's path. Hands behind your body, stick down beside you. Never lift a leg. Never turn sideways or turn your back on it. And stay on your feet until the puck has left the shooter's stick.

Everyone else boxes out, eyes on the puck, body on the player. Here is why. The shot is only dangerous because of the tip and the rebound. And you can't beat anyone to a rebound you didn't see leave the stick.

So take the inside, and then the same rule as always. Hold your spot. No shove and no lean. Never your stick across anyone, and never hit or push into a back. If you see their numbers, pull up. Never lift anyone off their feet. Even going slowly, a crash there can hurt, with the post and your goalie right beside you. Never walk anyone off a spot they already hold. That can be a penalty in any league.

Fourteenth idea. The back door.

While everyone watches the battle in the strong-side corner, an attacker drifts to the far post. One pass across, and it is an open net. Every system fears this chance most. It is worth giving up almost anything else to stop it.

If you are the weak-side defenceman, your eyes follow the puck, but your body stays between that attacker and the net. Use your position. Hold your spot. No shove and no lean. Never your stick across anyone, and never hit or push into a back. If you see their numbers, pull up. Never lift anyone off their feet. Even going slowly, a crash there can hurt, with the post and your goalie right beside you. If you find yourself facing the corner, you have already lost the attacker.

Lay your stick flat in the seam across the slot. The pass has to cross the slot, so a stick lying there takes it away even when the passer is open.

The weak-side winger helps too, by dropping into the middle rather than standing out at the point looking tidy. A body in the high slot is worth more than perfect point coverage.

And call it the moment the attacker appears. Back door! Not when the pass is already on its way.

Fifteenth idea. The walk-down.

Instead of shooting, their defenceman skates down from the blue line toward the top of the circle. That is about thirty-five feet from your goal line. They have roughly halved their distance to your net, and nobody has touched them. From there they can shoot, or feed the slot.

This, not the point shot, is the collapse's real weakness. A winger guarding an area will often let them go, because they have walked out of that winger's area and into nobody's.

How you answer is a coaching choice, so know your team's answer before you need it.

In the collapse, the usual instruction is to follow them a few feet and stay connected, but not to chase them into the corner. Call it loudly, so everyone knows a fourth attacker is arriving low.

In man-on-man, you go with them all the way. That is why the walk-down doesn't trouble man-on-man teams.

In the hybrid, you pick them up as your player the moment they cross the dots. That is the cleanest answer of the three, and the main reason hybrids exist.

Whichever it is, picking them up means staying goal side. Stick on the puck, not a hit. Whether you may hit at all is your league's call. And if you see their numbers, pull up.

And when a fourth attacker arrives low, you are outnumbered down there. Somebody has to give up their area. It is never the doorstep. Usually the centre takes the extra player, and the weak-side winger takes over the high slot.

Sixteenth idea, and it is short. Pinned in your own end on a long shift.

Forty seconds or more, legs going, and they are working the puck around your boards. This is when the shape breaks, because tired players start chasing.

Shrink, don't chase. Hold your area or your player, keep your stick in the lane, and let them work the boards. Chasing costs the most energy and opens the most ice.

Take the first clean chance to get it out, and get it out where it stays out. Off the glass and out, or chipped into the neutral zone, the middle of the rink between the blue lines. Not fired the length of the ice.

Here is why. If it crosses their goal line untouched, that is icing. The faceoff comes straight back to your end. And in some leagues your tired line can't change for that faceoff, so check yours. A hurt player can always come off.

Change only once the puck is out and heading the other way. A change with the puck loose in your end is one of the commonest ways to give up an odd-man rush. That means more attackers coming at you than you have back. The common approach is that whoever is nearest the bench goes first as the puck leaves. Some coaches want it dumped deep before anyone moves. So ask your bench which yours wants.

And say it as the puck leaves. Out, change!

Seventeenth idea. When the system breaks.

A bad change, a puck bouncing off shin pads, three of you below the goal line and nobody where the plan says. Players freeze, and freezing is what turns a scramble into a goal. So here is the order. Work down it, and don't skip a step.

One. Bodies to the front of the net, right away. Not to the puck. The most dangerous thing in a scramble is an attacker free at your doorstep. If you are nearest and unsure, go and stand between your goalie and the nearest opponent.

Two. Sticks in lanes. If you can't reach anyone, lay your stick flat in the passing lane through the slot. Broken plays are decided by passes that should never have got through.

Three. Find the most dangerous free attacker. Look at the house, not the puck. Ask who could score in the next two seconds, and go and stand with them. It is almost never the one with the puck.

Four. Count. Outnumbered down low? Somebody high comes down, and says so. Even numbers? Match up, loudly. I've got nineteen. You take the net.

Five. Get the puck out any way you can that keeps it in the rink. Off the glass, never over it, around the boards, off a shin pad. A clear that gives the puck away has still bought your team a line change, and a reset.

Six. Reset before you attack. The most common second goal is the one given up straight after surviving the first, because everyone relaxed while three attackers were still in your end.

And the habit underneath all of it. The moment you don't know who is yours, go to the middle.

Let me pull those plays together. Against the cycle, don't chase, and send two, never hit or push into a back. Against the low-to-high, the strong-side winger stays high. Against the back door, body on the attacker and stick in the seam. Against the walk-down, know your team's answer. And when it all breaks, bodies to the net, sticks in lanes, and go to the middle.

Last part. Getting the puck out.

Eighteenth idea. Win it, and leave as a group.

Decide before the puck is yours. Scan while the battle is still going. Then when the puck arrives, you already know whether it is going to the winger, the centre, or off the glass. Here is why. Taking the puck and only then starting to look is how a zone exit gets turned over.

In the collapse, your wingers are high while the other team has the puck. As you win it, they come down the boards to between the hash marks and the goal line. That is where the pass goes, not the blue line.

Going back for that puck at the boards, you are the one with your head over the puck and a forechecker coming behind you. A forechecker is an opponent chasing to win the puck back in your end. This holds in every league, checking or not. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the checker, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly. If you are already facing the boards, get your stick and gloves up on the glass, spread wide, head off it, and only when things have gone wrong.

Then pick your exit, in this order. First, a controlled exit, a pass and out with the puck, whenever it is really there. Second, a safe exit without the puck. Rim it, which means sending it hard around the boards to the next teammate along. Or chip it off the glass. Either way there is no chance against you, and your team gets to change. That is a good play, not a failure. Third, when it is bad, anything that ends the shift.

And leave as a group. Forwards leaving early is how a cleared zone turns into an odd-man rush the other way. The puck should be out of the zone before your third forward is. Some breakouts send a forward early on purpose, with names like a centre fly, a slash or a stretch. Find out if yours does, before you treat an early exit as a mistake.

Nineteenth idea. How you clear the puck.

Use the glass, and keep the puck in the rink. Off the glass and out is a good play. So is a puck that dies in the neutral zone.

But never over the glass. Putting it straight over the glass from your own end is two minutes in some leagues, even by accident, and that includes hockey in Britain. Flipping it out on purpose, from anywhere, is two minutes in every league. So aim high and wide off the glass, never over it.

Let me pull it all together.

Find out your system, and ask the corner question. Defend the middle before the outside, and stay goal side. Somebody always has the doorstep, and you both know who. Say what you are about to do, early. Don't chase. Take away the next pass with your stick flat in the lane. When you don't know who is yours, go to the middle.

At your own net, get there first and hold your spot, with no shove, no lean, and never hit or push into a back. Lift the stick low, up not sideways, and let go. Never walk a screener out, and never shove anyone into your goalie. In the corner, pin the puck, not the player. If you see their numbers, pull up.

When you win it, decide before it is yours, never take it across the front of your own goal, and leave as a group. At the boards, the same words every time. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the checker, never duck. Keep it up even going slowly. Clear it off the glass, never over it. And at your own crease, knock the puck down and play it with your stick.

Much of this depends on what your team plays. So here are three questions for your coach, and one for your league. When the puck is in our corner, does the winger stay up or come down? Who has the doorstep when both defencemen go low? What do we do when their defenceman walks down from the point? And for your league. Is body checking allowed in our games?

Go and ask. Their answers tell you where to stand, and now you know why each answer makes sense.

### 7. Consequences checked against the source wording, never cheaper

Each surviving consequence sentence was read against the source text rather than summarised:
- Hit from behind along the boards: "plan on being thrown out" — the floor is an ejection in five books and USA Hockey's Casebook requires the ejection or match for a forceful check on a player along the boards (:208). Not "most leagues" (item 15).
- "A hit in the back is never just two minutes, in any league" — :515, CM :862.
- Cross-check: "never just two minutes" is the source's own claim for the cross-check in the back (:151); "can eject with nobody hurt" (:151, NHL severity alone); "above the shoulders, for certain in some" (:600). The script says "a cross-check in the back" so it does not widen :151.
- Chop: slashing in every league and can eject with nobody hurt (:584-585).
- Swing: "can be a penalty even by accident" — softened from a draft that said "is", because USA Hockey and Hockey Canada are not written as an accidental-contact removal (:704).
- Walk-out: "can be a penalty in any league, checking or not" (:561).
- Shove into goalie: "can be a penalty on you in every league" (:155).
- Crease cover: penalty shot in every book (:722); net empty "in most leagues" given the goal (KT12, CM :874; five of six on the bare fact, :739).
- Icing change: "in some leagues" (:666, :844, :846). The remake said "many"; changed, because the source names only the NHL, IIHF, PWHL and Hockey Canada's top divisions.

### 8. Departures from the source and the template

- **"Hold only ice you got to first" is stated for every listener.** The source scopes it to IIHF women's rules (:563, :538) and gives Hockey Canada a near version. Stated universally it is stricter, not cheaper, and it removes the carve-out shape brief item 13 bans.
- **"Pin the puck, never the player" is stated unconditionally.** The source attaches a rule to IIHF women's hockey only (:203). The remake's "in women's hockey… against the rules" carve-out is gone.
- **The walk-out reason "they have no puck" is dropped.** The source uses it (:141, :561, :536). Brief item 1 bans a reason that implies a shove on a puck carrier is fine. The ban is stated without condition.
- **Man-on-man's "no holding, hooking or bumping" drops "while they have no puck"** (:512), for the same reason. Stricter.
- **Three limbs come from `defender.md`**, not the source: the facing-the-boards fallback, "never lift anyone off their feet" with the slow-speed reason, and charging. Brief items 5, 6 and 12 require them; the source carries none.
- **The pressure gate's "Whether you may hit at all is your league's call"** is the brief's wording. The source's equivalent is "check the game, not the age group" (:565) and "the eligibility gate" of the standard.
- **"Head off it" in the fallback** is the brief's wording; defender.md says "stick and gloves up and spread on the glass".
- **The hash marks** are defined as "the short lines on the sides of each faceoff circle, about level with the dot". That definition is not in either source; it follows the Winger pilot and belongs to `rink_map.md`.
- **The shape is not one end at a time.** The page covers one zone, so after the spine the parts are: the systems, the front of your net, the plays you will face, getting it out. Each part ends on a recap except "getting it out", which runs straight into the close (the close recaps it).

- **"Three questions for your coach, and one for your league."** The template has five and two. The league question is "Is body checking allowed in our games?", which matches the gate; the remake's "which rulebook" was dropped as rules-touring.

### 9. What I found unclear in the source

The script's "never both without knowing who" covers it.

- The source's walk-out teaching relies on "they have no puck", which is the exact "so" trap brief item 1 names. ### 10. Where this script is weakest

- **The median sentence is 10 words**, below the brief's "about 12". The fixed forms are chopped into short imperatives and repeated. It may sound like a list in places, especially the back-door paragraph, where the net-front form sits inside a positioning instruction.
- **The net-front form is said four times and the gate six.** That is what items 6 and 13 require, but the gate in the box-plus-one and overload units gates acts that are barely physical ("chasing", "squeezing"). A listener may tire of it. I chose repetition over a missing gate.
- **+49.95% is at the ceiling, with 16 characters to spare.** Any further addition needs a matching cut. Cut list item 1 is the first lever.
- **The "getting it out" part has no recap of its own.** It runs into the close.
- **The second body's charging line** comes from `defender.md`'s step-up teaching, not the corner. The fit is reasonable (accelerating into an unsuspecting player), but it is a borrowed limb (brief item 11) and a reviewer should check it fits.
- **Backward pointers were audited by hand for shape.** The remaining ones are "From here on I will use one short version of it", "the same rule as always" and "the same words as always", which restate what they point at.
