This is The Basics, topic six. Shooting, and going to the net.

The Basics is a series for newer players and younger players. Each episode covers the core things to get right first, and why each one works. Once these feel natural, the longer Playing principles episode and the conversation episode on shooting go deeper.

Before the ideas, a few words this episode uses. I will explain each one now, so nothing gets in the way later.

The slot. This is the ice right in front of the other team's net, between the two faceoff circles. It runs from near the net out to the tops of those circles.

The outside. In this episode, that means everywhere further out than the slot. Out by the boards, and up near the blue line.

The crease. This is the goalie's area, the blue paint in front of the net. The red line around its edge counts as part of it.

A screen. This is when you stand where the goalie is trying to look, so they can't see the shot coming.

A tip. This is when your stick touches the puck on its way to the net and changes its direction.

A rebound. This is when the puck bounces back out off the goalie.

A lane. This is a clear path from your stick to the net, with no legs or sticks in the way.

The goalie is set. This means the goalie is still, facing you, and ready for your shot.

That is all the words. Now six ideas, one at a time, and the reason each one works.

First idea. Shoot from the middle.

Try to take your shots from the slot. If you are out wide and a teammate is in the slot, pass it to them. If two strides toward the middle would put you in the slot, take those two strides first.

Here is why it works. Shots from the slot go in several times as often as shots from the outside. Not a little more often. Several times. So one pass, or two strides toward the middle, is worth more than any trick with your hands.

A shot from far out, at a set goalie who can see it, with nobody in front, is one of the easiest saves in hockey. Moving to the middle is the biggest thing you can do to score more.

Second idea. Shoot quickly, before the goalie is set.

When you get a chance, shoot from where you already are. Don't stop to settle the puck first. And don't stop your feet. Shoot while you are still skating.

Here is why it works. A goalie doesn't stop the puck by reacting to it. They watch your body, your hands and your stick, and get into position before the puck is on its way. If your shot arrives after they are set, it is an easy save, even if it is hard. If it arrives before they are set, it is a real chance, even if it isn't very hard.

Every little pause gives the goalie a warning. Settling the puck, looking up and planting your feet all take time. And stopping your feet is the loudest warning of all.

Here is how to take a basic shot, the wrist shot. Start the puck behind your back foot, the foot further from the net. Sweep it forward along the ice, and don't slap at it. Keep the puck on your blade the whole way. Move your weight from your back foot to your front foot as you sweep. Then follow through. Finish low and the puck stays low. Finish high and the puck goes high.

Here is why. The power comes from your legs and body, not just your arms. And where your follow-through finishes is how you aim the height.

Third idea. Get the puck through to the net.

Wanting to shoot is good. Take a good chance straight away, without needing to be talked into it. But don't shoot just because you have the puck.

Before you shoot, look for a lane. If you can't see the net, there is no lane.

Here is why it works. A shot that hits a defender's shin pad is worse than no shot at all. You lose the puck. The goalie never had to do anything. And the other team often races the other way with it.

If there is no lane, step sideways a stride or two. The defender is set up for where you were a moment ago, so moving makes them move too. Or fake the shot, so they drop into the lane, then step round them. Or pass the puck instead, and wait for a better chance.

If you are shooting from up near the blue line, the job is to get the puck through, not to shoot it as hard as you can. Keep it low, at knee height or below. Here is why. From out there, the shot is mostly for your teammates at the net. A low shot can be tipped or turned into a rebound. A high one is no use for a tip.

Before you shoot, ask yourself one question. What happens if this doesn't go in? If the answer is a rebound for a teammate, or a tip, then shoot. If the answer is that it hits a shin pad and they break away, don't.

Let me stop and repeat the first three ideas, because they decide most goals. Shoot from the middle, or pass to a teammate who is there. Shoot quickly, from where you are, without stopping your feet. And find a lane first, so the puck gets through.

Fourth idea. Make the goalie move, and aim where the goalie isn't.

A goalie who is set and can see the puck saves almost everything. So make them move first.

Pass the puck across the middle of the ice before the shot. Or carry it from behind the net and out the other side. Then shoot as soon as the puck arrives.

Here is why it works. A pass across the middle drags the goalie sideways. While they are still sliding across, they are not set. The far side of the net, down low, is the space they are moving away from.

So aim for where the goalie isn't. The gap between the goalie's pads is called the five-hole. It opens while the goalie is moving, but it is closed when they are down and set. Just after a pass across the middle, aim far side and low.

And if teammates are at the net, shoot low and hard at the goalie's pads. Here is why. That shot can go in, get tipped in, or bounce out as a rebound. That is three good chances, not one.

Some coaches teach aiming for the corners instead. Both ways are taught by good coaches, so ask yours which they want.

Fifth idea. At the net, screen the goalie and get your stick on the puck.

When a teammate is about to shoot, go and stand where the goalie is trying to look. Stand just outside the crease, and keep your skates off its red line too. Never touch the goalie. And never stand in the goalie's way back to their net.

Here is why it works. A goalie who can't see the puck has to guess. A screen turns an easy save into a guess.

A screen is a place you stand, not a push. Hold your spot with your position, not your weight. Leave the goalie room to move. If you get pushed into the blue paint, get your feet out before you play the puck.

While you screen, keep your stick on the ice, in the path of the shot. That is where a tip happens. Hold the stick loosely, and let the angle of your blade change the puck's direction. Don't swing at it.

Here is why tips work. The goalie has got ready for a shot going one way. A tip changes its direction right in front of them, too late for them to follow. Changing the puck's direction late beats making the shot harder.

Keep your stick low when you tip, between your knees and your chest, and below everyone's shoulders. Bring it back down after the tip as well. You are in a crowd, and a high stick can hit someone in the face. A puck tipped with your stick up high may not count, either.

Now the most important safety part of this episode. Standing at the net.

Defenders will try to move you. You are standing still, next to a steel goal frame, watching the shooter. So keep your head up and your chin off your chest, and never duck. If you meet a goal post, meet it with an arm or a leg, never your head.

Here is the one reason. You want to see what is coming, and keep your head and neck out of it. Hitting a post head first can hurt your neck, even at walking speed.

Sixth idea. Follow the shot to the net.

When you shoot, or a teammate shoots, skate to the net with your stick on the ice. Don't stop and watch the shot.

Here is why it works. A goalie who has just made a save is getting back into position, not set. The rebound goes to whoever gets there. So if a rebound lands on your stick, shoot it straight away. Don't stop it and pick a corner. If the goalie is down, lift it over the pad.

Play the puck, never the goalie. Pull up before you reach the goalie. And if a goalie has turned away from you, never go into their back. Stop, and take the space in front of the net instead.

Here is why. A goalie who is down, or turned away, can't protect themselves. Running into a goalie can hurt them, it can be called a penalty, and it can cost your team the goal.

One more safety point, for practice. In the warm-up, keep your shots off your goalie's head, and shoot from a distance, not from right in close. And never shoot at the other team's goalie. Here is why. No whistle comes in time to protect them, and a mask can hide how hurt they are.

Let me put it all together.

Shoot from the middle. If a teammate is in the slot and you are not, pass.

Shoot quickly, from where you are, without stopping your feet.

Find a lane first. Ask what happens if it doesn't go in.

Make the goalie move, then aim where the goalie isn't. With teammates at the net, shoot low and hard at the pads.

At the net, stand just outside the blue paint and off its red line, never touch the goalie, and keep your stick on the ice and below everyone's shoulders. Head up, chin off your chest, never duck.

And follow every shot to the net. Shoot the rebound at once, and play the puck, never the goalie.

Two questions to ask your coach. Do you want us aiming for the corners, or where the goalie isn't? And where do you want me when a teammate shoots?

Ask them. Their answers tell you how your team wants you to play.

Once these six ideas feel natural, you are ready for more. The longer Playing principles episode on shooting goes deeper into each idea, and why it works. The conversation episode talks it all through. Start there next.

### 6. Where this script is weakest

- **Idea 2 carries two things**: the quick release, and the whole wrist shot. A young listener may hear the mechanics as the idea.
- **Idea 5 is the longest and densest**: screen, tip and the safety part. It may need splitting into two ideas if the owner's ear test finds it heavy; the cost is one more idea (seven, still within the 4–7 range).

It is stricter than the rulebooks (incidental scramble contact is permitted in some), which is the conservative direction.
