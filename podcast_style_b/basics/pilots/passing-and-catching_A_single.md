===== SCRIPT START =====

A teammate is about to pass you the puck, and an opponent is skating towards you. What you do next decides whether your team keeps it.

This is The Basics, topic five. Passing and catching the puck. Five ideas to get right first, for newer and younger players.

First idea. Move the puck, then move yourself.

Say you pick up the puck in the middle of the ice, with a teammate open off to the side. The pull is to put your head down and skate. Instead, ask one question first. Who is open? Not, where can I skate?

The puck travels faster than any skater, so a pass makes the other team look around again and work out who to cover. Carry it, and they can just skate alongside you. You can't out-skate five people, but you can out-pass them.

That doesn't mean never carry. Carry it when nobody is open, or to give your teammates time to get open. But think pass first.

Once the puck has gone, keep moving to a new open spot, so you can get it back. A pass needs two players, and when you don't have the puck, your job is to be easy to pass to.

That means three things. Find a lane, which is a clear line between you and the puck, with no stick or skate in the way. Get there on time, not too early, since a player who arrives too early brings a defender with them.

And show a target. Your blade is the bottom part of your stick, the part that touches the puck. Put it flat on the ice in front of you, hold it still, and call for the puck. A still blade gives your passer something to aim at. A stick waving in the air makes them guess.

Your passer needs someone open, in a clear lane, right now. So when passes keep going wrong, look first at the players without the puck.

Second idea. Know your next play before the puck reaches you.

Picture a pass coming to you along the boards. The boards are the wall around the rink. You watch only the puck. It lands, you look up, and the other player is already there. By the other player, I mean the opponent closing in on you.

So take a quick look over your shoulder while the pass is still travelling, then look back at the puck before it reaches you. That look is free, since your eyes have nothing else to do while the puck travels. Wait until it lands, and the picture has already changed.

Make it a real look. Turn your head until your chin goes past your shoulder, because just moving your eyes shows you very little. Check both shoulders, not only your favourite one. A surprise usually comes from the side you weren't planning to use. And keep skating while you look.

Then decide while the puck is still coming. Say your next play to yourself in a word or two. Up the wall. Back to my defenceman. Your defencemen are the two players who mostly stay back. Pick a second choice too, in case the first one disappears. Decide early, and catching the puck starts your next play. Decide late, and the other player reaches you first. A good play now beats a perfect play too late.

Point your body that way as well. Come to the puck on an angle, not straight at your passer, with your hips and shoulders turned so you can see up the ice. That is called being open, and it puts your next play in front of you when the puck lands. Have to turn round to find it, and you've given the other player the time they needed.

Being open matters most by the boards. With your back to the play, the other player arrives behind you and you can't see them coming.

Now the part that keeps you safe. How you stand by the boards. Come in on an angle, with your feet apart and your knees bent. Skates parallel to the boards, which means pointing along them. Your forearm and hip are nearest the boards, never the point of your shoulder. Head up, and chin off your chest. Never your back to the other player. And never duck. All of that comes down to one thing. You want to see what's coming, and take it on your side, not on your head or neck. That is true even going slowly.

If you're already facing the boards and can't turn, put your stick and gloves up on the glass, spread wide, and keep your head off it. That's only for when things have gone wrong. It isn't a way to stand.

None of this looking works if you're staring at the puck. Keep it out in front of you, with a soft grip, because tight hands can't feel the puck and your eyes keep dropping to it.

Third idea. Pass to where your teammate is going, on the side away from the other player.

Imagine your teammate skating fast up the side of the ice, and you pass to where they are right now. By the time the puck gets there, it is behind them. They have to reach back, slow down or turn, and the speed that made the play dangerous is gone.

So pass to the spot just ahead of them, where they can skate onto the puck without slowing down. It's like throwing a ball for a dog that's already running. You throw it ahead of the dog, not at it.

Watch their skates, not their chest. The skates show where they're really heading. If you're not sure, lead them a little. A pass slightly ahead can be skated onto. A pass behind can't.

Then pick the side. If the other player is coming from your teammate's left, pass to their right, so your teammate's body sits between the puck and the other player. That side matters most, even if it puts the puck on their backhand, the weaker side of the blade for most players.

Leading your teammate also keeps them safe. A puck behind them, or in their feet, makes them look down to find it. If the other player is arriving near the boards just then, their head is down at the worst moment, and that is how players get hurt. Where you put the puck decides where your teammate's head is.

For the pass itself, sweep the puck along the ice rather than slapping it, and finish low, blade pointing at your target. A sweep guides the puck the whole way. A slap only touches it for an instant, so it goes wide.

Fourth idea. Catch the puck softly, and send your first touch into your next play.

Now picture a hard pass coming at you, and you hold your stick stiff. The puck hits your blade and bounces a foot or two away. That foot is all the other player needs to take it.

A stiff blade acts like a wall. So meet the puck out in front of you, hold your stick loosely, and as the puck touches the blade, let the blade give a little, back towards you. A soft blade stops the puck dead, ready to play.

Then make that first touch count. As you catch the puck, push it to where your next play needs it. Away from the other player. Forward, so you can keep skating. Or out to the side, ready to shoot. A puck stopped under your feet has to be moved again before you can do anything, and that costs you time. So test yourself after every catch. Could you pass or shoot straight away? If not, that touch wasted time. And keep your feet moving as you catch it. A player standing still to catch a pass is easy to reach.

And if a pass is bad, stop it first, with your stick, your skate or your shin pad. That's only for a pass. Stepping in front of a shot is a different skill, so don't use this to block shots. Once it's stopped, look up and play it. Don't try to catch a hard pass and make a play in one go.

Fifth idea. In your own end, make the safe pass.

Last scene. You have the puck in your own end. Your own end is the part of the rink where your goal is, behind your own blue line. A teammate is on the far side, and there's a gap right across the front of your net. It looks tempting. Leave it.

In your own end, never pass across the front of your own goal. Never pass blindly up the middle, to someone you can't see. And never pass across the ice from one side to the other. The middle of your own end is right in front of your net, so a pass picked off there is a chance against you, with your goalie not ready.

Lose a pass in their end, and you lose the puck. Lose one in your own end, and you can lose a goal. So in your own end, pass only when you're sure. In their end, you can take more chances.

When the other player is right on you, make the pass you know you can complete. Send the puck hard along the boards to a teammate, play it off the boards past the other player, or pass it back to your defenceman. Those are good plays, not failures. The boards are always there, and nobody can step in front of them. Know your safe play before you reach the puck, not after you're trapped. Players who look calm under pressure didn't think faster. They decided earlier.

These are common habits, not laws. Some teams build a planned pass behind the net into their play, so ask your coach which passes your team allows in your own end.

So, the five ideas. Pass first, then get open again. Look and decide before the puck arrives, and face where you're going. Pass to where your teammate is going, away from the other player. That keeps their head up, too. Catch it softly, into your next play. And in your own end, never pass across the front of your own goal. Make the pass you know you can complete.

By the boards, skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the other player, never duck.

Two questions to ask your coach. Which passes do you want us to make in our own end? And when I'm trapped on the boards, where do you want the puck to go? Their answers tell you how your team wants you to play.

Once these feel natural, the longer episodes on passing and scanning go deeper.
