===== SCRIPT START =====

This is The Basics, topic five. Passing and catching the puck.

The Basics is a series for newer players and younger players. Each episode covers the core things to get right first, and why each one works. Once these feel natural, the longer Playing principles episodes and the conversation episodes on passing, and on scanning, go deeper.

Before the ideas, a few words this episode uses. I will explain each one now, so nothing gets in the way later.

The blade. This is the flat part at the bottom of your stick, the part that touches the puck.

Your forehand and your backhand. The forehand is the side of your blade that cups around the puck. The backhand is the other side. Most players control the puck better on the forehand.

Receiving. This just means catching a pass with your stick. The player who catches it is the receiver.

The other player. In this episode, that means the player from the other team who is closing in on you, whether they are after the puck or just coming at you.

A lane. This is a clear line between the puck and your teammate, with no stick or skate in the way.

The boards. This is the wall around the rink.

Your own end. This is the part of the rink where your own goal is, behind your own blue line.

Your defencemen. These are the two players on each team who mostly stay back.

A scan. This is a quick turn of your head to look around, and then back.

That is all the words. Now seven ideas, one at a time, and the reason each one works.

First idea. Move the puck, then move yourself.

When you have the puck, think first, who is open? Not, where can I skate?

Here is why it works. The puck moves faster than any skater can. A pass makes the other team look around again and work out who to cover. If you carry the puck, they can just skate alongside you. You can't out-skate five people. But you can out-pass them.

And after you pass, keep moving. Skate to a new open spot, so you can get the puck back.

That doesn't mean never carry the puck. Carry it when nobody is open, or to give your teammates time to get open. But passing comes first.

Second idea. Look before the puck gets to you.

When a pass is coming to you, take a quick look over your shoulder while it is still on its way. Then look back at the puck before it lands on your stick.

Here is why it works. While the puck is travelling, your eyes have nothing else to do. So that look is free. If you wait until the puck lands, the other player is already on you, and the picture has changed while you looked.

A good look is a real turn of your head. Turn until your chin goes past your shoulder. Just moving your eyes shows you very little. Look over both shoulders, not just your favourite one. A surprise usually comes from the side you weren't planning to use. And keep skating while you look.

Then decide while the puck is still coming. Pick your next play, and say it to yourself in a word or two. Up the wall. Back to my defenceman. Then pick a second choice too, in case the first one disappears.

Here is why that works. If you decide before the puck arrives, catching it starts your next play straight away. If you decide after, the other player has time to reach you. A good play made now beats a perfect play made too late.

One more thing makes all this possible. You can only look around if you don't have to stare at the puck. Keep the puck out in front of you, and keep a soft grip. Here is why. Tight hands can't feel the puck, so you keep looking down at it. You can practise this off the ice. Stickhandle while a friend holds up fingers for you to call out.

Third idea. Pass to where your teammate is going, on the side away from the other player.

If your teammate is skating, don't pass to where they are now. Pass to the spot just ahead of them, so they can skate onto the puck without slowing down.

Here is why it works. A pass aimed at where a skating player is now will be behind them by the time it gets there. They have to reach back, slow down or turn. All of that kills the speed that made the play dangerous.

To know where they are going, watch their skates, not their chest. Their skates show where they are really heading. If you are not sure, lead them a little. A pass slightly ahead can be skated onto. A pass behind can't.

And put the pass on the side away from the other player. If the other player is coming from your teammate's left, pass to their right. Here is why. Your teammate's body then sits between the puck and the other player, like a shield. That side matters most, even if it puts the puck on their backhand.

Leading your teammate also keeps them safe. A puck that lands behind them, or in their feet, makes them look down to find it. If the other player is arriving near the boards just then, their head is down at the worst moment. Heads down near the boards is how players get hurt. So where you put the puck decides where your teammate's head is.

For the pass itself, sweep the puck along the ice. Don't slap at it. Finish low, with your blade pointing at where you want the puck to go. Here is why. A sweep guides the puck the whole way. A slap only touches it for an instant, and it goes wide.

Let me stop and repeat the first three ideas, because they make every pass work. Move the puck, then move yourself. Look over your shoulder while the pass is coming, and decide your next play before it lands. And pass to where your teammate is going, on the side away from the other player.

Fourth idea. Get open, and give your passer a target.

A pass needs two players. When you don't have the puck, it is your job to be easy to pass to.

Move into a lane, a clear line between you and the puck. Get there on time, not too early. Then show a target. Put your blade flat on the ice, out in front of you, and hold it still. And call for the puck.

Here is why it works. The passer is looking for someone who is open, in a clear lane, right now. If nobody is, there is no pass. Skating hard through a spot where an opponent's stick is in the way doesn't help. Arriving too early means a defender arrives with you. And a still blade on the ice is the passer's aiming point. If your stick is waving in the air, the passer has to guess.

So when passes keep going wrong, look at the players without the puck first.

Fifth idea. Cushion the puck, and send your first touch into your next play.

When the pass arrives, meet it out in front of you. Hold your stick loosely. As the puck touches your blade, let the blade give a little, back toward you.

Here is why it works. A stiff blade is like a wall. The puck hits it and bounces a foot or two away. That foot is all the other player needs to take it. A soft blade stops the puck dead, ready to play.

Then make that first touch count. Push the puck, as you catch it, to where your next play needs it. Away from the other player. Or forward, so you can keep skating. Or out to the side, ready to shoot.

Here is why. A puck stopped under your feet has to be moved again before you can do anything. That costs you time. The test is simple. After your first touch, could you pass or shoot straight away? If not, that touch wasted time.

And keep your feet moving as you catch it. A player standing still to catch a pass is easy to reach.

If a pass is bad, stop it first, with your stick, your skate or your shin pad. Then look up and play it. Don't try to catch a hard pass and make a play in one go. This is only for a pass. Stepping in front of a shot is a different skill, so don't try it from this.

Sixth idea. Catch the puck with your body open to the ice.

Open means your hips and shoulders are turned so you can see up the ice as the puck arrives. You are not facing your passer with your back to everything else.

Come to the puck on an angle, not straight at the passer. Decide which way you are going before the puck arrives, and be pointing that way when it does.

Here is why it works. Your next play is already in front of you when the puck lands. If you have to turn around to find it, the other player has had the time they needed.

This matters most next to the boards. If your back is to the play, the other player arrives behind you, and you can't see them coming.

So now the most important safety part of this episode. How you stand by the boards.

Come in on an angle, with your feet apart and your knees bent. Skates parallel to the boards, which means pointing along them. Your forearm and your hip are the parts nearest the boards, never the point of your shoulder. Head up, and chin off your chest. Never your back to the other player. And never duck.

Here is the one reason behind all of it. You want to see what is coming, and take it on your side, not on your head or neck. That is true even going slowly.

And if you are already facing the boards and can't turn, put your stick and gloves up on the glass, spread wide, and keep your head off it. That is only for when things have gone wrong. It is not a way to stand.

Seventh idea. In your own end, make the safe pass.

In your own end, never pass across the front of your own goal. Never pass blindly up the middle, to someone you can't see. And never pass across the ice from one side to the other.

Here is why it works. The middle of your own end is right in front of your own net. A pass picked off there is a chance against you, with your goalie not ready. In the other team's end, a lost pass costs you the puck. In your own end, it can cost you a goal.

So in your own end, pass only when you are sure. In their end, you can take more chances.

When the other player is right on you, make the pass you know you can complete. Send the puck hard along the boards to a teammate. Play it off the boards past the other player. Or pass it back to your defenceman. Those are all good plays, not failures. And know your safe play before you reach the puck, not after you are trapped.

Here is why. The boards are always there, and nobody can step in front of them. And players who look calm under pressure didn't think faster. They decided earlier.

These are common habits, not laws. Some teams build a planned pass behind the net into their play. So ask your coach which passes your team allows in your own end.

Let me put it all together.

Move the puck, then move yourself.

Look over your shoulder while the pass is coming, and decide your next play before it lands.

Pass to where your teammate is going, on the side away from the other player. That keeps their head up, too.

When you don't have the puck, get into a clear lane, on time, with your blade flat on the ice, and call for it.

Cushion the puck with soft hands, and send your first touch into your next play.

Catch it with your body open to the ice. By the boards, skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the other player, never duck.

And in your own end, never pass across the front of your own goal. Make the pass you know you can complete.

Two questions to ask your coach. Which passes do you want us to make in our own end? And when I'm trapped on the boards, where do you want the puck to go?

Ask them. Their answers tell you how your team wants you to play.

Once these seven ideas feel natural, you are ready for more. The longer Playing principles episodes on passing, and on scanning, go deeper into each idea, and why it works. The conversation episodes talk it all through. Start there next.
