===== SCRIPT START =====

This is The Basics, topic twelve. Playing goal.

The Basics is a series for newer players and younger players. Each episode covers the core things to get right first, and why each one works. Once these feel natural, the longer Playing principles episode and the conversation episode on goaltending go deeper.

Before the ideas, a few words this episode uses. I will explain each one now, so nothing gets in the way later.

The goalie. This is the player who guards the net. In this episode, that is you.

The crease. This is the blue paint in front of your net. It is your area.

The posts. These are the two upright sides of the net, part of its steel frame.

Your defencemen. These are the two players on your team who mostly stay back, near you.

Depth. This is how far out of your net you are.

Square. This means your chest, hips and shoulders are facing the puck.

A rebound. This is when the puck bounces back out off you after a save.

The butterfly. This is dropping to your knees with both pads out to the sides, and the gap between your legs closed.

A screen. This is a player from the other team standing where they block your view of the puck.

That is all the words. Now seven big ideas, one at a time, and the reason each one works.

First idea. Stand on the line, and be out, square and set.

Picture a line from the puck to the middle of your goal. Stand on that line, facing along it.

Here is why it works. When you are on that line, you cover the middle of the net. The shooter can only beat you at the edges.

Remember three steps, in this order. Be out, at the right depth. Be square to the puck. And be set, which means still, as the shot leaves the stick.

Here is why being set matters. A goalie who is still moving when the shot leaves can't push in any direction. Their weight is already going one way.

You can't look back at your net during play. So at every whistle, rebuild your picture of where it is. Skate your arc, tap both posts with your stick or glove, and find your spot at the top of the blue paint. Then you don't have to think about it when play starts again.

Second idea. Decide your depth before the shot.

Coming out toward the shooter makes the net look smaller to them. But it costs you something. The further out you are, the further you have to travel when they pass across.

So here is a simple guide. One shooter, nobody open for a pass, and the shot from far out. Come out to the top of the blue paint, or a little beyond. The puck in close, or a pass open across the front of your net. Get back.

Here is why it works. With nobody to pass to, coming out takes the shot away. With the puck in close, being out buys you nothing, and you need to be able to move across.

Against a screened shot from far out, coaches disagree. Some want you deeper, and some want you to stay close to the shooter. Ask your coach which one they teach.

Watch for two habits. Hiding deep in your net, and racing out at every shooter. Both usually mean your confidence has dipped, not that your technique is wrong.

Third idea. Watch the puck all the way in.

Follow the puck with your head, not just your eyes. Watch it right into your pad, your glove or your chest.

Here is why it works. Goalie coaches say almost every soft goal comes from losing sight of the puck. And if you saw the puck hit you, you know where the rebound went.

Keep your head still and your hands quiet. Here is why. If your arms fly and your head snaps back, you lose the puck just as it hits you.

When they pass, start moving as the pass leaves the stick, not when it arrives. Here is why. A goalie who stands and watches the pass travel is always late for the shot.

Let me stop and repeat the first three ideas, because everything else is built on them. Stand on the line from the puck to the middle of your goal, out, square and set. Pick your depth before the shot, out against a lone shooter, back when the puck is close or a pass is open. And watch the puck all the way into your body.

Fourth idea. Control the rebound.

A save that sends the puck back out in front of you isn't really finished. It turns a shot from far away into a shot from close in.

First, try to kill the puck dead. Let your stick give a little when the puck hits it. Relax into the shot, instead of bracing hard. Here is why. A stiff stick or a tense body bounces the puck straight back out. A soft one drops it at your feet.

If you can't kill it, steer it. Turn whatever the puck will hit, so it goes wide and low, toward the corners. Never into the middle, in front of your net. Here is why. Nobody is standing in the corner ready to shoot. The middle is where the other team's best players wait.

Just before the shot, know where their players are. That tells you where the rebound must not go.

Now covering the puck to stop play. In your crease, with a player from the other team right on you, cover the puck you have saved. If nobody is on you, put it down and play it. If your defenceman is coming with time, leave it for them. And outside your crease, play it or clear it. Don't fall on it out there.

Fifth idea. Stay on your feet until going down is the answer.

The butterfly is a good save. But it is a save you choose, not a habit.

Don't drop into the butterfly on every shot. Here is why. Once you are down, the top of the net is open, and you can't get back up in time. That is worse the smaller you are. A young goalie on their knees leaves a lot of open net above them.

So stay up, read the shot, then choose the save. After the save, get back to your feet when you can.

The same goes on a breakaway, when one player skates in on you alone. Come out to meet them, then back up at their speed. Stay on your feet. Here is why. The shooter's whole plan is to make you move first. The last one to move wins.

Sixth idea. Talk to your team, early, short and every time.

You see the whole play. A defenceman fetching a puck in your corner is facing the boards, the wall around the rink. You can see who is coming up behind them.

Here is why it works. If you don't say it, they have to guess. And a call costs you nothing.

If players start pushing or fighting, stay in your crease until an official tells you to leave it.

A few calls to know. Time means nobody is near, so they can lift their head and make a good play. Only say time when you are sure. A wrong time sends your defenceman into a hit they weren't ready for. Man on means someone is coming, so move the puck now. Back door means a player from the other team is alone at the far post. And screen means you can't see the puck.

Make screen a call you always make. Here is why. Nobody else on the ice can know that you can't see. It tells your teammates to move out of your way, or to stand beside that player and hold their own ground, without pushing.

While you wait for help, pick one side of the screen and see the puck clearly there. That beats half-seeing it on both sides.

Call early, keep it short, and call every time. Here is why. A goalie who only calls sometimes gets ignored. Agree your words with your team before the game, because words differ from team to team.

Seventh idea. Behind your net, keep it simple.

When the puck is sent hard around the boards behind your net, you can often stop it and leave it there for your defenceman. Shout leave it, early, while they are still coming. Here is why it works. A race to the far corner becomes an easy pickup.

How far behind your net you may play the puck changes from league to league. Some coaches also want their goalie to touch the puck as little as possible. So ask your coach what they want from you.

Don't leave your net for a puck you might not win. Here is why. Lose that race and your net is empty. And never pass the puck up the middle. A turnover there is a shot on an empty net.

If you do race to a puck by the boards, arrive with your head up and your chin off your chest. Never go head first into the boards or a post. That can hurt your neck, even at slow speed. Ask your goalie coach how to stand when a player is coming in behind you.

Now the safety part of this episode. Four things.

First, in your crease. Claim your spot early. Keep your stick and your body off the players from the other team, even when they are in your way. Shout for a teammate instead.

Second, your mask. If you need to, borrow the rest of your gear, but have your own mask. Here is why. The sticker on a mask tells you nothing about how many pucks it has already stopped. Wear a neck guard at every age, and a small flap hanging from your mask does not replace it. Replace gear you have outgrown, too, because gear that hurts you teaches you to flinch. And ask your teammates never to shoot at your head in the warm-up.

Third, a puck off your mask. If a hard shot hits your mask, say something at the next stoppage. Tell the official, your bench and your captain. Here is why. Nobody can see your face behind the cage. A concussion, which is an injury to the brain, doesn't need you to be knocked out. So don't play on to see how it feels. If you might have one, come off, and don't play again that day. A qualified medical professional decides when you play again, not you.

Fourth, warming up. Get warm and moving before anyone takes real shots at you. Here is why. The butterfly and the slides work your hips hard, and cold is when they cost you. If your groin or hip keeps hurting, get it looked at by a physiotherapist or a sports physician.

One last thing. Every goalie lets in goals, and some will be bad. What matters is what you do next. Have a reset that you do the same way every time. Skate a small circle, get a drink, and tap both posts. Here is why it works. Doing the same thing every time tells your body that the last shift is over. Think about the goal tomorrow, not during the game. And never point at a teammate, not with your hands and not with a look.

Let me put it all together.

Stand on the line from the puck to the middle of your goal. Out, square and set, and rebuild your picture at every whistle.

Pick your depth before the shot.

Watch the puck all the way in, and move as a pass leaves the stick.

Kill the rebound if you can. If you can't, send it wide and low to the corners, never into the middle.

Stay on your feet until going down is the answer, and on a breakaway, be the last one to move.

Talk early, short and every time. Time, man on, back door, screen, and leave it.

Behind your net, stop the puck for your defenceman, and never pass up the middle. Head up and chin off your chest at the boards.

In your crease, keep your stick and body off other players. Have your own mask and a neck guard. Say something after a hard shot off the mask. And warm up before real shots.

Three questions to ask your coach. How deep do you want me against a screened shot? How much do you want me to play the puck behind the net, and how far back may I go? And which words does our team use for its calls?

Ask them. Their answers tell you how your team wants you to play.

Once these seven ideas feel natural, you are ready for more. The longer Playing principles episode on goaltending goes deeper into each idea, and why it works. The conversation episode talks it all through. Start there next.
