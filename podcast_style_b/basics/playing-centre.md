===== SCRIPT START =====

This is The Basics, topic nine. Playing centre.

The Basics is a series for newer players and younger players. Each episode covers the core things to get right first, and why each one works. Once these feel natural, the longer Playing principles episode and the conversation episode on the centre go deeper.

Before the ideas, a few words this episode uses. I will explain each one now, so nothing gets in the way later.

The centre. In a full game, your team has three forwards on the ice. The two on the sides are the wingers. The one in the middle is the centre. Behind them are your two defencemen, the players who mostly stay back.

The middle. Picture the rink split into three long strips, from one end to the other. The strips down each side, next to the boards, belong to the wingers. The boards are the wall around the rink. The strip down the middle is yours, in all three parts of the rink.

High and low. In whichever end you are in, high means toward the blue line. Low means toward the goal line, the line the net sits on.

The slot. This is the space in front of the net, between the two big faceoff circles. It is where most goals come from. The high slot is the top part of it, further out from the net.

The crease. This is the goalie's area, the blue paint in front of the net. Treat the red line around its edge as part of it.

A rebound. This is when the puck bounces back out off the goalie.

The breakout. This is how your team gets the puck out of its own end.

The forecheck. This is chasing the puck in the other team's end, to win it back.

A faceoff, or a draw. The official drops the puck between two players to start play. Most of the time, the centre takes it.

That is all the words. Now seven big ideas, one at a time, and the reason each one works.

First idea. In your own end, get below the puck.

When your defenceman goes back to get the puck in your own end, swing low. Get into the inside half of the faceoff circle on the puck's side. Not out on the boards. Keep moving, stay open, and talk, so your defenceman always has someone to pass to.

Here is why it works. Your defenceman needs a safe pass. If you are too high, they have nobody to pass to. They get stuck with the puck, with players coming at them. And if you drift out to the boards, that is your winger's ice. One opponent can then cover you and your winger at once.

Teams play this differently. In one common version, the centre leaves early up the middle, and a winger drops low instead. So ask your coach which your team plays. The one thing that always goes wrong is nobody being low under the puck.

Second idea. Never leave the slot empty in your own end.

When the other team has the puck in your end, your job moves with the puck.

If the puck is in front of your net, hold the high slot. Mark whoever is standing there.

If the puck is in the corner, go in and help your defenceman win it. Go in with your head up. Two of you against one usually wins the puck.

Here is why it works. The slot is where goals come from. If you stay up in the slot while the puck is in the corner, your defenceman is alone. They will probably lose that battle. But if you go to the corner and nobody fills the slot, you have swapped a battle you might win for a chance nobody can stop.

So when you go into the corner, call the winger from the far side down into the slot behind you. Call it early, and call it loud. And if your defenceman and your other winger already have the corner, stay in the slot.

This is one way to play. In another, called man-on-man, each player follows one opponent wherever they go. Then nobody fills in behind you. So ask your coach which your team plays.

Third idea. Under pressure, put the puck to the outside.

If you have the puck in the middle of your own end, with players closing in, don't try to carry it through them. Get to the boards or behind your net first. Then move it.

Here is why it works. The middle of your own end is the worst place on the ice to lose the puck. Lost there, it turns straight into a shot at your net. The middle of the rink is risky too. A puck lost there lets the other team come at you with more players than you have back.

So move it to the outside. Give it to a winger on the boards. Or send it hard along the boards to a teammate further round. That is called a rim. Getting the puck out of danger matters more than making a great play.

Decide before the puck reaches you. While it is on its way, take a quick look over your shoulder. Here is why. Then you can move it the moment it lands, instead of finding out with a player already on you.

Now the most important safety part of this episode. Playing the puck from the boards, or from behind your net, means a player from the other team is often behind you. That happens almost every shift. So two rules on the boards.

Never turn your back to the other player near the boards, whether they are trying to take the puck or just coming at you. And never duck.

Here is the one reason behind both. You want to see what is coming, and take it on your side, not on your head or neck. That is true even when the play is slow.

So here is how you stand by the boards. Skates parallel to the boards, which means pointing along them. Your forearm and your hip are the parts nearest the boards, never the point of your shoulder. Head up, chin off your chest. Never your back to the other player. Never duck.

Get your feet turned that way before you reach the puck. If you are already facing the boards and can't turn in time, get your stick and gloves up and spread on the glass above the boards. That softens it.

Let me stop and repeat the first three ideas, because they are your job in your own end. Swing low, so your defenceman has a safe pass. Never leave the slot empty, and call your winger in when you go to the corner. And under pressure, put the puck to the outside. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the other player, never duck.

Fourth idea. Own the middle of the ice, going forward and coming back.

Going forward, carry the puck through the middle of the rink, with speed. If a winger is open, pass it and let them go in at full speed. If you have space, carry it yourself.

Here is why it works. From the middle, you can see both wingers and both defencemen. It is the best place on the ice to decide from.

Keep your head up while you do it. Here is why. You want to see what is coming. The view you carry the puck for is the same view that keeps you safe.

Now coming back. When the other team gets the puck, skate back hard, down the middle.

Look for the forward coming in late, behind the player with the puck. That late player is usually more dangerous than the player with the puck.

Here is why that player matters. Everyone else is watching the puck, so the late player is easy to forget. And they are heading for the slot, with nobody near them.

So get on their inside, between them and your net. Keep your stick on the ice, in the space where a pass to them would go. Not on their body. And don't trail behind their shoulder. Here is why. From behind them, you can't stop anything.

Don't chase the player with the puck. Your defencemen have that player. If you swing wide to help, you leave the exact ice your defencemen are protecting.

Don't step across the late player's path, and don't hit them or hold them. Beat them to the spot with your skating.

And whenever you do catch up with someone, play the puck, not the player. Never put your stick or your body into anyone's back. If you can see the numbers on the back of their jersey, pull up and play the puck.

Who takes the middle on the way back is your coach's choice. Some teams give it to whoever gets back first. Ask your coach. And if nobody has called it, take it.

Fifth idea. In their end, play the slot and finish at the net.

When you have the puck in the slot with space, shoot.

Here is why it works. A shot from the slot goes in several times more often than a shot from out wide.

One pass is worth more than that shot. It is a pass across the middle, in front of the net. Here is why. The goalie has to push hard to the side, and the puck gets there before they are ready.

When you don't have the puck, keep moving to get open. A centre standing still in the slot is easy to cover.

When your defenceman is about to shoot from the blue line, stop and stand in front of the goalie's eyes. Keep still as the shot goes. Here is why. A goalie can't stop what they can't see. If you shuffle about, they get glimpses of the puck.

Keep your stick blade on the ice, so you can tip the shot. And decide to go for the rebound before the shot, not after.

Stand just outside the crease, with your skates off its red line too. Never stand so close that the goalie can't move.

Now four things that keep you safe at the net.

First, know where the goal post is before you stand there.

Second, keep your head up and your chin off your chest, and never duck. If you meet the post, meet it with an arm or a leg, never your head first. Even going slowly, a post met head first can hurt your neck.

Third, a player may push you sideways while you stand there. Keep your spot, but don't push back or lean on them, and never put your stick across someone. And if you are pushed toward the goalie, let them have the spot, and move away from the goalie.

And fourth, never go into the goalie. Go for the rebound under control, and go around the goalie, never into them. If you get pushed into the blue paint, get out straight away, around the goalie, not through.

Sixth idea. On the forecheck, be first in, and steer.

In their end, you will often be the first forward to reach the puck. Skate at the player with the puck on an angle, so they have to skate toward one side of the rink. Steer them. Don't push them.

Here is why it works. Once they are heading for one side, your winger knows where the puck is going. Your winger can get there and close it off.

Arrive under control, not off a run. Here is why. A run at a player can be called charging, which is a penalty.

If a winger gets to the puck before you, they go first, and you support them. Right away, without swapping back.

And never go into their goalie, even when the goalie comes out to play the puck.

Sending two forwards in is one way to forecheck. Some teams send only one. So ask your coach how your team does it.

Seventh idea. Take the faceoffs that matter seriously.

Most faceoffs in a game don't decide much. A few matter a lot. The ones in your own end. The ones when a team has a player in the penalty box. And the ones in the last minute.

Here is why. Lose a draw cleanly in your own end, and the other team has the puck in a set play. Your team is standing still and facing the wrong way.

Before those draws, agree with your wingers where you are trying to win the puck. And tell them to stay back until the puck drops. If a winger creeps in early, you are the one thrown off the draw.

Get low with your knees and hips, not by pushing your head out over the dot. Here is why. If both centres lean their heads in, their helmets meet. That can get you thrown off the draw too.

If you lose the draw in your own end, stay with their centre. Go with them toward the slot, rather than chasing the puck. That is the usual job, but some teams give the centre a different player, so ask.

Let me put it all together.

In your own end, swing low, so your defenceman has a safe pass.

Never leave the slot empty. Help in the corner, and call your winger into the slot behind you.

Under pressure, put the puck to the outside. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the other player, never duck.

Own the middle. Carry through it with your head up, and on the way back, take the late player on their inside.

In their end, shoot from the slot, screen the goalie, and go for the rebound. Head up at the net, and never into the goalie.

On the forecheck, arrive under control and steer.

And take the draws that matter seriously.

Three questions to ask your coach. In our own end, do you want me low, or leaving early up the middle? When I go into the corner, who fills the slot? And on the way back, who takes the middle?

Ask them. Their answers tell you how your team wants you to play.

Once these seven ideas feel natural, you are ready for more. The longer Playing principles episode on the centre goes deeper into each idea, and why it works. The conversation episode talks it all through. Start there next.
