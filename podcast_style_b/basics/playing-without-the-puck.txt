===== SCRIPT START =====

This is The Basics, topic seven. Playing without the puck.

The Basics is a series for newer players and younger players. Each episode covers the core things to get right first, and why each one works. Once these feel natural, the longer Playing principles episodes and the conversation episodes go deeper.

Here is the place to start. You spend almost all of your time on the ice without the puck. So what you do without it is most of your game.

Before the ideas, a few words this episode uses. I will explain each one now, so nothing gets in the way later.

The carrier. This is the player on your team who has the puck.

Unmarked and available. Unmarked means nobody from the other team is near you. Available means the carrier can actually get the puck to you. They sound the same, but they are not.

The passing lane. This is the straight line the puck would travel, from the carrier's stick to yours.

The boards. This is the wall around the rink.

A loose puck. This is a puck that nobody has.

Above the puck. This means between the puck and your own net. A player above the puck is the one who stays back.

Driving the net. This means skating hard to the front of the other team's net, without the puck.

The crease. This is the goalie's area, the blue paint in front of the net. Treat the line around its edge as part of it.

A rebound. This is when the puck bounces back out off the goalie.

That is all the words. Now seven ideas, one at a time, and the reason each one works.

First idea. Know what is happening right now, and switch jobs the moment it changes.

Without the puck, you are always in one of four situations. Your team has the puck. The other team has it. Nobody has it. Or it has just changed hands.

Each one asks for a different job. When your team has it, you get open. When the other team has it, you defend. Defending has its own episode, topic eight. On a loose puck, somebody goes to get it. And when it has just changed hands, you act before the other team does.

Here is why it works. The situation changes without warning, many times in a shift. Most mistakes without the puck are not lazy. They are the right job for the situation a moment ago. So keep your head up, notice the change, and switch straight away.

When your team has just won the puck, turn and go at once. Go when it looks like your team will get it. Don't wait until you are sure.

Here is why. For two or three seconds after the puck changes hands, the other team is not organised. Players face the wrong way. A chance made then is made against nobody in position. If you go a little early and you were wrong, you can recover. If you go late, the chance is gone.

Second idea. Be available, not just unmarked.

Being alone is not enough. To be available, you need three things.

First, your stick on the ice, with the blade flat. That blade is the target, so give the passer something to hit.

Second, a straight line from the puck to your blade. No player standing in the way.

Third, no stick from the other team lying in that line.

Here is why it works. A stick held up in the air can't take a pass. A pass can't bend around a body. And a defender's stick lying flat across the lane stops the pass, even when you are far from everyone.

So when you get open, look at the passing lane, not at the defender. If the lane is blocked, move. Often three or four feet is enough to open it. A small move that opens the lane beats a big burst that doesn't.

And say something. A short call, like "here" or "back", tells the carrier where you are.

Here is why. The carrier's head is often down, looking at the puck. Your voice gives them what their eyes can't.

Third idea. Give the carrier two places to pass, at different angles. A triangle, not a line.

Picture three teammates in a line up the boards. One defender standing in that line blocks both passes with one stick. Now one of those teammates moves toward the middle. That makes a triangle.

Here is why it works. No single defender can block both lanes of a triangle. Covering one means turning away from the other. And after the pass, you still have a triangle. A line has to be rebuilt after every pass.

Now, how far away should you be? A good start is two or three stick lengths from the carrier. Closer when they are under pressure, further in open ice. That is a rule of thumb, so let your coach adjust it.

The real test is time. Ask yourself this. If the carrier passed right now, would the puck reach me before the defender does?

Here is why the distance matters. Too close, and one defender covers both of you. Too far, and the pass takes too long, and a stick gets into the lane.

Also think about the angle you give. Behind the carrier is the safe pass back. Level with them moves the puck across. Ahead of them gains ground. Look where your teammates are, and be the one that is missing.

One warning in your own end. Never take the pass across the front of your own net. Go behind the net instead. A pass picked off in front of your own net is a chance against you, right away.

Let me stop and repeat the first three ideas, because everything else is built on them. Know which of the four situations you are in, and switch the moment it changes. Be available, not just unmarked, with your blade flat on the ice and a clear lane. And give the carrier two options at different angles, a triangle, not a line.

Fourth idea. Skate to the space, not to the puck. And stay wide.

The puck pulls players toward it. On a new team, you often see lots of players in one small circle around it. Coaches call that the puck magnet.

So every time the puck moves, ask one question. Who is closest? If it is not you, and your coach has not sent you in, your job is the space.

Here is why it works. When you skate to the puck, your defender comes with you. Now one defender covers two of you. And pucks squirt out of piles into empty ice. If everyone is in the pile, that empty ice belongs to the other team.

The same goes when a teammate has the puck and is stuck on the boards. Don't skate into that battle. Move to open ice a few metres away, with your stick down.

Here is why. Your teammate needs to get the puck out. If you skate in, you bring another defender and block the way out. A few metres away, you are the way out.

There is an exception. If nobody has the puck at all, or your coach makes you the second player in, then go in. Go in for the puck. Head up, skates parallel to the boards, and never into anyone's back.

And if you are a winger, stay wide near the boards, even when it feels like you are out of the play.

Here is why. Two defenders can't cover the middle and the boards at the same time. If you drift in, your defender drifts in with you, and the middle closes. Staying wide keeps the middle open for a teammate.

Fifth idea. Time it. Arrive late, with speed.

A player who gets to an open spot early and stops is easy to cover. The defence just skates over to them.

So watch the passer, not the space. Go when the carrier's head comes up, or when their shoulders turn toward you. Until then, keep moving a little, with a curl or a drift. Then burst.

Here is why it works. Getting open too early is the same as not being open. The defender gets that second to recover. A player arriving at speed is much harder to cover than one standing still.

Change your speed, too. Slow down for a moment, then explode. A hard stop works as well.

Here is why. A defender can match your speed. It is much harder to match a change of speed, because reacting costs them a beat.

One exception. In your own end, don't wait. The carrier needs a pass right now, so get there.

And after you pass, move again straight away. Here is why. The teammate you just passed to now needs two options, and you are the nearest one.

Sixth idea. Drive the net, and finish every route you start.

When you go to the net without the puck, go hard, as if the puck is coming to you. A slow glide moves nobody.

Here is why it works. A player driving to the front of the net pulls a defender with them. No defender can leave you alone there. Your drive also makes a rebound or a tip possible. And it opens the ice behind you, where a teammate can shoot. Often, the point of your drive is that somebody else scores.

Finish your routes, too, even after the pass goes somewhere else. Here is why. If you give up on routes, the defenders learn to ignore you.

Now the safety part of driving the net. You are skating hard at a steel goal frame, with a goalie and a defender there too.

Know where the posts are. Never arrive head first. If you hit a post or the boards, take it with an arm or a leg, never your head.

Keep your head up and your chin off your chest, and never duck. You want to see what is coming. A head-down crash into a post or the boards can hurt your neck, even at walking speed.

And drive at the net, not at the goalie. Stop just outside the blue paint, with both skates off its line, and your stick on the ice. Never touch the goalie. Staying clear of the goalie is your job, even if you get pushed. If the goalie leans into you, move out of the way, then get back in front.

Seventh idea. On a loose puck, one player goes, one supports, and nobody else joins. And call it.

Who goes depends on how your team plays, so ask your coach. But one thing holds for every team. One player goes hard. One player supports. Nobody else joins.

Here is why it works. Two players racing to the same puck from the same side are both busy. Neither one can take a pass. So the third player is better off somewhere the puck can go next.

If it is not clear who is going, call it. "I got it." "Yours." "Help." A word settles it.

And somebody stays above the puck. Before you go down low, know who is staying back.

Here is why. That player is the insurance if your attack fails. If everyone goes low and the puck is lost, the other team gets a rush the other way.

Now the most important safety part of this episode. A race to a loose puck on the boards is a race into the boards. The other player means the player from the other team who is there with you.

So here is how you arrive. Come in on an angle, not straight at the wall. Feet apart, knees bent. Skates parallel to the boards before you reach them, which means pointing along them. Forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder. Head up, chin off your chest. Never your back to the other player. Never duck.

Here is the one reason behind all of it. You want to see what is coming, and take it on your side, not on your head or neck. A head-down hit into the boards can hurt your neck even at walking speed.

If you are already facing the boards and can't turn in time, put your stick and gloves up on the glass to spread the hit. That is a last resort, not the aim.

Winning the puck is never worth arriving any other way.

And never push or hit a player on the boards from behind. If their back is to you, go for the puck at their feet. Even a light hit from behind can hurt someone's head or neck.

Let me put it all together.

Know which situation you are in, and switch jobs the moment it changes. When your team wins the puck, go.

Be available, not just unmarked. Blade flat on the ice, a clear lane, and a call.

Give the carrier two options at different angles. A triangle, not a line.

Skate to the space, not to the puck, and stay wide.

Arrive late, with speed, and move again after every pass. In your own end, get there now.

Drive the net hard and finish your routes. Head up, chin off your chest, never duck, and never into the goalie.

And on a loose puck, one goes, one supports, nobody else joins, and somebody stays back. At the boards, remember how to arrive. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the other player, never duck.

Three questions to ask your coach. On a loose puck, who goes and who supports? Who stays above the puck? And how close do you want me to the carrier?

Ask them. Their answers tell you how your team wants you to play.

Once these seven ideas feel natural, you are ready for more. The longer Playing principles episodes on playing without the puck, and on puck support and spacing, go deeper into each idea. The conversation episodes talk them through. Start there next.
