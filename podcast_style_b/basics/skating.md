===== SCRIPT START =====

This is The Basics, topic three. Skating.

The Basics is a series for newer players and younger players. Each episode covers the core things to get right first, and why each one works. Once these feel natural, the longer Playing principles episode and the conversation episode on skating go deeper.

Skating is the skill under every other skill. And it gets better with practice, at any age.

Before the ideas, a few words this episode uses. I will explain each one now, so nothing gets in the way later.

An edge. The bottom of a skate blade is not flat. It has a groove down the middle, so each blade has two thin edges. You skate by tilting the blade onto one of them.

The inside edge is on the big-toe side of your skate. The outside edge is on the little-toe side.

A crossover. This is when you skate round a curve and one foot steps over in front of the other. It is how you build speed on a turn.

A tight turn. This is a sharp turn in a small space. Both skates stay on the ice, with your feet side by side.

A hockey stop. This is the stop where you turn both skates sideways and let the blades scrape the ice.

A shift. This is your turn on the ice, before you come off and a teammate goes on.

The boards. This is the wall around the rink.

The goal line and the blue lines. The goal line is the line the net sits on. The two blue lines mark off each end of the rink.

A loose puck. This is a puck that nobody has yet.

The other player. In this episode, that means a player from the other team who is near you, or coming at you.

That is all the words. Now seven big ideas, one at a time, and the reason each one works.

First idea. Stay low.

Bend your knees until they are over your toes. Look down, and your knees should hide your skates. Keep your chest up. Bend at your knees and hips, not at your waist.

Stay low all the time. Stay low when you glide, when you turn and when you stop.

Here is why it works. Your push comes from straightening a bent leg. A straight leg has nothing left to push with. A low player is also harder to knock over. From a deep bend, you can push off in any direction straight away. And you can't tilt your blade far onto an edge unless you bend your knees and ankles.

So standing tall costs you four things at once. Power, balance, quick reactions and your edges.

Here is a simple check. If your thighs don't burn after a hard shift, you probably weren't low enough.

Second idea. Eyes up.

Keep your eyes up and looking around the ice. Not just your chin up. Your eyes. Keep them up as you start, as you turn, and when you have the puck.

Here is why it works. You can't read a play you aren't looking at. And your head pulls your body with it. When you look down, your weight tips forward. So a player skating head down is also skating with their weight in the wrong place.

Try this. Pick something at the far end of the ice. Skate to it without looking down.

Third idea. Go into the boards on an angle, with your head up.

This is the most important safety idea in this episode.

When you skate toward the boards, come in on an angle. Never go straight at them. Keep your head up and your chin off your chest. Never go in head first.

Here is why it works. On an angle, a leg, your side or an arm takes most of the bump. You also keep better control of the puck. And you choose your angle several strides before you get there. So this is the part you control completely.

Two more habits near the boards. Never turn your back to the other player. And never duck.

Here is the one reason behind both. You want to see what is coming, and take it on your side, not on your head or neck. Even going slowly, meeting the boards head first can hurt your neck. And even a light bump from behind can hurt your head or neck. So these habits matter at every speed.

If contact is coming at the boards, here is how you stand. Skates parallel to the boards, which means pointing along them. Your forearm and your hip are the parts nearest the boards. Never the point of your shoulder, and not the whole shoulder if you can help it. Head up, chin off your chest. Never your back to the other player. Never duck.

Let me stop and repeat the first three ideas, because everything else is built on them. Stay low, with your knees over your toes. Keep your eyes up, not just your chin. And go into the boards on an angle, head up, chin off your chest. Never your back to the other player, and never duck.

Fourth idea. Win the first three steps.

Coaches find that most races in a game are won or lost in about three strides. That is a race to a loose puck. Or a race to get past a defender. Or a race back when you have been turned the wrong way.

So drive your first three steps hard, every time. Use short, quick steps on the front of your blades. Lean your body forward, but keep your eyes up. Push along the ice, not up into the air. Don't hop.

As you get moving, get onto the whole blade and let your strides get longer.

To start sideways, or to turn back the other way, start with a crossover.

Here is why it works. Hockey isn't long, straight sprints. It is short bursts, a second or two at a time. So your top speed rarely decides a race. Your first three steps almost always do. And a hop wastes your push going up, when you want to go forward.

A race to a loose puck in the corner ends at the boards. So drive your three steps, then arrive with your knees bent, head up, on an angle to the boards, and able to stop. Here is why. A player who wins the race stretched out flat has no balance left to keep the puck.

Fifth idea. Change your speed on purpose.

Don't skate at one speed all shift. Glide to read the play. Then burst.

Here is why it works. A defender facing a player at one steady speed just matches it. When you glide, they have to choose. Do they close in, or ease off? Then you burst, and whatever they chose, you have made space. It is the change in speed that gets you free, not your top speed.

And if you skate flat out all the time, you have no faster gear to change to. You also get no moment to look up.

It works without the puck too. Drift, let the player watching you relax, and then break away.

One more part of this idea. When a pass reaches you, keep your feet moving. Push the puck a little ahead of you and skate onto it.

Here is why. Slowing down to handle the puck gives up your speed just when you need it most. Skate first, and handle the puck second.

Sixth idea. Train your outside edges, and choose a crossover or a tight turn.

Most players are much stronger on their inside edges. Many players leave their outside edges untrained. Test your own, and find out which you are.

Here is why outside edges matter. Your inside edges give you power, for pushing, starting and stopping. Your outside edges give you control. Turning, cutting back and slipping away from the other player all come from your outside edges.

Leaning out over your skate feels like falling until you trust it.

You will fall while you learn this. Everyone does. So first, here is how to fall safely.

As you feel yourself going, get low. Bend your knees and sink. Go down on your side, onto your hip and thigh, which are padded. Slide with it, and don't fight the fall.

Keep your hands in on the ice. Never put a hand out to catch yourself on the ice. Here is why. A stiff arm puts all your weight through one wrist.

Now your chin. This part changes with where you are falling, so listen to both halves.

Going down onto the ice, tuck your chin toward your chest. Do it most of all if you fall backwards. That stops your head snapping back onto the ice.

Sliding toward the boards, do the opposite. Head up, chin off your chest. Turn so your skates or your side meet the boards first. If you can't, get your arms out. Anything but your head first.

Here is why it changes. On the ice, a tucked chin keeps your head off the ice. Going into the boards, a tucked chin puts your neck first in line.

So, onto the ice, chin in. Into the boards, head up.

If you end up facing the boards with no time to turn, put your stick and gloves up on the glass, spread wide. Keep your head off it. That is only for when things have already gone wrong. It is not how you aim to meet the boards.

The idea behind all of it is the same. Spread the bump over as much of your body as you can.

When you have fallen, stay down for two or three seconds first. Check that you are all right. If you are hurt, stay down, and let play be stopped.

A fall can give you a concussion. A concussion is an injury to the brain, from a knock to the head or a jolt that snaps the head. You don't have to be knocked out to have one. If you might have a concussion, come off straight away, and tell someone. Don't go back on that day. And don't go back on the ice until a medical professional says you can, even if you feel fine. Don't drive, and don't go home on your own. If you feel worse, get help straight away.

If you are all right, get up onto your hands and knees. Put one skate flat on the ice beside your hands. Push up, bring the other skate beside it, and come up into your low stance.

Now the drill. Skate a big circle on one foot, on the outside edge of the foot nearer the middle. Start at walking pace, on a wide circle, with your helmet on. Lean more only as the edge holds. Feeling wobbly is fine, because that is where you get better. Feeling out of control means slow down.

Now, crossover or tight turn. Cross over when you have room and want speed. Turn tight when you have no room, like coming off the boards or getting away from the other player.

Here is why. A crossover builds speed through a wide curve, and it needs room. A tight turn changes direction in the smallest space. When there is no room, a crossover is the wrong tool.

You can't turn tight standing up. Sink lower, lean your whole body in, and keep your chest and eyes up.

Seventh idea. Stop on both sides, then go straight back the other way.

When the play turns the other way, stop, and go back. Don't loop round in a big curve.

Here is why it works. A loop feels faster, but it isn't. It carries you away from the play.

Learn to stop both ways. Lots of players can only stop well one way. Test your own weaker side.

Here is why it matters. A player who can only stop one way turns instead, and ends up further from the puck. They also arrive at the puck slowing down, not ready.

If you can't do a hockey stop yet, use a snowplough. Point your toes in a little and push both blades out. It is slow, but it always works.

When you can, use a hockey stop. Keep your knees bent, and sink lower as you stop. Your legs soak it up. Don't lean back away from the stop. That is what puts you on your backside.

A stop is only worth what comes after it. Finish your stop low and balanced, so your first step out comes straight away.

Here is a simple way to practise. When you skate, stop at every blue line and every goal line. Make half of those stops on your weaker side. Then take three quick steps back the other way.

Let me put it all together.

Stay low, with your knees over your toes.

Keep your eyes up, not just your chin.

Go into the boards on an angle. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the other player, never duck.

Win the first three steps, with short, quick steps, pushing along the ice.

Change your speed on purpose, and keep your feet moving when the puck arrives.

Train your outside edges. Cross over when you have room, and turn tight when you don't.

Stop on both sides, then go straight back the other way.

And when you fall, onto the ice, chin in. Into the boards, head up. If you might have a concussion, come off, tell someone, and stay off until a medical professional says you can.

Three questions to ask your coach. Which way do I stop worse? Which way do I turn worse? And am I low enough?

Ask them. Their answers tell you what to work on first.

Once these seven ideas feel natural, you are ready for more. The longer Playing principles episode on skating goes deeper into each idea, and why it works. The conversation episode talks it all through. Start there next.
