===== SCRIPT START =====

This is The Basics, topic thirteen. Talking, teamwork and bouncing back.

The Basics is a series for newer players and younger players. Each episode covers the core things to get right first, and why each one works. Once these feel natural, the longer Playing principles episodes and the conversation episodes on communication, team play and the mental game go deeper.

Before we start, one thing that matters more than hockey. If you ever have any thought of harming yourself or of suicide, get help now, tonight. Call your local emergency number. That is nine nine nine in the UK and Ireland, one one two across Europe, and nine one one in the US and Canada. The Mental Game page on the website lists crisis lines too.

Now, a few words this episode uses. I will explain each one now, so nothing gets in the way later.

A call. This is a short word you shout to a teammate during play, to tell them something they can't see.

The puck carrier. This is the player who has the puck.

The boards. This is the wall around the rink.

Your linemates. These are the teammates who go on the ice with you, shift after shift.

The bench. This is where your team sits when it is not on the ice.

The officials. These are the referees and linespeople who run the game.

A reset. This is a short routine you do after a mistake, so you can let it go and play the next shift.

That is all the words. Now seven big ideas, one at a time, and the reason each one works.

First idea. Say what you can see. Man on, and time.

Man on means a player from the other team is closing in, and the puck carrier can't see them. Time means nobody is near, so they can lift their head and make the good play.

Here is why it works. The puck carrier is the player on the ice who knows least. Their eyes are down or forward. The player coming from behind is in the one place they can't look. Everyone else can see it. A call turns a blind guess into an informed choice, and it costs you nothing.

If you hear man on, stop trying to make a play. Protect the puck and move it. If you hear time, slow down and make the good play.

Two rules for calling these. Shout man on early, even when you are not sure. But never say time unless you are sure. Here is why. A false time sends a teammate into contact they weren't braced for. After that, they stop trusting your calls.

And one safety point, whether anyone calls or not. Near the boards, never turn your back to the other player, and never duck. Head up, chin off your chest. Here is why. You want to see what is coming, and keep your head and neck out of it.

Second idea. Call early, short and loud, and answer every call.

Make your call while the puck is still travelling, not as it arrives. Keep it to one or two words. Say it loud. Use names. Sam, man on.

Here is why it works. A call made early lets your teammate plan. The same call as the puck lands just startles them. Rinks are loud, and helmets muffle sound, so a quiet call didn't happen. And a name tells the right player it is for them.

Answer calls when you hear them. Yep, got it, or a raised glove is enough. Here is why. A call with no answer leaves two players hoping, and the player who called stops calling.

In your own end, one more call. Back door means a player from the other team is alone at the far post. The nearest player takes them, right now. Here is why. The player who can see that open player is almost never the one who can cover them.

There is no official list of hockey calls. Words change from team to team. So agree your words as a team, and practise them. Ask your coach which calls your team uses.

Third idea. Be where your linemates expect you.

Decide what you do, then do it every time. If you go to the boards on the breakout, go there every time.

Here is why it works. A lot of passes in hockey go to a space, before the teammate gets there. That pass is a guess about where you will be. If you are always in the same place, a teammate can pass without looking up. If you are somewhere new each time, they have to look first, and that is when they get caught.

Learn your linemates too. Where do they like the puck? How fast do they really skate? Then give them the pass that works for them. They won't change their game for you, but you can change what you give them.

Use the bench to talk. Say one useful thing for the next shift. I'm coming low on the breakout. Never say something that only judges a teammate, like you need to be sharper. That isn't information.

Fourth idea. Fill a teammate's spot, and get back to yours.

When a teammate leaves their spot to go after the puck, fill the space they left. When somebody has filled your spot, get back to it as soon as the play lets you.

Here is why it works. That exchange is what trust looks like on the ice. A player only leaves their spot to help if they believe someone will cover it. If nobody ever comes back, players stop leaving their spot to help.

Which spot you fill depends on how your team plays. Ask your coach.

Let me stop and repeat the first four ideas, because they are the core of playing as a team. Say what you can see, man on and time, and never time unless you are sure. Call early, short and loud, with a name, and answer every call. Be where your linemates expect you. And when a teammate leaves their spot, fill it, then get back to yours.

Fifth idea. When a teammate is having a bad game, keep giving them the puck.

Pass to them, and give them an easy one first. Praise what they do well, like a good backcheck.

Here is why it works. Cutting a player out of the play tells them what they already fear. Confidence comes back through simple things they actually complete.

Never show frustration at a teammate on the ice. No head shake, no stick slap, no pointing. Here is why. The other team sees where your weakness is. Your teammate gets more worried and plays slower. And while you are complaining, you are not backchecking. If something needs saying, say it after the game, calmly, about one play, once.

When your goalie lets in a soft goal, say something short. Next one. Tap their pads. Then play a simple shift in front of them. Don't talk the goal over with them.

Sixth idea. After your own mistake, run your reset.

Everyone makes mistakes. The skill is what you do in the next few seconds. Your reset has four steps. First, a small physical trigger, like tapping your shin pads with your stick. Second, one long, slow breath out. Third, a short cue word, like next, or feet. Fourth, say your next job in one sentence, before your skates hit the ice.

Here is why it works. Play doesn't stop for your mistake. If you keep replaying it, you are not doing your new job. And the next job is the most important step, because a plan pushes out the replay.

Don't fight the thought. Notice it, let it be there, and turn to your next job anyway.

On the bench, do three things. Get your breath back. Watch the game. Plan one thing for your next shift. Save thinking about the mistake for after the game.

Seventh idea. Keep your cool, with nerves, with the officials and with your coach.

Before a game you might feel your heart going fast. Call it my body getting ready, not I'm nervous. It is the same feeling with a better name.

Here is why it works. Nerves are your body preparing to work hard. They are a good sign, and they usually fade in your first shift.

If a call goes against you, say nothing to the referee. Skate away and run your reset. Let your captain speak to the officials. Here is why. Arguing almost never changes a call, and it can get you a penalty of your own.

Never get back at a player who wronged you. And if players start pushing and shoving, stay out of it, and stay on the bench. Here is why. Getting back at someone is how trouble grows, and it helps nobody.

When a coach corrects you, listen for the message, even if the way they said it was harsh. The message may still be right.

But there is a line. Criticism of your play is coaching. Being put down as a person, or humiliated in front of the group again and again, is not. Don't keep that to yourself. Neither is anything that leaves you dreading the rink. Tell the person at your club in charge of keeping players safe, another club official, or the league.

One safety point before we finish, for when a player is hurt. If someone is down and not getting up, stop playing and wave for the officials. If it might be their head or neck, treat it as a possible spine injury. Make sure somebody actually phones for medical help, straight away. Don't help them up, and don't move them, except to keep their airway clear so they can breathe. Leave their helmet on, and keep them still. Waving at the bench is not the same as calling for help.

And if worries about hockey start to follow you home, or into school or sleep, talk to a qualified professional. That is not weakness. It is the right tool for the job.

Let me put it all together.

Say what you can see. Man on early, and time only when you are sure. Near the boards, never your back to the other player, never duck, head up, chin off your chest.

Call early, short and loud, use names, and answer every call.

Be where your linemates expect you, and tell them on the bench what you will do.

Fill a teammate's spot, and get back to yours.

Keep giving a struggling teammate the puck, and never show frustration at them.

After a mistake, run your reset, and name your next job.

And keep your cool. Nerves are your body getting ready. Say nothing to the referee. Never get back at anyone. And criticism of your play is coaching, but being put down as a person is not.

Three questions to ask your coach. Which calls does our team use? Which spot do you want me to fill when a teammate leaves theirs? And what is one thing I can do better next game?

Ask them. Their answers tell you how your team wants you to play.

Once these seven ideas feel natural, you are ready for more. The longer Playing principles episodes on communication, team play and the mental game go deeper into each idea, and why it works. The conversation episodes talk it all through. Start there next.
