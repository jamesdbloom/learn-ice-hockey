===== SCRIPT START =====

This is The Basics, topic eleven. Playing defence.

The Basics is a series for newer players and younger players. Each episode covers the core things to get right first, and why each one works. Once these feel natural, the longer Playing principles episode and the conversation episode on defence go deeper.

Before the ideas, a few words this episode uses. I will explain each one now, so nothing gets in the way later.

A defenceman. In a full game, your team has three forwards and two defencemen on the ice. The defencemen are the two who mostly stay back. You and the other defenceman are a pair, and you are the last players before your goalie.

Goal side. This means being between the player you are covering and your own net.

High and low. In whichever end you are in, high means toward the blue line. Low means toward the goal line, the line the net sits on.

The boards. This is the wall around the rink. The glass is the clear panels along the top of it.

The slot. This is the space in front of the net, between the two big faceoff circles. It is where most goals come from.

The crease. This is the goalie's area, the blue paint in front of the net. The mouth of your goal is the ice just in front of it.

Strong side and weak side. The strong side is the side of the ice the puck is on. The weak side is the other side.

The point. In the other team's end, this is the area just inside their blue line, where you usually stand.

The forecheck. This is the other team chasing the puck in your end, to win it back.

The gap. This is the space between you and a player skating at you with the puck.

That is all the words. Now seven big ideas, one at a time, and the reason each one works.

First idea. Stay goal side.

When the other team has the puck, stay between the puck and your net. Stay between the player you are covering and your net. Keep checking where they are, not just where the puck is.

Here is why it works. A forward who gets behind you has beaten you without touching the puck. Now they are free, closer to your net than you are.

There are times you leave that spot on purpose. You might step up to take the puck, or join an attack. That is fine, but only once a teammate is covering behind you. Know who it is before you go.

Second idea. Work as a pair. One of you on the puck, one at the mouth of your goal.

When the puck is in your end, the defenceman on the strong side goes to the puck. The one on the weak side guards the mouth of your goal. Every time the puck crosses the middle, you swap jobs. Say it out loud, every time.

Here is why it works. The pair goes wrong most often when both of you go to the puck. Then nobody is in front of your net. Nobody is there for the player blocking your goalie's view, or the tip, or the easy tap-in. Swapping out loud is what keeps one of you there.

When you are the one on the puck, play the puck, not the player. Pin the puck against the boards with your stick or your skate, for a second or two, until help comes. Then move it.

This is one way to play. In another, called man-on-man, each player follows one opponent, and the jobs split differently. So ask your coach which your team plays.

Third idea. At the front of your net, get inside first, then lift their stick.

Forwards from the other team will stand in front of your net. Get to that spot before they do. Stand beside them, on the inside, between them and your goalie.

As the shot or the pass comes, lift their stick. Lift it up, not sideways, low on the shaft, well below their bottom hand. Then let it go.

Here is why it works. Once a forward already holds that spot, you can't shove them off it. So the race to get there first is the real battle. And you don't need to be bigger than them. Lifting their stick at the right moment takes away the tip, without a shoving match.

Now the limits, and each one has a reason.

Hold your spot by being there first. Don't push them, and don't lean on them. A shove there can be called a penalty anywhere. And never put your stick across them.

Lift the stick, don't hold it. Never lift at their hands, and never chop at the stick. Both can be called penalties.

If you and a forward get tangled in a scramble for a loose puck, go toward the corner. Never across the front of your goal. Here is why. That path has a goal post at one edge and your goalie at the other. A player going sideways or backwards along it sees neither. Even going slowly, a crash there can hurt. And never lift anyone off their feet. A player lifted off their feet can't get ready for what they hit.

Let me stop and repeat the first three ideas, because they are the heart of playing defence. Stay goal side. Work as a pair, one on the puck and one at the mouth of your goal, and swap out loud. And at the net, get inside first, lift the stick low, let it go, never shove, and never your stick across anyone.

Fourth idea. Never carry or pass the puck across the front of your own goal.

When you have the puck in your own end and want to get it to your partner, go behind the net. Pass it behind the net, or skate it there. Never through the slot.

Here is why it works. A puck lost behind your net is just a battle for the puck. A puck lost in the slot is a shot at your net, from close in.

Some teams pass across higher up instead, above the tops of the faceoff circles. That is a different route, not a riskier one. So ask your coach which your team uses. But the slot is never the route.

Fifth idea. When you get the puck back, move it early, and look up the ice first.

You will often go back into the corner, or behind your net, to get the puck. On the way, look over your shoulder. See which side the forecheck is coming from.

Here is why it works. Then the decision is already made when the puck reaches you. You aren't making it with a player already on top of you.

When you get the puck, look up the ice before you look down at it. Here is why. In the two or three seconds after your team wins the puck, the other team is at its least organised. A quick pass to an open teammate up the ice beats them before they are set. Take the first pass that is really open.

If a player is closing in, you have two choices. Send it hard along the boards, onward to a teammate further round. That is a rim. It beats a player who is pinning you against the boards, because it goes past them. Or send it back the way it came, to your partner behind you. That is a reverse. It beats a player who has skated past you, because it goes into the space they just left.

If nothing is on, send it off the glass and along it. Never over it. A puck over the glass can cost your team a penalty.

Now the most important safety part of this episode. Going back for pucks with a player behind you happens to you more than to anyone. So two rules on the boards.

Never turn your back to the other player near the boards, whether they are trying to take the puck or just coming at you. And never duck.

Here is the one reason behind both. You want to see what is coming, and take it on your side, not on your head or neck. That is true even when the play is slow.

So here is how you stand by the boards. Skates parallel to the boards, which means pointing along them. Your forearm and your hip are the parts nearest the boards, never the point of your shoulder. Head up, chin off your chest. Never your back to the other player. Never duck.

Get your feet turned that way before you reach the puck. And if you are already facing the boards and can't turn in time, get your stick and gloves up and spread on the glass. That softens it.

Sixth idea. When a player skates at you with the puck, win the gap with your feet.

Controlling the gap is the skill that defines a defenceman. Too big a gap, and the player comes at you with speed and lots of choices. Too small, and one move takes them past you.

Here is how. Start skating backwards early, before they reach you. Here is why. You can't close a gap from standing still. If you wait, they build up speed and go past you.

Watch their chest and hips, not the puck, their hands or their head. Here is why. A good player fakes with the puck, the stick and the head. But their chest and hips have to point where they are really going.

Close off the middle of the ice, so the only way left is toward the boards. Keep your stick on the ice, out in front, where a pass would go. And keep skating backwards for as long as you can. Here is why. Once you turn to skate forwards, you can't react to what they do.

Here are the limits. You steer them toward the boards with the way you skate. Don't ride them into the boards. Don't bump or push them. Play the puck. As they reach the boards they often turn away from you. If you can see the numbers on the back of their jersey, pull up and play the puck. Never into anyone's back.

Sometimes you step up to meet them, to take the puck early. That is closing the space, not a hit. Keep your feet moving, and play the puck. And if their head is down, that only tells you they can't change direction. It is never a reason to come in harder.

Now the hardest one. Two players come at you, and you are the only one back. That is called a two on one.

Take away the pass, and leave the shot to your goalie. Stay on your feet, between the two players, with your stick flat on the ice on the side of the pass.

Here is why it works. Your goalie can see the shot, and is ready for it. A pass across to an open player is a chance your goalie can't stop.

But if the second player has been caught by a teammate, and nobody is left to pass to, it isn't a two on one any more. Close in on the puck.

Teams play this differently, so ask your coach.

Seventh idea. In their end, keep the puck in, and don't get caught.

When your team has the puck in the other team's end, you and your partner stand at the point, just inside their blue line.

Your first job is to keep the puck in their end. If they try to clear it out along the boards, step in and stop it. Keep your own skates inside the blue line too. If you drift out, you are no longer someone your forwards can pass to.

Here is why it works. If you lose the blue line, or get caught deep in their end, the other team gets out cleanly. Then they can come at your net with more players than you have back.

So only one of you goes down low at a time. The other stays at the blue line. Before you go below the puck, know who is above it. If nobody is, stay at the line.

When you shoot from the point, find a clear path to the net first. Then keep the shot low, at knee height or below, with some pace. Here is why. A shot from out there rarely beats a goalie on its own. A low shot lets a teammate in front tip it, or score the rebound. If there is no clear path, move the puck until there is one.

Let me put it all together.

Stay goal side, between the puck and your net.

Work as a pair. One on the puck, one at the mouth of your goal, and swap out loud.

At the net, get inside first and lift the stick low. Never shove, never your stick across anyone, and never go across the front of your goal in a scramble.

Never carry or pass the puck across the front of your own goal. Go behind the net.

When you get the puck back, look up the ice first and move it early. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the other player, never duck.

Win the gap with your feet. Steer with your skating, play the puck, and on a two on one, take away the pass.

And in their end, keep the puck in, get your shot through low, and know who is above the puck.

Three questions to ask your coach. In our own end, do we play one on the puck and one in front, or man-on-man? Do we pass behind the net, or up higher? And on a two on one, how do you want me to play it?

Ask them. Their answers tell you how your team wants you to play.

Once these seven ideas feel natural, you are ready for more. The longer Playing principles episode on defence goes deeper into each idea, and why it works. The conversation episode talks it all through. Start there next.
