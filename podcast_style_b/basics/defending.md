===== SCRIPT START =====

This is The Basics, topic eight. Defending.

The Basics is a series for newer players and younger players. Each episode covers the core things to get right first, and why each one works. Once these feel natural, the longer Playing principles episodes and the conversation episodes go deeper.

Defending comes down to one thing. Stay between the puck and your own net.

Before the ideas, a few words this episode uses. I will explain each one now, so nothing gets in the way later.

The attacker. This is a player on the other team, coming toward your net. The one you are covering is your attacker.

The carrier. This is the attacker who has the puck.

Goal side. This means you are on the line between your attacker and your own net. You are closer to your net than they are.

The passing lane. This is the straight line a pass would travel, from the carrier to a teammate.

The gap. This is the space between you and the carrier.

The boards. This is the wall around the rink.

Backchecking. This means skating hard back toward your own end when your team loses the puck.

The middle lane. Picture the rink split into three long strips. The two outside strips run along the boards. The middle strip runs down the centre, between the faceoff dots. That middle strip is the middle lane.

That is all the words. Now six ideas, one at a time, and the reason each one works.

First idea. Stay goal side, all the time.

Keep your body between your attacker and your net. Every attacker, every time.

Here is why it works. Defending, everything turns on the front of your own net. A mistake there is a shot against you. If an attacker gets between you and your net, something has already gone wrong. Once they are past you, your goalie is doing your job. Almost every time a rush goes wrong, somebody lost goal side of somebody.

So don't chase. Unless your team has sent you to go after the puck, hold goal side, with your stick in the passing lane.

Here is why. If two of you go after one carrier, somebody on the other team is left free.

Second idea. Watch your attacker, not the puck.

When you are covering a player without the puck, keep them in view. Take short looks at the puck, then look back.

Here is why it works. If you watch only the puck, the player you should be covering walks in behind you.

When you are facing the carrier, watch the middle of their body. Their chest and hips. A good way is to look at the numbers on the front of their jersey. You will still see the puck out of the corner of your eye.

Here is why. A good carrier fakes with everything they can move. The puck, the stick, their head and their eyes can all fool you. Their chest and hips can't. Their body has to go where their chest and hips point.

And your feet follow your eyes. If you watch the puck and the puck fakes, your feet go the wrong way.

Third idea. Stick on the ice, in the passing lane.

Keep your stick blade flat on the ice, out in front of you. Put it in the line between the carrier and the teammate they most want to pass to.

Here is why it works. Your stick takes away a pass without you having to move your body. A stick up in the air defends nothing.

If you are covering a player without the puck, your stick goes in the lane, never across them. Laying it across a player to hold them up is a foul.

Only poke at the puck when you can really reach it. And don't show it before you do it.

Here is why. A poke that misses leaves your stick out of the way, and the carrier skates around it. And if they see it coming, they pull the puck back and go around you.

Sometimes an attacker gets away from everyone and skates in alone on your goalie. If you are chasing them from behind, your stick goes on the puck, and nothing else. Never hook them, trip them, or hold them. Stay on your feet.

Here is why. A chance like that is your goalie's save, and goalies usually make it. A poke at the puck is the one useful thing you can do from behind.

Let me stop and repeat the first three ideas, because everything else is built on them. Stay goal side, between your attacker and your net, and don't chase. Watch your attacker, and the carrier's chest, not the puck. And keep your stick flat on the ice, in the passing lane, and on the puck.

Fourth idea. Start skating backwards early, and keep the gap getting smaller.

When the other team is getting the puck ready in their end, start skating backwards then. Don't stand still and wait for them to come.

Here is why it works. If you are standing still when they arrive, they are at full speed and you are at zero. While you speed up, they gain on you. The gap opens at the moment you most need it closed. If you are already moving, you can keep the gap as tight as you like.

As they come toward you, keep the gap shrinking, all the way in. A rough guide is two or three stick lengths in open ice, and about one stick length by your own blue line. That is a guide, not a rule, so ask your coach how tight they want it.

Here is why. A big gap gives the carrier three things. Space to speed up. Time to look up and find a pass. And a clear lane to shoot. A small gap takes away all three at once.

When you are close, don't lunge at the puck. Keep your feet, keep your stick in the lane, and let them run out of room.

Here is why. A lunge is a guess about their next move. If you guess wrong, you are out of the play and they have the whole net.

Fifth idea. Take the middle, and give them the boards.

Don't line up chest to chest with the carrier. Stay a little to the inside of them, toward the middle of the ice. Then the only way they can see to go is toward the boards.

Here is why it works. The middle of the ice is the dangerous part. Shots from the middle beat goalies. Shots from out by the boards mostly don't. And a carrier pushed out wide has less ice, a worse angle to the net, and nowhere to cut back.

Do this with how you skate, not with your body. Steer them with your route. Play the puck, not the player. Never push them into the boards.

And if they turn so their back is to you, don't finish into them. If you can see the numbers on their back, go for the puck instead. Even a light hit from behind can hurt someone's head or neck.

Sixth idea. When your team loses the puck, backcheck. Get to the inside of your attacker, and make sure somebody has the middle.

When the puck is lost, turn and sprint first. Look after three or four strides.

Here is why it works. If you turn your head to watch first, you lose two strides you never get back. The play will still be there when you look up, and you will be closer to it.

Skate a route to the inside of your attacker. Get level with their inside hip, not behind them. Get to the ice ahead of them, and hold your lane. Don't veer into them.

Here is why. From the inside, you are goal side again, and your stick reaches the passing lane. Chasing from behind someone's shoulder is just following. You can't defend anything from behind.

Never let your route end in someone's back. Take the ice, or take the puck.

Somebody has to own the middle lane. Often the centre takes the middle and the wingers take the outside lanes. Other teams give the middle to whoever gets back first. So ask your coach who owns the middle on your team. What never changes is that somebody owns it.

Here is why the middle matters so much. The attacker most often missed is the one skating hard down the middle without the puck. Nobody looks at them, because eyes go to the puck. Then they arrive in front of your net with nobody near them.

One more thing on the way back. If your defenceman is already on the carrier and keeping a good gap, don't rush in to help. Skate past to the attacker nobody has.

Here is why. Two of you on one carrier leaves your own attacker free. Some teams do send the first forward back at the carrier, so ask your coach which way your team plays.

And talk on the way back. When you get there, say "I'm back".

Here is why. A defenceman facing a rush may be covering two attackers at once. Your call tells them they can stop. Call it early. A call as it happens helps. A call after it happens is too late to help.

Last, when the rush is stopped, don't relax yet. Find the front of your own net first. Then pick up the player you are covering.

Here is why. During a rush, nobody is given the front of the net. So when the rush ends, it can be empty. And a mistake in front of your own net is a shot against you.

Let me put it all together.

Stay goal side, between your attacker and your net. Don't chase unless your team sends you.

Watch your attacker, not the puck. On the carrier, watch their chest and hips.

Keep your stick flat on the ice, in the passing lane. Never across a player. From behind, your stick goes on the puck and nothing else.

Start skating backwards early, and keep the gap getting smaller. Don't lunge.

Take the middle and give them the boards, with your route, not your body. Play the puck, not the player, and never into anyone's back.

And when the puck is lost, sprint back, get to the inside of your attacker, and make sure somebody has the middle. Say you are back. And when the rush ends, find the front of your net first.

Three questions to ask your coach. How tight do you want my gap? Who owns the middle on the way back? And does our first forward back go at the carrier, or pick up a player?

Ask them. Their answers tell you how your team wants you to defend.

Once these six ideas feel natural, you are ready for more. The longer Playing principles episodes on core principles and on defending the rush go deeper into each idea. The conversation episodes talk them through. Start there next.

- **The sixth idea is long.** It carries the sprint, the inside route, the middle, not doubling up, the "I'm back" call and the net front. The call and the net front are the cheapest cuts, about 700 characters together, but they also keep the script over 9,500.
