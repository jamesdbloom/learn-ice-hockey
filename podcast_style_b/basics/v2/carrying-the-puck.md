===== SCRIPT START =====

COACH: You pick up a loose puck near the boards. The boards are the wall around the rink. Someone from the other team is already skating at you, and you've got a moment before they arrive.

PLAYER: That's usually when I lose it.

COACH: Then this episode is for you. This is The Basics, topic four. Carrying the puck. The Basics is for newer players and younger players. It covers the core things to get right first. I'm the coach, and with me is a newer player, asking the questions you might ask. Good puck handling buys you a little extra time. Every other choice you make with the puck needs that time. There are seven ideas, and we'll take them one at a time.

COACH: First idea. Play with your head up. Picture yourself skating up the ice with the puck, eyes fixed on it. A teammate is wide open across the ice. Someone from the other team is coming at you from the side.

PLAYER: And I can't see either of them.

COACH: Right. A player staring at the puck can't see the open teammate. They can't see the open ice. And they can't see the other player coming at them, so they can't get ready for contact. In this episode, the other player means a player from the other team who is near you, or coming at you to take the puck.

PLAYER: So where should my eyes be?

COACH: Up, looking at the ice in front of you. Not your chin pointing at the ceiling. Just your eyes up and forward. Then the puck sits at the bottom edge of what you can see.

PLAYER: I've tried telling myself to look up. I lose the puck straight away.

COACH: That's the hard part. Telling yourself to look up doesn't work. You look up, lose the puck, and look straight back down. So build feel first. Stickhandling means moving the puck back and forth on your stick, using your hands. Do it slowly, and notice how the puck feels on your blade. The blade is the flat part at the bottom of your stick, the part that touches the puck. It has a toe at the end, a middle, and a heel nearest the shaft. Feel the puck on the toe, the middle and the heel.

PLAYER: When am I allowed to look down?

COACH: Peek down only when you have really lost the puck. Then make the peeks shorter. Once you can feel where the puck is, your eyes come up on their own.

PLAYER: Does it matter how I hold the stick?

COACH: Two hand habits help a lot. First, hold the stick loosely. Firm enough that it can't escape, soft enough that you don't crush it.

PLAYER: Wouldn't a tight grip give me more control?

COACH: The opposite. A hand that squeezes can't feel the puck. The second habit is about your top hand. That's the hand at the top of your stick. Your bottom hand holds the stick lower down. Keep your top hand out in front of your chest, not stuck to your hip.

PLAYER: What does that change?

COACH: From there, your blade can reach all the way round your body.

COACH: Second idea. Keep your body between the other player and the puck. The other player is closing in on your right, and the puck is out on your right, nearest them. Whose stick is closer to it?

PLAYER: Theirs.

COACH: Then you've already lost it. So move the puck to your far side. The far side is the side of your body away from the other player. If they are on your right, the puck goes on your left. Your body becomes a wall between them and the puck.

PLAYER: And how should I stand?

COACH: Get wide and low. Feet wider than your shoulders. Knees bent and hips down. Chest up, so you can still see.

PLAYER: Does that really make a difference?

COACH: A big one. A tall, narrow player gets knocked off the puck. A low, wide player is very hard to move. Then turn side-on to the other player. Not chest on, and never your back. And keep your feet moving, with small, quick steps.

PLAYER: Even while I'm holding them off?

COACH: Especially then. A player who stands still gets pinned. A moving player can slip away.

PLAYER: Should I push back into them?

COACH: No. Keep your spot, and let them try to move you. Don't push them, don't lean on them, and don't back into them. Keep both hands on your own stick, and never push your stick into them. Never grab the other player, their stick or their cage.

PLAYER: Not even one hand to hold them off?

COACH: Both hands on your stick. Two hands on your stick are ready to move the puck the moment the pressure lets go.

COACH: Third idea. Know your way out before you get to the puck. Say you've got the puck on the boards, body between it and the other player, doing everything right. How long can you keep that up?

PLAYER: As long as I stay strong?

COACH: Not really. Keeping the puck safe only buys you a second. It doesn't buy you a minute. While you hold it, another player from the other team is on the way.

PLAYER: So what do I do with that second?

COACH: Use it on a plan you've already made. Decide while you are still skating to the puck. Who can I pass to? If nobody is open, where does it go? You have three ways out along the boards. A rim is a hard pass along the boards, so the puck slides on round the curve of the boards. A reverse is when you send the puck back the other way, against the way the other team is coming. And a chip is a short tap of the puck past the other player, often off the boards.

PLAYER: What if I don't decide until I've got it?

COACH: A player who decides early gets to the puck with a plan, and moves it on fast. A player who doesn't decide early keeps the puck safe for three seconds, and then loses it anyway.

PLAYER: When I've got no plan, I just get rid of it. I whack it anywhere, or pass without looking.

COACH: That's a panic pass. Panic is normal when you're new, and it spends time you actually had. A pass you didn't look at, to nobody in particular, just gives the puck away.

PLAYER: So how do I stop doing it?

COACH: The same way as this whole idea. Look before you get to the puck. Decide early. And pick a second choice too, in case the first one disappears. Then, when the pressure arrives, you're mostly not choosing. You're doing what you already picked.

PLAYER: Isn't a rim just getting rid of it, though?

COACH: Not if you chose it. A rim you meant is a safe play. A puck flipped to nobody is a giveaway. If your first plan has gone, go to your second. Outside your own end, you can also keep the puck safe a moment longer. That means side-on to the player coming at you, puck on your far side, and never your back. But in your own end, don't hold it. One look, one decision, move it. Any of those is better than a blind pass.

COACH: When the second has done its job, get out. Spin away from the other player and take the puck with you. Only spin off someone who's already leaning on you, never off someone still coming at you, however slowly. Or turn tight across their path, so your body stays between them and the puck.

PLAYER: How long should I hold it on the boards before I go?

COACH: That's your coach's choice. Some teams want it moved out straight away. Others want you to hold it until help arrives. Ask your coach which.

COACH: Let's stop and check the first three, because they keep the puck yours. Can you give them back to me?

PLAYER: Head up, by building feel first. Body between the other player and the puck, with the puck on my far side, low and wide. And know where the puck is going before I get there.

COACH: Spot on.

COACH: Fourth idea. On the boards, take any contact along your side, with your head up. This is the most important safety idea in this episode. You're near the boards with the puck, and the other player comes right at you.

PLAYER: My instinct is to turn away and cover up.

COACH: Never turn your back to them. Not here, and not anywhere on the ice. And never duck, however slowly they are coming.

PLAYER: Even if they're barely moving?

COACH: Even then. There's one reason behind both. You want to see what is coming, and take it on your side, not on your head or neck. Even slow or light contact can hurt your head or neck.

PLAYER: So how do I stand by the boards?

COACH: Skates parallel to the boards, which means pointing along them. Your forearm and your hip are the parts nearest the boards. Never the point of your shoulder, and not the whole shoulder if you can help it. Head up, chin off your chest. Never your back to the other player. Never duck.

PLAYER: Can I just stop, facing the boards, with the puck at my feet?

COACH: Don't stop dead, facing straight into the boards. That is how you get pinned, and the next player from the other team takes the puck. Angle yourself so there is always a way out along the boards, and keep your feet moving so you can take it.

PLAYER: And if I'm already stuck facing them?

COACH: If you do end up facing the boards and can't turn, put your stick and gloves up on the glass, spread wide. The glass is the clear wall above the boards. Keep your head off it. That is only for when things have already gone wrong. It is not how you aim to stand.

COACH: Fifth idea. In your own end, don't stickhandle. Move the puck. Your own end is the end of the rink with your own net in it. Say you get the puck behind your own net, with one player from the other team coming. You're feeling good. What would you do?

PLAYER: Try a move on them? If I beat them, the whole ice is open.

COACH: Not there. In your own end, get the puck moving. Pass it, rim it or reverse it.

PLAYER: But what if the move works?

COACH: And what if it doesn't? If you lose the puck in your own end, the other team is right by your net. There is no time to get it back before they shoot. Nobody is ever told off for a plain, simple way out of their own end.

PLAYER: Rims and reverses happen on the boards, though. With someone arriving.

COACH: Often, yes. So the boards habits hold here too. Never your back to the other player, and never duck.

PLAYER: What about the middle of the ice?

COACH: The same. Don't stickhandle through the middle of your own end, or the middle of the rink. The middle is where most of the other team is. A puck lost there goes straight toward your net, and there are no boards to help you.

PLAYER: Even if the middle's wide open?

COACH: If the middle is wide open, you can skate the puck through it. Just don't try to beat players there. And one more. If losing the puck would leave the other team with more attackers than you have defenders, don't try a move. Not ever. Pass it or move it on.

COACH: Sixth idea. Against a defender, look for the pass first. If you take them on, make them turn. You're carrying the puck up the ice. One defender is in front of you, skating backwards, in a good spot. A teammate is open, but not in a great place. What's your play?

PLAYER: Beat the defender. My teammate isn't in a good spot anyway.

COACH: Look for the pass first. A pass moves the puck faster than any player can skate. A defender can't poke it off your stick. And it moves the defence more than a move does. Even a pass to a teammate in a worse spot usually beats a move against a defender who is in a good spot.

PLAYER: And if there's no pass at all?

COACH: Then take them on, but don't skate straight at their chest. Skate at one of their shoulders.

PLAYER: What's wrong with the chest?

COACH: A defender you attack head on stays square, and blocks both ways past them. Make them turn their hips, and a gap opens that wasn't there before.

PLAYER: Which shoulder do I go for?

COACH: That's a choice your coach makes. Ask them which they want from you. Change your speed, too. Slow down, then burst. A defender who has just slowed down can't speed up backwards quickly.

PLAYER: Should I try a fake?

COACH: One. A fake is when you pretend to go one way, so a defender leans that way, and then you go the other. Sell it with your head and your shoulders, not just your hands. Then burst away.

PLAYER: Why not just with my hands?

COACH: A defender reads your body, not your stick. If your body doesn't fake, they aren't fooled. And the burst is what turns the fake into space.

PLAYER: So more fakes means more chances to fool them?

COACH: The opposite. One good fake beats four fancy ones. Every extra move is another chance for a stick to reach the puck.

PLAYER: What if their stick is right in my way?

COACH: Push the puck past it, and skate round the other side to collect it. You and the puck don't have to go the same way.

COACH: Seventh idea. Don't slow down when the puck arrives. You're flying up the side of the ice and a pass lands on your stick. Lots of players slow right down at that moment to handle it.

PLAYER: I do that. I want to get control first.

COACH: Don't. Keep striding. Slowing down hands the defence two free seconds. Your speed is the most useful thing you have. Good hands should cost you none of it.

PLAYER: But how do I handle it at full speed?

COACH: Carry it. Don't stickhandle every stride. Push the puck a little ahead of you and a little to the side. Take two or three hard strides, catch up to it, and push it ahead again. Touch it just enough that a defender can't time a poke at it.

PLAYER: And that's easier than stickhandling?

COACH: It needs very little looking. So your eyes stay up, reading the play, while your legs keep their speed.

COACH: Let me put all seven together.

COACH: Play with your head up, by building feel for the puck first.

COACH: Keep your body between the other player and the puck. Puck on your far side, wide and low, side-on, never your back, feet moving. Keep your spot without pushing or leaning. Both hands on your own stick, and never grab.

COACH: Know your way out before you get to the puck. A pass, a rim, a reverse, or a chip, and never a blind pass to nobody.

COACH: On the boards, take it along your side. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the other player, never duck.

COACH: In your own end and through the middle, don't stickhandle. Move the puck.

COACH: Against a defender, look for the pass first. If you take them on, make them turn, with a change of speed and one good fake.

COACH: And when the puck arrives, keep striding.

PLAYER: What should I ask my own coach?

COACH: Three questions. How long do you want me to hold the puck on the boards? Which shoulder do you want me to attack? And when do you want me to carry the puck, and when should I pass? Their answers tell you how your team wants you to play.

COACH: Once these seven ideas feel natural, you are ready for more. The longer Playing principles episode on puck handling goes deeper into each idea. The conversation episode talks it all through. Start there next.
