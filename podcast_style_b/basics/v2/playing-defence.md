COACH: The puck is in your end. Two players from the other team are coming at you, and your goalie is right behind you. Nobody else is back. What you do next decides whether they score.

PLAYER: That's the bit of playing defence that scares me.

COACH: Then this episode is for you. This is The Basics, topic eleven. Playing defence. The Basics is for newer players and younger players. It covers the core things to get right first. I'm the coach, and with me is a newer player, asking the questions you might ask. There are seven big ideas, and we'll take them one at a time.

PLAYER: Before we start, what exactly is a defenceman?

COACH: In a full game, your team has three forwards and two defencemen on the ice. The defencemen are the two who mostly stay back. You and the other defenceman are a pair, and you are the last players before your goalie.

COACH: First idea. Stay goal side. The other team has the puck in your end, and the player you are meant to be covering is drifting toward your net.

PLAYER: Do I go for the puck?

COACH: Stay goal side first. That means being between the player you are covering and your own net. Stay between the puck and your net, and between your player and your net. Keep checking where they are, not just where the puck is.

PLAYER: What happens if I lose track of them?

COACH: A forward who gets behind you has beaten you without touching the puck. Now they are free, closer to your net than you are.

PLAYER: So I can never leave that spot?

COACH: There are times you leave it on purpose. You might step up to take the puck, or join an attack. That is fine, but only once a teammate is covering behind you. Know who it is before you go.

COACH: Second idea. Work as a pair. One of you on the puck, one at the mouth of your goal. Imagine the puck goes into the corner on your side. Both of you see it.

PLAYER: And we both go and get it.

COACH: That's the one to avoid. The side of the ice the puck is on is the strong side. The other side is the weak side. The defenceman on the strong side goes to the puck. The one on the weak side guards the mouth of your goal.

PLAYER: The mouth of the goal?

COACH: The crease is the goalie's area, the blue paint in front of the net, and the mouth of your goal is the ice just in front of it. Every time the puck crosses the middle, you swap jobs. Say it out loud, every time.

PLAYER: Why does it matter if we both go?

COACH: The pair goes wrong most often when both of you go to the puck. Then nobody is in front of your net. Nobody is there for the player blocking your goalie's view, or the tip, or the easy tap-in. Swapping out loud is what keeps one of you there.

PLAYER: And if I'm the one on the puck?

COACH: Play the puck, not the player. Pin the puck against the boards, the wall around the rink, with your stick or your skate, for a second or two, until help comes. Then move it.

PLAYER: Does every team do it this way?

COACH: No. This is one way to play. In another, called man-on-man, each player follows one opponent, and the jobs split differently. So ask your coach which your team plays.

COACH: Third idea. At the front of your net, get inside first, then lift their stick. A forward from the other team wants to stand in front of your net. The slot is the space in front of the net, between the two big faceoff circles. It is where most goals come from.

PLAYER: So I push them out of there?

COACH: No. Get to that spot before they do. Stand beside them, on the inside, between them and your goalie. Then, as the shot or the pass comes, lift their stick. Lift it up, not sideways, low on the shaft, well below their bottom hand. Then let it go.

PLAYER: Are there things I mustn't do there?

COACH: Yes, and each one has a reason. Hold your spot by being there first. Don't push them, and don't lean on them. A shove there can be called a penalty anywhere. And never put your stick across them. Lift the stick, don't hold it. Never lift at their hands, and never chop at the stick. Both can be called penalties.

PLAYER: But why can't I just move them? I'd rather be strong.

COACH: Once a forward already holds that spot, you can't shove them off it. So the race to get there first is the real battle. And you don't need to be bigger than them. Lifting their stick at the right moment takes away the tip, without a shoving match.

PLAYER: What if we get tangled up going for a loose puck?

COACH: If you and a forward get tangled in a scramble for a loose puck, go toward the corner. Never across the front of your goal.

PLAYER: Why does the direction matter?

COACH: That path has a goal post at one edge and your goalie at the other. A player going sideways or backwards along it sees neither. Even going slowly, a crash there can hurt. And never lift anyone off their feet. A player lifted off their feet can't get ready for what they hit.

COACH: Let's stop and check the first three, because they are the heart of playing defence. Can you give them back to me?

PLAYER: Stay goal side. Work as a pair, one on the puck and one at the mouth of my goal, and swap out loud. And at the net, get inside first, lift the stick low, let it go, never shove, and never my stick across anyone.

COACH: Spot on.

COACH: Fourth idea. Never carry or pass the puck across the front of your own goal. You have the puck in your own end, and you want to get it to your partner on the other side. There's a straight line to them, right through the slot. What would you do?

PLAYER: Pass it straight across? It's the quickest.

COACH: Don't make that pass. Go behind the net. Pass it behind the net, or skate it there. Never through the slot.

PLAYER: What's the difference, if it gets there?

COACH: A puck lost behind your net is just a battle for the puck. A puck lost in the slot is a shot at your net, from close in.

PLAYER: Is behind the net the only way?

COACH: Some teams pass across higher up instead, above the tops of the faceoff circles. That is a different route, not a riskier one. So ask your coach which your team uses. But the slot is never the route.

COACH: Fifth idea. When you get the puck back, move it early, and look up the ice first. You will often go back into the corner, or behind your net, to get the puck. Players from the other team will be chasing you in there, to win it back. That chase is called the forecheck.

PLAYER: That's when I panic.

COACH: Most newer players do. So start on the way there. Look over your shoulder. See which side the forecheck is coming from.

PLAYER: How does that help, if I haven't got the puck yet?

COACH: Then the decision is already made when the puck reaches you. You aren't making it with a player already on top of you.

PLAYER: And when I get it?

COACH: Look up the ice before you look down at it. In the two or three seconds after your team wins the puck, the other team is at its least organised. A quick pass to an open teammate up the ice beats them before they are set. Take the first pass that is really open.

PLAYER: What if a player is closing in on me?

COACH: You have two choices. Send it hard along the boards, onward to a teammate further round. That is a rim. It beats a player who is pinning you against the boards, because it goes past them. Or send it back the way it came, to your partner behind you. That is a reverse. It beats a player who has skated past you, because it goes into the space they just left.

PLAYER: And if neither of those is there?

COACH: Send it off the glass and along it. The glass is the clear panels along the top of the boards. Never over it. A puck over the glass can cost your team a penalty.

PLAYER: Honestly, when they're on me, I just fire it up the middle and hope.

COACH: That's a panic pass, and it's the one to stop. A blind pass, to a teammate you haven't seen, or a straight clear up the middle, is the easy one for the other team to pick off. And from your own end, a puck picked off goes straight back at your net. A bad blind pass is worse than any of the safe plays.

PLAYER: But it feels like I've got no time.

COACH: You usually have more than it feels. Panic is normal for a newer player, and panic spends time you actually had. But in your own end, never hang on to the puck. One look, one decision, move it. The cure is what we just said. Look before the puck reaches you. Decide early. And know your bail-out before you get there. The rim, the reverse, or off the glass. Off the glass is always there when you are in trouble.

COACH: Now the most important safety part of this episode. Going back for pucks with a player behind you happens to you more than to anyone. So two rules, and the boards are where they matter most. Never turn your back to the other player, at the boards or anywhere on the ice, whether they are trying to take the puck or just coming at you. And never duck.

PLAYER: Is there one reason for both?

COACH: One. You want to see what is coming, and take it on your side, not on your head or neck. That is true even when the play is slow.

PLAYER: So how should I stand by the boards?

COACH: Skates parallel to the boards, which means pointing along them. Your forearm and your hip are the parts nearest the boards, never the point of your shoulder. Head up, chin off your chest. Never your back to the other player. Never duck. Get your feet turned that way before you reach the puck.

PLAYER: What if I'm already facing the boards and can't turn in time?

COACH: Get your stick and gloves up and spread on the glass. That softens it.

COACH: Sixth idea. When a player skates at you with the puck, win the gap with your feet. The gap is the space between you and a player skating at you with the puck. Controlling the gap is the skill that defines a defenceman.

PLAYER: How big should it be?

COACH: Too big a gap, and the player comes at you with speed and lots of choices. Too small, and one move takes them past you. So start skating backwards early, before they reach you.

PLAYER: Why early?

COACH: You can't close a gap from standing still. If you wait, they build up speed and go past you.

PLAYER: What should I be looking at?

COACH: Their chest and hips, not the puck, their hands or their head. A good player fakes with the puck, the stick and the head. But their chest and hips have to point where they are really going.

PLAYER: And where do I want them to go?

COACH: Close off the middle of the ice, so the only way left is toward the boards. Keep your stick on the ice, out in front, where a pass would go. And keep skating backwards for as long as you can. Once you turn to skate forwards, you can't react to what they do.

PLAYER: So do I steer them into the boards?

COACH: You steer them toward the boards with the way you skate. Don't ride them into the boards. Don't bump or push them. Play the puck. As they reach the boards they often turn away from you. If you can see the numbers on the back of their jersey, pull up and play the puck. Never into anyone's back.

PLAYER: Can I ever go and meet them?

COACH: Sometimes you step up to meet them, to take the puck early. That is closing the space, not a hit. Keep your feet moving, and play the puck. And if their head is down, that only tells you they can't change direction. It is never a reason to come in harder.

COACH: Now the hardest one. Two players come at you, and you are the only one back. That is called a two on one. What would you do?

PLAYER: Go at the one with the puck?

COACH: That opens the pass. Take away the pass, and leave the shot to your goalie. Stay on your feet, between the two players, with your stick flat on the ice on the side of the pass.

PLAYER: Isn't that letting them shoot?

COACH: Yes, and that's the point. Your goalie can see the shot, and is ready for it. A pass across to an open player is a chance your goalie can't stop.

PLAYER: Does that ever change?

COACH: If the second player has been caught by a teammate, and nobody is left to pass to, it isn't a two on one any more. Close in on the puck. Teams play this differently, so ask your coach.

COACH: Seventh idea. In their end, keep the puck in, and don't get caught. Your team has the puck in the other team's end. You and your partner stand at the point. That's the area just inside their blue line.

PLAYER: What's my job up there?

COACH: Your first job is to keep the puck in their end. If they try to clear it out along the boards, step in and stop it. Keep your own skates inside the blue line too. If you drift out, you are no longer someone your forwards can pass to.

PLAYER: Why is that blue line such a big deal?

COACH: If you lose the blue line, or get caught deep in their end, the other team gets out cleanly. Then they can come at your net with more players than you have back.

PLAYER: Can I ever go in deeper?

COACH: In whichever end you are in, high means toward the blue line, and low means toward the goal line, the line the net sits on. Only one of you goes down low at a time. The other stays at the blue line. Before you go below the puck, know who is above it. If nobody is, stay at the line.

PLAYER: And when I shoot from the point?

COACH: Find a clear path to the net first. Then keep the shot low, at knee height or below, with some pace. A shot from out there rarely beats a goalie on its own. A low shot lets a teammate in front tip it, or score the rebound. If there is no clear path, move the puck until there is one.

COACH: Let me put all seven together.

COACH: Stay goal side, between the puck and your net.

COACH: Work as a pair. One on the puck, one at the mouth of your goal, and swap out loud.

COACH: At the net, get inside first and lift the stick low. Never shove, never your stick across anyone, and never go across the front of your goal in a scramble.

COACH: Never carry or pass the puck across the front of your own goal. Go behind the net.

COACH: When you get the puck back, look up the ice first and move it early, and know your bail-out instead of a panic pass. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the other player, never duck.

COACH: Win the gap with your feet. Steer with your skating, play the puck, and on a two on one, take away the pass.

COACH: And in their end, keep the puck in, get your shot through low, and know who is above the puck.

PLAYER: What should I ask my own coach?

COACH: Three questions. In our own end, do we play one on the puck and one in front, or man-on-man? Do we pass behind the net, or up higher? And on a two on one, how do you want me to play it? Their answers tell you how your team wants you to play.

COACH: Once these seven ideas feel natural, you are ready for more. The longer Playing principles episode on defence goes deeper into each idea. The conversation episode talks it all through. Start there next.
