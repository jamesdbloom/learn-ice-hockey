===== SCRIPT START =====

COACH: Picture this. Your defenceman goes back into your own corner to get the puck. They turn, look up, and there is nobody to pass to. Two players from the other team are closing in.

PLAYER: So who's meant to be there?

COACH: In the usual setup, the centre. Getting there is the first idea in this episode, and some teams do it differently, so we'll come to that too. This is The Basics, topic nine. Playing centre. The Basics is for newer players and younger players. It covers the core things to get right first. I'm the coach, and with me is a newer player, asking the questions you might ask. There are seven ideas, and we'll take them one at a time.

PLAYER: Before we start. What does a centre actually do?

COACH: In a full game, your team has three forwards on the ice. The two on the sides are the wingers. The one in the middle is the centre. Behind them are your two defencemen, the players who mostly stay back. Think of the rink split into three long strips, from one end to the other. The strips down each side, next to the boards, belong to the wingers. The boards are the wall around the rink. The strip down the middle is yours, in all three parts of the rink.

COACH: First idea. In your own end, get below the puck. Back to that defenceman in the corner. Where should you be?

PLAYER: Up near the blue line, ready to go?

COACH: That's the spot that leaves them stuck. Two words first. In whichever end you are in, high means toward the blue line. Low means toward the goal line, the line the net sits on. When your defenceman goes back for the puck, swing low. Get into the inside half of the faceoff circle on the puck's side. The faceoff circles are the big painted circles in your end. Not out on the boards. Keep moving, stay open, and talk, so your defenceman always has someone to pass to. That first pass starts the breakout, which is how your team gets the puck out of its own end.

PLAYER: Why does it have to be me down there?

COACH: Your defenceman needs a safe pass. If you're too high, they have nobody to pass to. They get stuck with the puck, with players coming at them. And if you drift out to the boards, that's your winger's ice. One opponent can then cover you and your winger at once.

PLAYER: Does every team do it like that?

COACH: No. In one common version, the centre leaves early up the middle, and a winger drops low instead. So ask your coach which your team plays. The one thing that always goes wrong is nobody being low under the puck.

COACH: Second idea. Never leave the slot empty in your own end. The slot is the space in front of the net, between the two big faceoff circles. It's where most goals come from. The high slot is the top part of it, further out from the net. Now, the other team has the puck in your end, and it keeps moving. Your job moves with it.

PLAYER: Moves how?

COACH: If the puck is in front of your net, hold the high slot. Mark whoever is standing there. If the puck is in the corner, go in and help your defenceman win it. Go in with your head up. Two of you against one usually wins the puck.

PLAYER: But if I go into the corner, the slot's empty.

COACH: That's the trap. If you stay up in the slot while the puck is in the corner, your defenceman is alone, and they will probably lose that battle. But if you go to the corner and nobody fills the slot, you've swapped a battle you might win for a chance nobody can stop.

PLAYER: So who fills it?

COACH: When you go into the corner, call the winger from the far side down into the slot behind you. Call it early, and call it loud. And if your defenceman and your other winger already have the corner, stay in the slot.

PLAYER: Is that how everyone does it?

COACH: It's one way to play. In another, called man-on-man, each player follows one opponent wherever they go. Then nobody fills in behind you. So ask your coach which your team plays.

COACH: Third idea. Under pressure, put the puck to the outside. You've got the puck right in the middle of your own end, and players are closing in from both sides. What would you do?

PLAYER: Try to skate through them? Or just get rid of it fast, up the middle?

COACH: Neither. Don't try to carry it through them, and don't throw it away without looking. Get to the boards or behind your net first. Go round to it, never across the front of your own net. Then move it.

PLAYER: What's so bad about the middle?

COACH: The middle of your own end is the worst place on the ice to lose the puck. Lost there, it turns straight into a shot at your net. The middle of the rink is risky too. A puck lost there lets the other team come at you with more players than you have back.

PLAYER: So where does it go?

COACH: To the outside. Give it to a winger on the boards. Or send it hard along the boards to a teammate further round. That's called a rim. Getting the puck out of danger matters more than making a great play.

PLAYER: I always feel rushed there. I just whack it anywhere.

COACH: That's panic, and almost every newer player feels it. Panic spends time you actually had. The cure starts before the puck reaches you. While it's on its way, take a quick look over your shoulder. Decide your play, and pick a second choice in case the first one disappears. The rim is a good one to keep ready. Then you can move it the moment it lands, instead of finding out with a player already on you. A blind pass up the middle is worse than a safe play to the boards.

COACH: Now the most important safety part of this episode. Playing the puck from the boards, or from behind your net, means a player from the other team is often behind you. It happens almost every shift. Whether that player is after the puck or just coming at you, two rules on the boards. Never turn your back to the other player, at the boards or anywhere on the ice. And never duck.

PLAYER: Is there one reason for both?

COACH: One. You want to see what's coming, and take it on your side, not on your head or neck. That's true even when the play is slow.

PLAYER: So how do I stand?

COACH: Skates parallel to the boards, which means pointing along them. Your forearm and your hip are the parts nearest the boards, never the point of your shoulder. Head up, chin off your chest. Never your back to the other player, at the boards or anywhere on the ice. Never duck. Keep the puck on your far side, away from them. Get your feet parallel to the boards before you reach the puck.

PLAYER: And if I'm already facing the boards and can't turn in time?

COACH: Get your stick and gloves up and spread on the glass above the boards. That softens it.

COACH: Let's stop and check the first three, because they're your job in your own end. Can you give them back to me?

PLAYER: Swing low, so my defenceman has a safe pass. Never leave the slot empty, and call my winger in when I go to the corner. And under pressure, put the puck to the outside.

COACH: Good. And the boards, word for word. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the other player, never duck.

COACH: Fourth idea. Own the middle of the ice, going forward and coming back. Your team has just won the puck, and you pick it up in the middle, with space in front of you.

PLAYER: Do I pass straight away?

COACH: Carry the puck through the middle of the rink, with speed. If a winger is open, pass it and let them go in at full speed. If you have space, carry it yourself.

PLAYER: What's special about the middle?

COACH: From the middle, you can see both wingers and both defencemen. It's the best place on the ice to decide from. Keep your head up while you do it. You want to see what's coming. The view you carry the puck for is the same view that keeps you safe.

PLAYER: And coming back?

COACH: Say the other team gets the puck. Skate back hard, down the middle. One of their players has the puck, and another forward is coming in late, behind them. Who do you go for?

PLAYER: The one with the puck. That's where the danger is.

COACH: That's the mistake. The late player is usually more dangerous than the player with the puck. Everyone else is watching the puck, so the late player is easy to forget. And they're heading for the slot, with nobody near them.

PLAYER: So I pick them up. Where do I go?

COACH: Get on their inside, between them and your net. Keep your stick on the ice, in the space where a pass to them would go. Not on their body. And don't trail behind their shoulder. From behind them, you can't stop anything.

PLAYER: Shouldn't I help with the player who has the puck, though?

COACH: Don't chase that player. Your defencemen have them. If you swing wide to help, you leave the exact ice your defencemen are protecting. And with the late player, don't step across their path, and don't hit them or hold them. Beat them to the spot with your skating.

PLAYER: What if I catch up with someone?

COACH: Then play the puck, not the player. Never put your stick or your body into anyone's back. If you can see the numbers on the back of their jersey, pull up and play the puck.

PLAYER: Is the middle on the way back always mine?

COACH: That's your coach's choice. Some teams give it to whoever gets back first. Ask your coach. And if nobody has called it, take it.

COACH: Fifth idea. In their end, play the slot and finish at the net. You've got the puck in their slot, and there's space around you.

PLAYER: Shoot?

COACH: Shoot. A shot from the slot goes in several times more often than a shot from out wide.

PLAYER: Is there ever something better than that shot?

COACH: One pass. A pass across the middle, in front of the net. The goalie has to push hard to the side, and the puck gets there before they're ready. And when you don't have the puck, keep moving to get open. A centre standing still in the slot is easy to cover.

PLAYER: What about when my defenceman shoots from the blue line?

COACH: Stop, and stand in front of the goalie's eyes. Keep still as the shot goes.

PLAYER: Why still? Wouldn't moving put them off more?

COACH: A goalie can't stop what they can't see. If you shuffle about, they get glimpses of the puck. Keep your stick blade on the ice, so you can tip the shot. A tip is your stick touching the puck on its way in and changing its direction. And decide to go for the rebound before the shot, not after. A rebound is when the puck bounces back out off the goalie.

PLAYER: How close do I stand?

COACH: Just outside the crease. The crease is the goalie's area, the blue paint in front of the net. Treat the red line around its edge as part of it, so keep your skates off that line too. Never stand so close that the goalie can't move.

COACH: Now four things that keep you safe at the net. First, know where the goal post is before you stand there. Second, keep your head up and your chin off your chest, and never duck. If you meet the post, meet it with an arm or a leg, never your head first. Even going slowly, a post met head first can hurt your neck.

PLAYER: And if someone shoves me while I'm standing there?

COACH: That's the third one. A player may push you sideways while you stand there. Keep your spot, but don't push back or lean on them, and never put your stick across someone. And if you're pushed toward the goalie, let them have the spot, and move away from the goalie.

PLAYER: And the fourth?

COACH: Never go into the goalie. Go for the rebound under control, and go around the goalie, never into them. If you get pushed into the blue paint, get out straight away, around the goalie, not through.

COACH: Sixth idea. On the forecheck, be first in, and steer. The forecheck is chasing the puck in the other team's end, to win it back. In their end, you'll often be the first forward to reach the puck. One of their defencemen has it behind their net.

PLAYER: So I go flat out and hit them?

COACH: No. Skate at the player with the puck on an angle, so they have to skate toward one side of the rink. Steer them. Don't push them. It's like a sheepdog. It doesn't grab the sheep, it just closes off one way to go.

PLAYER: What does steering get us?

COACH: Once they're heading for one side, your winger knows where the puck is going. Your winger can get there and close it off.

PLAYER: Can I still come in fast?

COACH: Arrive under control, not off a run. A run at a player can be called charging, which is a penalty.

PLAYER: What if my winger gets there first?

COACH: Then they go first, and you support them. Right away, without swapping back. And never go into their goalie, even when the goalie comes out to play the puck.

PLAYER: Do all teams send two forwards in?

COACH: Sending two forwards in is one way to forecheck. Some teams send only one. So ask your coach how your team does it.

COACH: Seventh idea. Take the faceoffs that matter seriously. A faceoff, or a draw, is when the official drops the puck between two players to start play. Most of the time, the centre takes it. Think about how many you take in a game.

PLAYER: Loads. Do they all matter?

COACH: Most faceoffs in a game don't decide much. A few matter a lot. The ones in your own end. The ones when a team has a player in the penalty box. And the ones in the last minute.

PLAYER: What makes the ones in my own end so big?

COACH: Lose a draw cleanly in your own end, and the other team has the puck in a set play. Your team is standing still and facing the wrong way.

PLAYER: So what do I do before one of those?

COACH: Agree with your wingers where you're trying to win the puck. And tell them to stay back until the puck drops. If a winger creeps in early, you're the one thrown off the draw.

PLAYER: Any tips for the draw itself?

COACH: Get low with your knees and hips, not by pushing your head out over the dot. If both centres lean their heads in, their helmets meet. That can get you thrown off the draw too.

PLAYER: And if I lose it in my own end?

COACH: Stay with their centre. Go with them toward the slot, rather than chasing the puck. That's the usual job, but some teams give the centre a different player, so ask.

COACH: Let me put all seven together.

COACH: In your own end, swing low, so your defenceman has a safe pass.

COACH: Never leave the slot empty. Help in the corner, and call your winger into the slot behind you.

COACH: Under pressure, put the puck to the outside, and decide before it reaches you. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the other player, never duck.

COACH: Own the middle. Carry through it with your head up, and on the way back, take the late player on their inside.

COACH: In their end, shoot from the slot, screen the goalie, and go for the rebound. Head up at the net, and never into the goalie.

COACH: On the forecheck, arrive under control and steer.

COACH: And take the draws that matter seriously.

PLAYER: What should I ask my own coach?

COACH: Three questions. In our own end, do you want me low, or leaving early up the middle? When I go into the corner, who fills the slot? And on the way back, who takes the middle? Their answers tell you how your team wants you to play.

COACH: Once these seven ideas feel natural, you are ready for more. The longer Playing principles episode on the centre goes deeper into each idea. The conversation episode talks it all through. Start there next.
