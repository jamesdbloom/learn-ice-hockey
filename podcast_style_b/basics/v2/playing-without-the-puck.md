COACH: Think about your last game. How long did you actually have the puck?

PLAYER: Not long. A few seconds at a time, maybe.

COACH: That's normal. You spend almost all of your time on the ice without the puck. So what you do without it is most of your game. This is The Basics, topic seven. Playing without the puck. The Basics is for newer players and younger players. It covers the core things to get right first. I'm the coach, and with me is a newer player, asking the questions you might ask. There are seven ideas, and we'll take them one at a time.

COACH: First idea. Know what is happening right now, and switch jobs the moment it changes. Your team has the puck, and you're skating to get open. A defender pokes it loose. Now nobody has it. Then the other team picks it up. That's three different jobs in a moment.

PLAYER: Three jobs?

COACH: Without the puck, you are always in one of four situations. Your team has the puck. The other team has it. Nobody has it. Or it has just changed hands. Each one asks for a different job. When your team has it, you get open. When the other team has it, you defend. Defending has its own episode, topic eight. On a loose puck, somebody goes to get it. A loose puck is a puck that nobody has. And when it has just changed hands, you act before the other team does.

PLAYER: That sounds simple enough.

COACH: The hard part is that the situation changes without warning, many times in a shift. Most mistakes without the puck are not lazy. They are the right job for the situation a moment ago. So keep your head up, notice the change, and switch straight away.

COACH: Try this one. A teammate has just knocked the puck off the other team's stick, and it's sliding toward them. You're not sure they'll get it. Do you go, or wait?

PLAYER: Wait until I'm sure they've got it?

COACH: Go. When your team has just won the puck, turn and go at once. Go when it looks like your team will get it. Don't wait until you are sure.

PLAYER: What's the rush?

COACH: For two or three seconds after the puck changes hands, the other team is not organised. Players face the wrong way. A chance made then is made against nobody in position.

PLAYER: And if I'm wrong?

COACH: If you go a little early and you were wrong, you can recover. If you go late, the chance is gone.

COACH: Second idea. Be available, not just unmarked. You're standing alone in open ice, nobody from the other team anywhere near you. Your teammate with the puck looks over, and doesn't pass.

PLAYER: That drives me mad. I was open.

COACH: You were unmarked. Unmarked means nobody from the other team is near you. But were you available? Available means the carrier can actually get the puck to you. The carrier is the player on your team who has the puck. They sound the same, but they are not.

PLAYER: So what makes me available?

COACH: Three things. First, your stick on the ice, with the blade flat. That blade is the target, so give the passer something to hit. Second, a straight line from the puck to your blade, with no player standing in the way. That line is the passing lane, the straight line the puck would travel from the carrier's stick to yours. Third, no stick from the other team lying in that line.

PLAYER: Even if the defender's nowhere near me?

COACH: Even then. A stick held up in the air can't take a pass. A pass can't bend around a body. And a defender's stick lying flat across the lane stops the pass, even when you are far from everyone.

PLAYER: So what should I be looking at?

COACH: The passing lane, not the defender. If the lane is blocked, move. Often three or four feet is enough to open it. A small move that opens the lane beats a big burst that doesn't.

COACH: And say something. A short call, like "here" or "back", tells the carrier where you are.

PLAYER: Do they need me to shout? Can't they see me?

COACH: Often not. The carrier's head is often down, looking at the puck. Your voice gives them what their eyes can't. A call can also stop a panic. A carrier who thinks someone is on them can panic and throw the puck away. If nobody is near them, a call of "time" tells them to slow down and make the good play.

PLAYER: Can I shout time whenever I like?

COACH: Only when you're sure. A wrong call of time sends a teammate into a hit they weren't ready for.

COACH: Third idea. Give the carrier two places to pass, at different angles. A triangle, not a line. Picture three teammates in a line up the boards. The boards are the wall around the rink. One defender standing in that line blocks both passes with one stick. Now one of those teammates moves toward the middle. That makes a triangle.

PLAYER: What's better about a triangle?

COACH: No single defender can block both lanes of a triangle. Covering one means turning away from the other. And after the pass, you still have a triangle. A line has to be rebuilt after every pass.

PLAYER: How far from the carrier should I be?

COACH: A good start is two or three stick lengths. Closer when they are under pressure, further in open ice. That is a rule of thumb, so let your coach adjust it.

PLAYER: How do I know if I've got it right?

COACH: The real test is time. Ask yourself this. If the carrier passed right now, would the puck reach me before the defender does? Too close, and one defender covers both of you. Too far, and the pass takes too long, and a stick gets into the lane.

PLAYER: Does it matter whether I'm ahead or behind?

COACH: Yes. Behind the carrier is the safe pass back. Level with them moves the puck across. Ahead of them gains ground. Look where your teammates are, and be the one that is missing.

COACH: One warning in your own end. Never take the pass across the front of your own net. Go behind the net instead.

PLAYER: Even if I'm open there?

COACH: Even then. A pass picked off in front of your own net is a chance against you, right away.

COACH: Let's stop and check the first three, because everything else is built on them. Can you give them back to me?

PLAYER: Know which of the four situations I'm in, and switch the moment it changes. Be available, not just unmarked, with my blade flat on the ice and a clear lane. And give the carrier two options at different angles, a triangle, not a line.

COACH: Spot on.

COACH: Fourth idea. Skate to the space, not to the puck. And stay wide. Watch a game between new teams and you'll often see lots of players in one small circle around the puck. The puck pulls players toward it. Coaches call that the puck magnet.

PLAYER: I end up in that circle a lot.

COACH: So every time the puck moves, ask one question. Who is closest? If it is not you, and your coach has not sent you in, your job is the space.

PLAYER: Isn't it better to have more of us near the puck?

COACH: No. When you skate to the puck, your defender comes with you. Now one defender covers two of you. And pucks squirt out of piles into empty ice. If everyone is in the pile, that empty ice belongs to the other team.

COACH: Here's one to try. Your teammate has the puck, but they're stuck on the boards with someone from the other team on them. What would you do?

PLAYER: Go in and help them dig it out.

COACH: Don't skate into that battle. Move to open ice a few metres away, with your stick down. Your teammate needs to get the puck out. If you skate in, you bring another defender and block the way out. A few metres away, you are the way out.

PLAYER: Is there ever a time to go in?

COACH: Yes. If nobody has the puck at all, or your coach makes you the second player in, then go in. Go in for the puck. Head up, skates parallel to the boards, and never into anyone's back.

COACH: And if you are a winger, stay wide near the boards, even when it feels like you are out of the play.

PLAYER: Why stay out there if the play is in the middle?

COACH: Two defenders can't cover the middle and the boards at the same time. If you drift in, your defender drifts in with you, and the middle closes. Staying wide keeps the middle open for a teammate.

COACH: Fifth idea. Time it. Arrive late, with speed. You spot an open space, get there early, stop, and wait for the pass.

PLAYER: And a defender just skates over.

COACH: Exactly. A player who gets to an open spot early and stops is easy to cover. The defence just skates over to them. Getting open too early is the same as not being open. The defender gets that second to recover.

PLAYER: So when do I go?

COACH: Watch the passer, not the space. Go when the carrier's head comes up, or when their shoulders turn toward you. Until then, keep moving a little, with a curl or a drift. Then burst. A player arriving at speed is much harder to cover than one standing still.

PLAYER: What if the defender is as fast as me?

COACH: Change your speed. Slow down for a moment, then explode. A hard stop works as well. A defender can match your speed. It is much harder to match a change of speed, because reacting costs them a beat.

PLAYER: Is it always arrive late?

COACH: One exception. In your own end, don't wait. The carrier needs a pass right now, so get there.

COACH: And after you pass, move again straight away. The teammate you just passed to now needs two options, and you are the nearest one.

COACH: Sixth idea. Drive the net, and finish every route you start. Driving the net means skating hard to the front of the other team's net, without the puck. Say your teammate is carrying the puck down the side, and you're heading for the net. You don't really expect the pass.

PLAYER: So I just glide in.

COACH: Go hard anyway, as if the puck is coming to you. A slow glide moves nobody.

PLAYER: What does it matter if I never get the puck?

COACH: A player driving to the front of the net pulls a defender with them. No defender can leave you alone there. Your drive also makes a rebound or a tip possible. A rebound is when the puck bounces back out off the goalie. A tip is when a stick touches the puck on its way to the net and changes its direction. And your drive opens the ice behind you, where a teammate can shoot. Often, the point of your drive is that somebody else scores.

PLAYER: And if the pass goes somewhere else?

COACH: Finish your route anyway. If you give up on routes, the defenders learn to ignore you.

COACH: Now the safety part of driving the net. You are skating hard at a steel goal frame, with a goalie and a defender there too. Know where the posts are. Never arrive head first. If you hit a post or the boards, take it with an arm or a leg, never your head.

PLAYER: And my head?

COACH: Keep your head up and your chin off your chest, and never duck. You want to see what is coming. A head-down crash into a post or the boards can hurt your neck, even at walking speed.

PLAYER: How close do I get to the goalie?

COACH: Drive at the net, not at the goalie. The crease is the goalie's area, the blue paint in front of the net. Treat the line around its edge as part of it. Stop just outside the blue paint, with both skates off its line, and your stick on the ice. Never touch the goalie.

PLAYER: What if a defender pushes me into them?

COACH: Staying clear of the goalie is your job, even if you get pushed. If the goalie leans into you, move out of the way, then get back in front.

COACH: Seventh idea. On a loose puck, one player goes, one supports, and nobody else joins. And call it. The puck squirts into the corner. Nobody has it. You and two teammates all see it at the same time.

PLAYER: We all go for it.

COACH: That's the mistake. Who goes depends on how your team plays, so ask your coach. But one thing holds for every team. One player goes hard. One player supports. Nobody else joins.

PLAYER: Wouldn't two of us win it more easily?

COACH: Two players racing to the same puck from the same side are both busy. Neither one can take a pass. So the third player is better off somewhere the puck can go next.

PLAYER: What if we both think it's ours?

COACH: Then call it. "I got it." "Yours." "Help." A word settles it.

COACH: And somebody stays above the puck. Above the puck means between the puck and your own net. A player above the puck is the one who stays back. Before you go down low, know who is staying back.

PLAYER: What's that player for?

COACH: That player is the insurance if your attack fails. If everyone goes low and the puck is lost, the other team gets a rush the other way.

COACH: Now the most important safety part of this episode. A race to a loose puck on the boards is a race into the boards. The other player means the player from the other team who is there with you.

PLAYER: So how do I arrive?

COACH: Come in on an angle, not straight at the wall. Feet apart, knees bent. Skates parallel to the boards before you reach them, which means pointing along them. Forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder. Head up, chin off your chest. Never your back to the other player. Not at the boards, and not anywhere on the ice. Never duck.

PLAYER: Is there one reason behind all that?

COACH: One. You want to see what is coming, and take it on your side, not on your head or neck. A head-down hit into the boards can hurt your neck even at walking speed.

PLAYER: And if I'm already facing the boards?

COACH: If you can't turn in time, put your stick and gloves up on the glass to spread the hit. The glass is the clear wall above the boards. That is a last resort, not the aim. Winning the puck is never worth arriving any other way.

PLAYER: What if the other player gets there first, with their back to me?

COACH: Never push or hit a player on the boards from behind. If their back is to you, go for the puck at their feet. Even a light hit from behind can hurt someone's head or neck.

COACH: Let me put all seven together.

COACH: Know which situation you are in, and switch jobs the moment it changes. When your team wins the puck, go.

COACH: Be available, not just unmarked. Blade flat on the ice, a clear lane, and a call.

COACH: Give the carrier two options at different angles. A triangle, not a line.

COACH: Skate to the space, not to the puck, and stay wide.

COACH: Arrive late, with speed, and move again after every pass. In your own end, get there now.

COACH: Drive the net hard and finish your routes. Head up, chin off your chest, never duck, and never into the goalie.

COACH: And on a loose puck, one goes, one supports, nobody else joins, and somebody stays back. At the boards, remember how to arrive. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never duck. And never your back to the other player, anywhere on the ice.

PLAYER: What should I ask my own coach?

COACH: Three questions. On a loose puck, who goes and who supports? Who stays above the puck? And how close do you want me to the carrier? Their answers tell you how your team wants you to play.

COACH: Once these seven ideas feel natural, you are ready for more. The longer Playing principles episodes on playing without the puck, and on puck support and spacing, go deeper into each idea. The conversation episodes talk them through. Start there next.
