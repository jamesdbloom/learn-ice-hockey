COACH: A player from the other team picks up the puck and skates toward you. You are the last one in the way. The goalie is the player who guards the net, and in this episode, that's you.

PLAYER: That's the bit that makes me nervous.

COACH: Then this episode is for you. This is The Basics, topic twelve. Playing goal. The Basics is for newer players and younger players. It covers the core things to get right first. I'm the coach, and with me is a newer goalie, asking the questions you might ask. There are seven big ideas, and we'll take them one at a time, and then the safety part.

COACH: First idea. Stand on the line, and be out, square and set. Picture a line from the puck to the middle of your goal. Stand on that line, facing along it.

PLAYER: Why that line?

COACH: When you are on that line, you cover the middle of the net. The shooter can only beat you at the edges.

PLAYER: And out, square and set?

COACH: Three steps, in this order. Be out, at the right depth. Depth is how far out of your net you are. Be square to the puck, which means your chest, hips and shoulders are facing it. And be set, which means still, as the shot leaves the stick.

PLAYER: Why does being still matter so much?

COACH: A goalie who is still moving when the shot leaves can't push in any direction. Their weight is already going one way.

PLAYER: How do I know where my net is, if I'm watching the puck?

COACH: You can't look back at your net during play. So at every whistle, rebuild your picture of where it is. Skate your arc, and tap both posts with your stick or glove. The posts are the two upright sides of the net, part of its steel frame. Then find your spot at the top of your crease. The crease is the blue paint in front of your net. It's your area. Then you don't have to think about it when play starts again.

COACH: Second idea. Decide your depth before the shot. One shooter is coming at you, nobody else is open for a pass, and they are going to shoot from far out. What would you do?

PLAYER: Stay back in my net, so I've got time to see it?

COACH: That's the one to change. Come out to the top of the blue paint, or a little beyond. Coming out toward the shooter makes the net look smaller to them.

PLAYER: Then why not always come out?

COACH: It costs you something. The further out you are, the further you have to travel when they pass across. So the puck in close, or a pass open across the front of your net, means get back.

PLAYER: So out when it's one shooter and far away, back when it's close or there's a pass.

COACH: Yes. With nobody to pass to, coming out takes the shot away. With the puck in close, being out buys you nothing, and you need to be able to move across.

PLAYER: What about when I can't see the shot?

COACH: A screen is a player from the other team standing where they block your view of the puck. Against a screened shot from far out, coaches disagree. Some want you deeper, and some want you to stay close to the shooter. Ask your coach which one they teach.

PLAYER: Anything to watch for in myself?

COACH: Two habits. Hiding deep in your net, and racing out at every shooter. Both usually mean your confidence has dipped, not that your technique is wrong.

COACH: Third idea. Watch the puck all the way in. A shot comes in, you make the save, and you have no idea where the puck went.

PLAYER: That happens to me a lot.

COACH: Follow the puck with your head, not just your eyes. Watch it right into your pad, your glove or your chest.

PLAYER: Does it really make that much difference?

COACH: Goalie coaches say almost every soft goal comes from losing sight of the puck. And if you saw the puck hit you, you know where the rebound went. A rebound is when the puck bounces back out off you after a save.

PLAYER: What gets in the way of watching it?

COACH: Your own body. Keep your head still and your hands quiet. If your arms fly and your head snaps back, you lose the puck just as it hits you.

PLAYER: And when they pass instead of shooting?

COACH: Start moving as the pass leaves the stick, not when it arrives. A goalie who stands and watches the pass travel is always late for the shot.

COACH: Let's stop and check the first three, because everything else is built on them. Can you give them back to me?

PLAYER: Stand on the line from the puck to the middle of my goal, out, square and set. Pick my depth before the shot, out against a lone shooter, back when the puck is close or a pass is open. And watch the puck all the way into my body.

COACH: Spot on.

COACH: Fourth idea. Control the rebound. You stop a shot from far out, and the puck bounces back out in front of you, with players from the other team arriving.

PLAYER: But I made the save.

COACH: A save that sends the puck back out in front of you isn't really finished. It turns a shot from far away into a shot from close in.

PLAYER: So what do I do with it?

COACH: First, try to kill the puck dead. Let your stick give a little when the puck hits it. Relax into the shot, instead of bracing hard. A stiff stick or a tense body bounces the puck straight back out. A soft one drops it at your feet.

PLAYER: And if I can't kill it?

COACH: Steer it. Turn whatever the puck will hit, so it goes wide and low, toward the corners. Never into the middle, in front of your net.

PLAYER: Why the corners?

COACH: Nobody is standing in the corner ready to shoot. The middle is where the other team's best players wait. So just before the shot, know where their players are. That tells you where the rebound must not go.

PLAYER: When should I cover the puck and stop play?

COACH: In your crease, with a player from the other team right on you, cover the puck you have saved. If nobody is on you, put it down and play it. If your defenceman is coming with time, leave it for them. Your defencemen are the two players on your team who mostly stay back, near you. And outside your crease, play it or clear it. Don't fall on it out there.

COACH: Fifth idea. Stay on your feet until going down is the answer. The butterfly is dropping to your knees with both pads out to the sides, and the gap between your legs closed. It's a good save.

PLAYER: I drop into it on nearly every shot.

COACH: Don't drop into it on every shot. It is a save you choose, not a habit.

PLAYER: What's wrong with going down?

COACH: Once you are down, the top of the net is open, and you can't get back up in time. That is worse the smaller you are. A young goalie on their knees leaves a lot of open net above them. So stay up, read the shot, then choose the save. After the save, get back to your feet when you can.

PLAYER: What about when one player skates in on me alone?

COACH: That's a breakaway. What would you do?

PLAYER: Go down early and cover the bottom?

COACH: Stay on your feet. Come out to meet them, then back up at their speed. The shooter's whole plan is to make you move first. The last one to move wins.

COACH: Sixth idea. Talk to your team, early, short and every time. A defenceman is fetching a puck in your corner. They are facing the boards, the wall around the rink, and someone from the other team is coming up behind them. You can see it. They can't.

PLAYER: So I tell them?

COACH: You see the whole play. If you don't say it, they have to guess. And a call costs you nothing.

PLAYER: What do I actually say?

COACH: A few calls to know. Time means nobody is near, so they can lift their head and make a good play. Only say time when you are sure. A wrong time sends your defenceman into a hit they weren't ready for.

PLAYER: Why does time matter, if nobody's there anyway?

COACH: A player who thinks someone is on them when nobody is can panic and throw the puck away. Your time call stops that. Then there's man on, which means someone is coming, so move the puck now. Back door means a player from the other team is alone at the far post. And screen means you can't see the puck.

PLAYER: Is one of them more important?

COACH: Make screen a call you always make. Nobody else on the ice can know that you can't see. It tells your teammates to move out of your way, or to stand beside that player and hold their own ground, without pushing.

PLAYER: And while I'm waiting for them?

COACH: Pick one side of the screen and see the puck clearly there. That beats half-seeing it on both sides.

PLAYER: What if a fight or a scrum starts?

COACH: Wherever it is on the ice, stay in your crease. Never go in, to fight or to break it up. Leave only when an official tells you to.

PLAYER: Anything else about calling?

COACH: Call early, keep it short, and call every time. A goalie who only calls sometimes gets ignored. Agree your words with your team before the game, because words differ from team to team.

COACH: Seventh idea. Behind your net, keep it simple. The other team sends the puck hard around the boards behind your net.

PLAYER: Do I go and get it?

COACH: You can often stop it and leave it there for your defenceman. Shout leave it, early, while they are still coming. A race to the far corner becomes an easy pickup.

PLAYER: How far can I go to play it?

COACH: How far behind your net you may play the puck changes from league to league. Some coaches also want their goalie to touch the puck as little as possible. So ask your coach what they want from you.

PLAYER: What about a puck I might not get to first?

COACH: Don't leave your net for a puck you might not win. Lose that race and your net is empty. And never pass the puck up the middle. A turnover there is a shot on an empty net.

PLAYER: And if I do go for one by the boards?

COACH: Arrive with your head up and your chin off your chest. Never go head first into the boards or a post. That can hurt your neck, even at slow speed. Ask your goalie coach how to stand when a player is coming in behind you.

COACH: Now the safety part of this episode. Four things.

COACH: First, in your crease. Claim your spot early. Keep your stick and your body off the players from the other team, even when they are in your way. Shout for a teammate instead.

COACH: Second, your mask. If you need to, borrow the rest of your gear, but have your own mask.

PLAYER: Why my own mask?

COACH: The sticker on a mask tells you nothing about how many pucks it has already stopped. Wear a neck guard at every age, and a small flap hanging from your mask does not replace it. Replace gear you have outgrown, too, because gear that hurts you teaches you to flinch. And ask your teammates never to shoot at your head in the warm-up.

COACH: Third, a puck off your mask. If a hard shot hits your mask, say something at the next stoppage. Tell the official, your bench and your captain.

PLAYER: Even if I feel fine?

COACH: Even then. Nobody can see your face behind the cage. A concussion, which is an injury to the brain, doesn't need you to be knocked out. So don't play on to see how it feels. If you might have one, come off, and don't play again that day. A qualified medical professional decides when you play again, not you.

COACH: Fourth, warming up. Get warm and moving before anyone takes real shots at you. The butterfly and the slides work your hips hard, and cold is when they cost you. If your groin or hip keeps hurting, get it looked at by a physiotherapist or a sports physician.

PLAYER: One more thing. What happens when I let in a bad goal?

COACH: Every goalie lets in goals, and some will be bad. What matters is what you do next. Have a reset that you do the same way every time. Skate a small circle, get a drink, and tap both posts.

PLAYER: Why the same way every time?

COACH: Doing the same thing every time tells your body that the last shift is over. Think about the goal tomorrow, not during the game. And never point at a teammate, not with your hands and not with a look.

COACH: Let me put it all together.

COACH: Stand on the line from the puck to the middle of your goal. Out, square and set, and rebuild your picture at every whistle.

COACH: Pick your depth before the shot.

COACH: Watch the puck all the way in, and move as a pass leaves the stick.

COACH: Kill the rebound if you can. If you can't, send it wide and low to the corners, never into the middle.

COACH: Stay on your feet until going down is the answer, and on a breakaway, be the last one to move.

COACH: Talk early, short and every time. Time, man on, back door, screen, and leave it.

COACH: Behind your net, stop the puck for your defenceman, and never pass up the middle. Head up and chin off your chest at the boards.

COACH: In your crease, keep your stick and body off other players. Have your own mask and a neck guard. Say something after a hard shot off the mask. And warm up before real shots.

PLAYER: What should I ask my own coach?

COACH: Three questions. How deep do you want me against a screened shot? How much do you want me to play the puck behind the net, and how far back may I go? And which words does our team use for its calls? Their answers tell you how your team wants you to play.

COACH: Once these seven ideas feel natural, you are ready for more. The longer Playing principles episode on goaltending goes deeper into each idea. The conversation episode talks it all through. Start there next.
