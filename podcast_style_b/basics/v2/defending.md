COACH: Someone on the other team has the puck, and they are skating at your net. You are the one in the way. What you do in the next few seconds decides whether they get a shot.

PLAYER: That's when I never know what to do. Go for the puck? Wait?

COACH: Then this episode is for you. This is The Basics, topic eight. Defending. The Basics is for newer players and younger players. It covers the core things to get right first. I'm the coach, and with me is a newer player, asking the questions you might ask. Defending comes down to one thing. Stay between the puck and your own net. There are six ideas, and we'll take them one at a time.

COACH: First idea. Stay goal side, all the time. Say a player from the other team is coming toward your net. We'll call them an attacker, and the one you are covering is your attacker. Where do you want to be?

PLAYER: Close to them?

COACH: Not just close. On the right side. Goal side means you are on the line between your attacker and your own net. You are closer to your net than they are. Keep your body there. Every attacker, every time.

PLAYER: Why does that matter so much?

COACH: Defending, everything turns on the front of your own net. A mistake there is a shot against you. If an attacker gets between you and your net, something has already gone wrong. Once they are past you, your goalie is doing your job. Almost every time a rush goes wrong, somebody lost goal side of somebody.

PLAYER: So should I go and get the puck off them?

COACH: Don't chase. Unless your team has sent you to go after the puck, hold goal side, with your stick in the passing lane. The passing lane is the straight line a pass would travel, from the player with the puck to a teammate.

PLAYER: What's wrong with chasing?

COACH: If two of you go after one player with the puck, somebody on the other team is left free.

COACH: Second idea. Watch your attacker, not the puck. You are covering a player who doesn't have the puck, and the puck is over on the far side. Your eyes want to follow it.

PLAYER: They always do.

COACH: Keep your attacker in view. Take short looks at the puck, then look back. If you watch only the puck, the player you should be covering walks in behind you.

PLAYER: And if the one I'm facing has the puck?

COACH: We'll call that player the carrier. When you face the carrier, watch the middle of their body. Their chest and hips. A good way is to look at the numbers on the front of their jersey. You will still see the puck out of the corner of your eye.

PLAYER: But the puck is the thing I'm trying to stop. Why not watch it?

COACH: A good carrier fakes with everything they can move. The puck, the stick, their head and their eyes can all fool you. Their chest and hips can't. Their body has to go where their chest and hips point.

PLAYER: So the puck can lie, but their body can't.

COACH: Yes. And your feet follow your eyes. If you watch the puck and the puck fakes, your feet go the wrong way.

COACH: Third idea. Stick on the ice, in the passing lane. Think about where your stick is when you are defending. Down by your feet? Up by your waist?

PLAYER: Probably up, if I'm honest.

COACH: Keep your stick blade flat on the ice, out in front of you. Put it in the line between the carrier and the teammate they most want to pass to.

PLAYER: What does that do that my body can't?

COACH: Your stick takes away a pass without you having to move your body. A stick up in the air defends nothing. And if you are covering a player without the puck, your stick goes in the lane, never across them. Laying it across a player to hold them up is a foul.

PLAYER: When can I go for the puck with it?

COACH: Only poke at the puck when you can really reach it. And don't show it before you do it.

PLAYER: What happens if I try too early?

COACH: A poke that misses leaves your stick out of the way, and the carrier skates around it. And if they see it coming, they pull the puck back and go around you.

PLAYER: What about when someone gets right past everyone?

COACH: Sometimes an attacker gets away from everyone and skates in alone on your goalie. If you are chasing them from behind, your stick goes on the puck, and nothing else. Never hook them, trip them, or hold them. Stay on your feet.

PLAYER: Even if they're about to score?

COACH: Even then. A chance like that is your goalie's save, and goalies usually make it. A poke at the puck is the one useful thing you can do from behind.

COACH: Let's stop and check the first three, because everything else is built on them. Can you give them back to me?

PLAYER: Stay goal side, between my attacker and my net, and don't chase. Watch my attacker, and the carrier's chest, not the puck. And keep my stick flat on the ice, in the passing lane, and on the puck.

COACH: That's it.

COACH: Fourth idea. Start skating backwards early, and keep the gap getting smaller. The gap is the space between you and the carrier. Say the other team is getting the puck ready deep in their own end, and you are standing out near the middle of the ice. What would you do?

PLAYER: Wait for them to come to me?

COACH: That's the habit to change. Start skating backwards now, while they set up. Don't stand still and wait for them to come.

PLAYER: What difference does it make if I'm moving?

COACH: If you are standing still when they arrive, they are at full speed and you are at zero. While you speed up, they gain on you. The gap opens at the moment you most need it closed. If you are already moving, you can keep the gap as tight as you like.

PLAYER: How tight?

COACH: Keep it shrinking as they come toward you, all the way in. A rough guide is two or three stick lengths in open ice, and about one stick length by your own blue line. That is a guide, not a rule, so ask your coach how tight they want it.

PLAYER: What's so bad about a big gap?

COACH: A big gap gives the carrier three things. Space to speed up. Time to look up and find a pass. And a clear lane to shoot. A small gap takes away all three at once.

PLAYER: So once I'm close, I go for it?

COACH: No. When you are close, don't lunge at the puck. Keep your feet, keep your stick in the lane, and let them run out of room. A lunge is a guess about their next move. If you guess wrong, you are out of the play and they have the whole net.

COACH: Fifth idea. Take the middle, and give them the boards. The boards are the wall around the rink. Imagine you are skating backwards, lined up chest to chest with the carrier.

PLAYER: That sounds right. Straight in front of them.

COACH: Stay a little to the inside of them instead, toward the middle of the ice. Then the only way they can see to go is toward the boards.

PLAYER: Why would I want to send them toward the boards?

COACH: The middle of the ice is the dangerous part. Shots from the middle beat goalies. Shots from out by the boards mostly don't. And a carrier pushed out wide has less ice, a worse angle to the net, and nowhere to cut back.

PLAYER: So I push them out there?

COACH: You do it with how you skate, not with your body. Steer them with your route. Play the puck, not the player. Never push them into the boards.

PLAYER: What if they turn their back to me?

COACH: Then don't finish into them. If you can see the numbers on their back, go for the puck instead. Even a light hit from behind can hurt someone's head or neck.

COACH: Sixth idea. When your team loses the puck, backcheck. Backchecking means skating hard back toward your own end when your team loses the puck. Get to the inside of your attacker, and make sure somebody has the middle. You are deep in their end, and the puck turns over. Most players turn their head first, to see what's happening.

PLAYER: That's what I do.

COACH: Turn and sprint first. Look after three or four strides.

PLAYER: Won't I miss something?

COACH: If you turn your head to watch first, you lose two strides you never get back. The play will still be there when you look up, and you will be closer to it.

PLAYER: Where am I skating to?

COACH: Skate a route to the inside of your attacker. Get level with their inside hip, not behind them. Get to the ice ahead of them, and hold your lane. Don't veer into them.

PLAYER: Why the inside?

COACH: From the inside, you are goal side again, and your stick reaches the passing lane. Chasing from behind someone's shoulder is just following. You can't defend anything from behind. And never let your route end in someone's back. Take the ice, or take the puck.

PLAYER: You said somebody has to have the middle. The middle of what?

COACH: Split the rink into three long strips. The two outside strips run along the boards. The middle strip runs down the centre, between the faceoff dots. That middle strip is the middle lane. Somebody has to own it. Often the centre takes the middle and the wingers take the outside lanes. Other teams give the middle to whoever gets back first. So ask your coach who owns the middle on your team. What never changes is that somebody owns it.

PLAYER: Why that lane more than the others?

COACH: The attacker most often missed is the one skating hard down the middle without the puck. Nobody looks at them, because eyes go to the puck. Then they arrive in front of your net with nobody near them.

COACH: Here's a second one to try. You are racing back. One of your defencemen, the two players on your team who mostly stay back, is already on the carrier and keeping a good gap. What would you do?

PLAYER: Get in there and help them take the puck?

COACH: Don't rush in. Skate past to the attacker nobody has. Two of you on one carrier leaves your own attacker free. Some teams do send the first forward back at the carrier, so ask your coach which way your team plays.

PLAYER: Do I say anything when I get back?

COACH: Yes. Say "I'm back". A defenceman facing a rush may be covering two attackers at once. Your call tells them they can stop. Call it early. A call as it happens helps. A call after it happens is too late to help.

PLAYER: And once the rush is stopped, I can relax?

COACH: Not yet. Find the front of your own net first. Then pick up the player you are covering. During a rush, nobody is given the front of the net. So when the rush ends, it can be empty. And a mistake in front of your own net is a shot against you.

COACH: Let me put all six together.

COACH: Stay goal side, between your attacker and your net. Don't chase unless your team sends you.

COACH: Watch your attacker, not the puck. On the carrier, watch their chest and hips.

COACH: Keep your stick flat on the ice, in the passing lane. Never across a player. From behind, your stick goes on the puck and nothing else.

COACH: Start skating backwards early, and keep the gap getting smaller. Don't lunge.

COACH: Take the middle and give them the boards, with your route, not your body. Play the puck, not the player, and never into anyone's back.

COACH: And when the puck is lost, sprint back, get to the inside of your attacker, and make sure somebody has the middle. Say you are back. And when the rush ends, find the front of your net first.

PLAYER: What should I ask my own coach?

COACH: Three questions. How tight do you want my gap? Who owns the middle on the way back? And does our first forward back go at the carrier, or pick up a player? Their answers tell you how your team wants you to defend.

COACH: Once these six ideas feel natural, you are ready for more. The longer Playing principles episodes on core principles and on defending the rush go deeper into each idea. The conversation episodes talk them through. Start there next.

### 3. Safety limbs (each next to its instruction, as in the source)

- Stick in the lane, never across a player, laying it across is a foul (idea 3, recap).
- Chasing from behind: stick on the puck and nothing else; never hook, trip or hold; stay on your feet (idea 3, recap). The player's "Even if they're about to score?" is answered "Even then" before the reason, so the question cannot be heard as an exception.
- Steer with your route, not your body; play the puck; never push into the boards (idea 5, recap).
- Back turned: don't finish into them; numbers, go for the puck; even a light hit from behind can hurt someone's head or neck (idea 5).
- Inside route: hold your lane, don't veer into them; never let your route end in someone's back (idea 6).
