===== SCRIPT START =====

COACH: Your first game. The puck drops, everyone starts skating, and a whistle goes before you've worked out which way you're meant to be going.

PLAYER: That's pretty much how my first game felt.

COACH: Then this episode is for you. This is The Basics, topic one. How a game works. The Basics is for newer players and younger players. It covers the core things to get right first. I'm the coach, and with me is a newer player, asking the questions you might ask. This topic is about the game itself. Where everyone plays, what the lines on the ice mean, and the two rules that decide where you can go. They're called offside and icing.

PLAYER: Those two sound complicated.

COACH: Don't worry. Both are simpler than they sound. There are seven ideas, and we'll take them one at a time.

COACH: First idea. Know who is on the ice, and leave the goalie's space alone. Picture the start of a game. Count the players on the ice in one team's colours. How many do you get?

PLAYER: Six?

COACH: Six. Five skaters and one goalie. The skaters are three forwards and two defencemen. The forward in the middle is the centre. The other two forwards play on the sides, and they're the wingers. The two defencemen mostly stay back, nearer their own net.

PLAYER: And what makes the goalie different?

COACH: The goalie is the one player allowed to catch the puck and hold it. And the goalie has their own space. Look at either net. It sits on the goal line, which is the thin red line at each end, across the whole width of the ice. In front of the net is a patch of blue paint. That's the crease, the goalie's area. Treat the red line around its edge as part of it.

PLAYER: Can I skate through it?

COACH: Don't. When you go to the net, stand just outside the paint, and keep your skates off its red line too. And never go into the goalie.

PLAYER: What's the big deal if I'm just standing there?

COACH: The goalie needs that space to see the puck and move. A player standing in the paint, or bumping the goalie, can get a goal taken away from their own team. Bumping the goalie can be a penalty too.

COACH: And one safety point, for warm-ups and practice. Never shoot at a goalie who is not ready.

PLAYER: How do I know if they're ready?

COACH: A ready goalie is facing you, set, with their stick down. If the goalie is having a drink, fixing a strap, or talking, they are not ready.

PLAYER: Does it really matter in a warm-up?

COACH: It does. A goalie who isn't watching can't protect themselves from the puck. A shot they don't see can hurt them.

COACH: Second idea. Say our end and their end. Look down the ice and you'll see two wide blue lines across it. They're the blue lines, and they split the rink into three parts, called zones. The zone with your own net is your defensive zone. The zone with the other team's net is your attacking zone. The middle one, between the blue lines, is the neutral zone.

PLAYER: So the end we warmed up in is ours?

COACH: That's the part that confuses everyone. The zones are named after your team, not after the building. The same piece of ice is your defensive zone and the other team's attacking zone, at the same time.

PLAYER: And it stays that way all game?

COACH: No, it moves. Most games are split into three periods, and the teams swap ends after each one. So the end you defend in the first period is the end you attack in the second.

PLAYER: Then how am I meant to keep track?

COACH: Say our end and their end. Those words always follow your team. If a coach says get it deep, it means deep in their end. If you think of the zones as fixed places in the building, you can end up in the wrong half of the rink, doing exactly what you were told.

COACH: Third idea. Keep your shifts short, and go hard while you're out there. A shift is your turn on the ice, from when you go on until you come off. Say you've been out for what feels like ages. Your legs are heavy, and the bench is calling you off.

PLAYER: I usually stay out, in case something happens.

COACH: That's the habit to change. New players often stay out far too long. A good shift lasts about thirty to forty five seconds. Go as hard as you can, then come off and let a teammate on.

PLAYER: Isn't more time on the ice better?

COACH: Not when you're tired. Hockey is short bursts of full effort. A tired player is slow and sloppy. They end up reaching with their stick instead of moving their feet. A short, hard shift keeps you fast every time you're out.

PLAYER: When do I come off, though? In the middle of play?

COACH: Often, yes. When you change during play, change when your team has the puck, or the puck is going toward the other team's net. Never change when the puck is coming at your net. And get right to your bench before your teammate steps on.

PLAYER: Does the timing really matter that much?

COACH: A change is a gap in your team. Make it when the other team can't use it. And if both of you are on the ice, and either of you plays the puck, touches another player, or joins in, your team has too many players. That's a penalty.

COACH: Younger age groups often play these differently, so ask how your games work.

COACH: Let's stop and check the first three. Can you give them back to me?

PLAYER: Know who's on the ice, and stay out of the goalie's paint. Never shoot at a goalie who isn't ready. Say our end and their end, because the zones follow my team, and teams swap ends. And keep my shifts short, and go hard.

COACH: Spot on.

COACH: Fourth idea. The whistle stops play, and a faceoff starts it again. You're chasing the puck into the corner and the whistle goes. The puck is sitting right there on your stick.

PLAYER: I'd probably shoot it anyway. Just in case.

COACH: Don't. When the whistle goes, stop playing the puck. Leave it for the official to pick up. Don't shoot it away, and never fire it out of the rink.

PLAYER: What's the harm, if play's already stopped?

COACH: After the whistle, the puck is no longer yours to play. Shooting it away can get you a penalty. And the pushing around the net after a whistle is where players hand the other team penalties. Your job doesn't end at the whistle. It ends when you've skated away calmly.

PLAYER: What makes the whistle go, anyway?

COACH: Lots of things. A goal. A penalty. Offside or icing. The puck going out of the rink. The goalie holding on to the puck.

PLAYER: And how does it start again?

COACH: Every time, with a faceoff. The official drops the puck between two players, and they try to win it for their team. It happens at one of nine painted dots on the ice.

COACH: Fifth idea. Offside. The puck goes into their zone before you do. Picture a rush. Your teammate is carrying the puck up the ice, and you're flying alongside, a stride ahead, as you both reach their blue line. What would you do?

PLAYER: Slow down and wait for the puck?

COACH: You don't have to slow down. But first, the rule in plain words. When your team attacks, the puck must cross the blue line into their zone before you do. You are offside if both your skates are completely over the blue line before the puck has completely crossed it.

PLAYER: What about my stick?

COACH: Skates decide, not sticks. Your stick can be in the zone. And the puck must cross the whole line. A puck sitting on the line is not in yet.

PLAYER: So how do I keep my speed?

COACH: Keep your back skate on the line, and drag it along the line, until the puck is in. Keep the blade touching the line. Never lift it.

PLAYER: How does dragging one skate help?

COACH: The blue line is wide, about a foot across. While one skate is touching it, you are still onside. That is true in every rulebook. So you can get your body and your stick into the zone at full speed, and still be onside when the puck arrives. If you lift that back skate off the ice, some rulebooks call you offside.

PLAYER: And if I just go in too early?

COACH: Then you're offside, and your attack is usually over.

COACH: Sometimes the official raises an arm instead of blowing the whistle. That means leave the puck and the player who has it alone, skate back and touch the blue line, and wait until all your teammates have too. Then you can attack again.

PLAYER: So an arm up means get out, touch the line, wait for everyone, then go again.

COACH: That's it.

COACH: Sixth idea. Icing. Get the puck past the centre red line before you send it down the ice. The centre red line is the red line across the middle of the ice, halfway between the two nets. Now, you've got the puck in your own half, you're tired, and you want to get off. What would you do?

PLAYER: Fire it all the way down the other end, then change.

COACH: That's icing. The rule in plain words. If your team shoots the puck from your own half of the ice, all the way past the other team's goal line, and nobody touches it, that is icing. Play stops. Your own half means your side of the centre red line.

PLAYER: So what? Play just starts again.

COACH: Icing costs your team. The faceoff goes back into your own end, next to your own net. And in many games, the tired players who iced it are not allowed to change before that faceoff.

PLAYER: So my plan to get off goes wrong.

COACH: Exactly. So carry the puck past the centre red line, on your stick, before you send it in deep. One extra stride is often the whole difference. Once the puck itself has crossed the red line, you can send it into their end and it isn't icing. You keep the puck up the ice, and your team keeps its chance to change.

PLAYER: Is it always the red line?

COACH: Some leagues split the ice at a different line. So ask your coach where the line is in your games.

PLAYER: What about when I think someone's on me in my own end? I usually just whack it away.

COACH: Keep it down. The boards are the wall around the rink, and the puck stays in play when it bounces off them. Off the boards and the glass above them, and out of your zone, is good. Over the glass and out of the rink can be a penalty.

COACH: And about that whack. Lots of newer players panic when they think someone is on them, and fire the puck away without looking. Panic spends time you actually had. So look around before the puck reaches you, and decide early what you'll do with it. Have a second choice ready too, in case the first one disappears. In your own end, don't hang on to it. One look, one decision, move it.

PLAYER: So a quick look first, not a blind whack.

COACH: Yes. A blind pass into pressure just hands the other team the puck. And if a teammate or your goalie shouts time, it means nobody is close. Believe them, and use the extra second. And if you're ever the one shouting it, be sure. A false time sends a teammate into a hit they weren't ready for.

COACH: Younger age groups often play offside and icing differently, so ask how your games work.

COACH: Seventh idea. Keep your stick on the puck, and move your feet. A player skates past you, and they're getting away. Your stick is right there.

PLAYER: That's when I want to hook them.

COACH: That's the moment to watch. Some rule breaks are penalties. When you get one, you go to the penalty box, which is where a player sits when they get a penalty. You stay there for a set time, often two minutes. For most penalties, your team plays with one fewer skater while you sit there. So the other team has one more skater than you. That is called a power play.

PLAYER: Which penalties do new players get?

COACH: Most often, stick penalties. Hooking someone with your stick. Hitting their stick or their body with a chop. Tripping them. Grabbing and holding them. So keep your stick on the puck, and off the other player.

PLAYER: But what do I do when they're getting past me?

COACH: Those penalties are what a beaten player reaches for instead of skating. If a player gets past you, don't reach. Turn and skate. Moving your feet is the cheapest way there is to stay out of the penalty box. It keeps your team at full strength.

COACH: Let me put all seven together.

COACH: Know who is on the ice. Stay out of the goalie's paint, keep off its red line, and never go into the goalie. Never shoot at a goalie who is not ready.

COACH: Say our end and their end, because the zones follow your team.

COACH: Keep your shifts short, go hard, and change when the puck is going the other way.

COACH: When the whistle goes, leave the puck for the official.

COACH: For offside, the puck goes in first. Drag your back skate on the blue line until it does.

COACH: For icing, get the puck past the centre red line on your stick before you send it deep. And under pressure, look first, don't panic.

COACH: And keep your stick on the puck, and move your feet.

PLAYER: What should I ask my own coach?

COACH: Three questions. Do our games use offside and icing the usual way? Which line counts for icing? And when do you want us to change? Their answers tell you how your games work.

COACH: The next topic in The Basics is your gear and staying safe. And once these seven ideas feel natural, you are ready for more. The longer Playing principles episodes on the Rink Map and the Rules Primer go deeper. The conversation episodes on Getting Started, the Rink Map and the Rules Primer talk it all through. Start there next.
