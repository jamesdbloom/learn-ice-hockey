===== SCRIPT START =====

COACH: You're in the changing room before your first session, with a bag full of kit, wondering what goes where and how tight it should be.

PLAYER: And whether any of it actually fits.

COACH: Then this episode is for you. This is The Basics, topic two. Your gear and staying safe. The Basics is for newer players and younger players. It covers the core things to get right first. I'm the coach, and with me is a newer player, asking the questions you might ask. This topic has two halves. First, your gear, and how it should fit. Then, staying safe near the boards, and what to do if someone gets hurt. There are seven ideas, and we'll take them one at a time.

COACH: First idea. Your helmet is your own, and you replace it after a big knock. Say a friend offers you their old helmet. It looks fine, and it's free. What would you do?

PLAYER: Take it? It looks fine.

COACH: Leave it. Never wear a second-hand helmet. That means not bought used, not borrowed, and not handed down. A goalie's mask counts as a helmet too.

PLAYER: What about a taster session, where they lend you kit?

COACH: Take your own, or ask the host about their helmets first.

PLAYER: But if it looks fine, what's wrong with it?

COACH: The foam inside a helmet protects you by squashing when it is hit. Squashed foam does not spring back. And you can't see the damage from the outside. So a helmet that has already taken a big hit, yours or someone else's, may not protect you the next time.

PLAYER: So that goes for my own helmet too.

COACH: Yes. If your helmet takes a serious knock, replace it, even if it looks fine. And one more thing. No helmet can stop every head injury. So never play as though your helmet makes you safe from anything.

COACH: Second idea. Fit your helmet, and fasten it every time. Picture yourself about to walk out of the changing room, helmet on, strap hanging loose. How do you know it fits?

PLAYER: It feels OK?

COACH: There's a better check. The front edge sits about half an inch above your eyebrows, flat and level. Shake your head. The helmet should move with your head, not slide around on it. If it slides, it is too big.

PLAYER: And then the strap?

COACH: First the cage. That's the face protection on the front of your helmet. Some adult players wear a clear visor over the eyes instead, or a full clear shield. Put your chin right in the chin cup of your cage, snug against your chin. Then do up your chin strap, the strap under your chin that holds your helmet on, with no more than a finger's gap. Do this every time, before you step on the ice.

PLAYER: Does it matter if the strap's a bit loose?

COACH: A helmet that isn't fastened is just a hat. It can come off at the moment you need it. And a cage that doesn't close to your chin lets the whole helmet move when you are hit.

PLAYER: What if it comes off anyway, in the middle of play?

COACH: If your helmet or your neck guard comes off during play, don't stop to put it back on. Go to the bench. A neck guard is a soft collar you wear around your neck, under your jersey. We'll come back to it in a moment.

COACH: And while we're on things breaking. If your stick breaks, let go of it straight away. Leave it on the ice, out of the way of the play, and get a new one from the bench. Don't carry it, and never throw it over the boards, the wall around the rink.

PLAYER: Can't I just keep playing with what's left?

COACH: A broken stick can't play the puck. And carrying it, or throwing it off the ice, can get you a penalty.

COACH: Third idea. Find out what your league wants you to wear, then wear it every time. You're in the shop, about to buy a cage, a neck guard and a mouthguard.

PLAYER: Do I just get whatever's on the shelf?

COACH: Before you buy anything, ask what your league requires. The rules on cages, visors, neck guards and mouthguards are different in different leagues. They differ more than the playing rules do.

PLAYER: So what should I wear for sure?

COACH: Wear a neck guard. In Britain it is required for every player, at every age, adults too. Wear it every time you are on the ice, in practice as well as in games. Wear a mouthguard, a soft guard that fits over your teeth, even if your league doesn't make you. And wear the face protection your league asks for. A full cage meets every rule in Britain, at any age.

PLAYER: That's a lot of kit. Do I need every piece?

COACH: Each piece protects one thing the others don't. The neck guard helps protect your throat from a skate blade or a stick blade. The mouthguard protects your teeth and jaw. The cage protects your face, eyes and teeth. And in some leagues, turning up without the right gear means you sit out.

COACH: Let's stop and check the first three. Can you give them back to me?

PLAYER: My helmet is my own, never second-hand, and I replace it after a serious knock. Fit it so it moves with my head, and do up my chin strap every time, a finger's gap at most. And find out what my league requires, then wear it, including a neck guard and a mouthguard.

COACH: Spot on.

COACH: Fourth idea. Pads that fit, and stay where you put them. Your pads are the shin guards on your legs, the padded hockey pants over your hips and thighs, the elbow pads, and your gloves. You're back in the shop, holding two sizes of the same pads.

PLAYER: Should I get the bigger ones, so there's more padding?

COACH: Bigger pads are not more protection. Pads that are too big slide out of the way when you fall or get hit. So the pad ends up in one place, and the puck lands in another.

PLAYER: How do I check them, then?

COACH: Check how your pads fit in your skating stance, with your knees bent. Don't just check them standing up in the shop. Your hockey pants should come down over the top of your shin guards. Your elbow pads should be strapped so they can't spin round when you fall. And the cuff of your glove should meet the bottom of your elbow pad, with no gap.

PLAYER: What difference does bending my knees make?

COACH: Your gear only protects you where the pieces meet and overlap. The gaps open when you bend into your stance, so that is when you check.

COACH: And when you get home, take everything out of your bag, every time, and hang it up to dry.

PLAYER: Even when I'm tired?

COACH: Even then. Wet gear left zipped in a bag breaks down faster, and grows germs. Those germs can get into the small cuts and blisters that hockey gives you.

COACH: Fifth idea. Skates that fit, with sharp edges. You're trying on skates, and one pair costs twice as much as the other.

PLAYER: The expensive ones must be better.

COACH: Not if they don't fit. Skates are the most important thing in your bag, and fit matters more than price. Your heel should not lift inside the boot when you walk. Your big toe should just brush the front. Skates usually come in a smaller size than your shoes, so don't go by the number. Get measured.

PLAYER: I'm still growing. Can't I get them big and grow into them?

COACH: Half a size bigger is fine for growing room, but no more. In a boot that is too big, your foot moves before the blade does. So you can never lean properly onto an edge. A cheaper skate that holds your heel still is better than an expensive one that is too big.

PLAYER: What's an edge, exactly?

COACH: Your edges are the two sharp sides along the bottom of each skate blade. They grip the ice when you turn and stop. Keep them sharp. Get new skates sharpened before you first use them.

PLAYER: How do I know when they've gone dull?

COACH: Dull edges are the usual reason a player suddenly slides out of a turn, or can't stop. If your skates suddenly feel slippery or strange, get them sharpened.

COACH: Sixth idea. Head up, and know how to meet the boards. This idea is for everyone, in every kind of game. Players bump into each other in every kind of hockey, and the boards and goal posts are hard. Picture a puck sliding into the corner, and you chasing it, eyes on the puck.

PLAYER: That's me. Eyes glued to it.

COACH: Keep your head up. Always. Not just sometimes. You can't get ready for something you can't see. A body that is ready for a bump takes it much better than a body that is surprised.

PLAYER: And when I get to the boards?

COACH: Come in on an angle, not straight at the wall. On an angle, your side, an arm or a leg meets the boards, not your head.

COACH: In this episode, the other player means a player from the other team near you, whether they are trying to take the puck or just coming at you. That's on the boards or anywhere on the ice.

PLAYER: So how should I stand when they get there?

COACH: This is the most important safety part of the episode. Skates parallel to the boards, which means pointing along them. Your forearm and your hip are the parts nearest the boards, never the point of your shoulder. Head up, chin off your chest. Never your back to the other player, at the boards or anywhere on the ice. Never duck. Keep your feet wide and your knees bent, so you are low and hard to knock over.

PLAYER: Is there one reason behind never turning my back and never ducking?

COACH: One. You want to see what is coming, and take it on your hip and arm, not on your head or neck. That is true even when the play is slow. Even a light bump can hurt your neck if your head goes into the boards first.

PLAYER: What if I end up facing the boards and can't turn?

COACH: Put your stick and gloves up on the glass above the boards, with your arms out, and keep your head up. That spreads the bump across your arms and body, and keeps your head off the boards.

COACH: And the best protection is not getting stuck on the boards at all. Look around before the puck gets to you. Know what you will do with it. Then move it, or take it away from the wall, before anyone arrives.

COACH: Seventh idea. Look after each other. You're chasing a player who has the puck near the boards, and they've got their back to you.

PLAYER: Sounds like an easy chance to take it off them.

COACH: Play the puck, not the player. Never put your stick or your body into anyone's back. If you can see the numbers on the back of their jersey, pull up and play the puck.

PLAYER: Even if I'd win the puck?

COACH: Even then. A player with their back to you can't see you coming, so they can't get ready.

PLAYER: And if it's my teammate with their back turned?

COACH: If a teammate is going back for the puck with their head down, and someone is coming, shout. Their name, and behind you. Your shout is what lets them get ready.

COACH: And if things get heated, keep your gloves on and your helmet on. Put your hands down, and skate away.

COACH: Now, if someone is hurt. A teammate goes down near you after a knock, and they're holding their neck. What would you do?

PLAYER: Help them up and get them to the bench?

COACH: Don't. If a player may have hurt their head or neck, don't help them up. Don't move them, except to keep their airway clear. Leave their helmet on. And get someone to call for help straight away.

PLAYER: It feels wrong just leaving them lying there.

COACH: Moving a player with a neck injury can make it much worse. So the kind thing is to keep them still, and get help.

PLAYER: What if they get up by themselves after a knock to the head?

COACH: Then think concussion. A concussion is an injury to the brain, usually from a knock to the head. If a player takes a knock to the head and gets up, a suspected concussion means they come off the ice. They don't play again that day. And they see a medical professional before they play again, even if they feel fine.

PLAYER: Even if they say they're fine?

COACH: Yes. You don't have to be knocked out to have a concussion. And a player with a concussion will often say they are fine. So they don't get to decide. If it's you, come off, and tell your coach, even if you feel fine.

COACH: Someone stays with that player afterwards. The player doesn't drive, and doesn't go home on their own. If they get worse, get medical help straight away.

COACH: Let me put all seven together.

COACH: Your helmet is your own, never second-hand. Replace it after a serious knock, even if it looks fine.

COACH: Fit it so it moves with your head. Chin in the cup, and the strap done up every time, a finger's gap at most. If it comes off, go to the bench.

COACH: Find out what your league requires, and wear it. A neck guard every time you are on the ice, and a mouthguard.

COACH: Pads that fit and overlap, checked in your skating stance. And dry your gear after every time you play.

COACH: Skates that hold your heel, with sharp edges.

COACH: Head up, always. By the boards, skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never duck. And never your back to the other player, at the boards or anywhere on the ice.

COACH: And look after each other. Never into anyone's back. If someone may have hurt their head or neck, don't move them, leave the helmet on, and get help. A knock to the head means off the ice for the rest of the day, and a medical professional before they play again.

PLAYER: What should I ask my own coach?

COACH: Three questions. What gear does our league require? Who checks that our helmets fit? And who do we tell if someone gets hurt? Their answers help keep everyone safe.

COACH: Once these seven ideas feel natural, you are ready for more. The longer Playing principles episodes on Equipment and on Body Contact and Battles go deeper into each idea. The conversation episodes on those pages talk it all through. Start there next.
