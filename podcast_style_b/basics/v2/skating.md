===== SCRIPT START =====

COACH: Watch any game for a minute. Every player is passing, shooting, chasing or defending. And every one of them is skating while they do it.

PLAYER: And I'm still wobbling about on mine.

COACH: Then this episode is for you. This is The Basics, topic three. Skating. The Basics is for newer players and younger players. It covers the core things to get right first. I'm the coach, and with me is a newer player, asking the questions you might ask. Skating is the skill under every other skill. And it gets better with practice, at any age. There are seven ideas, and we'll take them one at a time.

COACH: First idea. Stay low. Think about how you stand when you first step on the ice. Tall and straight, like you're waiting for a bus?

PLAYER: Pretty much. It feels safer.

COACH: Try the opposite. Bend your knees until they are over your toes. Look down, and your knees should hide your skates. Keep your chest up. Bend at your knees and hips, not at your waist. And stay low all the time. Stay low when you glide, when you turn and when you stop.

PLAYER: What do I get for being low?

COACH: Four things. Your push comes from straightening a bent leg. A straight leg has nothing left to push with. A low player is also harder to knock over. From a deep bend, you can push off in any direction straight away. And you can't tilt your blade far onto an edge unless you bend your knees and ankles.

PLAYER: What's an edge?

COACH: The bottom of a skate blade is not flat. It has a groove down the middle, so each blade has two thin edges. You skate by tilting the blade onto one of them. The inside edge is on the big-toe side of your skate. The outside edge is on the little-toe side.

PLAYER: So standing tall costs me power, balance, quick reactions and my edges.

COACH: All four at once. A simple check. After a hard shift, which is your turn on the ice, your thighs should burn. If they don't, you probably weren't low enough.

COACH: Second idea. Eyes up. You're skating up the ice with the puck, and you keep glancing down to check it's still there.

PLAYER: That's me, every time.

COACH: Keep your eyes up and looking around the ice. Not just your chin up. Your eyes. Keep them up as you start, as you turn, and when you have the puck.

PLAYER: What's wrong with a quick look down?

COACH: You can't read a play you aren't looking at. And your head pulls your body with it. When you look down, your weight tips forward. So a player skating head down is also skating with their weight in the wrong place.

PLAYER: How do I practise keeping them up?

COACH: Pick something at the far end of the ice. Skate to it without looking down.

COACH: Third idea. Go into the boards on an angle, with your head up. This is the most important safety idea in this episode. The boards are the wall around the rink. Picture the puck sliding toward the boards, and you're chasing it hard. What would you do?

PLAYER: Go straight at it. It's the quickest way.

COACH: That's the habit to change. When you skate toward the boards, come in on an angle. Never go straight at them. Keep your head up and your chin off your chest. Never go in head first.

PLAYER: What does the angle do for me?

COACH: On an angle, a leg, your side or an arm takes most of the bump. You also keep better control of the puck. And you choose your angle several strides before you get there. So this is the part you control completely.

COACH: Two more habits. In this episode, the other player means a player from the other team who is near you, or coming at you. Never turn your back to the other player, at the boards or anywhere on the ice. And never duck.

PLAYER: Is there a reason behind both?

COACH: One reason. You want to see what is coming, and take it on your side, not on your head or neck. Even going slowly, meeting the boards head first can hurt your neck. And even a light bump from behind can hurt your head or neck. So these habits matter at every speed.

PLAYER: And if someone is actually coming at me there?

COACH: If contact is coming at the boards, this is how you stand. Skates parallel to the boards, which means pointing along them. Your forearm and your hip are the parts nearest the boards. Never the point of your shoulder, and not the whole shoulder if you can help it. Head up, chin off your chest. Never your back to the other player, at the boards or anywhere on the ice. Never duck.

COACH: Let's stop and check the first three, because everything else is built on them. Can you give them back to me?

PLAYER: Stay low, with my knees over my toes. Keep my eyes up, not just my chin. And go into the boards on an angle, head up, chin off my chest. Never my back to the other player, at the boards or anywhere on the ice, and never duck.

COACH: Spot on.

COACH: Fourth idea. Win the first three steps. A loose puck, which is a puck that nobody has yet, is sitting between you and the other player. You both see it at the same moment.

PLAYER: So whoever's fastest gets it?

COACH: Not quite. Coaches find that most races in a game are won or lost in about three strides. That is a race to a loose puck. Or a race to get past a defender. Or a race back when you have been turned the wrong way.

PLAYER: So top speed doesn't matter?

COACH: Hockey isn't long, straight sprints. It is short bursts, a second or two at a time. So your top speed rarely decides a race. Your first three steps almost always do.

PLAYER: How do I make those steps quick?

COACH: Drive your first three steps hard, every time. Use short, quick steps on the front of your blades. Lean your body forward, but keep your eyes up. Push along the ice, not up into the air. Don't hop. A hop wastes your push going up, when you want to go forward. As you get moving, get onto the whole blade and let your strides get longer.

PLAYER: What if I need to go sideways?

COACH: To start sideways, or to turn back the other way, start with a crossover. A crossover is when you skate round a curve and one foot steps over in front of the other. It is how you build speed on a turn.

COACH: And a race to a loose puck in the corner ends at the boards. So drive your three steps, then arrive with your knees bent, head up, on an angle to the boards, and able to stop.

PLAYER: Even if I'm winning the race?

COACH: Especially then. A player who wins the race stretched out flat has no balance left to keep the puck.

COACH: Fifth idea. Change your speed on purpose. You're carrying the puck up the ice at one steady speed, and the defender in front of you just keeps pace.

PLAYER: So I try to go faster?

COACH: Try changing speed instead. Don't skate at one speed all shift. Glide to read the play. Then burst.

PLAYER: How does slowing down help?

COACH: A defender facing a player at one steady speed just matches it. When you glide, they have to choose. Do they close in, or ease off? Then you burst, and whatever they chose, you have made space. It is the change in speed that gets you free, not your top speed.

PLAYER: So flat out all the time is a mistake?

COACH: If you skate flat out all the time, you have no faster gear to change to. You also get no moment to look up. And it works without the puck too. Drift, let the player watching you relax, and then break away.

PLAYER: What about when a pass comes to me?

COACH: When a pass reaches you, keep your feet moving. Push the puck a little ahead of you and skate onto it. Slowing down to handle the puck gives up your speed just when you need it most. Skate first, and handle the puck second.

COACH: Sixth idea. Train your outside edges, and choose a crossover or a tight turn. Think about the last time you tried to cut sharply one way and nearly went over.

PLAYER: That happens to me on one side all the time.

COACH: Then test which side. Most players are much stronger on their inside edges. Many players leave their outside edges untrained. Test your own, and find out which you are.

PLAYER: If the inside edges are stronger, what do I need the outside ones for?

COACH: Your inside edges give you power, for pushing, starting and stopping. Your outside edges give you control. Turning, cutting back and slipping away from the other player all come from your outside edges. Leaning out over your skate feels like falling until you trust it.

COACH: You will fall while you learn this. Everyone does. So first, how to fall safely.

PLAYER: There's a right way to fall?

COACH: There is. As you feel yourself going, get low. Bend your knees and sink. Go down on your side, onto your hip and thigh, which are padded. Slide with it, and don't fight the fall.

PLAYER: Shouldn't I put a hand out to catch myself?

COACH: No. Keep your hands close to your body. Never put a hand out to catch yourself on the ice. A stiff arm puts all your weight through one wrist.

COACH: Now your chin. This part changes with where you are falling, so listen to both halves. Going down onto the ice, tuck your chin toward your chest. Do it most of all if you fall backwards. That stops your head snapping back onto the ice.

COACH: Sliding toward the boards, do the opposite. Head up, chin off your chest. Turn so your skates or your side meet the boards first. If you can't, get your arms out. Anything but your head first.

PLAYER: Hang on. Chin in, and then chin up? How come it changes?

COACH: On the ice, a tucked chin keeps your head off the ice. Going into the boards, a tucked chin puts your neck first in line. So, onto the ice, chin in. Into the boards, head up.

PLAYER: Onto the ice, chin in. Into the boards, head up.

COACH: That's it. And if you end up facing the boards with no time to turn, put your stick and gloves up on the glass above the boards, spread wide. Keep your head off it. That is only for when things have already gone wrong. It is not how you aim to meet the boards. The idea behind all of it is the same. Spread the bump over as much of your body as you can.

PLAYER: And once I'm down?

COACH: Stay down for two or three seconds first. Check that you are all right. If you are hurt, stay down, and let play be stopped.

COACH: A fall can give you a concussion. A concussion is an injury to the brain, from a knock to the head or a jolt that snaps the head. You don't have to be knocked out to have one. If you might have a concussion, come off straight away, and tell someone. Don't go back on that day. And don't go back on the ice until a medical professional says you can, even if you feel fine. Don't drive, and don't go home on your own. If you feel worse, get help straight away.

PLAYER: And if I'm all right?

COACH: Then get up onto your hands and knees. Put one skate flat on the ice beside your hands. Push up, bring the other skate beside it, and come up into your low stance.

COACH: Now the drill. Skate a big circle on one foot, on the outside edge of the foot nearer the middle. Start at walking pace, on a wide circle, with your helmet on. Lean more only as the edge holds.

PLAYER: What if I feel wobbly?

COACH: Feeling wobbly is fine, because that is where you get better. Feeling out of control means slow down.

COACH: Now, crossover or tight turn. A tight turn is a sharp turn in a small space. Both skates stay on the ice, with your feet side by side. Cross over when you have room and want speed. Turn tight when you have no room, like coming off the boards or getting away from the other player. Make that turn while they're still too far away to reach you. If they're already on you, don't turn. Go side-on, and never your back to them.

PLAYER: Can't I just cross over every time?

COACH: A crossover builds speed through a wide curve, and it needs room. A tight turn changes direction in the smallest space. When there is no room, a crossover is the wrong tool.

PLAYER: My tight turns come out pretty wide.

COACH: You can't turn tight standing up. Sink lower, lean your whole body in, and keep your chest and eyes up.

COACH: Seventh idea. Stop on both sides, then go straight back the other way. Picture the other team picking off the puck and heading the other way, right past you. What would you do?

PLAYER: Curl round in a big loop and chase them.

COACH: That's the one to change. When the play turns the other way, stop, and go back. Don't loop round in a big curve. A loop feels faster, but it isn't. It carries you away from the play.

PLAYER: But I can only really stop one way.

COACH: Lots of players can only stop well one way. Learn to stop both ways. Test your own weaker side. A player who can only stop one way turns instead, and ends up further from the puck. They also arrive at the puck slowing down, not ready.

PLAYER: And if I can't do a proper stop yet?

COACH: Use a snowplough. Point your toes in a little and push both blades out. It is slow, but it always works.

COACH: When you can, use a hockey stop. That's the stop where you turn both skates sideways and let the blades scrape the ice. Keep your knees bent, and sink lower as you stop. Your legs soak it up. Don't lean back away from the stop. That is what puts you on your backside.

COACH: A stop is only worth what comes after it. Finish your stop low and balanced, so your first step out comes straight away.

PLAYER: How do I practise it?

COACH: When you skate, stop at every blue line and every goal line. The goal line is the line the net sits on, and the two blue lines mark off each end of the rink. Make half of those stops on your weaker side. Then take three quick steps back the other way.

COACH: Let me put all seven together.

COACH: Stay low, with your knees over your toes.

COACH: Keep your eyes up, not just your chin.

COACH: Go into the boards on an angle. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never duck. And never your back to the other player, at the boards or anywhere on the ice.

COACH: Win the first three steps, with short, quick steps, pushing along the ice.

COACH: Change your speed on purpose, and keep your feet moving when the puck arrives.

COACH: Train your outside edges. Cross over when you have room, and turn tight when you don't.

COACH: Stop on both sides, then go straight back the other way.

COACH: And when you fall, onto the ice, chin in. Into the boards, head up. If you might have a concussion, come off, tell someone, and stay off until a medical professional says you can.

PLAYER: What should I ask my own coach?

COACH: Three questions. Which way do I stop worse? Which way do I turn worse? And am I low enough? Their answers tell you what to work on first.

COACH: Once these seven ideas feel natural, you are ready for more. The longer Playing principles episode on skating goes deeper into each idea. The conversation episode talks it all through. Start there next.
