===== SCRIPT START =====

COACH: Picture this. Your teammate is skating back for the puck in your own corner. A player from the other team is closing in behind them, fast. You can see it. Your teammate can't.

PLAYER: So I shout.

COACH: You shout. And that one word is where this episode starts. This is The Basics, topic thirteen. Talking, teamwork and bouncing back. The Basics is for newer players and younger players. It covers the core things to get right first. I'm the coach, and with me is a newer player, asking the questions you might ask. There are seven ideas, and we'll take them one at a time.

COACH: Before we start, one thing that matters more than hockey. If you ever have any thought of harming yourself or of suicide, get help now, tonight. Call your local emergency number. That is nine nine nine in the UK and Ireland, one one two across the E U, and nine one one in the US and Canada. The Mental Game page on the website lists crisis lines too.

COACH: First idea. Say what you can see. Man on, and time. A call is a short word you shout to a teammate during play, to tell them something they can't see. The two that matter most are about the puck carrier, the player who has the puck.

PLAYER: What do they mean?

COACH: Man on means a player from the other team is closing in, and the puck carrier can't see them. Time means nobody is near, so they can lift their head and make the good play.

PLAYER: Why does the puck carrier need telling? They're the one with the puck.

COACH: The puck carrier is the player on the ice who knows least. Their eyes are down or forward. The player coming from behind is in the one place they can't look. Everyone else can see it. A call turns a blind guess into an informed choice, and it costs you nothing.

PLAYER: And when I'm the one who hears it?

COACH: If you hear man on, stop trying to make a play. Protect the puck. Put your body between them and the puck, and never turn your back to them. Then move it. If you hear time, slow down and make the good play.

PLAYER: Why would I need telling to slow down?

COACH: Because a newer player with the puck often feels pressure that isn't there. They panic, and throw the puck away without looking. Panic spends time you actually had. A time call stops that. And when nobody calls, the same cure works. Look before the puck reaches you, so you already know whether anyone's coming.

PLAYER: Are there rules for making the calls?

COACH: Two. Shout man on early, even when you're not sure. But never say time unless you're sure.

PLAYER: Why so careful with time?

COACH: A false time sends a teammate into contact they weren't braced for. After that, they stop trusting your calls. And one safety point, whether anyone calls or not. Anywhere on the ice, and most of all near the boards, the wall around the rink, never turn your back to the other player, and never duck. Head up, chin off your chest. You want to see what's coming, and keep your head and neck out of it.

COACH: Second idea. Call early, short and loud, and answer every call. Think of a pass sliding across to your teammate, and a player from the other team coming at them.

PLAYER: When do I shout?

COACH: While the puck is still travelling, not as it arrives. Keep it to one or two words. Say it loud. Use names. Sam, man on.

PLAYER: Does the timing really matter that much?

COACH: It does. A call made early lets your teammate plan. The same call as the puck lands just startles them. Rinks are loud, and helmets muffle sound, so a quiet call didn't happen. And a name tells the right player it's for them.

PLAYER: What if someone calls to me?

COACH: Answer. Yep, got it, or a raised glove is enough. A call with no answer leaves two players hoping, and the player who called stops calling.

PLAYER: Are there other calls?

COACH: In your own end, one more. Back door means a player from the other team is alone at the far post. The nearest player takes them, right now.

PLAYER: Why not the one who saw them?

COACH: The player who can see that open player is almost never the one who can cover them. One more thing. There's no official list of hockey calls. Words change from team to team. So agree your words as a team, and practise them. Ask your coach which calls your team uses.

COACH: Third idea. Be where your linemates expect you. Your linemates are the teammates who go on the ice with you, shift after shift. Say your defenceman has the puck behind your net, and they pass to the boards without even looking up.

PLAYER: How do they know I'll be there?

COACH: Because you're always there. Decide what you do, then do it every time. If you go to the boards on the breakout, when your team brings the puck out of its own end, go there every time.

PLAYER: Isn't it better to be a bit unpredictable?

COACH: Not to your own team. A lot of passes in hockey go to a space, before the teammate gets there. That pass is a guess about where you'll be. If you're always in the same place, a teammate can pass without looking up. If you're somewhere new each time, they have to look first, and that's when they get caught.

PLAYER: So I learn them, and they learn me.

COACH: Learn your linemates too, yes. Where do they like the puck? How fast do they really skate? Then give them the pass that works for them. They won't change their game for you, but you can change what you give them.

PLAYER: When do we talk about all this?

COACH: Use the bench. The bench is where your team sits when it's not on the ice. Say one useful thing for the next shift. I'm coming low on the breakout. Never say something that only judges a teammate, like you need to be sharper. That isn't information.

COACH: Fourth idea. Fill a teammate's spot, and get back to yours. Your teammate leaves their spot to go after the puck.

PLAYER: And their spot's empty.

COACH: So fill the space they left. And when somebody has filled your spot, get back to it as soon as the play lets you.

PLAYER: Why does getting back matter so much?

COACH: That exchange is what trust looks like on the ice. A player only leaves their spot to help if they believe someone will cover it. If nobody ever comes back, players stop leaving their spot to help. Which spot you fill depends on how your team plays. Ask your coach.

COACH: Let's stop and check the first four, because they're the core of playing as a team. Can you give them back to me?

PLAYER: Say what I can see, man on and time, and never time unless I'm sure. Call early, short and loud, with a name, and answer every call. Be where my linemates expect me. And when a teammate leaves their spot, fill it, then get back to mine.

COACH: Spot on.

COACH: Fifth idea. When a teammate is having a bad game, keep giving them the puck. One of your teammates has lost the puck three times tonight. You've got it, and they're open. What would you do?

PLAYER: Honestly? Pass to someone else.

COACH: That's the natural thing, and it's the wrong one. Pass to them, and give them an easy one first. Praise what they do well, like a good backcheck.

PLAYER: Won't they just lose it again?

COACH: Cutting a player out of the play tells them what they already fear. Confidence comes back through simple things they actually complete.

PLAYER: What if I'm annoyed with them?

COACH: Never show frustration at a teammate on the ice. No head shake, no stick slap, no pointing. The other team sees where your weakness is. Your teammate gets more worried and plays slower. And while you're complaining, you're not backchecking. If something needs saying, say it after the game, calmly, about one play, once.

PLAYER: And if it's the goalie who let in a bad one?

COACH: When your goalie lets in a soft goal, say something short. Next one. Tap their pads. Then play a simple shift in front of them. Don't talk the goal over with them.

COACH: Sixth idea. After your own mistake, run your reset. You give the puck away, and it ends up in your net. You skate to the bench and all you can think about is that pass.

PLAYER: That's me after every mistake.

COACH: Everyone makes mistakes. The skill is what you do in the next few seconds. A reset is a short routine you do after a mistake, so you can let it go and play the next shift. Yours has four steps. First, a small physical trigger, like tapping your shin pads with your stick. Second, one long, slow breath out. Third, a short cue word, like next, or feet. Fourth, say your next job in one sentence, before your skates hit the ice.

PLAYER: What does saying the next job do?

COACH: Play doesn't stop for your mistake. If you keep replaying it, you're not doing your new job. And the next job is the most important step, because a plan pushes out the replay.

PLAYER: But the thought keeps coming back.

COACH: Don't fight it. Notice it, let it be there, and turn to your next job anyway. On the bench, do three things. Get your breath back. Watch the game. Plan one thing for your next shift. Save thinking about the mistake for after the game.

COACH: Seventh idea. Keep your cool, with nerves, with the officials and with your coach. The officials are the referees and linespeople who run the game. Start before the game, though. You're sitting in the dressing room, and your heart is going fast.

PLAYER: That's just nerves, isn't it? I hate that bit.

COACH: Call it my body getting ready, not I'm nervous. It's the same feeling with a better name. Nerves are your body preparing to work hard. They're a good sign, and they usually fade in your first shift.

PLAYER: OK. And the officials?

COACH: Say a call goes against you, and you're sure it's wrong. What would you do?

PLAYER: Tell the referee they got it wrong?

COACH: Say nothing to the referee. Skate away and run your reset. Let your captain speak to the officials. Arguing almost never changes a call, and it can get you a penalty of your own.

PLAYER: What if a player does something to me?

COACH: Never get back at a player who wronged you. And if players start pushing and shoving, stay out of it, and stay on the bench. Getting back at someone is how trouble grows, and it helps nobody.

PLAYER: And my coach? Sometimes they shout.

COACH: When a coach corrects you, listen for the message, even if the way they said it was harsh. The message may still be right. But there is a line. Criticism of your play is coaching. Being put down as a person, or humiliated in front of the group again and again, is not. Don't keep that to yourself. Neither is anything that leaves you dreading the rink. Tell the person at your club in charge of keeping players safe, another club official, or the league.

COACH: One safety point before we finish, for when a player is hurt. If someone is down and not getting up, stop playing and wave for the officials.

PLAYER: And then?

COACH: If it might be their head or neck, treat it as a possible spine injury. Make sure somebody actually phones for medical help, straight away. Don't help them up, and don't move them, except to keep their airway clear so they can breathe. Leave their helmet on, and keep them still. Waving at the bench is not the same as calling for help.

COACH: And if worries about hockey start to follow you home, or into school or sleep, talk to a qualified professional. That is not weakness. It is the right tool for the job.

COACH: Let me put all seven together.

COACH: Say what you can see. Man on early, and time only when you're sure. Anywhere on the ice, and most of all near the boards, never your back to the other player, never duck, head up, chin off your chest.

COACH: Call early, short and loud, use names, and answer every call.

COACH: Be where your linemates expect you, and tell them on the bench what you will do.

COACH: Fill a teammate's spot, and get back to yours.

COACH: Keep giving a struggling teammate the puck, and never show frustration at them.

COACH: After a mistake, run your reset, and name your next job.

COACH: And keep your cool. Nerves are your body getting ready. Say nothing to the referee. Never get back at anyone. And criticism of your play is coaching, but being put down as a person is not.

PLAYER: What should I ask my own coach?

COACH: Three questions. Which calls does our team use? Which spot do you want me to fill when a teammate leaves theirs? And what is one thing I can do better next game? Their answers tell you how your team wants you to play.

COACH: Once these seven ideas feel natural, you are ready for more. The longer Playing principles episodes on communication, team play and the mental game go deeper into each idea. The conversation episodes talk it all through. Start there next.
