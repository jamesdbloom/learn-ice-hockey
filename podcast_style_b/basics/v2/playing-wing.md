===== SCRIPT START =====

COACH: Picture this. Your team is breaking up the ice, three forwards across. Your centre has the puck in the middle, and you're out on the side, right next to the boards. The puck's nowhere near you.

PLAYER: That's where I always end up. It feels like I'm out of the game.

COACH: You're not, and this episode is about why. This is The Basics, topic ten. Playing wing. The Basics is for newer players and younger players. It covers the core things to get right first. I'm the coach, and with me is a newer player, asking the questions you might ask. There are six ideas, and we'll take them one at a time.

PLAYER: So what is a winger, exactly?

COACH: In a full game, your team has three forwards on the ice. The one in the middle is the centre. The other two play on the sides, one on the left and one on the right. Those two are the wingers.

COACH: First idea. Stay in your lane, and stay wide. Think of the rink split into three long strips, from one end to the other. The middle strip belongs to your centre. The strip down your side, next to the boards, belongs to you. The boards are the wall around the rink. That strip is your lane.

PLAYER: So when we're going up the ice, I stay in it?

COACH: Yes. Stay out wide, near the boards on your side. Don't drift into the middle.

PLAYER: But the puck's in the middle. Wouldn't I be more use there?

COACH: The other team's defenders come back in a line across the ice. That line can't cover players who are spread out wide, not without spreading thin. If you drift into the middle, one defender can cover two of you. And when you stay wide, you pull the defenders apart. That opens the middle for your centre.

PLAYER: So I'm not out of the play out there.

COACH: No. You're making room for the whole line.

COACH: Second idea. Crash the net every time a shot goes toward it. Crashing the net means skating hard to the front of the other team's net when your team shoots. Say your defenceman winds up for a shot from way out. The defencemen are the two players on each team who mostly stay back. You're sure the goalie's going to save it.

PLAYER: Then I wouldn't bother going.

COACH: Go anyway. When a teammate shoots, skate hard to the net, and get there under control. Do it every time, even when you think the goalie will make the save.

PLAYER: Why, if it's going to be saved?

COACH: A clean shot from far out is one a goalie can usually handle. What beats a goalie is a tip, a rebound, or a player blocking the goalie's view. A tip is when your stick touches the puck on its way to the net and changes its direction. A rebound is when the puck bounces back out off the goalie. All of those need someone in front of the net when the shot happens. That someone is usually you.

PLAYER: And every single time?

COACH: Every time. The other team usually gets to the rebound first. The only way you win one is to decide to go before the shot, not after you see where the puck went. If nobody goes, a rebound can never become a goal.

PLAYER: Should I get there as early as I can?

COACH: Time it well. If you're skating in from the boards, try to arrive as the shot is taken. Get there too early, and a defender has time to tie you up and turn you away from the puck.

PLAYER: Where do I stand when I get there?

COACH: Just outside the crease. The crease is the goalie's area, the blue paint in front of the net. The red line around its edge counts as part of it, so keep your skates off that line too. That space belongs to the goalie. And keep your stick on the ice, because that's where a tip happens.

PLAYER: What if one of their defenders is already standing there?

COACH: Keep your spot and let them try to move you. Don't push them, and don't lean on them. And never put your stick across someone.

COACH: Now four things that keep you safe at the net. First, know where the goal post is before the shot, so you don't run into it. Second, stand braced, with your knees bent, your feet wide and your knees giving, so a push doesn't move you easily.

PLAYER: And the other two?

COACH: Third, keep your head up and your chin off your chest, and never duck. You want to see what's coming. Even going slowly, a post met head first can hurt your neck. And fourth, never go into the goalie. Stand in front of the goalie, not on top of them. If the goalie leans into you, move out of the way straight away, then get back in front. If you get pushed into the blue paint, get out, and go around the goalie, not through.

COACH: Third idea. When you have a good shot, take it. You come down your side with the puck, you've got a clear look at the net, and a teammate is already standing in front of it. What would you do?

PLAYER: Pass to someone in a better spot? I always look for one more pass.

COACH: A lot of wingers do. They pass when they should shoot. With a teammate already in front of the net, take the shot.

PLAYER: What's wrong with one more pass?

COACH: If you always look for it, the defenders learn it. They stop worrying about your shot and just wait for the pass. With a teammate at the net, a shot can turn into a rebound or a tip. The extra pass only gives the defence time to close in.

PLAYER: So I should never pass there?

COACH: It's a habit to lean toward, not a rule. If a teammate has a clear look at an open side of the net, pass it to them.

COACH: Let's stop and check the first three, because they're the ones that score goals. Can you give them back to me?

PLAYER: Stay in my lane and stay wide, so the defenders can't cover us all. Go to the net on every shot. And when I have a good shot, take it.

COACH: Good. And at the net, under control, just outside the blue paint, stick on the ice, head up, and never into the goalie.

COACH: Fourth idea. In your own end, stay high, and then come down the boards when your team gets the puck. Two words first. In whichever end you are in, high means toward the blue line. Low means toward the goal line, the line the net sits on. Now, the other team has the puck in your end. It goes down into the corner. What would you do?

PLAYER: Go down and help get it back?

COACH: That's the one to resist. Their defencemen stand up at the point. The point is the area just inside your blue line. Stay high, level with the defenceman on your side. Stand between that defenceman and your net. Don't chase the puck down into the corner.

PLAYER: What do I do from up there, though?

COACH: Two jobs at once. You stop their defenceman getting a free shot or walking in. And you're only a short trip from where your team will need you when it wins the puck. If you go into the corner, you lose both. Their defenceman is left free, and nobody is up the boards for your team to pass to.

PLAYER: Do I ever leave the point?

COACH: One time. Say the puck is in the corner, and your centre goes in to help your defenceman. If you're the winger on the far side, away from the puck, drop down into the slot. The slot is the space right in front of your net, between the two big faceoff circles. Stay high in it. The other winger stays on their point. Whoever sees it first should call it out.

PLAYER: Why does the slot need me then?

COACH: The slot is where most goals come from, and your centre usually guards it. When the centre goes into the corner, nobody is left there. When the puck leaves the corner, go back up to your point.

PLAYER: OK. And when we win the puck?

COACH: That's the second half. As soon as your team wins the puck, come down the boards toward your own goal line, to get open for a pass. That pass is how your team gets the puck out of its own end.

PLAYER: Why come down? I'm already up there, nearer their end.

COACH: A pass to you up at the blue line is long and flat, and it gets picked off. Coming down makes it a short pass instead.

PLAYER: When do I go?

COACH: That's the hard part. Turn your feet while your team is winning the puck, and go when the puck is yours. If you leave while the other team still has it, their defenceman is open.

PLAYER: Does every team play it this way?

COACH: No. Some ask you to follow one player wherever they go, even into the corner. So ask your coach how your team wants you to play in your own end.

COACH: Fifth idea. Winning the puck on the boards. A lot of the time, you'll have the puck near the boards with a player from the other team right next to you, trying to take it.

PLAYER: That's where I lose it most.

COACH: Then start with where the puck is. Keep it on the far side of your body, away from them. Your body becomes a wall between that player and the puck. If the puck is on that player's side, you've already lost it.

PLAYER: How should I stand?

COACH: Turn side-on, with your feet wide, and stay low. A wide, low player is very hard to move. A tall, narrow one gets knocked off the puck. And keep your feet moving. A player who stands still on the boards gets stuck there. A moving player can slip along the boards or spin away.

PLAYER: I usually work out where it's going once I've got it.

COACH: By then it's too late. Decide where the puck is going before you get to it. Once you're pinned against the boards, it's too late to choose. And have a second choice ready, in case the first one closes.

PLAYER: What if nothing's open? I tend to panic and just throw it somewhere.

COACH: Lots of players do. Panic spends time you actually had, and a blind pass is worse than a safe play. The safe play here is to send it hard along the boards to a teammate further round. That's called a rim, and it's a good play. It works because it goes past the player trying to take the puck. Never try to force a pass through the middle of your own end. A pass picked off there gives the other team the puck right where it hurts most.

COACH: Now the most important safety part of this episode. Whether the other player is after the puck or just coming at you, two rules on the boards. Never turn your back to the other player, at the boards or anywhere on the ice. And never duck.

PLAYER: Is there one reason for both?

COACH: One. You want to see what's coming, and take it on your hip and arm, not on your head or neck. That's true even when the play is slow.

PLAYER: So what does that look like?

COACH: Skates parallel to the boards, which means pointing along them. Your forearm and your hip are the parts nearest the boards, never the point of your shoulder. Head up, chin off your chest. Never your back to the other player, at the boards or anywhere on the ice. Never duck.

COACH: Sixth idea. When the other team gets the puck, sprint back. Your team has just lost the puck in their end, and everyone turns to chase.

PLAYER: Where do I go?

COACH: Skate back hard, down your own lane. And on the way back, look for the player who is coming in late, behind the play.

PLAYER: Why them, and not the one with the puck?

COACH: Everyone else is watching the puck, so the late player is easy to forget. A player sneaking in late with nobody near them is one of the easiest goals a team gives away. Your job is to make sure that doesn't happen.

PLAYER: So what do I actually do?

COACH: Stay between that player and the puck. Keep your stick on the ice, in the space where a pass to them would go. Watch the player, not just the puck. Try to get back to your spot before the puck does.

PLAYER: What does the stick do?

COACH: Your stick in the passing lane can stop the pass before it gets there. And if a pass does get through, you're already right there with them. Don't hit the late player or hold them. Beat them to the ice with your skating.

PLAYER: And if I catch the player who has the puck?

COACH: Play the puck, not the player. Never put your stick or your body into anyone's back. If you can see the numbers on the back of their jersey, pull up and keep your stick on the puck.

PLAYER: Is it always my lane on the way back?

COACH: Who goes where is your coach's choice. Usually the wingers take the outside and the centre takes the middle. Ask your coach how your team does it.

COACH: Let me put all six together.

COACH: Stay in your lane, and stay wide, so one defender can't cover two of you.

COACH: Go to the net on every shot, under control. Just outside the blue paint, stick on the ice, head up, and never into the goalie.

COACH: When you have a good shot, take it.

COACH: In your own end, stay high until your team wins the puck, then come down the boards for a short pass.

COACH: On the boards, keep the puck on the far side and your feet moving. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the other player, never duck.

COACH: And when the other team gets the puck, sprint back, find the late player, and stay between them and the puck.

PLAYER: What should I ask my own coach?

COACH: Three questions. Where do you want me in our own end? Where on the boards do you want me when we get the puck? And on the way back, who covers the middle? Their answers tell you how your team wants you to play.

COACH: Once these six ideas feel natural, you are ready for more. The longer Playing principles episode on the wing goes deeper into each idea. The conversation episode talks it all through. Start there next.
