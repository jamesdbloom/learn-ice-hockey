===== SCRIPT START =====

COACH: Picture this. A teammate is about to pass you the puck, and someone from the other team is skating straight at you. What you do next decides whether your team keeps it.

PLAYER: That's usually the moment it goes wrong for me.

COACH: Then this episode is for you. This is The Basics, topic five. Passing and catching the puck. The Basics is for newer players and younger players. It covers the core things to get right first. I'm the coach, and with me is a newer player, asking the questions you might ask. There are eight ideas, and we'll take them one at a time.

COACH: First idea. Move the puck, then move yourself. Picture this. You pick up the puck in the middle of the ice. A teammate is open off to the side. What's your first thought?

PLAYER: Probably, where can I skate?

COACH: That's the habit to change. Your first thought should be, who is open?

PLAYER: Why pass? I could just skate it.

COACH: Because the puck travels faster than any skater can. A pass makes the other team look around again and work out who to cover. If you carry it, they can just skate alongside you. You can't out-skate five people. But you can out-pass them.

PLAYER: So I should never carry it?

COACH: No, carrying is fine sometimes. Carry it when nobody is open, or to give your teammates time to get open. But think pass first.

PLAYER: And once it's gone?

COACH: Keep moving. Skate to a new open spot, so you can get the puck back.

COACH: Second idea. Look before the puck gets to you. Picture a pass coming to you along the boards. The boards are the wall around the rink. You watch only the puck. It lands on your stick, you look up, and the other player is already on you. In this episode, the other player means the player from the other team who is closing in on you, whether they're after the puck or just coming at you.

PLAYER: That's exactly what happens to me.

COACH: So while the pass is still on its way, take a quick look over your shoulder. Then look back at the puck before it lands on your stick. That quick look is called a scan. A turn of your head to look around, and then back.

PLAYER: Won't I miss the puck?

COACH: Not if your eyes are back on it before it lands. And that look costs you nothing. While the puck is travelling, your eyes have nothing else to do. If you wait until it lands, the other player is on you, and the picture has changed while you looked.

PLAYER: What counts as a proper look?

COACH: A real turn of your head. Turn until your chin goes past your shoulder. Just moving your eyes shows you very little.

PLAYER: Does it matter which shoulder?

COACH: Look over both, not just your favourite one. A surprise usually comes from the side you weren't planning to use. And keep skating while you look.

PLAYER: OK, I've looked. Then what?

COACH: Decide, while the puck is still coming. Pick your next play, and say it to yourself in a word or two. Up the wall. Back to my defenceman. Your defencemen are the two players on each team who mostly stay back. Then pick a second choice too, in case the first one disappears.

PLAYER: Can't I just wait and see what's there when it arrives?

COACH: Decide before the puck arrives, and catching it starts your next play straight away. Decide after, and the other player has time to reach you. A good play made now beats a perfect play made too late.

PLAYER: One problem. I stare at the puck the whole time.

COACH: That's the thing that makes all this possible. You can only look around if you don't have to stare at the puck. So keep the puck out in front of you, and keep a soft grip.

PLAYER: What's my grip got to do with my eyes?

COACH: Tight hands can't feel the puck, so you keep looking down to check it's there. You can practise this off the ice. Stickhandle while a friend holds up fingers for you to call out. If you look down, you miss the fingers.

COACH: Third idea. Pass to where your teammate is going, on the side away from the other player. Picture your teammate skating fast up the side of the ice, and you've got the puck. What would you do? Where do you aim?

PLAYER: At them? Right at their stick?

COACH: That's where the pass goes wrong. Think about what happens while the puck travels. They keep skating. A pass aimed at where they are now is behind them by the time it gets there. They have to reach back, slow down or turn. All of that kills the speed that made the play dangerous. So pass to the spot just ahead of them, where they can skate onto the puck without slowing down.

PLAYER: Like throwing a ball for a dog that's already running. You throw it ahead of the dog, not at it.

COACH: That's it. To know where they're going, watch their skates, not their chest. Their skates show where they're really heading.

PLAYER: And if I can't tell?

COACH: Lead them a little. A pass slightly ahead can be skated onto. A pass behind can't.

PLAYER: What's the side away from the other player?

COACH: If the other player is coming from your teammate's left, pass to their right. Then your teammate's body sits between the puck and the other player, like a shield.

PLAYER: Even if that puts it on their backhand?

COACH: Even then. Your blade is the flat part at the bottom of your stick, the part that touches the puck. The forehand is the side of the blade that cups around the puck. The backhand is the other side. Most players control the puck better on the forehand. But the side away from the other player matters most.

COACH: Leading your teammate also keeps them safe. A puck that lands behind them, or in their feet, makes them look down to find it. If the other player is arriving near the boards just then, their head is down at the worst moment. Heads down near the boards is how players get hurt.

PLAYER: So where I put the puck decides where their head is.

COACH: Yes. Then the pass itself. Sweep the puck along the ice. Don't slap at it. Finish low, with your blade pointing at where you want the puck to go.

PLAYER: What's wrong with a slap?

COACH: A slap only touches the puck for an instant, and it goes wide. A sweep guides the puck the whole way.

COACH: Let's stop and check the first three, because they make every pass work. Can you give them back to me?

PLAYER: Move the puck, then move myself. Look over my shoulder while the pass is coming, and decide my next play before it lands. And pass to where my teammate is going, on the side away from the other player.

COACH: Spot on.

COACH: Fourth idea. Get open, and give your passer a target. Your teammate has the puck, looks up, and there's nobody to pass to. Everyone is standing behind someone from the other team.

PLAYER: So that's the passer's problem?

COACH: It's yours too. A pass needs two players. When you don't have the puck, your job is to be easy to pass to. Move into a lane. A lane is a clear line between the puck and you, with no stick or skate in the way.

PLAYER: So I just skate about until I'm free?

COACH: Not quite. Skating hard through a spot where an opponent's stick is in the way doesn't help. And get there on time, not too early. If you arrive too early, a defender arrives with you. Then show a target. Put your blade flat on the ice, out in front of you, and hold it still. And call for the puck.

PLAYER: Why does it matter if my stick's still?

COACH: A still blade on the ice is the passer's aiming point. If your stick is waving in the air, the passer has to guess. The passer is looking for someone who is open, in a clear lane, right now. If nobody is, there is no pass.

PLAYER: So when a pass goes wrong, it isn't always the passer's fault.

COACH: Not always. When passes keep going wrong, look at the players without the puck first.

COACH: Fifth idea. Cushion the puck, and send your first touch into your next play. Catching a pass with your stick is called receiving, and the player catching it is the receiver. Say a hard pass is coming at you, and you're holding your stick stiff.

PLAYER: It bounces off.

COACH: A foot or two away. A stiff blade is like a wall. And that foot is all the other player needs to take the puck. So meet the pass out in front of you, and hold your stick loosely. As the puck touches your blade, let the blade give a little, back toward you. A soft blade stops the puck dead, ready to play.

PLAYER: Then I've got it. What next?

COACH: Make that first touch count. As you catch it, push the puck to where your next play needs it. Away from the other player. Or forward, so you can keep skating. Or out to the side, ready to shoot.

PLAYER: What's wrong with just stopping it, and then deciding?

COACH: A puck stopped under your feet has to be moved again before you can do anything. That costs you time. Here's a test for after every catch. Could you pass or shoot straight away? If not, that touch wasted time.

PLAYER: Do I stop skating to catch it?

COACH: No. Keep your feet moving as you catch it. A player standing still to catch a pass is easy to reach.

PLAYER: What about a really bad pass?

COACH: Stop it first, with your stick, your skate or your shin pad. Then look up and play it. Don't try to catch a hard pass and make a play in one go.

PLAYER: So getting my body in front of it is OK. Is that like blocking a shot?

COACH: For a pass, yes. But it isn't like blocking a shot. This is only for a pass. Stepping in front of a shot is a different skill, so don't try it from this.

COACH: Sixth idea. Catch the puck with your body open to the ice. Think about how you wait for a pass. Are you facing your passer square on, with your back to everything else?

PLAYER: Yes. That's how I normally stand.

COACH: Then try it the other way. Open means your hips and shoulders are turned so you can see up the ice as the puck arrives. Come to the puck on an angle, not straight at the passer. Decide which way you're going before the puck arrives, and be pointing that way when it does.

PLAYER: What do I gain by turning?

COACH: Your next play is already in front of you when the puck lands. If you have to turn around to find it, the other player has had the time they needed. And this matters most next to the boards. With your back to the play, the other player arrives behind you, and you can't see them coming.

PLAYER: So how should I stand by the boards?

COACH: This is the most important safety part of the episode. Come in on an angle, with your feet apart and your knees bent. Skates parallel to the boards, which means pointing along them. Your forearm and your hip are the parts nearest the boards, never the point of your shoulder. Head up, and chin off your chest. Never your back to the other player. And never duck.

PLAYER: That's a lot to remember. Is there one reason behind all of it?

COACH: One. You want to see what's coming, and take it on your side, not on your head or neck. That's true even going slowly.

PLAYER: What if I'm already facing the boards and can't turn?

COACH: Put your stick and gloves up on the glass above the boards, spread wide, and keep your head off it. That's only for when things have gone wrong. It isn't a way to stand.

COACH: Seventh idea. In your own end, make the safe pass. Your own end is the part of the rink where your own goal is, behind your own blue line. Say you've got the puck there. A teammate is open on the far side, and there's a gap right across the front of your net. What would you do?

PLAYER: Pass it across? It looks open.

COACH: Don't make that pass. In your own end, never pass across the front of your own goal. Never pass blindly up the middle, to someone you can't see. And never pass across the ice from one side to the other.

PLAYER: Why is my own end so different from theirs?

COACH: The middle of your own end is right in front of your own net. A pass picked off there is a chance against you, with your goalie not ready. In the other team's end, a lost pass costs you the puck. In your own end, it can cost you a goal.

PLAYER: So in my end, I pass only when I'm sure. In theirs, I can take more chances.

COACH: Exactly.

PLAYER: But what if someone's right on me, and nobody's open?

COACH: Make the pass you know you can complete. Send the puck hard along the boards to a teammate. Play it off the boards past the other player. Or pass it back to your defenceman.

PLAYER: Doesn't that feel like giving up the puck?

COACH: No. Those are all good plays, not failures. The boards are always there, and nobody can step in front of them. And know your safe play before you reach the puck, not after you're trapped. Players who look calm under pressure didn't think faster. They decided earlier.

PLAYER: So are those nevers rules, or just habits?

COACH: Habits. Common habits, not laws. Some teams build a planned pass behind the net into their play. So ask your coach which passes your team allows in your own end.

COACH: Eighth idea. Don't panic pass. Here's a moment you'll know. The puck comes to you, someone from the other team is charging in, and before you've even looked, the puck's gone. Off your stick, to nobody in particular.

PLAYER: That's me. I just want to get rid of it.

COACH: That's a panic pass. A pass made without looking, just to get the puck away from you. Panic is the normal feeling when you're new to the game. And panic spends time you actually had.

PLAYER: But it feels like I've got no time at all.

COACH: It nearly always feels shorter than it is. Every rushed pass, and every puck thrown to nobody, was a moment with more time than it felt. So ask yourself one question after every shift. Did I have more time than I thought? The honest answer is almost always yes.

PLAYER: So how do I stop? Just be braver?

COACH: Nobody can act on "be braver". The fix is the look from idea two. Get one more look before the puck reaches you, so you already know your answer when it arrives. And have your second choice ready. Panic is what happens when you run out of choices in the middle of a play.

COACH: Let's try one. You're outside your own end, out in the middle of the ice. The puck comes to you, someone is closing fast, and you haven't seen a teammate. What would you do?

PLAYER: Pass it anyway? Someone might be there.

COACH: That's the panic pass. When you don't know where to pass, keep the puck. Turn your shoulder to the player coming at you, with the puck on your far side, away from them. Never turn your back to them, anywhere on the ice. Keeping it costs you a second. A pass into the other team's players is a turnover, which means the other team gets the puck. Near the boards, stand the way idea six showed you.

PLAYER: So I just hang on to it?

COACH: Out there, for a second, yes. But not in your own end. In your own end, holding on to it is how you lose it. One look, one decision, and move it. That's where the safe passes from idea seven come in, like hard along the boards, or back to your defenceman.

PLAYER: Is there anything that helps in the moment?

COACH: Listen. If a teammate or your goalie shouts "time", it means nobody is close to you. Believe them. They can see what you can't. Slow down, lift your head, and make the good play. And if you're the one calling it, be sure. If you're not sure, say nothing. A false "time" sends a teammate into a hit they weren't ready for.

COACH: Let me put all eight together.

COACH: Move the puck, then move yourself.

COACH: Look over your shoulder while the pass is coming, and decide your next play before it lands.

COACH: Pass to where your teammate is going, on the side away from the other player. Leading them keeps their head up, too.

COACH: When you don't have the puck, get into a clear lane, on time, with your blade flat on the ice, and call for it.

COACH: Cushion the puck with soft hands, and send your first touch into your next play.

COACH: Catch it with your body open to the ice. By the boards, skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the other player, never duck.

COACH: In your own end, never pass across the front of your own goal, and make the pass you know you can complete.

COACH: And don't panic pass. Look before the puck arrives. If you can't see a good pass, keep it for a second, shoulder to them and never your back, except in your own end, where you move it.

PLAYER: What should I ask my own coach?

COACH: Two questions. Which passes do you want us to make in our own end? And when I'm trapped on the boards, where do you want the puck to go? Their answers tell you how your team wants you to play.

COACH: Once these eight ideas feel natural, you are ready for more. The longer Playing principles episodes on passing, and on scanning, go deeper into each idea. The conversation episodes talk it all through. Start there next.
