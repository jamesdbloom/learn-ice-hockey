===== SCRIPT START =====

This is The Basics, topic one. How a game works.

The Basics is a series for newer players and younger players. Each episode covers the core things to get right first, and why each one works. Once these feel natural, the longer Playing principles episodes and the conversation episodes go deeper.

This first topic is about the game itself. Where everyone plays, what the lines on the ice mean, and the two rules that decide where you can go. They are called offside and icing. Don't worry. Both are simpler than they sound.

Before the ideas, a few words this episode uses. I will explain each one now, so nothing gets in the way later.

The boards. This is the wall around the rink. The puck stays in play when it bounces off them.

The goal line. This is the thin red line at each end, across the whole width of the ice. The net sits on it.

The crease. This is the goalie's area, the blue paint in front of the net. Treat the red line around its edge as part of it.

The blue lines. There are two of them, wide blue lines across the ice. They split the rink into three parts, called zones.

The centre red line. This is the red line across the middle of the ice, halfway between the two nets.

A faceoff. This is how play starts again after the whistle. The official drops the puck between two players, and they try to win it for their team. It happens at one of nine painted dots on the ice.

A shift. This is your turn on the ice, from when you go on until you come off.

The penalty box. This is where a player sits when they get a penalty.

That is all the words. Now seven big ideas, one at a time, and the reason each one works.

First idea. Know who is on the ice, and leave the goalie's space alone.

Each team has six players on the ice. Five skaters and one goalie. The skaters are three forwards and two defencemen. The forward in the middle is the centre. The other two forwards play on the sides, and they are the wingers. The two defencemen mostly stay back, nearer their own net.

The goalie is the one player allowed to catch the puck and hold it.

The crease belongs to the goalie. Don't skate through it. When you go to the net, stand just outside the paint, and keep your skates off its red line too. Never go into the goalie.

Here is why it works. The goalie needs that space to see the puck and move. A player standing in the paint, or bumping the goalie, can get a goal taken away from their own team. Bumping the goalie can be a penalty too.

And one safety point for warm-ups and practice. Never shoot at a goalie who is not ready. A ready goalie is facing you, set, with their stick down. If the goalie is having a drink, fixing a strap, or talking, they are not ready.

Here is why. A goalie who isn't watching can't protect themselves from the puck. A shot they don't see can hurt them.

Second idea. Say our end and their end.

The two blue lines split the ice into three zones. The zone with your own net is your defensive zone. The zone with the other team's net is your attacking zone. The middle one, between the blue lines, is the neutral zone.

Here is the part that confuses everyone. The zones are named after your team, not after the building. The same piece of ice is your defensive zone and the other team's attacking zone, at the same time.

And it moves. Most games are split into three periods, and the teams swap ends after each one. So the end you defend in the first period is the end you attack in the second.

Here is why it works to say our end and their end. Those words always follow your team. If a coach says get it deep, it means deep in their end. If you think of the zones as fixed places in the building, you can end up in the wrong half of the rink, doing exactly what you were told.

Third idea. Keep your shifts short, and go hard while you are out there.

A good shift lasts about thirty to forty five seconds. Go as hard as you can, then come off and let a teammate on. New players often stay out far too long.

Here is why it works. Hockey is short bursts of full effort. A tired player is slow and sloppy. They end up reaching with their stick instead of moving their feet. A short, hard shift keeps you fast every time you are out.

When you change during play, change when your team has the puck, or the puck is going toward the other team's net. Never change when the puck is coming at your net. Get right to your bench before your teammate steps on.

Here is why. A change is a gap in your team. Make it when the other team can't use it. And if both of you are on the ice, and either of you plays the puck, touches another player, or joins in, your team has too many players. That is a penalty.

Younger age groups often play these differently, so ask how your games work.

Let me stop and repeat the first three ideas. Know who is on the ice, and stay out of the goalie's paint. Never shoot at a goalie who is not ready. Say our end and their end, because the zones follow your team, and teams swap ends. And keep your shifts short, and go hard.

Fourth idea. The whistle stops play, and a faceoff starts it again.

The whistle goes for lots of reasons. A goal. A penalty. Offside or icing. The puck going out of the rink. The goalie holding on to the puck.

Every time, play starts again with a faceoff at one of the nine dots.

When the whistle goes, stop playing the puck. Leave it for the official to pick up. Don't shoot it away, and never fire it out of the rink.

Here is why it works. After the whistle, the puck is no longer yours to play. Shooting it away can get you a penalty. And the pushing around the net after a whistle is where players hand the other team penalties. Your job doesn't end at the whistle. It ends when you have skated away calmly.

Fifth idea. Offside. The puck goes into their zone before you do.

Here is the rule in plain words. When your team attacks, the puck must cross the blue line into their zone before you do. You are offside if both your skates are completely over the blue line before the puck has completely crossed it.

Skates decide, not sticks. Your stick can be in the zone. And the puck must cross the whole line. A puck sitting on the line is not in yet.

So here is the trick. You don't have to slow down at the blue line. Keep your back skate on the line, and drag it along the line, until the puck is in. Keep the blade touching the line. Never lift it.

Here is why it works. The blue line is wide, about a foot across. While one skate is touching it, you are still onside. That is true in every rulebook. So you can get your body and your stick into the zone at full speed, and still be onside when the puck arrives. If you lift that back skate off the ice, some rulebooks call you offside.

Go in too early, and you are offside, and your attack is usually over.

Sometimes the official raises an arm instead of blowing the whistle. That means leave the puck and the player who has it alone, skate back and touch the blue line, and wait until all your teammates have too. Then you can attack again.

Sixth idea. Icing. Get the puck past the centre red line before you send it down the ice.

Here is the rule in plain words. If your team shoots the puck from your own half of the ice, all the way past the other team's goal line, and nobody touches it, that is icing. Play stops.

Your own half means your side of the centre red line.

Icing costs your team. The faceoff goes back into your own end, next to your own net. And in many games, the tired players who iced it are not allowed to change before that faceoff.

So here is the habit. Carry the puck past the centre red line, on your stick, before you send it in deep. One extra stride is often the whole difference.

Here is why it works. Once the puck itself has crossed the red line, you can send it into their end and it isn't icing. You keep the puck up the ice, and your team keeps its chance to change.

Some leagues split the ice at a different line. So ask your coach where the line is in your games.

And when you clear the puck out of your own end under pressure, keep it down. Off the boards and the glass, and out of your zone, is good. Over the glass and out of the rink can be a penalty.

Younger age groups often play offside and icing differently, so ask how your games work.

Seventh idea. Keep your stick on the puck, and move your feet.

Some rule breaks are penalties. When you get one, you go to the penalty box for a set time, often two minutes. For most penalties, your team plays with one fewer skater while you sit there. So the other team has one more skater than you. That is called a power play.

The penalties new players get most often are stick penalties. Hooking someone with your stick. Hitting their stick or their body with a chop. Tripping them. Grabbing and holding them.

So keep your stick on the puck, and off the other player.

Here is why it works. Those penalties are what a beaten player reaches for instead of skating. If a player gets past you, don't reach. Turn and skate. Moving your feet is the cheapest way there is to stay out of the penalty box. It keeps your team at full strength.

Let me put it all together.

Know who is on the ice. Stay out of the goalie's paint, keep off its red line, and never go into the goalie. Never shoot at a goalie who is not ready.

Say our end and their end, because the zones follow your team.

Keep your shifts short, go hard, and change when the puck is going the other way.

When the whistle goes, leave the puck for the official.

For offside, the puck goes in first. Drag your back skate on the blue line until it does.

For icing, get the puck past the centre red line on your stick before you send it deep.

And keep your stick on the puck, and move your feet.

Three questions to ask your coach. Do our games use offside and icing the usual way? Which line counts for icing? And when do you want us to change?

Ask them. Their answers tell you how your games work.

The next topic in The Basics is your gear and staying safe. And once these seven ideas feel natural, you are ready for more. The longer Playing principles episodes on the Rink Map and the Rules Primer go deeper. The conversation episodes on Getting Started, the Rink Map and the Rules Primer talk it all through. Start there next.
