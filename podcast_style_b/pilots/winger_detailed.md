===== SCRIPT START =====

This episode is about playing on the wing. It takes its time. We will build the job up one piece at a time, and for each piece I will tell you what to do and also why it works. If you know why something works, you can still do the right thing when the play looks a little different from the picture in your head.

Before any instructions, a few words you will hear all the way through. I will explain them now, so they don't get in the way later.

Your lane. Picture the rink split lengthways into three strips. The middle strip belongs to your centre. The strip down your side, next to the boards, is yours. That strip is your lane.

High and low. In whichever end you are in, high means toward the blue line, and low means toward the goal line, the line the net sits on.

Strong side and weak side. The strong side is the side of the ice the puck is on. The weak side is the other side. So the same winger can be strong side one moment and weak side the next.

The point. This is the area just inside the blue line where defencemen usually stand. It is an area, not the line itself.

The half-wall. This is the stretch of boards roughly level with the faceoff dot, between the corner and the point.

The slot. This is the ice in front of the net, between the faceoff circles. Its top part, between the faceoff dots and the tops of the circles, is called the high slot.

That is the vocabulary. Now the job.

A winger's game is decided on two patches of ice. One is the front of the other team's net. The other is your own lane. Most of what follows comes back to one of those two.

I will start with four big ideas, because they matter most. If you only listen to the first part of this episode, take those four with you. After that we will go back through the rink, one end at a time, and add the detail.

First idea. Crash the net every time a shot goes toward it.

Crashing the net means skating hard to the front of the net when a teammate shoots. Here is why it matters so much. A goalie handles a clean shot from the point most of the time. What beats a goalie is one of three things. A tip, where a stick changes the direction of the puck. A screen, where a body blocks the goalie's view. Or a rebound, a puck that bounces back out off the goalie. All three need a player in front of the net at the moment the shot is taken. On your team, that player is usually you.

Coaches agree that rebounds and tips are where a winger's goals come from. That is coaching know-how, not a counted ranking.

And here is why you go every time, even on shots you are sure the goalie will save. The defending team gets to most rebounds first. The only way you win one is to decide to go before the shot, not after you have seen where the puck went. If nobody goes, a rebound can never become a goal.

Now the timing, because it depends on which job you have.

Sometimes you are driving in from the boards or from your faceoff circle. Then you arrive as the shot is released, not well before it. Get there early, and a defender has time to tie you up and turn you away from the puck. Get there too late, and the rebound is already covered.

Other times, the front of the net is your assigned job. Then the timing runs the other way. You are planted there before the shot, standing on the line between the goalie's eyes and the puck. A screen you are still walking into is not a screen yet. That split between the two jobs is coaching know-how, not a measured finding.

Where exactly do you stand? Just outside the crease. The crease is the goalie's area, the blue paint in front of the net. The red line around its edge counts as part of the crease too, so keep your skates off the red line, not just out of the blue. Keep your stick on the ice, ready for a tip. Your job there is to take away the goalie's view without getting into the goalie's space.

Now the part that keeps you safe there.

At the front of the net your eyes are on the puck, and they should be. That means a defender pushing you sideways is someone you feel, not someone you see. And the path that defender moves you along has a steel goal post at one end and the goalie at the other.

So do two things. First, know where the near post is before the shot is taken, so you don't find out by hitting it. Second, take the shove braced. Wide base, knees bent, weight low, knees and hips giving rather than locked stiff.

And keep your head up, chin off your chest, and never duck, so you never meet a post head first. That holds even at slow speed, and in every league, because a box-out, where a defender holds you away from the net with their body, or a shove, can put you there.

There is one body at the net front you never take braced, and that is the goalie's. Never go into the goalie. If the goalie leans into you, give ground straight away, then set the screen again.

So that is the first idea. Go to the net on every shot, timed by your job, just outside the crease, stick down, head up, and never into the goalie.

Second idea. Win the puck on the boards.

A lot of a winger's game happens along the boards, usually with an opponent right on top of you. The player trying to take the puck off you is called the checker. Here is how you keep the puck.

Keep the puck on the far side of your body from the checker. Then your body is a wall between the checker and the puck. If the puck is on the checker's side, you have already lost it.

Get side-on to the checker, with your hip and backside toward them, and your feet wide and low. A wide, low base is very hard to move. Standing tall and narrow is why players get knocked off pucks. If your league doesn't allow checking, hold your spot and don't bump or lean on them; in a Canadian adult league on the CARHA rulebook, stay out of the contact.

Keep your feet moving. A player standing still on the boards gets pinned there. A player with some speed can slip along the boards or spin out toward the middle. Only spin off someone who's already leaning on you — never off someone still coming at you, however slowly.

And decide where the puck is going before you get to it, not once you are pinned.

Two things override all of that, at any speed.

Never turn your back to the checker. And never duck.

Both protect your head and neck. Turn your back, and you can't see the hit coming or brace for it. Duck, and your head meets the boards first. That is true even at slow speed, so a slow checker doesn't make it safe.

So here is the position you aim for. Skates parallel to the boards. Forearm and hip to the boards. Never the point of your shoulder, and not the whole shoulder if you can help it. Your shoulder faces the checker. It is the boards it should not meet. Head up, chin off your chest.

If you are already facing the wall and can't turn parallel, facing it square is the last resort, not a choice. Get your stick and gloves up and spread on the glass, with no head contact at all. And never turn your back to the checker.

That position matters more than anything else in this second idea. From here on I will use one short version of it, the same words every time, so you can learn it like a line. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the checker, never duck.

Third idea. In your own end, stay high, then come down the boards the moment your team wins the puck.

Let me set the picture first. The other team has the puck in your end. Their two defencemen are up at the points. Your own defencemen and your centre are down low, near the net and in the corners, fighting for the puck.

Where are you? High, level with their defenceman on your side, between that defenceman and the slot. Not drifting down into the corner.

Why there? It pays you twice. First, it stops their defenceman getting time to shoot or walk in. Second, it leaves you one short trip from where you will pick up the puck when your team wins it. Drift into the corner and you lose both. Their defenceman walks in free for a shot, and nobody is on the boards for your team to pass to.

That is the job in one system, called the low zone collapse. It is a coaching choice, not a law. Teams do this differently, so you need to ask which way yours plays.

Now the second half of the idea. The moment your team wins the puck, your job flips. You come down the boards to between the hash marks and the goal line. The hash marks are the short red lines on the side of the faceoff circle, level with the dot. That is where you take the first pass out of your end. That clean exit from your own end is called the breakout.

The hard part is when to go. Leave before your team has the puck, and you have left the point open while they still own it. Leave late, and the pass to you at the blue line is long and flat, and it gets picked off. So turn your feet as the battle is being won, and go as the puck becomes yours.

Fourth idea. When the puck turns over, get back through your lane and pick up the late forward, not the puck.

A turnover means the other team has just taken the puck. Now they are coming at your net, and you have to get back.

Sprint back inside your lane. While you skate, look for one player in particular. When a team rushes up the ice, their third forward often arrives late, behind the play, while everyone is watching the puck. That player is called the trailer. The trailer is the one you pick up.

Why the trailer and not the puck? Because in the systems this episode assumes, the defencemen take the puck, and the forwards take lanes and players. A trailer sneaking in unmarked is one of the cheapest goals a team gives up off a rush. That is coaching agreement, not a counted figure.

Stay between the trailer and the puck, goal side of them, which means between them and your net. Keep your stick in the passing lane, not on them. Watch the player you are covering, not the puck, and get to your spot before the puck does.

Never hit the trailer. And whoever you catch up with, the trailer or the player with the puck, if you can see their numbers, pull up and play the puck. A hit on a player's back is checking from behind, whether they have the puck or not, and in most leagues it gets you thrown out of the game.

Let me pull those four ideas together, because they are the core of the job.

One. Crash the net on every shot. If you are driving in, arrive with the shot. If the net front is your job, be planted before it. Just outside the crease and off its red line, stick on the ice. Know where the post is, take the shove braced, head up, and never into the goalie.

Two. On the boards, puck on the far side, feet moving, decision made early. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the checker, never duck.

Three. In your own end, stay high until your team wins the puck, then come down the boards for the first pass.

Four. When the puck turns over, sprint back through your lane, find the late forward, and if you see their numbers, pull up.

Everything else in this episode adds detail to one of those four. So now let's go back through the rink, starting in your own end.

Fifth idea. Your own end, and the system your team plays there.

Staying high is the job in one system. Here are the three you are likely to meet, and how each one works.

The first is the low zone collapse, the one we have been describing. Each player covers an area rather than a person. The battle down low belongs to the defenceman on the strong side and the centre. You stay high on the point and you do not go into the corner. Coaches often describe this as the system taught first in minor, junior and recreational hockey in North America. That is coaching consensus, not a survey.

The second is man-on-man. Here each player has a person to cover, not an area. You follow your player wherever they go, corner included. The points get covered by whoever's player is up there.

The third is a hybrid. It mixes the two. Down low, close to the net, you play man-on-man. Up high, you play the area. So you follow your player down only once they cross a line your team picks. Some teams put that line at the faceoff dots. Some put it at the hash marks. Some put it at the tops of the circles.

Why does this matter so much? Because coaches say a breakdown in your own end is usually two players playing different systems, not a lack of effort. Both players are working hard, just to different plans. So ask which system your team plays, before your first shift, and if it is a hybrid, ask where the line is.

For the rest of this episode, I will assume the low zone collapse.

Now three times when you leave your spot, even in the collapse.

First, if the defenceman you are covering skates down off the point toward the battle, follow them a few feet to stay with them. Don't chase all the way into the corner. And call it out, so your centre or your defenceman knows you have moved with your player.

Second, if the puck squirts up the boards toward you, go and get it. That is not a corner battle. That is your breakout starting, so grab it and get your feet moving.

Third, say your centre has gone into the corner to help your defenceman. Now there is a hole in front of your net where the centre usually is. So the winger on the far side, the weak side, drops into the high slot to fill it. As soon as the puck leaves the corner, that winger goes back up to the point. Anyone with a clear view can call that switch.

One more job up at the point. When their defenceman winds up to shoot, get into the shooting lane, between that defenceman and the net, so the shot has to go through you or go wide. Do it close to the shooter, on your feet, and square to them. Head out of the lane. Stick flat on the ice beside you, never out in front. Hands behind your body, with the backs of your gloves out. If you can't get there in time, take away their pass to the far side instead, and let your goalie see the shot. Don't stand halfway, where you neither block it nor stay out of your goalie's eyes.

Sixth idea. Getting the puck out, and why you come down the boards.

Your team has won the puck. You have come down the boards to between the hash marks and the goal line. The defenceman with the puck might pass to you, to the centre, or to their partner, depending on who is open.

That spot on the boards belongs to the conventional breakout, called the swing breakout, which is the one this episode assumes. Here is why it is built that way. You started high, so the trip down is short and you are the first player ready for the pass. Taking it lower keeps the pass short. A winger who never leaves the point is as wrong as one who left too early. A pass to a winger standing at the blue line is long and flat, it gets picked off, and it arrives at your back.

Not every team runs the swing. Some hold you higher, toward the blue line. Some send one winger up the ice early. In some breakouts the centre leaves early up the middle, and the weak-side winger drops in to give the low support instead. So ask which breakout your team runs. A winger standing at the hash marks when their team wanted them up the ice is just as wrong as the reverse.

When the pass comes to you, move it straight away. Carry it out along the boards, or pass to the centre if the centre is in a better spot. Your first look is always up the ice. Don't hold the puck in your own end longer than you need to.

Now say you get the puck with a checker right on you. It is the boards battle again. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the checker, never duck. Don't turn into the pressure the instant the puck arrives. Take it, take the contact, then spin off. Only spin off someone who's already leaning on you — never off someone still coming at you, however slowly.

And if nothing is open, you have two simple outs. Rim it, or reverse it.

A rim is a hard pass around the boards that follows the curve of the rink to a teammate further round. A reverse sends the puck back the way it came, to a teammate arriving behind you.

They are opposite plays, and you choose by where the checker is. A rim beats a checker who is sealing you against the boards, because it goes past the checker. A reverse beats a checker who has come in too hard and skated past you, because it goes into the space the checker just left.

Make that choice before the puck reaches you. While the pass is still travelling, take a quick glance over your inside shoulder. Then look back at the puck just before it arrives.

Never force a pass through the middle of your own end. Rimming it is a completely good play. It moves the puck past the pressure, and it gives your team time. A pass picked off in your own end gives them the puck where it hurts most.

What about your free hand, the one not on your stick? It can fend the checker off. It can never grab, and it never pushes off to make space. In most leagues a forearm or an open hand to keep the checker away is allowed, though not in every league, and the conditions vary. Close your fingers on their jersey or their stick, and it is holding, which puts you in the penalty box. In some leagues holding can get you thrown out of the game. So the safe habit everywhere is simple. Keep your free hand on your own stick, and win the boards with your feet and hips.

One last thing about leaving the point. Getting ready early is good, as long as you could still get back if you have read it wrong. Leaving the player you are covering just because you hope your team will win the puck is not. Watch the battle, not the puck, and read who is going to come out of it with the puck. The test is whether you could recover if you are wrong.

Seventh idea. The backcheck, and the systems behind it.

The main job, once more. Sprint back through your lane, pick up the trailer, stay between the trailer and the puck. Now the detail.

Who takes which lane on the way back is a coaching choice. The usual way is wingers outside and the centre in the middle. Some teams give the middle to whoever gets back first, so as a winger you can end up there. Others pick up man-on-man, starting from the puck and working outward. Whichever it is, somebody owns the middle, and nobody assumes it is somebody else.

Some teams coach the opposite on purpose. It is called hound the puck. The first forward back chases the puck carrier all the way in, and the defencemen hold the middle. That is a real system. So ask for yours by name. What is wrong under any system is deciding in the middle of a rush to leave the player you are covering and double the puck, because nobody else on the ice expects it.

So how close can you get to the trailer? Take the ice in front of the trailer, not the trailer. Get there with your skating. Never hold them back with your stick or your free hand. In some leagues, once you are beside or ahead of the trailer, you can use your body to make their path to the puck longer, but never by changing your lane or your speed to hit them. In others you can't, and there you shadow the trailer, keep only the lane you already have, and avoid contact. So check your league. And a stick laid across a player without the puck is a penalty, not a backcheck.

Now the hit you must never make. As a backchecker you are coming from behind, every time. Checking from behind counts whether the player has the puck or not. Hit a player in the back and in most leagues you are thrown out of the game. Drive them into the boards when they can't protect themselves and you will almost certainly be thrown out, and in some leagues it is automatically a match penalty, which goes past being thrown out.

So pressuring a carrier never means finishing a check into the back of a player skating toward their own end boards. Take the puck, take the ice, or angle them off it. Then settle into your own-end job. Cover your point, and be ready to break out.

Let me pull your own end together before we move up the ice. Find out which system your team plays, and if it is a hybrid, where the line is. In the collapse, stay high, and know your three exceptions. Come down the boards for the first pass as the puck becomes yours, not before. Rim it or reverse it rather than force it through the middle. Free hand on your own stick. And on the backcheck, take the ice, not the player, and never anyone's back.

Eighth idea. The neutral zone. Hold your lane and stay wide.

The neutral zone is the middle of the rink, between the two blue lines. When your team moves up the ice through it, with the puck or without it, stay in your lane along the boards.

Here is why width matters. Their defenders come back in a flat line across the ice. A flat line can't cover three forwards who are spread wide, not unless it spreads thin. Drift into the middle, and one defender can cover two of you. And your width does something for your centre. It pulls the defenders apart, and the gap between two defenders, called the seam, is the space your centre uses.

Now the rushes. When there are more of you than defenders coming back, it is called an odd-man rush. The key read on an odd-man rush is the defender, not the goalie.

On a two-on-one, with the puck on your stick, drive toward the net from your lane, so the one defender has to choose. Stop short of the goalie. If the defender slides across to take the pass, shoot, low to the far pad, which makes a rebound for your teammate. If the defender steps to you, the pass is on. If you are the one without the puck, drive to the far post with your stick on the ice. That is where the pass and the rebound both arrive.

On a three-on-two, take your defender wide with speed. If no lane to the net opens, pull up along the half-wall and let the late teammate arrive into the space behind you, rather than forcing it into two defenders. That is the common approach. Some coaches want the two-on-one carrier to pass early and make the goalie move instead. Ask which your team wants.

Ninth idea. When they have the puck in the neutral zone.

What you do here depends on your team's system, and there are two very different kinds.

The first kind is called containment. Shapes you may hear named are the one-two-two, the one-two-two trap and the one-three-one. The whole idea is to deny the middle of the ice and wait for the other team to make a mistake. Your job is to stay in your lane, block the passing lanes on your side, and force the carrier wide toward the boards.

Why not chase? Because in containment, the shape only works if everyone holds their spot. Leave your lane to chase the puck, and you open a gap. The one defenceman behind the line can't cover two lanes, so the other team uses the gap you left.

The second kind is an aggressive press. Here the instruction reverses. Forwards hunt the puck, and the winger on the far side does leave their lane to pressure. Why would a team do that? Because the point of a press is to take the puck back, not wait for it. The team accepts the gap in exchange for winning the puck sooner. The discipline in a press is to pressure together, not on your own.

Most teams play two or three of these, depending on the score and the shift. So ask which one, and what the call is to switch.

Tenth idea. Under pressure in the neutral zone, dump it in on a read, not by default.

Say you have the puck, and two players are closing on you near their blue line. Don't try to stickhandle through them. Coaches say this is where odd-man rushes against you get made, from turnovers in the middle of the ice. Not every one starts here, and nobody has counted them.

But that doesn't mean you should dump it in every time. Tracked games in top professional hockey found that carrying the puck into the zone was worth more than dumping it in, and that players give the puck up at the blue line too easily. That was top professional hockey, not your league, so take the direction, not the numbers. Dump it when nobody is arriving with you, or when your support is late.

You take more dump-ins than anyone on your team, so learn the kinds.

A hard rim goes around the boards behind their net to the far corner. It beats a defence that has crowded into the middle, and it arrives where your first forechecker is heading.

A soft dump is a gentle chip into an empty area, meant to stop dead rather than bounce. Use it when you know you will win the race to it. A hard dump there would just give it to their defenceman.

A chip off the boards past a defenceman who is stepping up lets you skate onto the puck yourself. That one keeps the puck for your team. It is the best choice against a defenceman stepping up at their own line.

A cross-corner dump goes into the far corner, away from where their defence is heading, so they have the longest skate to get it.

And a flip, lifted high over the middle, beats a trap, a team sitting back and clogging the neutral zone. It lands in the open ice behind their defence.

Then chase it properly, because the chase matters more than the dump. Don't skate straight at the puck. If you do, the player getting it can go either way. Come at it on an angle instead. Take away that player's easier way out, usually the boards on the puck side. Then they are forced toward where your support is arriving. A dump-in with nobody chasing on an angle is just giving the puck away.

One thing to watch when you dump it. Icing is shooting the puck from your own half across the other team's goal line, with nobody touching it. Play stops, and the faceoff comes back to your end. You are the player most likely to do it, and dumping from behind the centre red line is the classic way.

Two fixes. Carry it one more stride, so the puck is past the red line on your stick before you let it go. Then, in most leagues, the dump is fine however far it travels. Or make the dump stop short of their goal line. A flip from your own side of the red line has to land past their defence but before the goal line. And if you do ice it and chase, and you are clearly beaten, ease up. You gain nothing, and a crash into the end boards can hurt you.

Eleventh idea. Arriving at their blue line.

You are the player who hits their blue line widest and fastest, so this one is yours to get right.

Offside means both of your skates have completely crossed their blue line before the puck has. So one skate still touching the line keeps you onside. That gives you a technique. Drag your back skate along the line while your body, stick and speed get ahead of it. Done right, you are a full stride into the zone, at speed, and still onside.

Keep that blade touching the ice. Don't lift it. A dragged, touching skate keeps you onside wherever you play, and a lifted one doesn't, in some leagues.

Keep your head up as you cross. Your body, hands and stick are in the zone before your skate is, in the ice a defenceman standing up at their own line is defending. You can't brace for a hit you can't see.

And don't coast at the line to be safe. Arriving late and slow costs your line more than a whistle would.

If you do go in too early, there is often a second chance, called a delayed offside. The linesperson's arm goes up, but play carries on. Your team fixes it by tagging up, clearing the zone and touching the blue line. That means all five of you, not just you. Nobody goes back in until every teammate has cleared. As the winger who is usually first to the line, you are the one who goes back in too early. So tag up, then look before you go. Not every level plays delayed offside this way, so ask how it works in your games.

Let me pull the middle of the ice together. Hold your lane and stay wide, because a flat line can't cover three wide forwards. On a two-on-one, drive the net and make the defender choose. When they have the puck, know whether your team contains or presses. Dump it in on a read, pick the right kind, and chase it on an angle. Carry it past the red line before you dump it. And at their blue line, drag your back skate with the blade on the ice, and keep your head up.

Twelfth idea. In their end, shoot when the look is yours, and never stand still.

Your spots in their end are the half-wall, your own faceoff circle, and the side of the net.

From the half-wall, you have three things to look for. A shot. A pass to your centre in the slot. Or a pass back to your defenceman at the point. If you have space to drive the net, do it, under control, and never into the goalie. A winger standing still is easy to cover, so keep moving.

Most wingers pass up good shots rather than take bad ones. That is coaching know-how, not a counted finding. Here is why it matters. If you always look for one more pass, the defence learns it. They can sit in the seam and wait for the pass instead of coming at you. So when a teammate is already at the net front, take the shot from your circle or the half-wall. The extra pass only gives the defence time to close.

Shoot first is a habit to lean toward, not a rule. A teammate with a clear look at an open side of the net is always worth the pass. What you are breaking is the habit of always looking for one more.

From your faceoff circle, be ready for a one-timer, a shot straight off a pass without stopping the puck. Set your feet before the pass arrives, with your body open toward the passer and your weight already on your back foot. Start your backswing as the pass is released, not when it arrives. Hit the ice just behind the puck. Aim low to the far side, or for a tip. And if the pass is bad, don't force it. A missed one-timer usually ends up in the neutral zone going the other way. Take the extra touch.

What about the high slot? It usually isn't yours, unless your team's setup sends you there. Don't crowd it when someone is already there, and find out who that should be rather than assume it is the centre. The front of the net, right in close, is a different patch of ice, and it is very often yours.

Now say you are in the corner, under pressure. It is the boards again. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the checker, never duck. Protect the puck and wait for your centre to come and help, rather than forcing it out through heavy traffic. A rushed pass out of the corner gives the puck away.

And before you pass back to the point, look up. Your defenceman may have stepped down toward the top of the circle to help.

When nothing is open, cycle it. Protect the puck down the boards and leave it on the boards behind you for the teammate coming in low, usually your centre. Then skate up and away from the wall, to get open for the return pass or a shot.

Here is why the cycle works. The defender who followed you down the boards is now out of position. The one who stayed is facing two of you. And if the boards are sealed, reverse it behind the net to the far side instead.

That low cycle is the common approach. Some teams go back up to the point early and get bodies to the net. Ask which yours runs.

Thirteenth idea. The forecheck.

Forechecking means pressuring the other team in their own end, to win the puck back rather than skate back and wait.

The forecheck has three roles, called F one, F two and F three. They are set by who arrives first, not by position. Whoever gets to the puck first is F one. The second player in is F two. The highest is F three. So if you dumped it in and got there first, you are F one, even with your centre on the ice. Don't wait for the centre.

As F two, come in behind F one on the puck side. Read where F one has pushed the carrier, and take the carrier's next way out, usually the boards or behind the net. Pin the puck against the boards, not the player. Never wrap the player up against the boards with your arms. That is holding.

As F three, the highest forward, stay up in the zone and read the play. In the usual setup you cover the middle and the far side. That stops an opponent slipping up the middle, and it covers a pass across the ice to the far side. And you are ready to go the other way the moment your team wins the puck.

Some teams play it differently. In the stacked version, F three shifts to the puck side instead. All three forwards overload one corner, and the far-side pass out is given up on purpose. Teams also differ on how many go deep. A two-one-two forecheck sends two forwards in. A one-two-two sends one. Ask which your team runs.

On the forecheck, the angle you take matters more than how fast you go. If everyone chases the puck, the other team just passes around you. So stay in your role, and trust your linemates to be in theirs.

Fourteenth idea. The front of their net, in more detail.

Back to the front of their net, because there is more to know.

Screening from outside the crease, without contact, is one of the most useful things you do. Body out of the blue paint, skates off the red line around it. Staying off that line is the answer that holds wherever you play.

Keep your stick out of the crease too, even with both your skates outside, or it can cost you the goal.

Take the goalie's eyes, never the goalie's movement. Standing so the goalie can't move in the crease can cost your team the goal, even from outside it.

Leave the goalie a path home. If the goalie has come out to play the puck and is heading back, don't stand in the way. Blocking that route on purpose can be a penalty with no contact at all. In some leagues it always is, and the goal comes off as well.

If a defender shoves you into the crease, get out, and go around the goalie, not through. Being pushed in doesn't excuse running into the goalie when you made no effort to avoid them. You can still be penalised, the goal can still be taken away, and if the goalie is hurt you can be thrown out of the game, and in some leagues that is automatic. What counts is that you tried to avoid them, and a last-second try may not be enough. So crash the net under control.

Never go into the goalie, in or out of the crease. Deliberate contact with a goalie is a penalty everywhere, whether or not the puck goes in. Charging into one, which means hitting hard after building up speed, can get you thrown out of the game in every league, and in some that is automatic. A charge meant to hurt the goalie is a match penalty in most leagues. So stand outside the crease to block the view, and never go into the goalie at all, slow or fast.

Make room with your feet, never with your stick across someone. A cross-check means using the shaft of your stick, held in both hands, to hit or shove a player. It is a penalty whoever you put it on. If it rides up toward the head, injures them, or is reckless, you can be thrown out of the game. In several leagues that is automatic, whether you meant it or not. And under USA Hockey, plan on a match penalty for a reckless one, which goes past being thrown out.

Never put your stick or your body into anyone's back at the net front. The player boxing you out usually has their back to you, close to the post, and a goalie's back is no different. Hold your ground, or work that player from the side. Where body checking isn't allowed, keep your body off the defender and tie up the defender's stick with yours instead, no chops and never a hand on it. Hit a player from behind and in most leagues you are thrown out of the game. Drive them into the goal frame when they can't protect themselves and you will almost certainly be thrown out, and in some leagues it is automatically a match penalty.

Now, the leagues where body checking isn't allowed. Holding the spot you already have is fine anywhere. Shoving someone off their spot isn't allowed where body checking is barred, with your body as well as your stick. There, hold your spot without leaning on anyone to keep it, and make them move you. In a lot of youth, girls' and women's, and adult hockey, that is the game you are playing. So find out whether your league allows body checking before you play.

And even where hitting is allowed, don't pile a hit onto a player who is already battling someone else for the puck. In some leagues that is a penalty in every game, and a reckless one gets you thrown out.

Let me pull the front of their net together. Take the goalie's eyes, never the goalie's movement or path. Outside the crease, off the red line, stick out of it too. Shoved in, get out and go around the goalie. Never into the goalie, never a stick across anyone, never into anyone's back. And where checking isn't allowed, hold your spot rather than shove for one.

Fifteenth idea. Faceoffs.

Your centre takes the draw. You line up at the hash marks. One winger stands on the inside hash, facing the slot. The other stands on the outside hash, facing the boards. Usually the winger whose natural side matches the faceoff spot takes the outside, but teams vary, so ask. Your job on every draw is the same. Read who wins it and react straight away. Jump on a loose puck, support your centre, or get to your defensive spot.

Don't creep inside the circle or over your hash marks before the puck drops. If you do, your centre gets thrown out of the faceoff, not you, and your team takes the draw with its second choice. Do it twice on the same draw and it is a penalty, and in some leagues the penalty is yours. Keep your stick blade back too.

Keep your hands to yourself as well. In most leagues a glove or a shoulder on the player beside you before the drop costs your centre the draw, exactly like a skate over the line. In a non-checking game it can put you in the penalty box too. So stand still until the puck lands. And give the official room to get out of the circle.

Be ready to take the draw yourself. Centres get thrown out several times a game. Know before the draw whether you are the backup. Go for a clean tie-up of the other player's stick with yours, never a hand on it. And have a second name ready, in case it can't be you.

Once you step in to take the draw, contact with the other player before the drop can be a penalty as well as a violation, and in some leagues that is automatic. Get low from your knees and hips, not by pushing your head out over the dot. If the linesperson tells you to move back, move back the first time.

Even with a safe stance, two heads end up a few inches apart on every draw. No rule makes that space safe. Keep your head back.

Sixteenth idea. The power play, when your team has an extra player.

On the power play you are almost always in one of two spots, the half-wall or the front of the net.

From the half-wall you are the main passer on your side, and you have four options. The point. The bumper, the player in the slot between the point and the front of the net. The net front. And the far half-wall, through the seam.

Here is why the half-wall is such a good job. The penalty-killing team can take away any one of those four options, but not all four. So walk down toward the goal line to drag a defender with you, then use the space you just made.

From the net front, your job is screening, tips and rebounds. Get in the goalie's eyes. Body out of the crease, skates off the red line, and don't start contact or crowd the goalie. Deliberate contact with the goalie is a penalty whether or not you score, so it can cost you the power play you are on.

From either spot, move the puck quickly. A penalty kill can cover any one option, but not three in two seconds. And ask whether the bumper is yours. It is usually the centre, but plenty of teams put a winger there.

Seventeenth idea. The penalty kill, when your team is a player short.

Your job is to shrink the ice, not to chase the puck. Your team plays in a shape, and the shape only works if you hold your place in it.

In a box, the standard shape, you are one of the two high players, each covering one point.

In a diamond, the centre usually takes the top, and you take a middle spot, covering a half-wall.

In a wedge plus one, you are often the plus one, the single player working outside the triangle. That job is pressure, not pursuit. Get at whoever has the puck. When the puck moves, you stop and drop into the triangle, and the nearest player steps out as the new plus one. If you follow the puck around the outside, the power play pulls the shape apart and beats it. The route you take out to the puck is your coach's choice, so ask which one you run.

Force the players at their points into poor shots. How much your team blocks shots at all is a coaching choice, so ask your coach and your goalie what they want.

If you block, here is the whole of it, and it goes together. Do it from close and on your feet. Square to the shooter. Head out of the shooting lane. Hands behind your body, with the backs of your gloves out. Stick flat on the ice beside you, never out in front, because a puck off a blade held out in front tends to ride up toward your face. That last part is coaching know-how, not measured. Tucking in behind your shin pads is for when you go down, not for standing. Standing, it folds you forward and puts your head down into the lane. Going down to block is a different technique, and this episode doesn't teach it. And never block from in front of your own goalie's eyes. A puck deflected off a body your goalie couldn't see is one they can't save. Block properly, or get out of the lane and let them see it.

Clear the puck whenever you get it. Don't be fancy. On a penalty kill, shooting it the length of the ice is usually allowed without icing being called, but not at every level, so ask about yours. And don't chase the puck around the outside and pull the shape apart. That is exactly what the power play wants.

Eighteenth idea, and it is short. Line changes.

Some games, especially for younger players, change lines in a set way, and then your coach and the officials tell you how. In games where your line chooses when to change, change as a group, with your centre. When the centre calls it, go with them, rather than staying out for one more look. Here is why. A line that drifts off one player at a time spends part of every shift a player short in the middle of the ice.

In those games, never change while the puck is in your own end and your team doesn't have it. In the collapse, you are the point coverage. Leave, and their defenceman is alone at the blue line with the puck coming to them.

Let me pull it all together.

Start at their net. Crash it on every shot. Driving in, arrive with the shot. On the net-front job, be planted before it. Just outside the crease, off the red line, stick on the ice, and never into the goalie. Know where the near post is, take a defender's shove braced, and keep your head up.

On the boards, puck on the far side and feet moving, with the decision made before you get there. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the checker, never duck. Free hand on your own stick.

In your own end, stay high, level with their defenceman, until your team wins the puck. Then come down the boards for the first pass. Rim it or reverse it rather than force it through the middle.

When the puck turns over, sprint back through your lane, find the late forward, and if you see their numbers, pull up.

Through the middle, hold your lane and stay wide. Dump it in on a read, and chase it on an angle. Drag your back skate at the blue line, with your head up. And in their end, shoot when the look is yours, and never stand still.

Much of this depends on what your team plays. So here are five questions for your coach, and two for your league. Which system do we play in our own end, and if it is a hybrid, where is the line? Which breakout do we run, and where on the boards do you want me? On the backcheck, who takes the middle? In the neutral zone, do we contain or press? Who takes the draw if our centre gets thrown out? And for the league. Which rules do we play under? And is body checking allowed?

Go and ask. Their answers tell you how your team wants you to play, and now you know why each answer makes sense.
