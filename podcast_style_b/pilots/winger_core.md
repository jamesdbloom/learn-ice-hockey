===== SCRIPT START =====

This is The Basics, topic ten. Playing wing.

The Basics is a series for newer players and younger players. Each episode covers the core things to get right first, and why each one works. Once these feel natural, the longer Playing principles episode and the conversation episode on the wing go deeper.

Before the ideas, a few words this episode uses. I will explain each one now, so nothing gets in the way later.

A winger. In a full game, your team has three forwards on the ice. The one in the middle is the centre. The other two play on the sides, one on the left and one on the right. Those two are the wingers.

Your lane. Picture the rink split into three long strips, from one end to the other. The middle strip belongs to your centre. The strip down your side, next to the boards, belongs to you. That strip is your lane. The boards are the wall around the rink.

High and low. In whichever end you are in, high means toward the blue line. Low means toward the goal line, the line the net sits on.

The point. In your own end, this is the area just inside your blue line, where the other team's defencemen stand. The defencemen are the two players on each team who mostly stay back.

The crease. This is the goalie's area, the blue paint in front of the net. The red line around its edge counts as part of it.

Crashing the net. This means skating hard to the front of the other team's net when your team shoots.

A rebound. This is when the puck bounces back out off the goalie.

A tip. This is when your stick touches the puck on its way to the net and changes its direction.

That is all the words. Now six big ideas, one at a time, and the reason each one works.

First idea. Stay in your lane, and stay wide.

When your team is going up the ice, stay out wide, near the boards on your side. Don't drift into the middle.

Here is why it works. The other team's defenders come back in a line across the ice. That line can't cover players who are spread out wide, not without spreading thin. If you drift into the middle, one defender can cover two of you. And when you stay wide, you pull the defenders apart. That opens the middle for your centre.

So staying wide isn't you being out of the play. It is you making room for the whole line.

Second idea. Crash the net every time a shot goes toward it.

When a teammate shoots, skate hard to the net, and get there under control. Do it every time, even when you think the goalie will make the save.

Here is why it works. A clean shot from far out is one a goalie can usually handle. What beats a goalie is a tip, a rebound, or a player blocking the goalie's view. All of those need someone in front of the net when the shot happens. That someone is usually you.

And here is why you go every time. The other team usually gets to the rebound first. The only way you win one is to decide to go before the shot, not after you see where the puck went. If nobody goes, a rebound can never become a goal.

Time it well. If you are skating in from the boards, try to arrive as the shot is taken. Get there too early, and a defender has time to tie you up and turn you away from the puck.

Stand just outside the crease, and keep your skates off its red line too. That space belongs to the goalie. Keep your stick on the ice, because that is where a tip happens.

If a defender is already there, keep your spot and let them try to move you. Don't push them, and don't lean on them. And never put your stick across someone.

Now four things that keep you safe at the net.

First, know where the goal post is before the shot, so you don't run into it.

Second, stand braced, with your knees bent, your feet wide and your knees giving, so a push doesn't move you easily.

Third, keep your head up and your chin off your chest, and never duck. You want to see what is coming. Even going slowly, a post met head first can hurt your neck.

And fourth, never go into the goalie. Stand in front of the goalie, not on top of them. If the goalie leans into you, move out of the way straight away, then get back in front. If you get pushed into the blue paint, get out, and go around the goalie, not through.

Third idea. When you have a good shot, take it.

Lots of wingers pass when they should shoot. If a teammate is already in front of the net, take the shot.

Here is why it works. If you always look for one more pass, the defenders learn it. They stop worrying about your shot and just wait for the pass. With a teammate at the net, a shot can turn into a rebound or a tip. The extra pass only gives the defence time to close in.

But it is a habit to lean toward, not a rule. If a teammate has a clear look at an open side of the net, pass it to them.

Let me stop and repeat the first three ideas, because they are the ones that score goals. Stay in your lane and stay wide, so the defenders can't cover you all. Go to the net on every shot, under control, just outside the blue paint, stick on the ice, head up, and never into the goalie. And when you have a good shot, take it.

Fourth idea. In your own end, stay high, and then come down the boards when your team gets the puck.

When the other team has the puck in your end, their defencemen stand up at the point. Stay high, level with the defenceman on your side. Stand between that defenceman and your net. Don't chase the puck down into the corner.

Here is why it works. You do two jobs at once from up there. You stop their defenceman getting a free shot or walking in. And you are only a short trip from where your team will need you when it wins the puck. If you go into the corner, you lose both. Their defenceman is left free, and nobody is up the boards for your team to pass to.

Now the second half. As soon as your team wins the puck, come down the boards toward your own goal line, to get open for a pass. That pass is how your team gets the puck out of its own end.

Here is why you come down. A pass to you up at the blue line is long and flat, and it gets picked off. Coming down makes it a short pass instead.

The hard part is when to go. Turn your feet while your team is winning the puck, and go when the puck is yours. If you leave while the other team still has it, their defenceman is open.

Teams play this differently. Some ask you to follow one player wherever they go, even into the corner. So ask your coach how your team wants you to play in your own end.

Fifth idea. Winning the puck on the boards.

A lot of the time, you will have the puck near the boards with a player from the other team right next to you, trying to take it.

Keep the puck on the far side of your body, away from them. Here is why. Your body becomes a wall between that player and the puck. If the puck is on that player's side, you have already lost it.

Turn side-on, with your feet wide, and stay low. Here is why. A wide, low player is very hard to move. A tall, narrow one gets knocked off the puck.

Keep your feet moving. Here is why. A player who stands still on the boards gets stuck there. A moving player can slip along the boards or spin away.

Decide where the puck is going before you get to it. Here is why. Once you are pinned against the boards, it is too late to choose.

If nothing is open, send it hard along the boards to a teammate further round. That is called a rim, and it is a good play. It works because it goes past the player trying to take the puck. Never try to force a pass through the middle of your own end. A pass picked off there gives the other team the puck right where it hurts most.

Now the most important safety part of this episode. Two rules on the boards.

Never turn your back to the other player near the boards, whether they are trying to take the puck or just coming at you. And never duck.

Here is the one reason behind both. You want to see what is coming, and take it on your hip and arm, not on your head or neck. That is true even when the play is slow.

So here is how you stand by the boards. Skates parallel to the boards, which means pointing along them. Your forearm and your hip are the parts nearest the boards, never the point of your shoulder. Head up, chin off your chest. Never your back to the other player. Never duck.

Sixth idea. When the other team gets the puck, sprint back.

When your team loses the puck, skate back hard, down your own lane.

On the way back, look for the player who is coming in late, behind the play.

Here is why that player matters. Everyone else is watching the puck, so the late player is easy to forget. A player sneaking in late with nobody near them is one of the easiest goals a team gives away. Your job is to make sure that doesn't happen.

Stay between that player and the puck. Keep your stick on the ice, in the space where a pass to them would go. Watch the player, not just the puck. Try to get back to your spot before the puck does.

Here is why that works. Your stick in the passing lane can stop the pass before it gets there. And if a pass does get through, you are already right there with them.

Don't hit the late player or hold them. Beat them to the ice with your skating.

When you catch up with the player who does have the puck, play the puck, not the player. Never put your stick or your body into anyone's back. If you can see the numbers on the back of their jersey, pull up and keep your stick on the puck.

Who goes where on the way back is your coach's choice. Usually the wingers take the outside and the centre takes the middle. Ask your coach how your team does it.

Let me put it all together.

Stay in your lane, and stay wide, so one defender can't cover two of you.

Go to the net on every shot, under control. Just outside the blue paint, stick on the ice, head up, and never into the goalie.

When you have a good shot, take it.

In your own end, stay high until your team wins the puck, then come down the boards for a short pass.

On the boards, keep the puck on the far side and your feet moving. Skates parallel, forearm and hip to the boards, never the point of your shoulder, head up, chin off your chest, never your back to the other player, never duck.

And when the other team gets the puck, sprint back, find the late player, and stay between them and the puck.

Three questions to ask your coach. Where do you want me in our own end? Where on the boards do you want me when we get the puck? And on the way back, who covers the middle?

Ask them. Their answers tell you how your team wants you to play.

Once these six ideas feel natural, you are ready for more. The longer Playing principles episode on the wing goes deeper into each idea, and why it works. The conversation episode talks it all through. Start there next.
